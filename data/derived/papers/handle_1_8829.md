# Epistemic Games With Emergent Systems Microworlds

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper is about designing learning environments that support students’ epistemically agentic participation in a science classroom. We use Collins and Ferguson’s idea of epistemic games to investigate students’ participation and learning with an Emergent Systems Microworld (ESM)-based learning environment. ESMs are computational models that are designed using agent-based representations and constructionist design principles. Qualitative analysis of student responses to embedded curricular questions reveals different epistemic games they engaged in using the ESM. An in-depth analysis of an epistemic game demonstrates how agent-based representations supported student investigations of a phenomenon related to natural selection. The findings illustrate how ESM-based learning environments can support students’ agentic participation in epistemic games to make sense of emergent phenomena.

## Introduction

Science educators, science education researchers, and designers of learning environments have argued that student participation in science classrooms should go beyon d ‘knowing about’ disciplinary ideas in science and  the practices of scientists and include authentic parti cipation in scientific inquiry practices to ‘make s ense of the world’ (Chinn & Malhotra, 2002; Miller et al., 2018; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 1991; Schwarz et al., 201 7). To design for such authentic and epistemically agentic participation, we build on two design theoretical frameworks, constructionism (Papert, 1980) and agent-based mode ls to investigate and learn about emergent systems phenomena (Wilensky, 2001; Wilensky & Rand, 2015).

We use ‘epistemic games’ as a theoretical construc t in this paper to analyze students’ participation in the learning environment that we designed. The notion o f epistemic games is inspired by Collins and Fergus on’s conceptualization of epistemic games (Collins & Ferguson, 1993). Another commonly used conceptualization of epistemic games in the field of Learning Sciences is Shaffer’s framework of epistemic frames and games (Shaffer, 2006). Though there are similarities between these conceptualizations at the fundamental level-both are about strategies related to knowledge construction-thei r operationalizations differ. Collins and Fergusson  define epistemic games as strategies and rules that practitioners of science use for constructing knowledge about natural phenomena whereas Shaffer’s work is about educational role-playing games which are framed as epistemic games (Arastoopour & Shaffer, 2013; Collins & Ferguson, 1993). In our work, we use Collin and Fergusson’s definition in the context of science education. We frame students’ epistemic games as strategies that they use to investigate and construct knowledge about natural phenomena in a learning environment designed for this purpose.

In this paper, we study students’ epistemic games i n a type of learning environments, which we call Emergent Systems Microworlds (ESM)-based learning e nvironments (Dabholkar et al., 2018). These learning environments use ESMs which are agent-based computa tional models that are designed as interactive constructionist microworlds (Edwards, 1995; Papert, 1980). We use the term ESM-based learning environm ent to include an ESM, curricular activities, and pedag ogical practices. We investigate how students engag ed in different epistemic games and how that engagement s upported their learning of disciplinary ideas. We a lso investigate how agent-based representations used in  the ESM supported learning of an emergent phenomen on modeled in the ESM. We first discuss theoretical and design frameworks that informed this work, which we see as our contributions to approaches for designing learning environments. Then we present our findings and discuss how they demonstrate the effectiveness of our design approach for supporting students’ agentic learning. Theoretical Frameworks Epistemic Forms and Games The theoretical framework used in our analysis is inspired by Collins and Ferguson’s notion of epistemic forms and games (Collins & Ferguson, 1993). Epistemic forms are the target structures that guide a scientifi c inquiry, and epistemic games are the rules and strategies that researchers use to generate epistemic forms. A metaphor that Collins & Ferguson (1993) use is the game tic-tac-t oe. The nine squares form a target structure to be filled and thus determine the epistemic form. The rules that players follow and the strategies they use to place crosses and noughts (Xs and Os) are part of the epistemic game.  Epistemic forms are generative frameworks with slo ts and ICLS2022 Proceedings 480 © ISLS constraints regarding filling those slots. The desired result of an epistemic game is to complete a target structure that corresponds to a particular epistemic form. Fo r example, making a list of invasive species in a p articular habitat is an epistemic game and the target epistemic form is the list itself. In this paper, we use these notions in a novel approach to inform the design of learning environments and analyze student learning with those curricula. Agent-based restructurations Another theoretical framework that is used for the design of the ESM-based learning environment is cal led the theory of restructurations (Wilensky, 2020; Wilensk y & Papert, 2010). The theory of restructurations i s about investigating how new representational forms reformulate knowledge in various disciplines often with dramatic effects. Agent-based modeling of emergent systems is an example of restructuration and consequently one of the central design features of an ESM. Dynamic computat ional agent-based representations are restructurati ons of emergent phenomena. While these are typically taught with differential equations or static models (Wilensky & Papert, 2010), the power (the analytical power to e xpress and investigate an emergent phenomenon) and learnability (cognitive, social, affective, and div ersity) properties of agent-based representations m ake them effective for investigating and learning about emergent phenomena. Design Framework Emergent Systems Microworlds The ESM design framework combines agent-based modeling and constructionism and is inspired by similar prior work (for example, see Wilkerson-Jerde & Wilensky, 2010) and a related design framework called Emergen t Systems Sandboxed (ESS) (Brady et al., 2015). The f ramework for design of ESMs and ESM-based curricula draws its theoretical foundations from Wilensky and  Papert’s theory of restructurations (Wilensky, 202 0; Wilensky & Papert, 2010). In addition to using agen t-based restructurations, ESMs are designed as inte ractive microworlds for students to explore and investigate phenomena (Dabholkar et al., 2018). A microworld, which is a constructionist design concept, is an encapsulated open-ended computational exploratory environment in which a set of ideas can be investigated that lead to knowledge construction (Edwards, 1995; Papert, 1980). Epistemic form and epistemic games with an ESM We use the Epistemic Forms and Games theoretical framework to analyze an ESM-based learning environment designed for students to engage in epistemic games to generate a desired epistemic form (Collins & Fer guson, 1993). The Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS)  pushes for the learning of practices in disciplina ry contexts, which can take various epistemic forms (N GSS Lead States, 2013). We developed one such form for pedagogical purposes, which we have used to design an ESM-based curriculum (Figure 1). Figure 1 A target epistemic form of an ESM-based curriculum that is aligned with NGSS recommended practices. This target structure in an ESM-based curriculum often takes the form of what Collins and Fergusson refer to as process analysis (Collins & Ferguson, 1993). Process analyses attempt to characterize behaviors of dynamical systems. ESMs are models of a dynamic complex system that provide easy access to visualization and investigation of emergent patterns that arise from agent interactions. Users have visual access to agent-level interactions and system-level emergent patterns. As evidenced in the data that we present later in this paper, when students use an ESM to engage in this epistemic form (Figure 1), their explanations about emergent properties of a system (such as a change in a population because of natural selection) are often based on the mechanistic details of agent integrations in the system (such as inheritance, predation, reproduction).

## Participants and setting

Evolution of Populations is a ten-day ESM-based bio logy unit designed by the lead author in consultati on with high school biology teachers. Ms. Lydia (pseudonym), a biology teacher in a large Midwestern city’s pu blic ICLS2022 Proceedings 481 © ISLS school, taught this unit in 2018 to freshman students in four classes (n = 88). 100% of the participating students were on a free lunch program (an indicator of belon ging to an underserved population). Students were predominantly of Hispanic or Latino (75%) and African American (11%) ethnicity. ESM-based Curriculum The ESM-based curriculum was designed to introduce students to population dynamics, prey-predator relationships in an ecosystem, and natural selection. Activities were delivered through an online curriculum portal (https://ct-stem.northwestern.edu/curriculum/preview/681/) and were split into lessons. Each lesson consisted of 3-4 pages. Typically, on each page, students read a  prompt with a description of a computational model  and suggestions for exploration. Then, students answere d 2-5 embedded assessment questions on each page. T he ESMs in the unit are built using NetLogo (Wilensky, 1999), an agent-based modeling platform that is intentionally designed to foreground emergent systems modeling for educational and research purposes. An ESM in this unit was designed as a computational  experimental system for students to investigate phenomena related to population dynamics, populatio n genetics, and natural selection. The design of th e ESM was inspired by a short film designed by Howard Hughes Medical Institute (HHMI)1 about natural selection and adaptation in populations of rock pocket mice. The HHMI film gives a geological history of the America n Southwest and discusses the research about changes in populations of rock pocket mice. Rock pocket mice belong to a species of mice Chaetodipus intermedius, which are mainly found in rocky outcrops in the d eserts of the southwestern United States and Mexico. Mice that camouflage well with the surroundings have a higher chance of surviving predation by avian predators. The mice that are found in sandy areas typically have a light fur-coat, whereas the ones found in rocky areas have a dark-c olored fur-coat. Evolutionary biologists and molecu lar geneticists who have studied these populations in New Mexico have attributed this adaptive variation in the color of fur coat primarily to mutations in a gene called the melanocortin-1-receptor gene, Mc1r (Nachman et al., 2003). We designed the curricular activities to scaffold s tudents’ use of the ESM to design and conduct investigations using the ESM (Figure 2). The curric ular activities included - (a) students watching th e HHMI video and deciding research questions they want to investigate about rock pocket mice, (b) students’ open-ended explorations of the ESM, (c) refinement of the research questions such they can be answered using the ESM, (d) scaffolded activities to understand specific aspect s of the ESM such as inheritance or predation, (e) final refinement of research questions, self-driven experimental investigations of those research questions. ## Methods

We performed a qualitative analysis of student resp onses to the questions in the ESM-based curriculum to investigate their epistemic games (Miles et al., 2014). Student responses to the following question were coded to identify their epistemic games: Come up with a question about natural selection in the case of the pocket mice, which can be answered using this model. One example of a question that could be answered using the model: If we introduce a mutant (with a darkfur-coat) in a population of mice with light-fur-coats that are living in a mixed background environment, how will to population change after 500 generations?

Note that this question in the curriculum provides students with an example question, which is likely to have guided framing of student responses. Neverthel ess, as seen in the data presented in the findings section, students designed several different kinds of epistemic games. ## Findings

The ESM supported designing and conducting experime nts to engage in various kinds of epistemic games. Students participated in five kinds of epistemic ga mes. The majority of students participated in mutan t survival (n= 31) and phenotype environment (n = 33) games (See Figure 4). The mutant survival game is the same as the one mentioned in the example research question provided to the students. This game involves introducing mutant mice in a specified environment and studying their survival. A phenotype survival game is about investigating the survival of mice of a specific phenotype (dark fur or light fur) in a specific environment (dark/light /mixed background, predators present/absent). Environment predation game is a more sophisticated game, which 7 students participated in. This game involved investigating the effects of predation rates on the survival of mice. 8 students played combinations of games – 7 played mutant survival + phenotype environment game and 1 played environment predation and phenotype environment game.

## Discussion

The findings of our study demonstrate how the ESM-b ased learning environment supported student engagement in different kinds of epistemic games, which helped them learn about disciplinary ideas by engaging in science practices in epistemically agentic ways.  Characterization of students’ epistemic games reve lated that they played five different kinds of epistemic games using the ESM. Each type of game is helpful to study different aspects of the natural phenomenon being investigate d and develop mechanistic explanations regarding th e phenomenon. The epistemic games that students playe d were not assigned to them or even pre-decided in the curriculum. These games were identified and charact erized based on students’ research questions. The E SM served as an experimental system or a playground fo r students to devise and play their epistemic games  to investigate those research questions. These findings demonstrate that the ESM design and ESM-based curriculum supported investigations of different aspects of a phenomenon by encouraging self-driven agentic learning. The qualitative analysis of Emma’s case demonstrate s how agent-based representations and constructionist design features of the ESM supporte d her participation in her epistemic game. Emma’s experimental design was aligned with her research question. It involved manipulation of the composition of the initial population by specifying agent properties a nd system-level environmental conductions. Her expe riment was meticulously designed, which included ten trial s. The data analysis helped her develop mechanical explanations regarding natural selection. Her answer shows how she considered the effect of agent properties and system-level environmental conditions in determining their survival and reproduction, which would in turn affect the passing of traits in the next generation and survival of those traits in the population. This analysis demonstrates how Emma’s epistemic game using the ESM for creatin g the epistemic form (Figure 1) helped her learn ab out some of the fundamental ideas regarding natural selection. In the future, we want to focus on how student groups in an ESM-based learning environment can learn from the epistemic games that other student groups in the class play.
