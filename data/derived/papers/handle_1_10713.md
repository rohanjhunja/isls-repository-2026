# Intricate but Often Overlooked Challenges of Elementary Students’ Programming with Educational Robotics: A Case Study

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The integration of educational robotics (ER) in K-12 classrooms has grown substantially recently, with research indicating a positive impact on academic achievement and learning. These educational robotics have been particularly beneficial in advancing students’ Computer Science and Computational Thinking (CT). While prior research has predominantly studied the effect of educational robotics on overall cognitive computational thinking skills, limited research has examined students’ robotic programming processes in detail. In this paper, we analyzed the findings of a case study, shedding light on the beyond CT frameworks faced by upper elementary students as they engaged with educational robotics. By offering a close-up examination, we found difficulties in understanding the robot’s serial program processing, that students adopt an egocentric perspective, and are unable to think in terms of the notion of ‘flow of control.’ These findings will be beneficial for teachers and curriculum designers in developing proper activities for upper elementary students.

## Introduction

Wing (2006) proposed that computational thinking (CT) is an essential skill for all and should be taught alongside reading, writing, and arithmetic. There is no single consensus on the definition of C T so far, while most scholars agree CT is a problem-solving process. We adopt the definition of the Int ernational Society of Technology Educators (ISTE), which defines CT as an essential literacy for all students, combining four components: decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, and algorithms. Decomposition is the ability to break  down a problem into smaller manageable pieces. Pattern recognition indicates finding certain patte rns or trends within similar problems. Abstraction entails identifying similarities and di fferences among similar problems to work toward solutions. Algorithmic thinking is the ability to set step-by-step instructions that can be used for automated problem-solving (ISTE, n.d.). Understanding these CT skills makes more effective and efficient use of technology and promotes problem-solving with and without technology. This skill set  is in high demand in the modern workforce and will benefit students in their future education and career prospects (Chen et al.,2017; Wing, 2006) Several research studies have demonstrated that educational robotics are effective tools for learning CT skills at the elementary school level (Bers et al., 2014; Chiazzes et al., 2019) as robotics provide engaging and fun contexts (Shute et al., 2017). However, the introduction of educational robotics does not naturally lead to the advancement of CT skills. For example, Chevalier et al. (2020) observed a tendency for students to often engage in unproductive trial-and-error. Huang & Looi (2020) reviewed a good number of studi es and found that unplugged activities, traditional paper-and-pencil activities without devices, are a  beneficial supplement to promote students’ CT skills. However, programming robots appear to have unique challenges for young students, which are often not evident in unplugged activities. Most prior studies only focused on students' challenges with programming tasks, such as  misconceptions that are related to understanding variables, loops, cond itional, and Boolean in programming languages. (Grover & Basu, 2017; Swidan et al., 2018). Our study aims to explore challenges in learning CT  skills using robots and  visual programming supplemented by unplugged activities. Our findings will be the base for developing interventions for the following studies.

## Research design, participants, and procedure

In this exploratory case study, four researchers closely observed seven upper elementary students (grades 4 and 5). Four of them were boys and three girls. These stu dents enrolled voluntarily for free robotics weeken d day camps in a Midwest suburban area in spring 2023.

Over a 5-week period, children worked on tasks presented in Table 1 for 3 hours every week. Each child was provided with an iPad  and a Lego Spike Kit, and the kids received instruc tions on programming at the beginning of each session. Participants were given tasks and mostly worked independently, a nd they were prompted to ask questions to researchers when they encountered difficulties. The researchers also provided assistance, such as scaffolding questions or prompts, when participants struggled with a certain task for too long. They were also encouraged to think aloud during the tasks. At the end of each session,  the researcher conducted a semi-structured interview with each child, focusing on the difficulties they had and how they solved them. This was followed by a debriefing session involving all four researchers, in which they discussed their observations of the children.

## Data analysis

According to Dyson and Genishi (2005), in a case st udy, a researcher is striving to understand “the me ssy complexity of human experience” (p. 3). That is, th e researcher is not only observing how children perform but should seek to understand the meanings children asc ribe to their own behaviors, i.e., the experiences from the children’s points of view (Baxter & Jack, 2008; Elliot & Timulak, 2005). Case studies encourage the use of various sources of data to provide a more holistic represen tation of the phenomenon (Yin, 2014).  Therefore, t he current study used multiple sources of data, including on-site observations, video recording s of children’s activities, artifacts (children’s programs and programming processes captured on screen recording with audio), individual interviews, and debriefing, and a total of 80-hour video recordings were analyzed.  All debriefing sessions were transcribed and coded to find themes, and researchers cross-confirmed themes using video recordings of children’s activities and screen recordings of children’s tablets.

We believe that underlying them is a fundamental mi sunderstanding about  how robots ‘think’ or function. As human beings, we can carry out several threads of thinking at any given time (some consciously, others subconsciously). For example, when people cr oss a street, they automatically monitor the traffi c on both sides of the street and can react to unexpected events. In contrast, the Lego robot executes the commands line by line and must be given explicit instructions  to check the environment. The participants programmed the robot as if it were a human. Participants tend ed to believe that once their robots are  told to check the environment, they will always do so, even without further instructions.

We also observed that children appeared to “ disregard” detailed instructions to robots but only remembered the key “signature” moves, especially when facing a rather complex task. For example, when Nancy was creating a program for her robot to go through the Pathfind ing challenge, which required the robot to avoid obstacles and reach the assigned goal using a color  sensor. She only included the turns the robot should make when meeting an obstacle but kept disregarding the ‘go forward’ command after turns. In a sense, Nancy translated her experience with her actions into code without c onsidering that many human actions are composed of sub-actions, often subconscious ly. In real life, when asked for directions to a certai n place, people will pick the “signature” moves, such as “turn left at the next s top sign,” and tend to ignore the insignificant events, assuming the questioner can subconsciously fill in the gap. However, robots need to be explicitly told to carry out each step. As a result, her robot couldn’t go beyond avoiding obstacles but kept spinning in front of the obstacles. In addition, she seemed to have trouble understanding the cause of the problem until a researcher reminded her to a dd a “go straight” command after each turn. Later interviews  revealed that participants concentrated their attention on the prominent steps deciding where the robot should be moving toward. Resorting back to an egocentric perspective when switching from unplugged activity to robotic programming To prepare participants to guide the robot through the complex maze, we provided a series of shorter programming tasks to train them to use loops and conditionals at the beginning of each session. Participants also learned how to make and use ‘MY BLOCK,’ which replaces a repeated long sequence of code blocks. In addition, participants were asked to jot down the instructions they would give to the robot line by line next to a paper version of the maze (unplugged activity) before programming. For those participants who could not identify patterns from the maze, the researcher prompted them to look for patt erns so that they could effectively use conditional  loops, variables, and ‘MY BLOCK’ to make their robot run  through the maze efficiently in unplugged activities. All 7 participants could identify the repeated pattern in the task. After successfully completing the unplugged activities, participants programmed (parallel plugged activities)  for their robots. However, participants demonstrated difficulties in programming their robots, although it looked exactly the same as the diagram presented on a paper worksheet.

P articipants applied  an egocentric perspective  (Clarke-Midura et al., 2021), seeing the physical maze from their view and going back to step-by-step sequences for each move instead of loops or “MY BLOCK.” For example, Tom programmed the robot to move his vehic le, following the arrows one after another  sequentially. One of the more productive and efficient ways was t o use a nested conditional loop because there are r epeated patterns when the vehicle faces the same color obstacles. However, Tom was not able  to identify those patterns as repeated chunks of the task to program with cond itional loops, even after a researcher prompted him to thi nk of repeated patterns. Like Tom, five other participants  appeared to have difficulty retrieving the  understanding they acquired in the paper-based (unplugged) exercise where they can have an allocentric reference frame, seeing the maze from a birds-eye view. They directly embodied themselves with their robots and started tackling the task with an egocentric view when working with the concrete maze, which caused difficulty finding patterns. Difficulty in understanding the ‘control flow’ Grover & Basu (2017) documented young students’ dif ficulties with loops already, but it is not yet cle ar what underlies this difficulty. We advance the hypothesi s that students do not think of a program in terms of ‘control flow.’ The ‘control flow’ means the order of execution of each command in a program. A nested loop means a loop is nested inside another loop. When the robot executes a nested loop, it runs the iterations of t he inner loop before exiting the inner loop to carry out the commands in the outer loop. This concept was a tough challenge for the participants to grasp. For example, Nancy was able to draw a square with her vehicle using a repeat lo op. When she was asked to draw two squares using two repeat loops, she was unsure about the number of repeats she had to put in for each loop.  She was supposed to add an outer repeat loop with 2 to repeat twice the inner loop that she drew a square earlier. However, it took several trials for her to truly understand the control flow would pass from the outer loop to the inner loop and then back to the outer loop. Things became more difficult for the participants when the researcher asked them to combine loops with conditionals. For example, when Tom was working on the Pathfinding challenge, he started with two conditionals (IF loop) separately with the REPEAT loop inside of each IF loop and put them together sequentially. He believed the robot should always be looking  for colored obstacles (refer to the  first theme). Also, in his belief, the subsequent codes would be executed whenever an “if” condition was met. However, he didn’t understand that the robot would carry out the first IF loop if it met a red obstacle, completing the REPEA T loop (inner loop) before reaching the second IF loop. In other words, the robot would not look for red and yellow at the same time.

## Discussion

This research aimed to understand how elementary students learn to program with an educational robot  focusing on encountered difficulties. Prior research rarely provided an in-depth description of the challenges and misconceptions for 4 th and 5 th graders that could be inhibiting  these students from moving forward with more complex and meaningful computational thinking skill s. This study fills this gap by examining students’ performance on unplugged and plugged activities. Three main recommendations for teachers and learning designers emerging from this exploratory case study can be summarized as:

• Students need to understand the difference between a robot’s serial processing and a human’s heuristic

parallel thinking to program their robot, applying an allocentric perspective.

• Students need more practice in breaking down a prob lem into codable chunks and finding repeated

patterns.

• Students need to learn to think in terms of the flow of control, namely that an instruction can be executed

even if it is not positioned within a sequence.

The findings of this study will provide insights in to the supplementary and scaffolding activities educators and researchers could design to help novi ce programmers recognize and overcome these difficulties. The findings would also shed light on the design of an effective curriculum that confronts unnecessary challenges and teaches CT skills efficiently using proper strategies.
