# Learner Pathway Analysis: An Approach for Evaluating STEM Persistence in Learning Ecosystems

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learning ecosystems provide children and youth with opportunities to explore STEM and non-STEM topics across in-school and out-of-school (OST) settings. Supporting learners in navigating the opportunities available to them across their local learning ecosystems is critical to ensuring learners can effectively investigate their interests. To do so, we introduce Learner Pathway Analysis (LPA), a Design-Based Research approach that operationalizes a pathways heuristic to evaluate STEM learning ecosystems. Buil ding on patient pathway analysis in healthcare and other fields, LPA assists in longitudinal tracking of individual participation and transitions across programs. We illustrate the approach with TGR Foundation’s afterschool programs, showing how LPA reveals patterns of enrollment, continuity, and transitions. By combining conceptual framing, principled measurement, and practical tracking tools, LPA provides a framework for evidence-informed design and coordination of connected, interest-driven STEM learning opportunities.

STEM learning ecosystems encompass a network of formal classrooms, afterschool programs, community organizations, and home environments, offering learners multiple pathways to explore and develop their interests (Bevan et al., 2015; Falk & Dierking, 2018).  While these ecosystems present rich opportunities, they also create challenges: learners must navigate diverse programs, topics, and transitions, often without consistent guidance or feedback about the alignment between their interests and available opportunities (NRC, 2015).

To address these challenges, we adopt a pathways heuristic grounded in ecological learning theories (Barron, 2006; Bronfenbrenner, 1994) and connected learning principles (Ito et al., 2013). Connected learning emphasizes linking personal interests, supportive relationships, and opportunities for academic and civic engagement, centering youth in their educational trajectories and connecting their passions to learning and career pathways (Ito et al., 2020). However, existing evaluation frameworks rarely capture learners’ multi-context trajectories, limiting insights into how social relationships, programs, and ecosystems collectively shape STEM engagement (Stephens, 2016; Maul et al., 2017).

We introduce Learner Pathway Analysis (LPA) as an approach to operationalize the pathways heuristic, providing a structured method for tracking learners’ transitions across STEM and non-STEM experiences, identifying gaps in opportunity, and diagnosing misalignments between learner interests and program offerings. This paper presents the Design-Based Research process for developing LPA, describes the framework’s conceptual and operational metrics, and demonstrates its application in a case study with TGR Foundation (TGR), a nonprofit organization that partners with a network of providers to deliver STEM and college readiness programs. By examining enrollment patterns, continuity, and transitions, we illustrate how LPA provides actionable insights for fostering equitable, interest-driven STEM learning pathways across connected learning ecosystems. The findings have implications for strengthening organizational strategies, systematically connecting youth interests to program offerings, and contributing to theoretical frameworks for studying and assessing these outcomes.

Connected learning and designing ecosystems to support STEM learning Our general approach to STEM learning is rooted in an ecosystems view that prioritizes  connected learning (Ito et al., 2020). Connected learning is an educational framework that emphasizes linking students' personal interests, supportive relationships, and opportunities for academic and civic engagement to create meaningful learning experiences. Rooted in the understanding that learning is most effective when it is relevant and socially connected, connected learning envisions environments where young people can explore their passions while developing critical skills. High-quality learning ecosystems that build on these principles are inclusive, adaptable, and responsive to the diverse needs of learners, providing opportunities for them to thrive academically and socially. These ecosystems leverage technology, mentorship, and community resources to bridge gaps between informal and formal learning spaces, fostering collaboration and creativity. 840 Barriers to learners' discovery and enrollment in relevant out-of-school programs can stem from limited resources, restricted access, or breakdowns in organizational capacity to coordinate, broker, or make learning visible between programs and settings (Ito et al., 2020). Coordination refers to the processes through which organizations build shared infrastructure —exchanging information and resources, establishing common standards, and cultivating mutual agreements (Augustine et al., 2021). Brokering, in contrast, involves systems-level practices where individuals or teams develop interorganizational relationships to connect young people to educational and career opportunities (Akiva et al., 2020). Additionally, organizations can strengthen engagement by making learning visible  across settings through networked infrastructures such as online affinity networks, digital badges (Pitt et al., 2019), or learner portfolios. Fostering interorganizational collaboration is essential for addressing learner needs and interests. When organizations work together to offer youth a wider array of choices, they create opportunities for stronger alignment between what learners want and w hat programs provide. Data systems that help program leaders analyze this alignment can reveal where opportunities are lacking and generate insights that inform both internal planning and external partnerships. Metrics derived from such data can further sustain learning ecosystems by identifying gaps between learner interests and existing programs, generating strategies to bridge those gaps, and guiding decisions about when and where to form collaborative relationships. The utility of the pathways heuristic across fields The pathway concept has proven valuable across multiple fields. In medicine, patient pathway analysis has improved care coordination between organizations, increased consistency in treatment, enhanced patient satisfaction, and optimized resource use (Denekere et al., 2012; Rotter et al., 2010; Vanhaecht et al., 2007). In career development, Savickas (2005) conceptualizes career paths as dynamic and individualized, shaped by personal interests, experiences, and transitions over time. These applications demonstrate how the pathway heuristic can systematically enhance outcomes through improved coordination, consistency, and resource optimization. We believe the pathway heuristic presents similar value for education, particularly in understanding how inter-organizational collaborations between afterschool programs, schools, and community-based organizations can enrich options and improve alignment between learners' STEM interests and available opportunities. While STEM interest and ability may be evenly distributed, opportunities to explore and learn STEM content are not, especially for youth in rural and economically disadvantaged communities (Bevan & Michalchik, 2013).

Design-Based Research (DBR) approach To systematically design and refine ecosystem-level supports, our project adopts a Design-Based Research (DBR) framework. DBR offers a methodological approach for iteratively developing, testing, and improving interventions through cycles of design, implementation, data collection, analysis, and revision (Brown, 1992; Collins, 1992; Cobb et al., 2003). It is inherently collaborative, engaging researchers and practitioners in real-world contexts to address complex educational challenges while producing both practical solutions and theoretical insights. Within the DBR tradition, researchers and practitioners co-develop overarching conjectures about what contributes to desired outcomes (Sandoval, 2014). In our case, DBR guided the development, testing, and refinement of an evaluation framework that aligns with connected learning principles while maintaining high utility and ease of use for educ ational program staff. The framework’s elements include the constructs to be measured, the associated metrics, the required processes and data, and the intended analyses. The research questions guiding the DBR process are: 1) What core constructs are most relevant for tracking learners' STEM learning opportunities across informal and formal settings? 2) What data are needed to support inferences about these constructs? 3) How can collected data be synthesized to inform the improvement and alignment of STEM learning opportunities with learners' interests?

## Research context: TGR foundation

This study was conducted in partnership with the STEM Next 50 State Afterschool Network, a national initiative supporting the development of youths’ interest and engagement in STEM across the United States. The network provides an ideal context for investigating our ecological approach to STEM learning, as many member organizations intentionally connect in-school and out-of-school opportunities to promote sustained STEM engagement. Within this network, we focused on TGR Foundation (TGR), based in Southern California, as an indepth case. Since launching its STEM learning focus in 2001, TGR has developed a portfolio of afterschool and summer programs—including the TGR Learning Lab, career exploration initiatives, and youth golf programs — that together offer sustained exposure to STEM inquiry. Each year, TGR enrolls local middle and high school students in multi-week afterschool experiences and summer camps, while also partnering with sc hool districts to 841 facilitate field trips and college readiness activities. TGR’s capacity to collect and manage longitudinal participation data made it a strong site for co-developing and testing our Learner Pathway Analysis (LPA) framework. In the 2023-2024 school year, TGR Foundation was well-positioned to gather longitudinal data on learner enrollments within TGR programs. Their data from 29 unique learning experiences (16 of which included STEM content) from that year used a unique identifier for each learner enrolled in one or more of their programs. The identifier also included learner demographic information for grade and gender, as well as detailed information about learners’ enrollments in TGR’s after school experiences. The latter included the name of the learning experience, the learning content category of the experience(s), and the particular term it was offered. TGR Foundation implemented this design to follow youth participation in multiple STEM initiatives, both within and beyond their programs. The result was a data frame indexed by learners’ unique ID, with subsequent columns for their gender, grade, fall term enrollment(s), winter term enrollments and spring term enrollments.

Principled measure design Our approach draws from traditions of principled measurement design, which emphasize the systematic and iterative development of assessment tools within a theory-driven framework. Evidence-Centered Design (Mislevy et al., 2003) and the BEAR Assessment System (Wilson, 2023) both provide methodological scaffolds for defining the construct of interest, identifying sources of evidence, and determining rules for interpreting that evidence. Within the Design-Based Research process, we adapted these principles to develop measures for informal STEM 842 learning contexts. Iterative co-design cycles with partner organizations resulted in instruments for assessing three core outcomes: (1) Program enrollment patterns—including transitions between STEM and non-STEM activities over time; (2) Alignment between learner interests and available STEM opportunities; and (3) Interorganizational collaboration —the extent to which partnerships expand access to enriched STEM learning resources.

These outcomes informed the evolving structure of the Learner Pathway Analysis (LPA) framework: To start, we designed a lightweight individual tracking system enabling partner organizations to record student-level data with minimal burden. Built as a share d online spreadsheet tool, the system used preconfigured unique identifiers to link each learner’s record across programs and time. Organizations documented demographic information (grade and gender), program characteristics, and term-by-term enrollment, producing a de-identified dataset that could be merged for analysis. This instrument emphasized flexibility and scalability—local sites could adapt the tool to their internal workflows while maintaining consistency in key fields for cross-site comparison. During the first phase of instrument design, the research team identified major challenges in collecting consistent, individual-level enrollment data—namely, limited infrastructure, inconsistent data practices, and high staff turnover at local sites. In th e second phase, the team worked iteratively with participants to develop a generalized system for tracking individual learner enrollments within and across organizations, designed for ease of local implementation. The resulting instrument (Table 2) was an editable online spreadsheet with preformatted fields and unique learner identifiers.

843 Learner Pathway Analysis (LPA): Concepts and metrics With the data in hand outlined above, Table 3 details the expansion of the Learner Pathway Analysis framework, which expanded to nine core concepts and associated metrics across three broader categories over the course of the study (see Table 3). The STEM programming category aids practitioners and researchers in characterizing opportunities to investigate and learn STEM afforded by programs or organizations. STEM coverage points to the extent to which a program's offerings include opportunities to investigate or learn STEM content. Depth of STEM coverage indicates the number of unique STEM-focused experiences offered by a program, with the idea that when more unique experiences are offered within a particular branch of STEM, students have greater opportunity to investigate that content in depth. Breadth of STEM coverage indicates the proportion of STEM disciplines treated in a given term within the program, relying on the Higher Education Research Institute taxonomy (HERI, n.d.). The student states-traits category introduces student-level constructs relevant to understanding enrollment choices. Multiple measures of student traits are included. This category is useful for gauging alignment between STEM opportunities in students' environments and their socia l and psychological characteristics, whether slow-changing traits or dynamic states like engagement. The student behaviors  category includes five concepts characterizing student enrollment behaviors. STEM enrollment indicates the proportion of students enrolled in STEM-focused experiences at a program or organization. Pathway delay defines the span of time between STEM enrollments for a given student. STEM re-enrollment provides a term for describing student persistence in STEM investigation and learning. The two transition concepts characterize when and how often students initiate STEM involvement and when they attrite or break their STEM involvement within a program or organization. Together, these metrics offer an empirical lens for describing how learners navigate ecosystems of opportunity and where gaps or bottlenecks emerge.

Sankey diagrams: Sample visualizations In addition to the analysis framework, visual forms can help summarize the complex information captured by the pathway heuristic. One useful type is the flow diagram, a directed graph that depicts pathways between related events such as courses or activities (Harris, 2000). Sankey diagrams—a subset of flow diagrams —use nodes and arcs to visualize transitions, such as students moving from one course or domain to another. Figure 1 shows a Sankey diagram summarizing TGR’s student enrollment transitions from fall to spring (2023– 2024) by content type, highlighting in blue those who began in the arts. In Sankey diagrams, node size and arc width represent the number of students, allowing for quick comparison of transitions over time (e.g., an arc for 20 students is twice as thick as one for 10). When nodes are vertically stacked and organized by time steps, the diagram is called an alluvial diagram. This form enables comparison across multiple time points but requires individual-level data, not just aggregated counts.

The TGR Foundation case highlights how implementing individual-level Learner Pathway Analysis (LPA) within a Design-Based Research framework can generate actionable insights about learners’ engagement and transitions in STEM. By linking longitudinal partic ipation data to visualization tools such as Sankey and alluvial diagrams, the analysis revealed how students —particularly female learners —move into and sustain participation in STEM experiences over time. These visualizations not only function as analytic tools but also as design artifacts that help practitioners view their programs as part of a larger ecosystem of interconnected learning opportunities. At the same time, the case revealed persistent challenges in using data to represent the full complexity of learning ecosystems. Staff turnover, decentralized record keeping, and the absence of interoperable data systems limited the completeness and comparability of records across programs. Addressing these challenges will require coordinated development of shared data standards, streamlined reporting processes, and lightweight digital infrastructures that can be realistically maintained by community-based organizations. Future design cycles will focus on co-developing and testing these coordination practices to balance local flexibility with ecosystem-wide visibility. Applying LPA at the organizational and ecosystem levels can strengthen coordination between inschool and out-of-school STEM programs by making patterns of participation and alignment visible. By identifying where learners’ interests do not align with available opportunities, LPA could help program leaders form partnerships that bridge those gaps—ensuring, for instance, that learners introduced to STEM concepts after school have clear pathways to pursue them in the classroom. This data-driven approach encourages a holistic learning ecosystem in which both formal and informal settings contribute to sustained engagement and interest development. Because LPA integrates data on learner interests, parti cipation, and transitions, it offers a shared evaluative language for educators, program leaders, and policymakers. For afterschool organizations in particular, it provides an evidence base for refining programming and improving equity in access to STEM opportunities. When learners’ progress can be tracked across settings, the ecosystem becomes  more responsive—able to adapt programming to real patterns of engagement rather than relying on isolated snapshots of participation. This paper introduced Learner Pathway Analysis as a Design-Based Research approach for evaluating and improving STEM ecosystems. Analysis demonstrated how individual-level tracking can reveal meaningful patterns in enrollment, re-enrollment, and transition s across programs. The LPA framework’s metrics on programming, learner states, and behaviors provide a structured means of identifying alignment gaps and coordination opportunities. Findings indicate that students were more than twice as likely to transition into STEM experiences as away from them, underscoring the role of well-designed afterschool programs in cultivating STEM interest. Yet the persistence of gender disparities and data coordination challenges reveals the ongoing need for equity-focused design and shared infrastructure. LPA offers a pathway toward evidence-informed ecosystem design that supports learners as they explore, deepen, and sustain interests across contexts.
