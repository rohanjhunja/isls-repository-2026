# Critical Biomaking: Socioscientific Issues as Contexts for Life Science Maker Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Advancements in life science education technologies have expanded contemporary perspectives on making—to now include biomaking where organisms can be altered to produce personalizable and useful products. These innovations have brought with them concerns about the consequences of material production, including impacts on society. It also raises questions about learning environment designs to support engagement with these issues in both life science and traditional maker contexts. In this exploratory study, we examine a group of six adult participant perspectives and artifacts in a biomaking workshop that uses socioscientific issues (SSIs) to frame learning. Our analysis suggests that biomaker activities provide a viable access point to engage complex SSIs. We also observed that participants took up making to solve personal and societal problems, they also critically posed problems about material production and its impact. We discuss how these implications might advance what we know about maker affordances in STEM teaching and learning.

## Introduction

The maker movement has undoubtedly made significant  inroads in science, technology, engineering, arts,  and mathematics (STEAM) teaching and learning (Blikstein, 2018; Dougherty, 2012; Papavlasopoulou et al., 2017). This progress is reflected in the growing body of literature that examines making in relation to such areas as access, learning, and equity (Halverson & Sheridan, 2014; Russo et al., 2021). Despite progress maker perspectives have had on how the learning science community understands learning environments, processes, outcomes, and scales (Cohen et al., 2017; Peppler et al., 2016; Searle e t al., 2019)—we know far less about how makers them selves contemplate and grapple with the accompanying socia l or cultural tensions that sometimes surround thei r productions (sometimes referred to as socioscientific issues or SSIs) (Zeidler, 2014). Examples include computer programs whose algorithms place end users at security risk, or maker products and practices that reify participation exclusion or stereotypes (Ames, 2018; Vossoughi et al., 2016). Thus, many of the SSI-based insights we  have about making involve activities where objects are used to draw attention to social issues (e.g., the democratization of technology) rather than stances that highlight how objects themselves might create them (e.g., critical making) (Chou, 2018; Deibert, 2014; Greenberg et al., 2020;  Lachney, 2017; Ratto, 2011). This raises questions  about public awareness and literacy surrounding material production (Walker, 2021) and how, if at all, making attends to this educational priority. These concerns thus u nderscore an important need to examine how makers e ngage more critically with SSIs associated with production in the maker sphere. A challenge in addressing this need is that most maker activities in education involve inanimate objects whose life cycle reaches an apparent stop once productions are no longer in use—and are out of sight and out of mind. This issue is reflected in learning science a nd education research where there are fewer perspec tives on learning experience and design to support engagemen t with the complex social and cultural ramification s associated with maker productions or material artifacts long after they have been produced (Shaw et al., 2019). Synthetic biology—where organisms can be genetically modified for practical uses (e.g., art, food, sensing, etc.)— provides a unique frame within which to examine how  makers contend with the relationship between their productions and society. This is, in part, because the lifespan of biological materials continues long after use— these material artifacts can proliferate into the environment. To explore these issues, we use a biomaker themed workshop (Lui et al., 2019; Kafai et al., 2017; Kafai & Walker, 2020; Walker, et al., 2018) as a frame to investigate the ways a group of six adult participants grapple with the potential impacts synthetic biology products have on their local communities. Set in an informal learning environment located in a western borderland region of the United States, this exploratory research uses workshop artifacts and interview data to qualitatively exami ne what happens when biomaking is the access point for participants to critically consider SSIs associated with production. This study is guided by two research questions:

(1) what do participants have to say about SSIs surrounding synthetic biology products and (2) what do workshop

artifacts reveal about how participants view synthe tic biology production and utility. We highlight tw o case examples drawn from our workshop and discuss the af fordances and constraints biomaking has as a lens t o critically engage SSIs associated with synthetic biology. We also address implications for other areas of making. ICLS2022 Proceedings 607 © ISLS

## Background

Maker education While there are a number of conceptualizations for making—many of which are distinguished by context a nd practice—central characteristics of this movement i nclude material construction, interest-driven pursu its, and social engagement as learners build, design and share their productions (Dougherty, 2012; Papavlasopoulou et al., 2017). Maker approaches to K-12 STEM education have  illuminated important insights about the nature of material artifacts and their role of mediating important learning processes and outcomes (Ames, 2018; Valente & Blikstein, 2019). Research along this trajectory in cludes examination of the various affordances makin g has in promoting STEM learning and is especially prominent in engineering and computer science education (Pep pler et al., 2016)—areas where many efforts have sought to address learning outcomes as well as persistent issues related to domain access and participation parity (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2018). From using Lego blocks, wood cutters, and 3D printers to design and construct robots, to sewing conductive thread in LEDs and microcontrollers to fabricate electronic textiles, making has shown to be a promising context for designing and examining learning in STEM domains. This includes research on how making encourages and sustains learner interests, knowledge acquisition, skill development and mindset shifts (Chou, 2018; Lin et al., 2020). A growing area of research has examined making beyond digital and computational areas of STEM and into life sciences (Kafai, et al., 2017; Lui et al., 2019; Viñas et al., 2020). Momentum in this area of STEM learning science and science education research is the resul t of enabling biotechnologies (e.g., bioengineering, synthetic biology, biodesign, etc.) that have recently made u se of these tools less costly, portable and thus ac cessible for educational purposes. Examples include tools that enable the genetic modification of bacteria or yeast to produce fruit or wintergreen scents, vitamins, or color pig ments for use in food products or art (Kuldell et a l., 2015). Learning science research in this area has emphasized learning design affordances and constraints of this approach to K-12 life science education. There is also a growing corpus of literature that examines ways to support material construction consistent with maker perspectives (Lui et al., 2019) in this genre of making with life science often referred to as biomaking. Biomaking has now made it possible for non-expert audiences to participate in biologybased material productions not ordinarily found in nature. Synthetic biology is distinct from other fo rms of biotechnology in that it borrows perspectives from computing to frame cells as programmable units that  can be designed to generate novel outcomes (Kuldell et al., 2015). This technology is responsible for next ge neration innovations in medicine, manufacturing, and agricul ture. Biomaking extends this perspective by leverag ing synthetic biology for teaching and emphasizing materials, rather than genes or DNA exclusively, where learners can make art, bake, or produce other materials of p ersonal interest. These developments raise questions that far less research has addressed—about how learners consider or grapple with the impact production and objects have on the community and society overall. For instance,  plastic consumption and computer programs are rife  with thorny SSIs around who has access to maker products, where those products end up in their life cycle, and how those products (and especially in their digital form) manifest and shape the world around us—after all computer programs can take on a life of their own after reac hing global networks where they can be modified by others. Biomaking, synthetic biology for maker-style teachi ng and learning, makes these issues more apparent d ue, in part, to the fact that biological materials require special handling to mitigate the potential impact they might have on society and the environment. Thus, biomaking provides a unique vantage point to begin examination of maker production in the context of teaching and learning,  including around areas of how we might begin to su pport upfront and critical engagement with material production in STEM maker education and the accompanying SSIs associated with those objects.

Socioscientific issues Socioscientific issues (SSIs) are controversial science topics that require complex considerations often grounded in cultural, ethical, and/or moral values (Zeidler, 2014). Examples include topics related to climate change and its impact on society and the planet, or the risks associated with digital technologies and privacy. In STEM education and research, SSIs are frequently used to support l iteracy practices such as data analysis, argumentat ion and decision-making where learners reason through the often complicated social and cultural issues associated with these topics (Presley et al., 2013). Significant research in the learning science and science education literature has revealed a number of affordances of using SSIs as a  frame for teaching and learning STEM (Dauer & Forb es,

2016) including opportunities for learners to make meaningful connections between science topics and t heir

everyday lives. This line of research is especially  prominent in life science education, where burgeon ing biotechnologies have, for decades, drawn attention for the ethical and moral dilemmas they present. Ex amples ICLS2022 Proceedings 608 © ISLS include issues associated with consuming geneticall y modified food products, the implications of consu ming genetically cloning livestock, the risks associated with gene therapies to treat inborn disease, or the use of HeLa human cell lines that were taken, grown, and exploited for science discovery (Leiden, 2000; Savadori et al., 2004; Skloot, 2017; Solter, 1998).

Despite the influence maker education has had in STEM education, there is a dearth in the literature on topics at the intersection of making and SSIs (Shaw et al., 2019)—this is despite the fact that scholars have mostly examined related topics like how learners use digital productions to critique or disrupt complex and often thorny social and cultural issues related to STEAM and its impact on society. This gap in the literature also exists at the intersection of SSIs, making and the life sciences. This is due in part to the fact that the latter two areas—making in life science—have only recently been possible. Areas like biomaking therefore present a whole new w ave of SSIs, and accompanying public attitudes (Walker, 20 21), where material production and considerations s pan beyond scaled medical and agricultural applications, and into an era where genetic modification has proliferated locally. These developments create an early opportu nity to examine life science maker learning in rela tion to socioscientific issues in a shift from discussing largely hypothetical scenarios or biotechnologies that have existed for decades, toward engagements that involve actual material products with real world implications. This research seeks to examine this context in order to shed early light on how biomaking affords, constrains, or extends what we know about SSIs in the context of production in life science education.

## Context

This study took place as a workshop in a fabricatio n lab (FabLab), a not-for-profit organization that provides community access to fabrication tools and used for skill development, workforce training, creative collaboration, prototype development, and product manufacturing. The site is located in the western region of the United States near the Mexican border. The study consisted of a workshop that incorporated synthetic biology lab activities.  The workshop ocurred over a period of five days for about four to six hours each day. The workshop included tasks: cell-line genetic modifications, design application prototyping, socioscientific issue discussions, and speculative design presentations (shown left to right in Figure 1a-d). ## Workshop design, participants, and data sources

The BioMaker workshop was structured along four mai n tasks. Participants, first, carried out genetic modifications (shown in Figure 1a) developed by the BioBuilder Educational Foundation (BioBuilder Educational Foundation, 2021). In this process participants produced bacteria that emitted wintergreen or banana smells, green, purple, and black pigments, as well as vitamin A. T he second task (shown in Figure 1b) involved design prototyping where participants used genetically modified cell lines in a design application of their own designed applications for their synthetic biology lab products. This included such applications as making bioart and baked products infused with yeast they genetically modifi ed. The third task (shown in Figure 1c) involved a series of SSI-based case study discussions about biomaker and synthetic biology-based applications and the impact those applications could have on society and the planet. Here participants had the opportunity in a think-pa ir-share ICLS2022 Proceedings 609 © ISLS format to develop and express their opinions about biomaking used to genetically modify organisms (as they had done). In the last task (shown in Figure 1d), parti cipants put together and presented speculative applications for future biomaker projects. In these presentations, t hey highlighted the benefits and potential harms of  using biomaker projects in the community.

The biomaker workshop included six participants (ag es ranging from 25-52)—four of whom selfidentified as female and two of whom did as men. All self-identified as Latinx. Participants ranged in professional background that included a: high-school biotechnolo gy teacher, homeschool teacher-mom, trained profess ional designer, elementary grade teacher, a Fab-Lab employee and adjunct social science instructor, and a mechanical engineer. While participants had diverse reasons for taking up the workshop, they all coalesced around  a desire to learn and access Fab Lab activities, which offer ways for adults to do so informally and affordably. Primary study data sources were workshop artifacts including: worksheets participants completed during SSI-based case study discussion (see Figure 1c) and presentation slides (see Figure 1d) participants used to present their own biomaker speculative design application. Secondary data sources included semi-structured interviews (conducted by authors two, three and four) and surveys (administered using an online survey instrument) where participants shared their opinions about biomaking and genetic modification in general. These secondar y data sources were used to corroborate insights gleaned from workshop artifacts. Analysis Data sources were analyzed using a qualitative appr oach. First, workshop artifacts (e.g., worksheets a nd presentation slides) were digitally scanned and/or uploaded to an encrypted electronic cloud drive sha red by authors. Workshop artifacts were then analyzed and coded by two researchers (authors two and three) us ing an inductive approach (Ravitch & Carl, 2019) wherein r esearchers identified themes related to what participants wrote about real world biomaker or synthetic biolog y-based products. In this process, authors two and three identified code categories (e.g., application type, context, and consideration type) based on what participants wrote in response to prompts about biomaking in relation to its impact on themselves, their community, and/or the planet as a whole. Those codes were discussed and developed iteratively in collaboration with authors one and four. A final codebook was then developed and applied to artifacts independently a second time by authors two and three. Code application disagreements were discussed and d eliberated with all authors until 100% consensus wa s reached. Authors two and three then generated frequ ency charts and pie graphs (not shown in this paper) to visualize data and patterns. Those observations wer e then used to develop illustrative case study acco unts. Interview data were used as secondary sources to tr iangulate ideas represented in workshop artifacts. This included participant comments about biomaking and its myriad uses.

## Findings

Biomaking provides entry point for SSI discussions about production in life science When analyzing written responses during biomaker SS I workshop activities, we observed that participant s collectively considered a wide range of factors in relation to synthetic biology-based products and the potential impact they could have in society. Those considerations varied across application type (e.g., which organism to which synthetic biology was being applied), context  (e.g., the purpose of the synthetic biology produc t), and values (e.g., moral/ethical consideration, risk/benefit assessments, etc.). We present two case examples of written perspectives to illustrate what participants had to  say about these applications—including what they e xpressed about the various tensions associated with syntheti c biology and the myriad impacts their products pos e to the environment and society as a whole.

Alexis Padilla, a department chair in the biology department in a local high school, and Alejandra Garcia, a fashion designer trained at a school of design in a city located in the northeastern region of the United States, both joined the biomaker workshop for professional reasons. Ale x had an interest in finding ways to innovate and e xpand curricular offerings at the school where she teaches. Having had significant training in fashion on the use of novel and biologically-produced textiles, Alejandra joine d the workshop in hopes to continue, at a low cost,  her explorations and experiences with materials. After reading an advertisement for the biomaker workshop in an email distribution list associated with the local Fab Lab—both registered as a way to support these personal goals. During the workshop, there were opportunities to learn and carry out synthetic biology, which involved genetically modifying cells and then using them in maker-like w ays such as to design, personalize and fabricate pr oducts (e.g., baked products and bioart). In between these activities participants had opportunities to reflect and discuss their thoughts about synthetic biology overall through case studies and prompts provided by a worksheet. When presented with applications involving the use of synthetic biology in plants (to produce vitamin infused rice for ICLS2022 Proceedings 610 © ISLS human consumption) or microbial (to spider silk for shoes) applications, both participants had a lot to write about regarding the issues these applications raise (shown in Figures 2a-d.). Alexis addresses (Figure 2a.) the potential social and environmental impacts these areas of production could have as she wrote, “of course if it works to the positive, the individual is fine as is society, but if it goes bad, the risk has a trickle-down effect. These researchers need to think long term not if it can be done” (Think-Pair-Share Argumentation Worksheet, Workshop Day 1). In another instance, Alexis weighed such concerns as the consequences that could result if an application is not used, and in relation to the potential risk to an individual person as she explained, “If I have limited or no access to fresh food, I probably don’t have access to health care so something that cannot only provide nutrient but [also] create health benefit as well would be something I could use” (Think-Pair-Share Argumentation Worksheet, Workshop Day 3). These instances reflect how, for Alex, prod uction using these biotechnologies creates competin g perspectives and potential consequences, in broad terms for the environment all the way down to single individuals. In addition to environmental factors, Alejandra grappled (Figure 2c) with additional factors as she described, “it’s a privilege to be healthy in this highly industrial world so if we can create a democratic solution to provide healthy options for everyone we should” (Think-Pair-Share Argumentation Worksheet, Workshop 3). Alejandra grounds her viewpoint around a geopolitical landscape—takin g a stance beyond her personal benefit and in relat ion to society overall. Alejandra, like others, took simil ar positions about children who “can’t make [decisi ons] themselves” and other species that “have been on th is planet” and whose “disappearance could cost our ecosystem” (Think-Pair-Share Argumentation Worksheet, Workshop 3). Participant considerations (shown in Figure 2a-d.) were expansive as they contemplated the impact these life science productions could have at the intersection of SSIs with consequential implications (e.g., personal, environmental, socioeconomic, geopolitical, etc.). ## Participants use biomaker projects to address personal and social issues

Examination of participant biomaker presentations i nvolved synthetic biology applications that cut acr oss a variety of personal and social issues related to interests (e.g, making products for pets), community infrastructure (e.g., creating ways to mitigate monsoon-driven floods), and the environment (e.g., undoing harm to endangered ICLS2022 Proceedings 611 © ISLS wildlife). We present projects from case examples to illustrate participant views about synthetic biology utility in relation to themselves and their community. Alexis Padilla presented about how synthetic biology could be used to fabricate synthetic and animal friendly essentia l oils scents that could be used for household pet cats. Alexis argued that cats have important personal and househ old value (e.g., rodent control, companionship, lon gevity, etc.) that could be enhanced through the use of calming essential oils. She went on to point out that these products pose harm risks because they contain an ingredient (e.g., glucuronidation protein) that is not metabolized by cats, leaving them “vulnerable to organ failure” (Final P resentation, slide 3, Workshop Day 5). Alex specula ted that synthetic biology could be used to synthesize essen tial oils without the protein deleterious to cats. During the workshop, Alexis shared her fondness for cats and expressed how she cared for her own—pets that meant a lot to her personally. Taken together, this evidence showed that to Alexis synthetic biology could be used as  a tool to address an issue endemic to household feline pets. In a different instance, Alejandra considered using synthetic biology in a markedly different way—raising concerns about the environmental impact of the U.S./Mexico border wall on native species in the region. She argued that the binational barrier was primarily a “human problem” that “threatens the lives of over 90 endangered species” in the region (Final Presentation, slide 2, Worksh op Day 5) shown in Figure 3a-c below. Alexis speculated the possibility of using synthetic biology approaches to isolate a gene found in a steel metabolizing bacteria (H. She argued that this plant, with its barbed branches, would create a structurally feasible human barrier that over time—using H. Titanicae-derived metabolic processes —decomposes the existing border wall while simultane ously allowing native species to pass unobstructedly throughout the region. ## Implications

Socioscientific issues and maker education Maker education, with its paradigmatically distinct focus on design and material production has had an undeniable impact on active approaches to STEM education and r esearch. In this study we build on traditions that engage making to draw attention to social issues in partic ipation (Greenberg et al., 2018; Searle, Tofel-Greh l, & Breitenstein, 2019), learning (Russo et al., 2021),  and practice (Vossoughi, Hooper, & Escude, 2016) b y also examining maker activities that address social issu es that are the result of material production. Our biomaker activities, framed in the context of SSIs, revealed that biomaking—powered and enabled by biotechnologies like synthetic biology—is rife with complex issues that include both discipline-specific considerations (e. g., those related specifically to life science and its impact) and those that span across fields (e.g., those co nnected moral values or ideological orientations). This is reflec ted in the well-developed opinions participants had  about synthetic biology and its various uses. We also obs erved in our workshop that these opinions reflect s entiments typical of SSI views about biotechnology (Solter, 1 998; Savadori et al., 2004; Leiden, 2000; Shaw, Wal ker & Kafai, 2019), and included a myriad other considera tions (e.g., personal, societal, socioeconomic, geo political, etc.). We consider this a reflection of the fact th at the synthetic biology field brings with it a who le new set of possibilities (e.g., manufacturing pigments, food ingredients, etc.) that expand the corpus of SSI cases to engage. These connecting points also highlight the a ffordances making has as a context to have productive discussions around production and SSIs topics that have been shown to be successful in promoting STEM literacy (Presle y et al., 2013). Future research along this traject ory might consider how making with electronics and computatio nal artifacts might also extend traditions (Zeidler, 2014) about how learners critically discuss, argue, and develop literacies around SSIs in innovation and production.

## Critical biomaking: A context to solve and pose problems

Our examination of final presentations suggests that participants fundamentally viewed synthetic biology utility in two ways—as a tool to either solve or pose probl ems. Alejandra’s presentation, distinct from Alexis  who pursued a personal interest, showed a synthetic biology application designed and used as a provocation—around a complex geopolitical landscape rife with socioscientific implications. Rather than the typical frame of being a service to the community, both cases framed synthetic biology's utility as an  instrument in service of the community, its interests, and politics. This critical biomaking approach is consistent with views in critical making (Deiber, 2014; Vossoughi, Hooper & Escudé, 2016) wh ere participation enables both problem solving and the problematizations of practice. In a genre of making that continues to flourish as a field and method for enhancing STEM education, future research should examine crit ical biomaker focused learning designs that might b e leveraged to support learning outcomes and reevaluate perspectives about making and its utility in the practice of modern science teaching and learning.
