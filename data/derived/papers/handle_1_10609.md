# Translanguaging in High School Science: Epistemic and Relational Sensemaking

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Translanguaging pedagogies have the potential to support new kinds of learning that empower multilingual students. Yet, in science education, translanguaging research has focused primarily on bilingual classrooms rather than on the English-dominant classrooms in which most US students learn. Moreover, research demonstrates that science learning involves both epistemic and relational sensemaking. Yet, in science education, translanguaging research has primarily focused on epistemic outcomes. In response, in this study, I consider the role of translanguaging in an 11th/12th grade English-dominant environmental science class. I analyze interviews with students to amplify their experiences of translanguaging in this classroom, considering the role of translanguaging not only in epistemic sensemaking (about disciplinary concepts and practices) but also in relational sensemaking (about students’ interactions).

## Objectives and significance

Translanguaging theory describes the ways that bili ngual and multilingual individuals flexibly and flu idly use multiple modes (meaning ‐making resources, such as image, writing, speech, a nd action) for reasoning and expression (Blackledge & Creese, 2017; Li, 2018). G rounded in translanguaging theory, translanguaging pedagogies “empower bilingual children so they use their unitary semiotic repertoire to make meaning for themselves” (García & Li, 2014, p. 86). In this way, translanguaging  pedagogies have the potential to support new kinds of learning, not only about language and content, but also about what counts as legitimate and valuable participation in institutional spaces, like schools (e.g., Makoni & Pennycook, 2007; Otheguy et al., 2015). Despite this expansive potential, translanguaging research in science education has primarily focused on outco mes in terms of canonical science and language learning (e.g., Licona & Kelly, 2019; Poza, 2018). Less is k nown about the role of translanguaging in other dimensions of learning, such as students’ relational sensemaking. Moreover, translanguaging research most often takes place in bilingual cla ssrooms in which teachers and students share linguistic resources (e.g., Poza, 2018; Probyn, 2015). Yet, in the US in particular, most students learn in Englishdominant classrooms (Shin, 2017). In response, I analyze the role of translanguaging in an 11th/12th grade Englishdominant environmental s cience class. The class was designated by the school as an  “English as a Second Language” (ESL) class, and all students were bureaucratically classified as English learners. I analyze interviews with students to amplify their experiences of translanguaging in this classroom, considering t he role of translanguaging not only  in epistemic sensemaking (about disciplinary concepts and practices) but als o in relational sensemaking (about students’ interactions, in-school and out-of-school).

## Literature review: Translanguaging and sensemaking in science

Research about translanguaging originated in biling ual classrooms in which students shared linguistic resources (e.g., students identified as speakers of both English and Welsh; Williams, 1994). Foundational st udies about translanguaging primarily aimed to understand stude nts' ways of being, interacting, and learning in su ch spaces (García, 2009). While researchers have begun to explore strategies for inviting and leveraging translanguaging in English-dominant classrooms, these studies have primarily focused on literacy learning, not on science learning, and most studies have taken place in elementary classrooms (e.g., Machado & Hartman, 2019; Zapata & Laman, 2016; an exception is Smith et al., 2020, a review of m ultimodal translanguaging in secondary classrooms). In science education, translanguaging research typically focuses on two outcomes for learning: canonical science learning and language learning. Research demonstrates that translanguaging in science can promote language development, high‐order thinking, and conceptual understanding (Espinosa & Herrera, 2016; Oliveira et al., 2019; Poza, 2018), contributing to more equitable learnin g environments for bilingual students (Karlsson et al., 2019; Lin & Wu, 2015; Probyn, 2015). In addition, research demonstrates that translanguaging can support engagement in specific disciplinary science practices, like ar gumentation (Licona & Kelly, 2019; Reigh & Miller, 2020; Wu et al., 2019) and modeling (Pierson et al., 2021; Pierson & Grapin, 2021). Yet, studies of both professional science and classroom science  suggest that focusing on disciplinary content and p ractices alone is insufficient for understanding learning, because emotions and social relationships are not simply an cillary to disciplinary work but a generative part of learning (e.g., Fox Keller  1985; Jaber & Hammer, 2016; Pugh et al., 2019; Williams, 2023). Krist and Suárez (2018, p. 425) explain that science education typically has foregrounded  “fidelity to knowledge” (an individualist paradigm characteristi c of Euro-centric science). Instead, they  advocate for simultaneously attending to “fidelity to persons” (“the commitment of one pe rson to the growth and well-being of another, grounded in caring relationships”). They argue that orienting to both knowledge and persons offers an image of science that “uses the context of co-constructing ideas to develop ethical, caring relat ionships” through which “genuinely shared epistemological commitments  and consensus ideas can emerge.”  Following from this framework, I aim to amplify students’ experiences of translanguaging in  science, focusing on both epistemic dimensions of learning (fidelity to knowledge) and relational dimensions of learning (fidelity to persons). To contribute to the growing body of research about translanguaging beyond bilingual elementary classroom settings, I analyze data from a linguistically-diverse English-dominant secondary classroom. In this context, I as k: How can multilingual and multimodal translanguaging support epistemic and relational science learning?

## Method

This study was conducted in a public high school in  a large metropolitan school district in the midwes tern US in collaboration with Mr. M, a teacher who taught “ESL” s cience classes. All of Mr. M’s students were bureaucratically classified as English learners. Mr. M’s 11th/12th grade environmental science class participated in this project  (16 students). Students participated in a  5-week modeling curriculum co-designed by the author and Mr. M. The unit was aligned to state standards,  and it was intended to support students’ sensemaki ng about watersheds (mapping and modeling a watershed, representing threats to the watershed such as pollution and algal blooms, and exploring social solutions and engineer ing solutions). Learning activities emphasized multilingual and multimodal  representations to encourage translanguaging. Lesso ns took place 3-4 times per week during students’ 50-minute environmental science class. In order to understand students’ experiences of translanguaging, I analyze interviews conducted with 6 students at the end of the 5-week unit. These students were selected by Mr. M to represent a wide range of backgrounds, academic performance, and engagement. In the interviews, students reflected on (a) each modeling activity, (b) their learning across the unit, and (c) their language us e within the unit. My analysis uses inductive coding to identify themes in students’ experiences (Charmaz, 2006). In this paper, I focus on students’ experiences of multilingual learning (learning with more than one language) and multimodal learning (learning with more than one representatio nal form) within modeling activities. Within these broad categories, I identi fy both epistemic dimensions of learning (building knowledge) and relational dimensions of learning (cultivating relationships with human or more than human beings).

## Findings and discussion

Using students’ interviews, this section describes how students’ experiences of multilingual and multi modal translanguaging supported epistemic and relational dimensions of science learning. In their interviews, most students reported using languages other than Englis h for science learning (multilingual). One student, Ebrima, was an exception: she said, “I was only using English…mostly, I’m thinking in English.” However, she added that it could be helpful to use languages other than Englis h, and she noted that using languages other than English was helpful to her peers. 5 of the 6 students interviewed described using their full linguistic repertoires as helpful to their understanding. For example, Eve lyn explained that in her group “we all speak the same language, so we are strong throu gh Swahili.” Speaking Swahili was useful to Evelyn because her “English still only goes to a limit…Swahili mak es me understand more.” Omar similarly appreciated the opportunity to “talk to a lot of friends” in Somali, because he “u nderstand[s] the language very clearly that my mom and my family speaks.” Here, Omar contextualized Somali by referencing his fri ends and family, highlighting the significance of the language as a way to connect with people, rather than as a tool t hat is used only in service of science sensemaking. In particular, Omar appreciated talking with  a classmate, Halima. He explained, “she speaks very clearly so she tells me what to do  and how to do.” In reflecting on the experience of working with Omar and other Somali-speakers, Halima explained that moving between English and Somali was not simply a translation exercise, but a generative  science learning opportunity. She explained, “The big words that we use…we can’t use it in Somali. So I would h ave to use it in a different word…for the other per son to understand me. When I’m working with kids, with my classmates or whatever, I would have to use another word or like different languages for me to understand wh at the actual word is. Until then I would just know  is that “algae” … one word. Tons of words enable for the pe rson to understand.” Here, Halima explained that working with classmates in Somali involved more than translating terms like “algae;” it involved breaking down these “big words” into “tons of words” to make sense of learning activities. Adea also mentioned that it was helpful to work in a group where students “all speak [the] same lan guage” because, “it is just our language, and like,  you know, our culture.” Here, Adea emphasized the importance of culture implicit in  sensemaking processes. She also described how using Swahili in class helped her continue sensemaking at home. As an example, she referenced a model of point-source pollution that the students created in class: “When we went home, we was trying to put — to try this thing [model]. This one, really, I was trying, with my family. I tell them our school was doing this, so I want to try.” Adea considered her conversations with her family an ext ension of her use of Swahili in class, connecting across contexts with her linguistic repe rtoire. Whereas most students  focused on collaborative sensemaking with others (classmates, friends, family), Manish saw Nepali as a refuge to work through ide as on his own, explaining “sometimes, it’s, you don’t wan t to say it in English, where other people might he ar it. So if I say my own language, I’ll be able to understand it myself and other people will not judge.” With this statement, Manish acknowledged the vulnerability that processi ng new ideas might entail for students, particularl y when they are not completely comfortable with the language of instruction in their classroom. Despite students’ strong belief that using languages other than English supported their science learning, Adea noted that she did not always feel comfortable using Swahili at school. In her experience, speaking in languages that teachers do not understand can make teachers uncomfortable: “When we see teachers, or we just speak only Swahili at school, we think they can think we talked bad about them, because the different language is not the same language.” Adea was concerned that her teachers might assume that she was speakin g badly of them rather than working with her group.  Such relational concerns might prevent students from leveraging their full linguistic repertoires in school. In addition to multilingual learning, students’ exp eriences with multimodal representations supported their epistemic and relational sensemaking. Whereas  the interviews explicitly asked students to reflec t on their use of language in the class, they did not prompt s tudents explicitly to reflect on multimodal sensema king. Still, multimodal sensemaking emerged as a theme in my anal ysis of students’ interviews: 5 of the 6 students valued multimodality as a support for learning that was un ique in this curriculum compared to their experience in other classes. When asked about the unit overall, Omar explained, “this is my first time doing anything experiment…so I’m going to say it was very good. I loved it…I talked to lots of friends.” This was the second time in his interview that Omar brought up that he “talked to lots of friends,” emphasizing the importance of getting to collaborate with peers. Typically, his classes were “paperwork, pape r stuff.” Adea also described an emphasis in their classes on paperwork and literacy, describing typical assignments as “you can find the article and then we can read together, and we can question,” and Manish described typical assignments as “pap ers” and “computer research.” Instead, in the watershed unit, students participated in fie ld work and constructed several physical models. Wh ile Omar emphasized that multimodal learning offered more opportunities to interact with peers, other students emphasized that multimodal learning was more generative than “paperwork, paper stuff.” For instance, Halima described how field work and modeling made the watershed map unde rstandable: “first, we got a picture of it, and the n we actually went there to see how it actually was. I f elt like it was like the [in-class physical model] that we spre ad water on. But just like a map it wouldn’t be unders tandable, by just looking at it.” With this stateme nt, Halima described how coordinating multiple representations made the watershed “understandable” in a way that wouldn’t be possible with “just a map.” This work of coordinating representations was not only epistemic, but relational— in their field work, students explored different parts of the watershed and brought their findings together to create a representation. In addition to supporting sensemaking in class, stu dents saw their multimodal work as being more relevant to out-of-school experiences. Both Halima and Ebrima described testing water for toxins and designing water filters as “helpful.” Halima connected these activities to her experience colle cting and drinking rainwater—she appreciated having hands-on knowledge of how to “clean up the water” and tell “if it’s clean  or not” for herself or for others who might be depend on ra inwater. Ebrima saw these multimodal activities as relevant not only for other humans but also other m ore than human beings at an ecosystem level. She suggested using Algebra II (an additional mathematical mode o f representation) to model “how things can spread a nd how things can get stopped.” These examples show that students ’ multilingual and multimodal translanguaging support ed both epistemic and relational  sensemaking. Using multiple languages  and modes supported epistemic sensemaking because it helped students deepen their understanding of the phenomena they studied (breaking down “big words” into “tons of words,” coordinating representations to make watersheds “un derstandable”). In additio n, multilingual and multimodal learning supported relational sensemaking: it helped students cultivate connections with their peers (talking with “a lot of friends,” in “our language…o ur culture”), and,  beyond the classroom, it extended the scope of students’ investigations  (engaging family in learning, considering ecosystem-level extensions). Still, there is much to learn about translanguaging  in English-dominant science classrooms. In this classroom, translanguaging was often reciprocally b eneficial (e.g., Omar relied on Halima’s interpretation of “what to do and how to do,”  and Halima deepened her understanding of concepts b y describing them to Omar). Yet in other contexts, multilingual students take on significant care work (translating, interpreting) that may not similarly support their sensemaking. Sengupta et al. (2021) n ote that in STEM, such care work is often devalued and disappeared. Further research is needed to ampl ify the epistemic and relational contributions of multilingual students in English-dominant science classrooms.
