# Peer Feedback and Student Values about Geometric Proofs

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Mathematical proving is a disciplinary practice guided by cultural values. Students often experience mathematical proving as alienating, partly because the norms and values underlying the practice are either implicit or imposed by an external authority. Understanding what learners themselves value in proofs is essential for designing learning experiences that are responsive and inclusive. Using iterative values coding, we examined what youth valued in peer-written proofs in a summer geometry course. We found that learners focused on function-and form-oriented characteristics of proofs, often drawing connections between them. Some learners attended to the proofs’ ability to reflect the proving process, as well as the texts’ attunement to readers and authors. We argue that building on these identified values can support youth to recognize and appreciate the epistemic goals of disciplinary proving practices and allow them to meet those goals in ways that are both normative and meaningful to them.

## Background

The values that communities hold are reflected in the practices they engage in,  and in the expectations they have for new members’ participation  (Chang, 2008; Kilpatrick et al., 1999). In a learning community, this can  have implications for whose participation is recognized as legitimate, valued and accepted (Nasir & de Royston, 2013). Values can also be reflected in communities’ choice of tools to  structure their practices (Saxe, 2015), including epistemic forms they might use to organize epistemic activities (Collins & Ferguson, 1993). In mathematics, such values can manifest in the ways mathematicians engage in proving practices, and in structural characteristics of written proofs as an epistemic form that organizes such engagement (Dawkins & Weber, 2017). In the case of math proofs, the final form of the proof does not typically reflect the proving process (Karunakaran, 2018), and the values that the proof-form strives to uphold are not necessarily the same that guide the activity that led to its construction. This disconnect is also present in classrooms-the proof-forms students typically see do not reflect the process they usually need to go through to cons truct a proof. Understanding what students value in proof-forms can shed light on the proving practices they find meaningful, and the goals they have for engaging in them. Prior research on valued proof uses and features  focused on adult students and mathematicians. Weber et al. (2022)  found that mathematicians relied on examples and authority when validating proof s, and on being able to understand them. Moore (2016) showed that understanding was important to math ematicians also in a teaching contex t, along with clarity, fluency, and logical structure. Sommerhoff and Ufer (2019)  found that a logical chain of reasoning and the structure of a proof were attributes that both mathematicians and adult students valued. They also showed that understanding was the fourth most frequent criterion adult students used to accept a proof as valid. This aligns with findings from Stewart and Thomas (2019), who added that understanding was often enhanced by examples, visual supports, and deductive reasoning.  Thus, for adult students and mathematicians, there seems to be a connection between features of proof artifacts (e.g., linear deduction, use of a diagram) and readers’ ability to understand and validate a proof. In this paper, we add to this literature by looking at the values younger learners (12-15 years old) conveyed when providing peer feedback on mathematical proofs. Peer feedback (PF) activities support learning both for the students offering and those receiving it (e.g. Knop et al., 2021). In the context of K-12 mathematics, PF activities supported learners to make connections between their own and their peers’ ideas and engage in productive disciplinary discussions  (Husband & Nikfarjam, 2022). Amongst college students, activities that incorpora ted PF helped learners develop greater ownership over a proof  and the process of constructing one (Reinholz & Pilgrim, 2021). Importantly, engaging and reflecting on PF activities created conditions for learners to recognize the role that communication and peers can play in mathematics (Calkins et al., 2020). Because PF, as an activity, creates a context for evaluative behavior that is naturalistically embedded in meaningful com municational situations (conveying a proof to peers), and in so doing promotes attunement to the communicative f unctions different proof-features might have, we identified PF as a fruitful context for surfacing and examining what learners value in proofs. We explore the question: What do secondary school students value in their peers’ geometric proofs as reflected in their peer feedback?

## Methods

The data and analysis discussed in this paper were part of a design-based research project that aimed to study how the use of technology and collaborative activities can support secondary students’ flexible engagement in proving. The first author designed and taught a 12-lesson academic enrichment course on geometric thinking, offered via Zoom in summer 2021 at a large public research university in the USA. Ten of the 20 students (12-15 years old) enrolled in the course chose to participate in the study. Study participation was b lind to the instructor-researcher until all teaching-related responsibilities had concluded. Data collected included screen recordings of whole class and some breakout room sessions, all student work, chat logs, and student and teacher reflections. Peer feedback activities were framed as opportunities for affirmation and collective learning (Figure 1). There were six structured peer feedback activities in this course and four additional opportunities for students to (re)view their peers’ work without a structured fee dback session.  During structured peer feedback activities, learners could provide feedback verbally and/or in writing, both in in-class activities and homework assignments. Figure 1 Lesson Slides Used to Frame and Guide Peer Feedback Activities. We focused our analysis on the feedback students provided to their peers on their proofs during Lesson 3 (week 2), Lesson 6 (week 3), and Lesson 9 (week 5) of the course. The first author collected anonymized student responses in a single document, which included transcriptions of verbally provided feedback. She then employed an iterative values coding approach (Saldaña, 2009) using both deductive and inductive codes  (inductive codes are noted with asterisk (*) in Figure 2 below). After two coding cycles by the first author, the second author used the refined coding rubric to code 15% of the data independently. We then met, discussed differences in code assignment and interpretation, and revised the rubric together. Following this revision, we  collaboratively coded all the data. Since one of the coders was also the teacher and designer of this course, we thought that a consensual approach to coding could leverage the first author’s implicit knowledge of student work, while at the same time unearthing assumption she might be making about it. Together, we discussed and resolved differences as we progressed, refined the definitions of our codes, and respectively updated applied  codes as needed. We applied each code only once to a single student response, and w hen applicable, multiple different codes to the same student response. We assigned a total of 102 codes to 39 student responses. Once we finished coding, the first author used MAXQDA 2022 (VERBI software, 2021)  for further data analysis, including the calculation of frequency statistics and exploring co-occurrences of codes. Informed by the literature  on values in proving  (Dawkins & Weber, 2017; Sommerhoff & Ufer, 2019) and a sociocultural  theoretical framework  that conceptualizes proving as a form-mediated activity  (Collins & Ferguson, 1993), w e organized the final codes in 5 categories: process-oriented codes, form-oriented codes, function-oriented codes, agency-oriented codes, and, other. We used “process-oriented” to label feedback that focused on the perceived proving process a proof reflects, such as a proof that was perceived as exemplifying a particular problem-solving process. We used “function-oriented” to code feedback that attended to a proof’s epistemic functions, such as advancing understanding, and “form-oriented” to describe feedback that focused on attributes of the proof-form itself. Finally, we used “agency-oriented” codes to capture feedback that focused on the author(s) or audience of a proof, and “other” for anything that did not fit in the above categories.

## Findings

We found that function-oriented feedback was as frequent as form-oriented feedback in our data (43 counts each), and both were far more frequent than all the other feedback-types. Student peer feedback focused less frequently on what process a proof described (5 counts), or the agency of the author(s) and audience(s) of a proof (7 counts). We share the breakdown of the codes we applied, organized by type of code, in Figure 2. Amongst the functionoriented codes (blue), the most frequent type of feedback in our data was about the clarity of a proof (22 counts). This included positive comments about a proof being clear, as well as suggestions for how to make a proof clearer. Amongst the form-oriented codes (green), t he most common type of feedback in our data was about a proof’s structure (16 counts). This included comments about the organ ization of a written proof in paragraphs, or the organization of a proof in “steps.” An illustrative example of student feedback that included both function-oriented (clarity, understanding) and form-oriented (proof structure) comments is that of Sam (Lesson 9): [1] “the way they split it [the text in paragraphs] is easier to read. It’s like easier to read [2] and understand.” Sam commented on the organization of the proof in p aragraphs attending to proof structure (line 1), as well as on the ways in which that organization help ed him follow (clarity) and understand (understanding) the proof (lines 1-2).

Upon closer examination of the most frequently co-occurring codes in peer feedback responses, we found that learners often described proof structure as supporting proof clarity. In particular, when students commented on the structure of a proof, they often focused on certain characteristics that they felt helped them follow the proof. These structural characteristics were the organizat ion of a proof in paragraphs (5 counts), the presen tation of a proof as a step-by-step process (7 counts), and occasionally, the explicit articulation  of an objective and/or a concluding statement in the proof (3 counts).  Youth were both attuned to different types of struc tural characteristics of proof-artifacts, and they saw those features as connecting to communicative functions. ## Discussion

Our findings suggest that secondary students  in our study, like mathematicians (Dawkins & Weber, 2017)  and mathematics educators (Sommerhoff & Ufer, 2019)  in previous studies, noticed, positively evaluated and drew connections between the structure and clarity or understanding of a proof. The structural characteristics  they appreciated were often those constitutive of a proof’s linear, deductive structure. In the past, researchers have argued that students may struggle to move from non-linear (abductive) argumentation to linear (deductive) proofs (Pedemonte, 2007). Our findings suggest that youth can recognize that the deductive structure of a proof can serve communicative goals that they value. By drawing explicit connections between the communicative goals one might have for different epistemic activities, and the ways in which  different epistemic forms can support these goals (e.g. linear structure supporting clarity), educators can render  the values that shape mathematical proving more transparent to young learners, and support the m to engage flexibly in mathematical epistemic activities. A concrete pedagogical implication is that peer-feedback, as a classroom activity, can be productiv ely used to support youth to articulate what they value in proo f, attune to the connections between a proof’s form and its communicative functions, and engage in collaborativ e reflection. Reflecting on these goals and values can offer youth opportunities to explore alignment between the values they hold, and those imposed by external authority (e.g., teachers), and in turn, empower them to reimagine what mathematical proving can look and feel like. In comparing our findings to those from prior resea rch on adults, it is important to note that besides the difference in age group and prior experience, our s tudy uses a different context  to solicit p eer feedback: proof evaluation rather than validation. Were we to invite youth to validate researcher generated proofs, rather than provide feedback on their peers’ work, different goals and values could have emerged. Thus, we see these findings as a first and far from conclusive  step towards learning about secondary students’ goa ls and values with respect to proofs. To better understand the breadth and variety of younger students’ proof values, as well as how to build on these values to create more flexible and welcoming lear ning environments, further research is needed that examines how youth engage in proof evaluation in difference contexts of disciplinary activity.
