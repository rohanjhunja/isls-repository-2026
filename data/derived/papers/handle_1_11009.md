# Pedagogical Planning and Practices to Support Student Experiences in School-Based Makerspaces

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This case study focuses on the pedagogical planning and practices of a teacher in a California public K-8 school-based makerspace, particularly for kindergarten through second grade students. The pedagogical practices in this study, including scaffolding, front loading, and the use of a teddy bear as an inanimate instructional aide will be compared to, and contrasted with, prior studies of facilitation in formal and informal makerspaces.

Makerspaces and the Maker movement entered K-12 education spaces over a decade ago, reintroducing structures and pedagogical practices that had been present  in K-12 schools prior to assessment-based reforms (Blikstein, 2013). Similar to public or member-accessible makerspaces, fab labs, or hackerspaces, the emphasis in school-based makerspaces has  often been on DIY-an acronym for “do it yourself”. Accordingly, much of the data on makerspaces in schools has focused on the learner and what they are able to create and/or learn.  In their seminal text on equity and power in educational makerspaces, Vossoughi, Hooper and Escudé (2016) illustrate through two vignettes how important the facilitation choices made by the adult in the makerspace, be it a teacher or other facilitator, is in impacting the outcomes of the youth who enter into the space. The terminology of facilitation and facilitator within maker-centered learning is complicated, as there is a tendency to contrast facilitators with teachers, which Vossoughi and colleagues (2016) argue downplays the role of pedagogy in maker-centered learning and “tends to treat the word ‘teacher’ and the practices associated with being a teacher as inherently didactic or problematic” (p. 220). This study, while conducted solely in a school-based makerspace and classrooms within the same school, observing the work of a certificated teacher, addresses pedagogical planning and practices  that can apply beyond formal school spaces. While it may seem somewhat semantic, the use of pedagogy and pedagogical moves is intentional in this study to emphasize the thought and intention behind the choices the teacher in the makerspace makes in support of student learning. This is in no way meant to suggest that the moves can only be made by a teacher, nor is it meant to deemphasize the agency of the youth who engage in learning in the space. Instead, intentional pedagogical choices can support greater equity and inclusion toward maker-centered learning, especially in time-constrained environments. RQ1: What pedagogical planning and practices does a teacher supporting maker-centered learning use? RQ2: How do a teacher’s pedagogical planning and practices  change over the course of a school year with the same classes of students?

## Literature review

Facilitation in a makerspace can vary widely, from completely unsupervised and unguided-perhaps all materials are put away when one is finished with them and those who enter into the space are also responsible for all the upkeep, to supervised and overly guided-such as in the case of a school-based makerspace with a teacher who leads students through a scripted lesson. Research on the pedagogy and facilitation of making is relatively nascent, with most peer-reviewed papers having been published in the last decade. Most of those studies have occurred in informal and non-formal learning spaces, with only a handful focused on school-based makerspaces. Pedagogical, or facilitative, decisions highlighted across multiple studies include those which occur prior to the students engaging in the activity and those which occur during a learning experience. Decisions made prior to the activity, also called pedagogical planning, include project or task design, the inclusion of  scaffolds, consideration of students’ background knowledge and intended learning trajectories, and the design of the space, including the accessibility and visibility of tools and materials (Becker and Jacobsen, 2021; Campos et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2018; Looney, 2023). Pedagogical practices enacted during the learning experiences focus largely on the use or non-use of interventions by the adult(s) in the space, including who prompts the intervention, if the educator chooses to intervene, and what the focus of the intervention is (Becker and Jacobsen, 2021; Campos et al., 2019; Gutwill et al., 2015; Kajamaa et al., 2020; White et al., 2021).

## Method and data sources

Anne Campbell School is a public school in the San Francisco Bay Area, serving about 700 TK-8 students. About 60% of students are identified as white, 25% as Hispanic, 10% Asian, with single-digit percentage representation 2345 of stude nts identified as Black, Pacific Islander, or mixed race. About 15% of students qualify for free or reduced lunch, the commonly used indicator of low-income families for the state of California. Lauren, the teacher whose practice is the focus of this case study, taught at Anne Campbell for four years prior to the start of the academic year in which observations occurred. Lauren, an Asian American woman, began teaching a decade ago in the same school district, with a small stint with an educational technology company before she returned to the district in her current role. As of the start of the academic year, she was one of three veteran makerspace teachers in the district and has been  considered by colleagues and administrators to have strong practices in supporting K-8 students with maker-centered learning. As part of the ethnographic process, the researcher is embedded in the makerspace for one to three days per week across the 2023-2024 academic year, following several classes  in kindergarten, first grade, and second grade as they engage in repeat visits to the space.  The classes of students visit the makerspace weekly for 45 minutes, with a one-week “asynchronous” gap each month. The researcher contemporaneously jot s field notes during each classroom observation  and follows up with a memo of expanded notes (Emerson et al., 2011). The researcher engages in regular recorded one-on-one interviews with Lauren, the vice principal, and the district STEAM coach, and attends nearly all makerspace-related professional development sessions.

## Anticipated findings / discussion

Pedagogical planning and practices appear pivotal to the success of formal learning activities, as for informal or non-formal learning activities (Einarsson & Hertzum, 2019). The success of an activity is based on the  teacher’s preparation, the structures they put in place to promote a community of practice, and the choices they make when choosing how and when to intervene in a student’s work (Kajamaa et al., 2020). Of special importance to the school-based context, and one which may find application in for mal and informal settings, is the 45-minute time constraint placed upon each session, requiring the teacher to strategically have certain materials available and to front load certain knowledge and skills prior to setting students off to their individual or group making activities. Additionally, the use of a stuffed teddy bear as an integral part of the teacher’s course of introducing making to students creates comfort and a memorable aspect of the experience to support engagement and recall  and may be of exceptional note as the researcher’s observations in the makerspace continue through the academic year.
