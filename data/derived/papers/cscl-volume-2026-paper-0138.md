# Encouraging Deeper Learning with GenAI: A Review of Pedagogical Constraints on GenAI and Their Impact in Higher Education

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper reviews how generative AI (GenAI) can be pedagogically constrained to promote deeper learning in higher education. A nalyzing 15 intervention studies comparing constrained GenAI use with free or no GenAI use, we found three delivery methods (process, activity, and tool levels), operationalized through five mechanisms. Results show primarily positive impacts on performance, motivation, and higher order thinking, though nearly half rely on self-reports. The findings highlight the role of constraints in GenAI use, and invite further research explicitly linking constraints to outcomes. Setting There has been rapidly growing interest in finding ways to integrate generative AI (GenAI) into higher education classrooms. Meanwhile, there is little clarity on how GenAI use can be moderated to better facilitate learning. Existing reviews have either explored strategies for GenAI use or evaluated the impact of having GenAI in learning as a whole, but have yet to distinguish the specific moderating or constraint mechanisms and their impact on learning (Batista et al., 2024; Almasri, 2024). To bridge this gap, our review goes beyond a binary view (use versus no use) with the following guiding questions: ● What student-facing constraint strategies for GenAI have been tested in higher education? ● What impact has been observed so far, on what types of learning outcomes? The current paper presents preliminary findings from an ongoing systematic review. We screened journal articles from 2022 (i.e., release of ChatGPT) to 2025, following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) standard (Page et al., 2021). To be included, studies had to 1) investigate the impact of GenAI in higher education learning; 2) design and clearly describe a constrained or moderated GenAI use condition; 3) employ a (quasi -)experimental design, where academic or cognitive outcomes were compared between a treatment group of constrained use and a control group of free or no use of GenAI. Systematic search across Scopus, Web of Science, and ACM yielded 1,910 articles, among which 30 met the inclusion criteria. The present analysis reports findings based on half (15) of the final dataset, selected alphabetically by the first author's last name (see full list in References). All studies were collaboratively coded by the first author and at least one other author, with discrepancies resolved through discussion for this preliminary analysis. Results Context of Studies: Interventions showed a balanced representation between eight in STEM and seven in non-STEM disciplines. ChatGPT (or its API) was the primary tool in 14 of 15 cases. Constraint Delivery: Our coding process revealed three main GenAI delivery methods. First, process-level guidelines (n=10) explicitized expectations for use, by delineating permissible forms of assistance (e.g., guiding usage with metacognitive support prompts; Xu et al., 2025). On the other hand, activity-level scaffolds (n=10) embedded constraints within the design of a learning activity, specifying the stages, timing, and conditions under which GenAI may be used (e.g., requiring independent initial attempt, followed by interaction and revision with GenAI; Jiang & Nakatani, 2025). Lastly, tool-level constraints (n=11) configured tools for specific interaction modes, such as limiting outputs to hints (Lee et al., 2024). Constraint Mechanisms: Across interventions, five mechanisms emerged across the four stages of problem-solving: understanding, planning, execution, and revision (Polya, 1945). One mechanism constrained GenAI use to the early stage of draft or brainstorming, with the expectation that these ideas would be refined by the learner. The second and third mechanisms applied to GenAI use in the execution stage: solution first required students to create their own solution first, while no answers primed GenAI to not give out whole answers and instead provide Socratic guidance. Lastly, in the revision stage, mechanisms included revision and verification, 734 as well as attributing and reflecting, where learners were either required to revise and verify AI output, or required to reflect on, and potentially report, how they used GenAI to perform the task. A single intervention often had multiple constraint delivery methods and/or mechanisms, reflecting layered approaches rather than reliance on a single lever. Overall, the pattern indicates a shift from merely restricting access to deliberately structuring how GenAI participates in learning, with mechanisms intuitively chosen to externalize cognition, require evidence-seeking, and align tool behavior with instructional intent. Impact on student learning Learning Outcome: On average, 4.4 outcomes were measured per study. The most common outcome category was Performance (e.g., exam grade, skill evaluation score), totaling 28 outcome measures across 13 (86.7%) studies. Other outcome categories included Motivation and Affect (e.g., intrinsic motivation and engagement), Higher Order Thinking (e.g., critical thinking), Quality of Student Artifact, and Metacognitive Skills. Direction of impact: Treatment groups showed largely positive impacts across student learning outcomes compared to control groups: 75% positive on Performance, 75% on Motivation & Affect, 80% on Higher Order Thinking, and the remaining two categories showed more mixed results. Figure 1 shows the breakdown of directions of impact for each outcome category, combined with a second-level breakdown of measurement type. Measurement Type: Nearly half of the 66 outcomes (48.5%) relied on self-reports, which may have led to positive bias. Notably, outcomes related to Higher Order Thinking and Motivation & Affect, while showing predominantly positive results, relied exclusively on self-report (see Figure 1). Discussion Empirical findings point to a generally positive impact of constrained GenAI use on student performance, with more mixed results for metacognitive skills or quality of work. However, this direction may be influenced by several biases. In addition to common sources of bias such as the observer effect, limitations of self-reports, or the novelty effect, a unique issue was that in some cases, assessments were made on the GenAI-assisted task itself. Students may have shown higher performance because they had assistance, and not because they truly learned more. Another limitation was that most of these results were gained from comparing constrained GenAI use to no GenAI use. There was a surprising dearth of research (20%) that compared free versus constrained use of GenAI (c.f. comparing no and constrained use of GenAI), which could help provide stronger evidence of pedagogical value by isolating the effects of constraint design rather than GenAI presence alone.
