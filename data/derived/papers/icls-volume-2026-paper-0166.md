# Do You Even Science, Bro? Epistemic and Meta-Epistemic Practices in an Online Fitness Community

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In the context of informal online engagement with scientific information, this study explored how a community of non-experts approaches knowledge from epistemically distinct sources. Using the AIR model of meta-epistemic reasoning as an analytical tool, we examined 363 Reddit comments discussing a biological phenomenon. A mixed-methods approach showed that over half of the comments mentioned sources of knowledge, mainly scientific authority or personal experience. Importantly, many comments demonstrated met a-epistemic reasoning by discussing goals, ideals, or processes for evaluating knowledge. Science was rarely challenged as an institution, but was critically examined for its relevance to real-life situations. Commenters also distinguished between “science” and “bro-science.” The findings portray laypeople as active epistemic agents whonegotiate credibility and relevance within socially mediated environments. We conclude by suggesting that educators could use authentic reasoning practices, in which meta-epistemic discourse serves as a bridge between real-life experiences and scientific norms.

Public engagement with science involves the various ways people seek, evaluate, reason, and use scientific information and data in their daily lives and civic decision-making. This concept emphasizes how non-experts collaboratively develop their understanding and influence regarding science in daily life contexts (Feinstein, 2011; Feinstein & Baram-Tsabari, 2024; Potochnik & Jacquart, 2025; Tabak, 2015). Such engagement can present itself through  many forms of scientific literacy, including reading  and commenting on science news (Kantor & Rafaeli, 2025), interpreting data visualizations (Tabak & Dubovi, 2023), discussing scientific topics online (Mintz et al., 2023), or considering evidence when making everyday health decisions (Radinsky & Tabak, 2022). These practices enable non-experts, sometimes referred to as “ competent outsiders” (Feinstein, 2011), to communicate with experts and support decision-making processes. Efforts to promote scientific literacy often emphasize citizens’ understanding of facts, positioning knowledge as a prerequisite for engagement. However, recent work shows that factors such as social identities (Potochnik & Jacquart, 2025), trust in scientific institutions (Radinsky & Tabak, 2022), and the nature of science understanding (Kampourakis & McCain, 2019) shape how knowledge is interpreted, debated, and repurposed. Recently, scholars have argued that enhancing the literacy of competent outsiders requires going beyond supporting basic understanding (Feinstein, 2011; Feinstein & Baram-Tsabari, 2024). This includes fostering metaepistemic engagement—evaluating scientific information and the credibility of sources and  pursuing goals such as truth or practical understanding (Barzilai & Zohar, 2016). From this perspective, scientific literacy involves reasoning about the credibility of multiple sources, including experts and personal experience. To develop models of science literacy that reflect these demands, we must examine how non-experts evaluate evidence and expertise in informal settings. Yet educational research often relies on idealized assumptions about how people should engage with scientific information (Feinstein, 2011). E ven when scholars focus on real-world contexts, they often infer the practices needed to interpret data without examining whether these practices align with how the public actually engages with scientific information. Feinstein and Baram-Tsabari (2024) argue that public engagement with science is social, involving interactions with peers and experts in decision-making and group discussions. Science education should help individuals evaluate and use their social epistemic networks. Daily engagement often involves combining sources like scientific evidence, expert authority, and personal experience to assess claims and make decisions. However, this often proves challenging because such sources are often seen as competing rather than complementary (e.g., Mezza, 2025; Whyte & Crease, 2010).

Consequently, this study examines how laypeople engage with scientific information in informal, socially mediated contexts. We analyze the epistemic practices that shape such engagement in naturalistic settings to inform models of scientific literacy that better reflect how people encounter and use science in everyday life. Therefore, o ur research question was:  What epistemic practices characterize laypeople’s engagement with scientific information in informal online contexts?

## Theoretical framework

The competent outsider (Feinstein, 2011) is not a scientist and therefore not expected to engage in firsthand research. Rather, competent outsiders communicate with sources around them while assessing  them and the applicability of the information they provide. While laypeople might sometimes discuss mechanisms or raw data, their reliance on the expertise of others suggests they must assess the reliability of external sources and the relevance of provided information in light of the problem they seek to solve (Feinstein & Baram-Tsabari, 2024). The discussion of sources, their credibility, and their applicability has been described as meta-epistemic discourse, characterized by the AIR model (Barzilai & Chinn, 2018; Chinn et al., 2020). This model  includes three facets: epistemic Aims and values, epistemic Ideals, and epistemic Reliable processes. Epistemic aims and values refer to the goals individuals or communities pursue. While scientists and science educators often place ‘understanding’ or ‘truth-seeking’ as default epistemic aims (Kapon & Berland, 2023), other aims may predominate in real-world situations, including aiming for practical solutions to specific problems (e.g., Feinstein, 2014). For example, in educational settings, epistemic aims have been shown to shape how  students pursue mechanistic explanations (Livni-Alcasid & Haskel-Ittah, 2026). Epistemic ideals are the standards used to evaluate epistemic products, such as determining whether evidence is high-quality or relevant to the case at hand. Finally, reliable epistemic processes encompass the methods used to achieve aims, including protocols for conducting observations or experiments.

Notably, AIR has often been used in classroom and disciplinary settings where scientific norms, such as controlled experiments and mechanistic explanations, are foregrounded. However, public engagement with science outside academic contexts often involves balancing scientific evidence, expert authority, and personal experience (Feinstein & Baram-Tsabari, 2024; Feinstein, 2011). This study adopts a plural approach: rather than treating scientific knowledge as the sole standard,  our interest lies in how participants apply reliable epistemic processes while negotiating epistemic ideals in light of real-world goals. We therefore use the AIR framework to examine how laypeople not only judge but alsonegotiate source credibility. Here, epistemic discourse refers to discussions of knowledge sources answering the question “How do we know?”, while meta-epistemic discourse examines how participants evaluate a source’s credibility and relevance, addressing the question “How do we know that a source is valid?”. This framework, further elaborated in the Data Analysis section, captures not only expressions of trust in science and the science community’s ideals but also the foundations, limitations, and applicability of the different sources  available to laypeople.

## Context and data collection

Much of the research on public engagement and scientific  literacy has focused on politically charged issues such as vaccination and climate change. In these, questions of trust, social identity, and perceptions of risk and harm often dominate discussions (e.g., Felton et al., 2024; Radinsky & Tabak, 2022). While these studies are important, it remains unclear whether their findings generalize to less risky, less political settings. Our study examined online communities discussing fitness-related topics that could be framed as scientific, enabling epistemic and meta-epistemic discourse. We focused on the “anabolic window” phenomenon, the belief that a 30-minute post-exercise period is critical for protein intake to support muscle growth and recovery. Although this claim has been challenged in scientific literature, pointing to a longer 24–48-hour window (Aragon & Schoenfeld, 2013), it continues to provoke debate in online fitness forums. To create our dataset, we used ChatGPT (OpenAI, GPT5) to generate Python scripts that queried the Reddit API for fitness-related subreddits (e.g., fitness, bodybuilding, nutrition, supplements, powerlifting, crossfit) with at least 10,000 subscribers. After manually excluding off-topic communities (e.g., memes, gaming, politics), we collected posts published between January 2012 and August 2025 that contained the phrase “anabolic window,” along with their associated comments, subreddit names,  and author metadata. This yielded 132 posts and 4,619 comments across 32 subreddits.

This paper analyzes a single subreddit —r/Fitness —which had the most posts (23). Data included 363 comments, from 196 distinct users, as 135 purely social comments were excluded (e.g., “Thanks”). While other subreddits featured longer discussions, r/Fitness was selected for its evidence-oriented discourse. As of September 2025, it reported 691,000 weekly visitors and 1,600 concurrent viewers (Reddit, 2025).

## Data analysis

To analyze our dataset, t his study employed a qualitatively driven explanatory sequential design (Cheryl N., 2023), combining in-depth discourse analysis with supporting quantitative description. The qualitative phase formed the core of the analysis, examining how lay participants constructed, justified, and challenged knowledge 1313 claims within informal online discussions. The subsequent quantitative phase provided descriptive confirmation, illustrating the overall prevalence and distribution of the identified discourse types. In the first qualitative phase of our research, focusing on participants ’ knowledge claims and their responses to peers, we analyzed the dataset to identify three forms of discourse (Table 1): content (what we know), epistemic reasoning (how we know, often in the form of  source citation), and meta-epistemic reflection (how we assess a source’s credibility). The subsequent quantitative phase involved counting coded instances to show that these discourse types were common enough to deserve attention. This complemented qualitative insights. Finally, in a second qualitative phase, we re-examined the coded segments to deepen the analysis, identifying recurring types of epistemic and meta-epistemic statements. This stage allowed us to refine our categories and to produce a deeper, richer description of recurring themes. Unsurprisingly, most comments in our dataset (79%) contained some form of Content, mainly mechanistic explanations, causal claims, training routines, and prescriptive instructions. Importantly, most (57%) included Epistemic statements. Almost half of these cited scientific authorities, while a quarter cited personal experiences, and some cited para-scientific experts, such as coaches and educators.  Epistemic statements  involved citing sources to support Content claims, yet at times  focused on source credibility (these were also  coded as Metaepistemic). The Meta-epistemic code appeared in 31% of the statements. These included a combination of declared aims, epistemic ideals, and epistemic processes. An in-depth view of the M eta-epistemic statements revealed key observations. When discussing aims, most commenters mentioned practical aims, such as adding muscle mass or dietary restrictions. A notable example of this featured a commenter sharing their own experience and asking, “What does science have to say about this?” In other cases, real-world goals  were the lens  through which commenters debated  deep scientific knowledge, such as biological mechanisms. Contrarily, practical aims sometimes overshadowed epistemic interest in underlying mechanisms. For example, when discussing a  source’s suggestion to consume large amounts of protein, a commenter stated his acceptance of advice as a black box: “Norton talks about this and explains… I’m not going to pretend I understand all the science, because quite frankly I don’t give a s#!t.” Second, s cience as an institution was rarely subject to criticism. When critique arose, it centered on epistemic ideals and processes related to a  study’s generalizability (e.g., the examined population), external 1314 validity (the conditions under which the study was conducted), and construct validity (the variables measured). As a recurring pattern, most criticism of science itself focused on its applicability to goals and aims. Criticism of experiential knowledge mainly targeted its failure to meet epistemic ideals like reproducibility and attention to non-causality. For example, commenting on another member’s shared experiences, a commenter said: “Then you don’t know how to science. Yes, using your own results as evidence, you can typically make the right conclusions, but it’s not a sure thing.”  Similar remarks about experiential knowledge crossing epistemic boundaries often include d the term “bro-science.” For instance, a commenter stated, “Anabolic window is bro science,”  to denote flawed knowledge sources and dismiss the phenomenon. Opposingly, personal experience was often seen as an initial step in discussing scientific phenomena, as evidenced by comments such as: “People always use it as an insult, but bro-science normally has some kind of basis in reality.” Personal experience was also used to provide context for scientific claims by comparing research Exceptions to this, where a source —either scientific or experiential—was completely considered irrelevant, were rare. One example is the comment that dismisses the credibility of experiential knowledge and para-scientific expert advice: “I used to believe most of the pseudoscience Jeff Cavaliere spews.” Our results reveal that informal online discussions about science-related topics are not merely casual exchanges of advice or opinion. Most comments engaged substantively with content, epistemic reasoning, and metaepistemic reflection, indicating that participants were reflecting on how knowledge is established and evaluated. Although meta-epistemic statements appeared in fewer comments than epistemic ones, their presence was neither marginal nor superficial. When users engaged in meta-epistemic reasoning, they often discussed methodological concerns such as generalizability, c onstruct validity, and the scope of experimental findings —a discourse that necessitates  metacognitive skills  (Barzilai & Zohar, 2016; Chinn et al., 2020). Notably, these everyday contexts, such as fitness and nutrition. This adds to previous observations pointing to laypeople’s sophisticated engagement with data and data sources (e.g., Kantor & Rafaeli, 2025; Mintz et al., 2023). Importantly, the results point to a diversity  of epistemic authorities—scientific sources coexist ing with appeals to personal experience and para-scientist-expert authorities. The recurrent invocation of “ bro-science” further illustrates how community members negotiate the boundaries between formal and informal expertise. While the term was sometimes used to reject anecdotal reasoning, it also served as a cultural shorthand to differentiate between kinds of evidence that are “scientific enough” for practical purposes. Previous discussions have called for viewing public engagement with science as embedded  in social settings (Feinstein & Baram-Tsabari, 2024), ye t studies often highlight the tension between scientific and experiential knowledge (e.g., Mezza, 2025; Whyte & Crease, 2010). Our findings show  how this tension is negotiated into a constructive discussion within a natural, real-world context. Crucially, they show that meta-epistemic discourse helps define the boundaries of epistemically distinct knowledge sources, exemplifying laypeople’s engagement through non-hierarchical participation. Taken together, these findings portray laypeople  not as passive consumers of information but as active epistemic agents who engage in a sensemaking process, drawing on tools, experiences, and social resources.

## Implications for science education

From an educational standpoint, our findings  underscore the need to treat public engagement with science as a sophisticated process. This process involves synthesizing epistemic sources without  establishing a hierarchy among them. Our findings show that such a position is manageable, scientifically appropriate, and aligns with laypeople’s behavior. Laypeople can coordinate epistemically distinct sources of knowledge to  solve ‘mundane’ problems. Moreover, they cando so by employing meta-epistemic reasoning that outlines epistemic boundaries. This has two key implications. First, it suggests that incorporating epistemically diverse sources can be a crucial entry point into science education. This aligns with earlier calls in the science education community to adopt a more inclusive, socially embedded view towards science education (Feinstein & Baram-Tsabari, 2024). Second, our findings reveal a mechanism that supports integrating, rather than replacing, scientific discourse with experiential knowledge. Meta-epistemic discourse sets boundaries for each source, enabling a negotiation of each source’s place within a combined knowledge base. In this setting, s cience can provide ideals such as reliability and causal validity, while personal accounts can support the evaluation of context and generalizability. We, therefore, find promise in emphasizing meta-epistemic discussions in the science classroom.
