# Reframing Teen Services: A Participatory Approach to Revealing and Aligning Staff Goals and Organizational Priorities

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper shares findings from a participatory study with a large urban library system to center staff perspectives in shaping teen services. Through surveys, site visits, and collaborative analysis, staff highlighted disconnects between their relational goals and system-level priorities and shared strategies for design and connection. Findings surfaced opportunities for cross-branch learning and system-level shifts in priorities and support.

## Introduction

Staff who work with young people in informal learni ng environments are often required to meet outcome-driven measures imposed by funders and policymakers which don’t always align with the relational and communit ycentered approaches that are most effective in youth work. Baldridge (2020) argues that this paradox places youth workers in the position of navigating the demands o f accountability while attempting to provide affirm ing and supportive spaces for young people. In the United States, youth workers are encountering additional overlapping stressors impacting both those they serve and the i nstitutions they work for. Teen mental health is a significant and growing concern, with recent data indicating th at over 40% of teens report persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness (CDC, 2023), with a multitude of contributing factors including academic stress and socio-political issues, compounded by the lingering effects of the COVID-19 pandemic, which disrupted routines and support systems (Pew Research Center, 2023). Simultaneously, the organizations that emp loy youth workers—including libraries, nonprofits, and other community institut ions—are under immense strain. Workforce shortages, declining donor support, political pressure, and in creased demands for services, have left many strugg ling to sustain operations (Hays, 2023; Steward et al., 2021).

Aligned with the ISLS 2025 theme, Educating for world-making: Envisioning and enacting sustainable solutions to global crises, this paper explores these dynamics through an ana lysis of how staff navigate systemic expectations, challenges, and opportunities to provide inclusive youth spaces and experiences. We share findings from a New York Public Library (NYPL) study intended to strengthen the model for library teen services. This work employed participatory approaches that invited staff to share and co-create solutions, aligning organizational goals with on-the-ground realities. By focusing on staff knowledge and perspectives, the study identifies areas of innovation as well as disconnect —both systemic and relational —and seeks to reframe practices in ways t hat enable progress even within the constraints of stressed organizational systems. Drawing on Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (Engeström, 1987), we view these disconnects as contradictions within activity systems—between roles, tools, and objectives—that when made visible and negotiable can catalyze shifts in practice and organizational learning (Penuel, 2019). These efforts offer valuable insights into how inclusivity is both claimed and shaped, and how systemic progress demands multi-level attention. The paper is organized along three key questions: (1) How do staff working directly with teens define what is important? (2) How are staff goals and values aligned with organizational roles, expectations, and available supports? (3) How do staff navigate the physical and social tensions inherent in creating inclusive, youthcentered spaces? By examining these questions and highlighting methods for foregrounding staff voices and interpretation, we argue that making visible the disconnects between staff and systemic priorities not only fosters mutual understanding but also creates pathways for collaborative transformation.

## Methods

A key goal of this research process was to establis h staff and leadership as partners in design and ev aluation of New York Public Library teen services, inviting exp loration of questions raised in a pilot study focus ed on teen perspectives conducted the previous year and planning approaches for new staff-centered data collection. An advisory committee of staff members, some of whom work in direct service with teens and some of whom support teen services at the system level, engaged in codes ign activities before and after the data collection to inform the development of measures and collectively interpret data with an external research-evaluation partner. Together we are the authors of this paper, and continue to m eet to consider implications and next steps and how  to best share findings with various internal and external audiences. The data shared in the paper represents a scan of the landscape of NYPL teen services, centering staff voices from 75 unique system sites located across 123 square miles of diverse communities populated by over 3.5 million people. An online survey was developed to query staff goals, experiences, and perceptions of teens and teen service within their community and workplace, and included inventories of material resources, staff supports, and perceived system goals. The initial survey invitation came from staff on the advisory committee and 98 staff responded, representing 86% of NYPL site locations. To more deeply explore patterns and trends in the survey, 11 site visits were conducted. Locations were selected by grouping sites with similar profiles and selecting a random subset from each group to ensure range and representation of community (geographic and demographic), space (location size and dedication of teen spaces), and perspective (staff with different views of roles and support). Visits were conducted by the research-evaluation partner and included observation and semi-structured interviews. In total, 20 staff members who worked directly with teens and 6 branch managers were interviewed. Interviews were recorded and transcribed with permission. Visits yielded 16.5 recording hours and 25 pages of field notes, and over 200 photos. Quantitative analysis was conducted with SPSS and qualitative data were explored through open-coding and thematic interpretation, with refinement and further insights generated during advisory committee meetings.

## Findings

How do staff working directly with teens define what is important? Staff stress the diversity of teens and the differe nt ways they use the space. Some locations have a c ore group of highly engaged teens who regularly participate in a ctivities, others see more independent teens who ut ilize the space differently from day to day. Some teens gravitate towards technology-focused activities while others prefer creative expressive arts. Regardless, staff agree t hat most teens are not there to attend scheduled pr ograms, estimating that while more than half of the teens come regularly (at least a few times a week), most (68%) are not there to participate in specific programs or opportunities. This influences what staff prioritize. Developing creativity and agency by providing community-oriented spaces and mentorship Staff were asked to select their primary goals from  a list of 15 priorities for teen services (includi ng both goals for service and intended outcomes of service) gener ated from New York Public Library marketing materia ls, insights from the advisory committee, and best practices in youth development literature about out-of-school time support (e.g. Ito et al., 2013). The item also incl uded a write-in “other” option. Providing a safe and supervised public space for teens  was a priority for nearly everyone (94%) and the co llection of goals prioritized by over 50% of staff (see Figure 1) demonstrates a strong commitment to fostering teen creativity and agency by creating community and offering guidance and mentorship.

Figure 1 Five Most Commonly Selected Staff Goals for Teen Spaces Indicating the Proportion of staff who Selected Each Fostering dignity, wellness, and belonging through relationships Qualitative descriptions of staff goals and commitments illustrate how providing safe and supervised public spaces is tightly connected to wanting to ensure teens hav e places where they experience dignity and compassi on. Staff prioritize creating a safe and welcoming space wher e teens can relax, socialize, and escape external p ressures. They describe how regardless of what is going on at home, in the teen  space “they have like extended family taking care of them. This is their place.” Some consider the access to the library space and community as a “path to wellness for our youth” at a time when “free speech seems to be declining and violence and poverty is rising.” One staff member talks about building collective se nse of worth and addressing systemic inequities by not only providing access to a comfortable space with valuab le resources but making sure tee ns feel entitled to it, “ I've always lived in low income communities, and we're often overlooked. So [a goal is] having the space for them to stay in, just to feel like, ‘Oh, I deserve this.’” A place where they will “not only be seen but heard and respected.” Most consider consistent and personal interactions as “the most meaningful” approach and where, “I can  see I have real impact […] it's like learning someone's name, like that kind of stuff makes a really big difference to feel like, really seen and known and like a part of this  world.” Relationships with staff are considered in fluential to teens returning, knowing what is available, and mak ing decisions about how to participate. In fact,  40% of staff report that teens attend programs at their branch as a favor to staff. How are staff goals aligned with organizational roles, expectations, and supports? Perceived disconnect of real-time relational goals and outcomes compared to system future focus Most staff (80%) agreed that their overall goals for teen services are aligned with what the organization prioritizes but comparing specific goals was more nuanced. Diff erences highlight staff perceptions that the system  focuses on technology access and prioritizes more tradition al measures of academic and workplace success than they do (i.e., developing STEM skills and knowledge (17% di fference), access tonew technology and tools (14% difference), academic achievement (14% difference), and workforce development (11% difference)). Alternately, more YA staff report attention to relational work a nd identity-linked outcomes more so than their view of what the system prioritizes (i.e., fostering creativity and self expression (23% difference), community dev elopment (21% difference), mentorship (14% difference), and wellbeing (12% difference)). Staff roles are flexible, but running programs and monitoring spaces limits time alongside teens Most staff agree that their role is flexible (88%) but just over half (57%) feel they have the time to  engage teens in regular conversations and build relationships. W hen asked about roles played frequently from a list  of nine (generated based on existing research and insights from the advisory committee) and a write-in “other” option, most common were roles related to running programs (“facilitator,” 90%, and “designer”, 77%) and super vising the space (“monitor”, 88%). Not only were programmi ng and management roles played by most staff, they are also where staff spend most of their time—shown when staff were asked to rank roles in terms of the time required day-to-day (where 1 = most frequent, see figure 2). So, wh ile programs are not the primary reason teens come to the space, most of staff time is spent planning and  delivering programs. A perceived and perhaps histo ric focus on scheduled teen program quotas and expectations was identified by staff as potentially driving this use of time. And despite the fact that providing guidance and me ntorship is a prioritized goal for 63% of staff, wh ile half of the staff find ways to prioritize caregiving (i.e., 49% ranked it in their top five most frequently played roles), most report less time and attention in roles identified as particularly transformational for teens, includi ng supporter, broker, and mentor (e.g. Barron et al., 2014; Ito et al., 2013). Figure 2 Staff Ranking of Role Frequency, with a Midline Split between Roles Played More (ranked 1-5) and Less (6-10) Differentiated spaces influence relational practices but many teen spaces are shared The advisory committee emphasized the importance of  a distinct and designated space for teens to foste r engagement and a sense of belonging. About half of staff (51%) considered their teen space to feel separate. Staff working in teen spaces that they cons idered separate were significantly more likely to p rioritize providing mentorship and guidance  compared to other staff (71% v. 54%) and play signi ficantly more roles regularly — including being more likely to broker, mentor, and support teens—than those staff working in spaces with less differentiation between the rest of the library. The monitor and advocacy roles often encompass the work staff do to keep the space separate and reserved for teens, ensuring that teens are within the boundaries of what is expected by the system and other people and advocating for teens when these expectations are unfair. How do staff navigate tensions and opportunities to shape inclusive spaces? Arranging physical space for a sense of separation and access Over three quarters of staff (76%) agreed their space is inviting and comfortable, encouraging teen s to hang out and spend their time there. When asked to describe the “vibe,” most emphasize  a relaxed atmosphere that is “welcoming” and “chill.” In locations where teen spaces are shared with adults or children, staff navigate tensions by creating clear distinctions and advocating for a nd with teens. They procure and arrange movable fur niture to establish boundaries. They signal ownership of the space by teens by engaging teens in co-creating flexible and visible use agreements and posting signage about access to resources and tools that are not visible. Moving beyond technology as a lure and better connecting with “why” of service Many staff reflect a recognition that focusing sole ly on technology as an attraction (e.g. 3D printers) may not address teens’ deeper needs. Staff emphasize that t echnology is most impactful when integrated into me aningful activities that connect with teen s’ interests and creativity. Some consider the basi cs—internet, outlets, and charging ports—to be most important, allowing teens to navigate on  their own devices. Arranging activities that combine physical and digital (e.g., sewing machines  with digital de sign capabilities; tablets using Procreate to enhance scanned-in sketches) support flexible contributions over time and connections to hands-on work. Assembling opportunities for low-pressure creativity and exploration While not recognized as part of the official partic ipation numbers collected by the system, staff purp osefully arrange activities and resources that teens can engage with independently or in small groups on their own. Several staff emphasize the critical need for such areas to  counter pressures teens face. These areas often in clude board games, puzzles, and drawing materials, while some a ssemble more creative, expressive, and open-ended opportunities for teens to pick up and participate or not for as long as they want, like crochet or sewing, or openended collective art installations that invite contributions for weeks or months. Leveraging scheduled programs as a way to build relationships For many staff, especially those without dedicated teen spaces, system staffing models and expectation s for facilitating scheduled programs has meant that prog ram time serves as the primary avenue for fostering relationships. Staff describe intentionally d esigning programs that create a relaxed atmosphere where teens can connect with peers and staff. For many, consistency  and flexibility are key design principles, “That f amiliarity makes them want to attend, even if they just chill and don't do the activity.” Programs like Sewing Saturdays or Teen Lounge provide routine and reliability that foster trust a nd community. Staff often use this time together in a shared space to deepen relationships by recognizing individual teens’ interests and achievements.

## Discussion

Recognizing and celebrating the ways library staff circumnavigate systems to serve their communities p rovides critical insights into the larger system. By amplif ying staff perspectives, this study highlights how organizations can uncover disconnects that inhibit alignment and progress, understanding infrastructure as malleable  (Hayden & Roque, 2024; Penuel, 2019) and thinking about rea llocation and shifts together. Making these disconn ects visible enables systems to address barriers, adapt policies, and communicate limitations transparently, fostering mutual understanding and support within the organiz ation. Findings from this study have already result ed in system administrators exploring new scheduling mode ls to ensure staff have more consistent shared time  with teens needed to build relationships. Next steps inc lude integrating more teen voices and sharing findi ngs across the organization to jointly develop a multi-year plan to improve teen services across the syste m. Continued joint interpretation amplifies shared commitment to equitable opportunities for teen learning and development, while better integrating design and practice in service of immediate needs alongside longer-term trajectories. This work has broader implications for studying and  shaping learning in complex, multi-site environments, emphasizing approaches that balance s ystemic coherence with local practice and adaptabil ity.

Using participatory strategies to better align orga nizational priorities with practitioner insights al so offers a promising path for libraries seeking to advance the UN Sustainable Development Goals and strengthen their role as resilient, community-centered institutions (IFLA, 2019).
