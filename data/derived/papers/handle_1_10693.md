# Elementary Students’ Emergent and Divergent Goals in Collective Embodied Modeling Activities

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper aims to understand how elementary students construct and pursue individual goals in a loosely structured collective embodied modeling activity. We explore how engaging in negotiating diverse goals impacts students’ sensemaking about an ecosystem they are modeling. We used Interaction Analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to discern students’ individual goals in their embodiment and communication and compared them to the collective goals stated by the facilitator and/or group strategies. Our findings lead to recommendations for educators and designers, emphasizing the importance of supporting students’ diverse goals to enhance their agency and foster a more inclusive learning environment.

## Introduction

In formal schooling, learning objectives and classroom norms are typically predetermined by schools and teachers, with students expected to conform to them (Davis et  al., 2020). Previous research frequently examines learning in situations where students’ goals appear to align  with those set by designers/facilitators. However,  focusing solely on students’ adoption of goals set by designers/facilitators may stifle meaningful and pr oductive interactions with students (Gutierrez et al., 1995). Moreover, many st udies suggest that students pursuing individual goals can enhance learning by enabling them to observe new patterns and practice negotiating multiple perspectives (DeLiema et al., 2019; Langer-Osuna, 2018). However, the challenge remains in identifying varied goals in embodied learning environments where goals  are not always explicitly articulated verbally. Th is study investigates fifth graders’ emergent and divergent goals in a collective embodied learning context in GEM-STEP (https://embodiedplay.org/), a mixed-reality learning environment (Danish et al., 2022).  We designed a loosely structured embodied modeling activity to support students’ exploration of major events and interactions within an aquatic ecosystem. Our conjecture was that the flexibility would encourage students to develop and pursue diverse goals, and engaging in negotiations of different go als would impact their sensemaking. We asked: 1) How did students respond to a facilitator’s framed activity goal in their embodied modeling and communication? And what does this suggest about the goals they were pursuin g? 2) How did students’ observations and discussion of emergent and divergent goals facilitate their sensemaking of the model and model refinement?

## Theoretical framework

This analysis follows the Learning in Embodied Activity Framework (LEAF; Dansih et al., 2020), linking Cultural Historical Activity Theory (CHAT; Engeström, 1999) with views on embodied cognition (Lindgren et al., 2019). CHAT views human actions as goal-oriented, and individuals in a collective activity pursue b oth individual and collective goals. We follow this theorization and utilize LEAF’s CHAT-based perspective on embodied activity as impacted by multiple mediators, with embodiment serving a critical role in the mediation. We emphasize how individual’s embodied acts reveal the potentially pursued goals and mediators that impact the goal's evolution. In this study, individual students act as fish to interact with other agents. They may hold an individual survival goal or work toward a collective survival. Individual goals can evolve into a collective goal. Yet, individual goals may continue to emerge (Saxe, 1991) despite an established collective goal (Yamagata-Lynch & Haudenschild, 2009), for example, a student may withdraw from the activity to observe others’ modeling. We view learning as a change in students’ modeling practice over time (Gr een & Engeström, 2014). Scientific modeling is a  key practice that deepens students’ knowledge about  science and understanding of the scientific enterprise (National Research Council, 2 012). A core part of modeling practice is the socia l and interactional sensemaking work that students collectively pursue (Passmore et al., 2014), through thinking across phenomena representations, including physical and simulated (Manz & Georgen,  2023). This study involved students’ exploration of intertwined participatory and computational models of ecosystems (Danish et al., 2022). By cycling between models of the same phenomena, students may develop ideas about explanatory mechanis ms and modeling practice. We identified and explained changes in students’ modeling practices to examine learning.

## Methods

Design The present modeling activity us ed a mixed-reality embodied learning environment GEM-STEP (Danish et al., 2022), which integrates a motion-tracking computational modeling simulation to media te students’ playful exploration of agent-based models of ecosystems. Students take an agent’s role by walking in a tracked area with a wearable tag on their hats or dragging the agent on an iPad. Six anchors track the hat tags, sending location data to a computer, allowing students to observe their virtual agents in a simulated ecosystem (Figure 1). This analysis examines an aquatic ecosystem model where students, acting as fish gain energy from algae that gain energy from the sun. Each agent has an energy bar showing energy levels in three colors: orange (unhealthy), green (healthy), and red (dangerous). The agent turns transparent, if the energy depletes.  Our design intentionally gave students the choice to move freely in the space and decid e whether to coordinate their actions as they collect energy fro m the limited algae. Students could focus on their fish’s individual survival, or they could coordinate with each other to maintain collective survival. We aimed for students to realize that coordination could enhance ecosystem health and then discuss how environmental factors lead to similar behaviors in nature. A facilitator suggested aiming for a healthy ecosystem with all fish alive. However, s tudents were not obligated to follow this goal. We identified the emergence and traced the evolution of multiple goals, through which we explored how goals were mediated in the activity and how negotiations of these goals impacted students’ sensemaking and inquiry processes. The analysis began with examining content logs, noting where student exhibiting confl icting interpretations of modeling goals. Day 2 sho wed a recurring pattern of individuals prioritizing personal fish survival, and the class discussed this issue and generated strategies to tackle it. We conducted iterative Interaction Analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) on Day 2’s data, including repeatedly viewing three consented groups’ modeling, discussing their interpretations, and summarizing patterns in analytical memos.  We identified three types of individual student goa ls and three patterns of goal evolution. Impacts on l earning were examined by focusing on instances when students verbalized a target understanding of the ecosystem or provided insights for model refinements.

## Findings

RQ1: Three types of goals and three goal evolution patterns. Goal type 1: Individual survival goal We categorized instances of students prioritizing individual survi val as cases of pursuing a divergent individual goal (as it differs from the facilitator-proposed goal), which became salient when they view ed outlasting other fish survival as winning. Student Y considered her survival as winning and shared her strategy, “How I won was I ate the algae, and then I went to the algae that has like ((mimic gesturing the energy bar)) the lef t, the food left. …regain my energy… I have mine.” The goal emerged w hen students strategically consumed algae’s energy based on proximity and energy availability. Student  Y visited the twonearest algae and spent a consid erable amount of time (13 seconds) on the first  one to fully replenished her energy, which exhauste d the alga’s energy. Then, she moved to the only available energy source, where she stayed until the alga was depleted. Student Y’s reflection and embodiment revealed an individual su rvival goal, which could be mediated by mechanics w ithin the simulation that was gamified by the student (the agent-specific energy bars). Goal type 2: Collective survival goal We observed students embodying a collective goal when they verbally adopted the facilitator-framed collective goal and/or modeled it through embodiment as exampl es of following the collective goal. Student R shared her strategy, “I kinda eat something, and then I moved away. So, it could regrow.” The emergence of this collective goal during embodied modeling typically occurred when students consumed less energy (or shorter stay duration) and relocated even with low energy level. Student R stayed briefly at her first alga for 5 seconds, a comparatively brief period, in contrast to other students (e.g., Student Y and B) in the same group,  and she avoided energy depletion. She remained at the second algae for 3 seconds and then meandered around that alga. Her reflection and modeling behaviors seemed to feature a social c onsciousness of others and revealed a collective go al of maintaining a healthy ecosystem. Hence, we interpre ted her embodiment and communication as indicative of following the facilitator-framed activity goal and enacted by not depleting algae energy and leaving it for others. Goal type 3: Observation goal We noted cases of students pausing embodied modeling but remaining engaged with the simulation as indicating a divergent observation goal. This goal emergence w as characterized by students pausing their movements and distancing themselves from modeling peers while continuing to gaze toward the simulation. Student B paused the modeling at the 22nd second and distanced himself from others but maintained focus on the simulation. Students inquiring about simulation’s reactions to others’ e mbodiment can further evidence this goal emergence. Student B inquired about the control of Fish 019 and initiated an observation, “The algae never regrow.” His actions of pausing the modeling, stepping out of the fish’s ro le, and asking question potentially suggest an obse rvational goal, mediated by a desire to understand the simulation or just refraining from consuming algae for others. Goal pattern 1: Moving from a divergent individual survival goal to a collective survival goal We observed students shifting from individual survival goals to collective ones. For example, Student D in Group 2 did not follow the group’s strategy of going to t he alga closest to the sun. Instead, he started wit h the bottomright alga, which was planned as the final one to c onsume. He consumed energy for 17 seconds until his energy bar was charged to green and depleted this alga’s energy. His embodiment showcased an individual goal. However, upon hearing observers’ advice, “guys, wai t for it to regrow”,  he moved away from an alga still containing energy while another student continued energy consumption. His e mbodied modeling started with an individual survival focus; however, he was willing to adjust his actions based on observers’ feedback. Goal pattern 2: Moving from a collective focus to a divergent individual survival goal We identified cases where students shifted from a c ollective goal/plan to an individual goal, exemplif ied by Student B’s modeling (through an iPad) in Group 2. He initially aligned with the group’s strategy, interacting with bottom-left alga as planned and avoiding depleting energy, showing a collective focus. However, his later choice to consume energy at the fifth alga, despite having  sufficient energy indicated a shift to an individu al focus. The emergence of this individual goal might have been m ediated by other students deviating from the plan and depleting energy or by a personal desire to have more energy at the end of the modeling. Goal pattern 3: Working toward the collective survival goal but with divergent individual actions We observed Student GB and BL’s modeling in Group 2 exhibited a collective focus but deviated from a collective plan as a case of working towards collective goals with divergent ind ividual actions. These two students did not seem to follow the collective plan, but their behaviors aligned with the collective goa l, as evidenced by their minimal energy consumption to leave resources for o thers. For example, GB started the modeling with an unplanned alga, but limited energy consumption to only two seconds to avoid energy depletion for others.  GB continued her limited energy consumption until the end. Thus, while GB did not strictly adhere to the steps of the collective strategy, she did regulate her actions to conserve energy for  others. Her embodiment reflected an orientation toward the collective survival goal.

RQ2: Students’ discussion of goals supported model understanding and refinement Our analysis of small groups and the whole-class debrief revealed that pursuing different goals facilitated students’ sensemaking of the model. Student B in group 1, who  potentially held a divergent observation goal, sha red an important observation after modeling, “The algae ne ver regrew.” He also pointed out that algae received energy from the sun when responding to a follow-up question from the facilitator regarding how algae regrow. Observing the algae regrowth in the simulation required focused attention toward changes in the algae’s energy bar when the sunbeam passed over it. Student B’ focused observat ion might have been more challenging to make had he constantly been moving. Student B’s observation further motivated Students Y and R to share observation regarding why algae did not regrow. Student Y, who had an individual survival goal, mentioned that alg ae’s spatial positions impact the chance of it regaining  energy from the sun, “... the algae was too low.” Student Y targeted the nearest algae in the lower pond to gain energy, aligning with her individual focus because movement consumes energy and fish energy diminishes over tim e. Her sensemaking on this model was grounded in her embodiment that was driven by the individual survival goal. Student Y’s observation led the next groups to move the algae to the pond’s upper section. Student R, focused on the col lective survival goal, contested Y’s observation, “… the highest one still did not grow even when the sun is over it”, noting that upper algae also failed to regrow despite the brighter sunlight, suggesting  other influencing factors. Her collective goal foc us led her to eat a little and await algae regrowth. Her observations regarding the upper algae not regrowing were rooted in her embodiment of achieving the collective goal. Her expansion on Student Y’s observation motivated the next group to move half of the algae to the top and half to the bottom to test how position impacts algae regrowth.

## Discussion

This paper aims to advance our understanding of how researchers can identify the way students’ individual goals emerge and support learning in a loosely structured  collective embodied modeling activity. Students’ g oals were revealed in embodiment strategie s they employed to attain those goals and were thus  discernable even when students did not explicitly articulate them. Students debrief activities provided them with an opportunity to clarify their goals and build upon each other’s observation s as insights for model refinement in subsequent ro unds. The results were in accordance with our design intention to foster the emergence of various types of goals and engage students in negotiating them. This finding adds to the extant literature by showing how the intuitive embodied actions of young students, which might seem rather chaotic to an outsider, are goal directed, and that making those goals visible is powerful for both sensemaking (dur ing debriefs) and analysis after the fact. We recom mend that designers consider ways to open up their activity structures to invite diverse framing from students’ perspectives and plan spaces for them to share interpretations and leverage them as sense-making resources.
