# Connected Spaces: Opportunities for Identity Development in Distributed Maker Communities

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Connected Spaces (C/S) is a collection of technologies that serve as an ecosystem for connecting students traditionally underrepresented in STEM+C to peers and mentors for collective maker identity development. To understand the efficacy of C/S, this study follows Isaiah as he uses C/S tools to seek help with his summer maker camp project. Using an adaptation of Nasir & Cooks (2009) identity resources framework, we document Isaiah’s choices of which C/S technologies to use, and when. This analysis highlights how the affordances of each C/S technology enabled Isaiah to find support from remote mentors in an adaptive manner and how this process contributed to the development of his computational identity, and recognition as a valuable member of a distributed network of makerspaces.

## Introduction

Inequitable conditions in many STEM+C (Science, Tec hnology, Engineering, Math, and Computing) learning environments continue to limit access for minoritized students (Shah et al., 2020). Makerspaces may democratize computing education (Peppler et al., 2016)  but due to typically informal and unstructured participation, require innovative approaches to  develop communities of practice (Wenger, 1998). Further, facilitating computational identity development in a personally relevant manne r can be beneficial for students  recognizing their agency, communities, and histories within the computing spa ce. Scholars suggest that a n individual’s practice-linked identity (Nasir & Hand, 2008) or affinity identity (Gee, 2000) is associated with their shared participation within a socia l practice like learning (Trujillo & Tanner, 2014). However, many students la ck access to mentors or community centers that can offer the frequent and k nowledgeable support (Thomas et al., 2018) that is vital for novices to cope with the challenges they encounter while making (Hall et al., 2023). In response, Connected Spaces (C/S) was developed as an ecosystem of collaborative tech nologies that aim to support remote collaboration among geographicall y separated makers by provi ding access to material, relational, and ideational resources to foster a se nse of community that helps students develop identi ties compatible with STEM+C fields (Nasir & Cooks, 2009; Wenger, 1998). This study aims to bridge theory building and practical impact by answering the following  questions: 1) How are the suite of tools, materials, and social arrangements that make up the C/S ecosystem, used by learners to connect to a local and geographically distributed community? 2) How do these interactions support the formation and enactment of their computational identities?

## Methods

This study examines the 2023 iteration of the Connected Spaces tool suite, designed to foster collaboration and mentorship among middle school students and remote experts during a two-week maker camp  at a large Midwestern university. Learners were supported by  community mentors and researchers. Additional facilitators in New York, Costa Rica, and Italy  offered remote guidance via C/S tools. In this paper, we follow Isaiah ’s (12, identifying as Black/African American) making journey to understand his moves and choices to collaborate within the C/S ecosystem. Given the constraints of the paper, we focus on key instances when Isaiah’s identity within the space seemed to be impacted as identified by the first author, who was also an instructor in the space. The C/S ecosystem tools include: T he Dashboard, an ambient display showcasing students’ skills and interests, to foster community and recognition; The Persistent Live Video Channel  (PLVC), a continuous video link (Zoom) between camp participants and remote facilitators; The REACH  Projector, an interactive projector system that enabled tangible collaborative interactions at a distance  (Smith & Tissenbaum, 2024); The Work-inProgress Carousel (WIP) Display, where students share project photo/video updates, to build connections with their peers.

## Data collection and analysis

This study analyzes video  and audio recordings (Room, REACH, PLVC), dashboard/WIP photos, and physical artifact photos. We adapted Nasir & Cooks ’ (2009) framework of identity resources and expanded it to reflect features of the C/S ecosystem to examine the formation and enactment of participants' comput ational identities. Material resources, defined by Nasir & Cook s as “the way in which the physical environment, its organ ization, and the artifacts in it support student's sense of connection to the practice,” encompassed the students using  or interacting with  the Dashboard, PLVC, REACH, WIP Carousel, Maker Materials or Worksheets. Relational resources, “positive relationships with others in the context t hat can increase connection to the practice ” (Nasir & Cooks) captured the nature of interpersonal interaction (local/remote) within the community: solo, individual, group, and community, as well as the individuals' recognition in that space  (Bawankule & Tissenbaum, 2023). Lastly, Ideational resources, “the ideas about oneself and one's relationship to and pl ace in the practice and the world, and ideas about what is valued and good ” were adapted using simplified identity constructs d erived from meta-analysis to identify factors that influence a stude nt’s computational identity  (Bawankule & Tissenbaum). These included positive and negative displays of agency within space, interest in computing, usefuln ess in computing, confidence in computing, and competence in computing. The resulting codes (Y axis in Figure 1) were used to identify instances where Is aiah was observed utilizing an identity resource, hi ghlighting how the design of the C/S tools, environment, and curriculum supported these resources. The first and second authors  analyzed video (3hr55min) achieving interrater reliability (κ = 0.81), with further disagreements resolved via discussion. CORD TRA diagrams provided a chronological visualization, for the team to identify themes and significant incidents in Isaiah’s identity development (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2009).

## Findings

Early days: Establishing connections On Day One students got familiarized with the camp, each other, the facilit ators (in-person and remote ly), the electronics, and the C/S tools. Isaiah opted to sit alone, and w ith encouragement, he added “ I’m black” to his Dashboard profile before returning to a web game. On Day Three, when prompted he edited his Dashboard, adding graphics and affinities (most not align ing with his work)  and shared  “my favorite basketball player is James Harden, and my favorite game to play is Call of Dut y.” On Day Three, he encountered issues connecting LED strips and was advised to seek  help via the PLVC. With the remote mentor, he  had moments of positive Recognition (when a mentor recognized his Dashboard profile), n egative Interest (when he was unable to screenshare), positive Agency (working with a local instructor to set up screen share), positive Competence (screen sharing his code), and a shift from negative to positive Confidence with successful debugging on PLVC. Excited by his progress, he shared his accomplishment on the WIP display with the broader maker network. The rest of the day was focused on developing ideas for projects th at connected to the students' lives in meaningful w ays. ## Discussion

What begins to emerge is the notion that the technology presented in C/S is not a key identity resource itself, but rather extends the ability to connect to relational, ideational, and material resources that may otherwise be unavailable. Even in a well-resourced study setting, Isaiah frequently required more atte ntion than the local facilitators could provide. In some cases, his need for relational resources were met through interactions with local facilitators as in the case of the mind map activit y on Day Four (Figure 1d) which also provided access to both material and ideational resources through a process of exploring the usefulness of computing to address personally relevant, real-world problems. At other times, Isaiah’s need for r elational resources were met through focused idea sharing with remote facilitators as on Day Four (Figure 1p, 1u), debugging sessions with a group of remote facilitators using the PLVC on Day Three, and a combination of the REACH and PLVC on Day Six (Figure 2). Exercising agency in an extended network of makers Isaiah’s story demonstrates Wenger’s (1998) idea that identity can be thought of as “learning as becoming ” and community can be thought of as “learning as belongi ng.” To build his identity, he alsoneeded to establish his sense of agency in the extended C/S space. The design and study of the C/S ecosystem provides insights into ways that technology-enhanced interactions can support participants in f eeling more comfortable exercising their agency in informal maker environments and develop their practice-linked identities within the community. The observations from Day Four illustrate some of the challenges in establishing agency in informal settings and how Isaiah exercises his agency in the  camp. When the offensive image requires removal of the laptops at midday, Isaiah’s agency and interest in the work are negatively affected (Figure 1g, 1i). His discomfort with the situation is made apparent when he stops h is work and leaves the room. The one-on-one attention from the facilitator Isaiah then receives, is key in understanding his frustration with the situation, his anger at the posted image, and trying to bring him back to his work and ensuring that he feels safe in the space. Once re-engaged with the relational and material work of recording his ideas in the worksheet, another facilitator lightens the mood and offers recognition for Isaiah’s work. Despite typically working alone,  Isaiah begins to roam the room quietly listening to the groups. beginning to engage with the groups more, and we begin to see his confidence and positive agency restored (Figure 1j). When he sits down to complete his design worksheet, we see all the identity resources present and a sustained period of interest, and confidence leading into his comfort and excitement in showing his design to the remote facilitators (Figure 1p, 1u). His ability to connect with someone outside the makerspace using the C/S tools enabled him to exercise his agency and find the confidence to create. The C/S framework, and the recognition and support from remote facilitators, helped him navigate this friction with the local community and to reestablish a sense of agency and confidence, while also encouraging him to engage with the overall community. Adaptive help seeking While iterations of the C/S tools have provided increasing levels of remote support (Smith & Tissenbaum, 2024), there are interesting lessons in participants tool  adaptations to meet their immediate needs. For example, on Day Four Isaiah uses REACH to show his design worksheet and explain his ideas to the remote facilitators with high degrees of confidence, interest, and agency (Figure 1o, 1p, & 1r). Isaiah then wants to show the facilitators a cardboard box he is considering for the project. Wh en it is too large to share with the REACH system, he adapts to the situation, stepping back to show the collaborators the box via the PLVC (Figure 1o, 1u). The remote sessions tended to have more focused recognition for his work (Figure 1p) and allowed for sustained periods of tailored support allow for opportunities for competence, which we believe may have provided the additional motivation to maintain these remote facilitators connections. In Isaiah’s exit interview, he said that he enjoyed using REACH and PLVC as he was able to talk to people from NY and that they were able to help him with his project. He also said that these remote members felt like a part of the community. This notion of an extended community  is evidenced in the collaborative moves he intentional ly makes to maintain these connections despite tech nical challenges.

Isaiah also encountered challenges in sharing his screen to collaborate on code. The C/S tools provided enough flexibility to adapt to Isaiah's emergent screen sharing needs and provided opportunities for him to display competence with the tools when engaging with the remote facili tators. When a need for screen sharing is first encountered on Day Three, rather than accept help from the local facilitator, Isaiah chooses instead to continue to seek help from the remote facilitators a nd requests help in setting up screen sharing. On D ay Six this adaptive help seeking appears again when Isaiah is seeking hardware help via REACH but realizes he needs coding help and transitions from REACH to PLVC and sets up screen sharing by himself based on his Day Three experience. Although Isaiah did work with groups, he mostly worked alone or in one-on-one interactions. However, competence, confidence, and agency seemed to increase when he connected with others. As days progress, we see him utilize the C/S technologies with increasing comfort to satisfy thi s need for collaboration. When the racial Chromebook event negatively impacted his identity development on Day Four, he made a connection with remote facilitators who were removed from the negative event, bringing his confidence and sense of agency back up.

## Conclusion

Research highlights the importance of practice-linked identity development in learning environments (Gee, 2000; Nasir & Cooks, 2009; Pinkard et al., 2017). Althoug h makerspaces aim to democratize STEM+C learning, t hey require unique approaches to foster com munities of practice. The C/S ecosystem addresses this by creating local and distributed environments that support culturall y relevant computational making, build ing practice-linked identities and establishing a broader community of practice (Wenger, 1998). While our findings showed that the C/S tools supported the development of Isaiah’s computa tional identity, it is essential to note that they did so across sustained time periods, utilizing a combinat ion of material, relational and ideational resources. This multi-dimensional approach enabled him to engage in settings that were personally meaningful using material resources and C/S tools suited to his  needs. The C/S ecosystem provided Isaiah access to relational resources that fit his needs: the Dashboard allowed him to represe nt himself publicly and reflect on changes in his computational identity; REACH and PLVC allowed him to display his agency and competence while collaborating with remote mentors in a distributed community; and the WIP let him display and view the community’s work and have his own work  recognized. These multiple entry  points enabled his connections with members of the extended community.

For students from underrepresented and underserved communities in STEM+C, identity and affect play a significant role in how they see themselves in th ese fields (Trujillo & Tanner, 2014). Ensuring that  STEM+C communities of practice, and their associated technologies, are created with these students’ identities in mind can allow for a more democratic, culturally-relevant, and inclusive learning experience for all community members.
