# Reflections on Flexible and Constrained Agency as Affordances for Embodied Learning Design

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As one pathway toward generating more transparent and systematic descriptions of embodied learning design decisions, we integrate two design frameworks – the Learning in Embodied Activity Framework (LEAF) and another highlighting Viewpoint, Embodiment, and Roles – and foreground this synthesis in reflections on our own past design efforts. Through this reflection, we provide a generative account of how embodied designs constrain agency to give students more or less latitude in their movements as pathways to learning.

## Introduction

Designers of embodied learning technologies make nu merous choices when creating a technological build, embedded learning activities,  and accompanying pedagogy. Committing to  an “embodied” technology only nominally delimits the state space of design decisions. Grappling with this open-endedness, two frameworks – the Learning in Embodied Activity Framework (LEAF; Danish et al., 2020) and another highlighting Viewpoint, Embodiment, and Roles (Lindgren & DeLiema, 2022) – outline distinct, and in our view, compatible constructs and design pathways. In this poster, we honor a cal l from both papers to have more transparent and sys tematic conversations about design decisions in embodied le arning, and along the way, we integrate the framewo rks and raise an under-examined question for the field about the agency of students in embodied learning spaces. In order to get early feedback from colleagues and avoid con straining our reflection with costly resources, we move the conversation forward in this poster proposal through personal reflections on our past embodied design efforts.

## Background

Embodied learning designs often take place within i mmensely complex collaborative learning environment s, calling for a sensitivity foregrounded in the LEAF framework for researchers to attend to participants ’ rules, divisions of labor, communities, objectives, and to ols (Danish et al, 2020). At the same time, designe rs of embodied learning technologies have to make concret e decisions about the visual viewpoint that anchors  what learners see (e.g., observer, character, or hybrid), how the technology will sense the body’s movement (through a physicalized interface, embodied manipulation, situational embodiment, or representational embodiment), and the distribution of roles adopted by participants in th e activity (Lindgren & DeLiema, 2022). Building on Keifert et al.’s (2017) study of students’ agency in a play-based technological setting, which documented intertwined forms of agency (e.g., directors, supporters, sources of authority), our team reflected on ongoing research into power in social interaction (see Philip & Gupta’s 2020 review) and how flexibility and limitations within students’ agency in embodied design create openings for learning.

## Method

We come to this work having each worked in embodied  learning design for over a decade and recognizing the speed and impact of design decisions. We felt compelled to pause and reflect on consequential past design choices from the perspective of these design frameworks. The past designs that we consider inclu de developing multiple complementary embodied models of ecosystems where 5th grade students alternated between acting as characters (e.g., a fish in a pond) and as representations of energy (Danish); developing a feedback system for an embodied simulation of planetary astronomy (Lindgren); and collaborating to build an augmented reality simulation for 1st and 2nd graders pretend-playing as particles  (DeLiema). Across several months, we met to discuss the two frameworks, envisioned possible embodied learning d esigns for a range of topics, reflected on our past  designs, and started to formulate an integrated framework that raised in our mind a common tension in our past designs.

## Findings

An integrated framework recognizes that at each nod e of the LEAF activity system, a designer makes consequential choices regarding visual viewpoint, e mbodiment, and roles. For example, the object or purpose of the activity may vary depending on whether participants emphasize the visual viewpoint highlighting how to win (e.g., by moving an avatar to the finish), the embo died affordances highlighting how it feels as a cha racter (e.g., zip around like a bee), or the roles in the communi ty (e.g., strengthening a bond with a friend in the  room). By intersecting these frameworks in this way, we recog nized their complementarity and that students’ agen cy could ebb and flow depending on and relative to the emphasis on viewpoint, embodiment, and roles at each facet of the activity and over the course of learning/social interaction. We reflect on three past examples below. Exploring particle configurations to save a robot DeLiema reflected on how 1st and 2nd graders approached play in a game-based, embodied simulation. The game, anchored from the observer viewpoint of a robot escaping an island, shaped the object of the activity so much that students placed considerable pressure on each other  to collectively enact the rules of particle moveme nt – their own full body movements in this case – that would trigger successful robot actions. Latitu de to move any which way as a particle became immensely constrained by a visual observer viewpoint that foregrounded the robot’s journey, and by focusing students’ inquiries on agency for the robot, the students converged on rules that effectively deployed the robot’s actions and learned about distinct states of matter. Playing as an asteroid moving through a field of astronomical forces Lindgren reflected on a whole-body physics simulation that allowed students to ro le-play as asteroids (seeing from their character viewpoint) and move through a room-size space to predict the asteroid’s trajectory as it encountered various forces (e.g., gravitational pull of a nearby planet). Because the goal of the simulation was for students to develop an intuitive sense of how objec ts move in  space, a frequent design suggestion we received was to use a rope to apply forces and increase the embodied salience of the simulation. The design team ultimately resisted these suggestions, partially because of pragmatic concerns, but also because it was felt that this constraint on student agency would overpower the natural representational dissonance created when students freely predicted the location of the asteroid with their body compared to the actual position within the physics simulation. Playing as beavers in a wetland ecosystem Danish reflected on how 5th grade r took on different roles in a Mixed Reality implementation where learners explored a wetland ecosystem. Learners embodied the roles of beavers (seeing from its character viewpoint) who could navigate the environment and collect material s to build a dam, which would deepen the water or lead to more fish (seen from an observer viewpoint). With s uch open-endedness in this role, students had flexibility and latitude to move in ways they deemed would coordinate their collective survi val. However, this left mechanisms for how the dam impacts the ecosystem unclear. Later in the activity sequence, a second model limited students’ agency by having them articulate one at a time how they would act each day, and explicitly highlighted the interaction between system elements. This led students to begin recognizing ne w underlying environmental mechanisms which then informed discussion when they returned to the original, less constrained model.

## Conclusion

Combining these two frameworks for exploring embodied learning helped to lay out many of the potential design choices we each faced, highlighting their impact. We are also learning to see agency as a potential constrained by designers’ choices about the viewpoints, forms of embodiment, and roles within particular systems of objectives, rules, divisions and labor, and tools. The confinin g or broadening of latitude for forms of agency tha t clash even temporarily with the values and expectations of oth er unaccented parts of a design space can be generative to inquiry, introducing opportunities both to observe new, even counterfactual parts of a simulation and to discuss with students the epistemics of what it means to represent and analyze STEM systems.
