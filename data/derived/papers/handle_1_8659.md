# Development and Formative Evaluation of a Learning Game for Overcoming Typical Difficulties in Historical Thinking

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research has been conducted on teaching materials for historical thinking using historical sources. However, historical thinking encounters challenges in the use of historical sources, and short-term interventions have limited effectiveness. This study developed a learning game by combining the ideas of a studying-errors guideline and game-based learning to overcome typical difficulties in historical thinking using historical sources. The formative evaluation showed that the learning game affected challenging historical thinking using historical sources.

## Introduction

Historical thinking is an important part of educati on in many countries. Although there are multiple d efinitions of historical thinking (Wineburg, 1991; Van Boxtel & Vandrie, 2018), historical thinking using histor ical sources (UHS) is emphasized in all. History is unique in how it manipulates and interprets historical sources for reasoning about the past. Here, historical thinking  UHS refers to understanding or interpreting the pa st with historical sources.

Several methods for fostering historical thinking U HS have been developed, but short-term interventions have limited effects (Fox & Maggioni,  2018). There are two reasons for this. First, Wine burg’s

(1991) novice-expert research shows that beginner students have difficulty with sourcing (the act of looking first

to the source of the document before reading the bo dy of the text), corroboration (comparing documents  with one another), and contextualization (situating a do cument in a concrete temporal and spatial context) when interpreting the past using historical sources. Gen erous and step-by-step support is needed to overcom e these typical difficulties. Second, controlling the compl ex historical discussions necessary for developing historical thinking is difficult. It is essential to have an i deal historical discussion that promotes historical  thinking UHS and includes individual feedback.

This study develops a learning game that provides a n ideal and individualized learning experience to overcome typical difficulties in historical thinking UHS and supports formative evaluation. Development This study focused on two teaching methods to address the challenges in fostering historical thinking UHS. The first is the studying-errors guideline, a method th at involves a mixture of correct and incorrect exam ples—both of which students learn from. This approach is more effective than learning from correct examples alone (Renkl, 2018). The second approach is game-based learning, which can provide step-by-step, individualized lear ning experiences and individualized feedback that is adapted to individual players. A learning game called Rekiseeker (Figure 1) was developed by combining these methods  to foster historical thinking UHS. This game’s objective is t o present a valid historical account using historic al sources. The concrete goal of the game is to locate errors i n the historical account of the opposing characters  who interpret the past without sourcing, corroboration,  or contextualization and to demonstrate valid hist orical accounts. Wh en a player makes an incorrect choice, scaffolding and feedback are provided by an allied character. These features then enable an ideal hist orical discussion to overcome typical difficulties in learning historical thinking UHS among all players. One play  of the learning game takes about 15 minutes. The g ame’s theme centers on labor issues during the Industrial  Revolution, which is covered early in the Japanese  history curriculum in high school. The historical thinking UHS learned in this game is intended to support lea rning in later units (e.g., the Lincoln unit).

Formative Evaluation A formative assessment was developed to measure whether the historical thinking UHS acquired in the learning game, especially sourcing, contextualization, and corroboration, would transfer to other history unit tasks. Data were collected in an 80-minute session with six Jap anese high school students (three pairs) after clas ses on October 26, 2021. Each student read historical sour ces related to the transfer task, and each pair dis cussed during the transfer task. Then, each student played  the learning game, and each pair discussed during the same transfer task, and each student responded to the post-questionnaire. In the transfer task, the question “What kind ICLS2022 Proceedings 1952 © ISLS of person was Lincoln?” was approached using three historical sources that provided contradictory vers ions of Lincoln’s image. Historical thinking UHS is needed to resolve this. The post-questionnaire determined whether the learning game impacted students’ post-discussio ns in the transfer task and, if so, what kind of aw areness it promoted in free-text format.

After collecting data, we investigated whether stud ent statements related to sourcing, corroboration, and contextualization increased in the post-discuss ions in the transfer task relative to the pre-discu ssions and whether sourcing, corroboration, and contextualizat ion were mentioned in the post-questionnaire. The e ffects were discussed along with the results.

## Results and Discussion

While sourcing was not performed by all pairs in th e pre-discussion task, references to it were found in two of three pairs’ post-discussions. Although no student explicitly mentioned it as an effect of the learnin g game, analysis of the conversations in the transfer tasks  suggested that the learning game affected the stud ents. With regard to corroboration, all pairs compared the his torical sources with one another in the pre-discuss ion task, and there were no changes in the post-discussion ta sk. However, considering that one student in Pair 1  and one in Pair 2 mentioned the impact of contextualization in the post-questionnaire, both pairs seem to have compared historical sources with one another in the post-dis cussion task. Therefore, this learning game supplem ented the historical thinking UHS that had been lacking in each pair. All pairs situated the historical sources in a concrete temporal and spatial context during the pre-discussion task, and two of three pairs were aware of and referred tonew historical contexts in the post-discussion task. In the post-questionnaire, four people (one each in Pairs 1 and 2 and two in Pair 3) wrote about the impact of contextualization. This indicates that this learnin g game helped develop greater awareness of the historical context in the students. These results show that the learning game successfu lly combines the ideas of a studying-errors guideline and game-based learning to overcome typic al difficulties in historical thinking UHS. Future issues include conducting more elaborate experiments and v erifying whether such a learning game format is als o effective in developing thinking skills in other subjects.
