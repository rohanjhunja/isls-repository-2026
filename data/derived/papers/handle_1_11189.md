# Epistemic Trajectories: Classroom Communities’ Development of Socio-epistemic Disciplinary Norms

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Science is a social practice involving epistemic engagement with scientific norms, which are established by the scientific through epistemic discourse. Similarly, classroom communities can engage in discourse towards developing socio-epistemic disciplinary (SED) norms: shared norms around what constitute appropriate contributions to science classroom discussions. Using the AIR model of Epistemic Cognition and the Apt-AIR framework, this research presents an analysis of the discourse in a middle school science inquiry intervention to explore how SED norms were negotiated by class communities—i.e., the epistemic trajectories of the norms. The findings highlight two different epistemic trajectories: in one, an SED norm was used and renegotiated throughout the intervention. In the other, the class community was faced with its limited utility and phased it out. This paper furthers our understanding of how science class communities develop norms that inform and are informed by their engagement with authentic scientific practices.

## Introduction

It is now common knowledge that s cience is a social practice  involving epistemic engagement with norms regarding what questions to pursue, how to evaluate knowledge products, and how to develop these products. The AIR model of epistemic cognition (Chinn et al., 201 1, 2014)  specifies three components of this epistemic engagement—epistemic aims, epistemic ideals, and reliable epis temic processes —around which scientific communities build shared norms. Epistemic aims encompass the goals that the science community establishes as meaningful, such as coming to greater understanding  of phenomena, or determining which of several theo ries is most accurate. Epistemic ideals are the criteria th at the community  uses to evaluate epistemic products like models, arguments, or evidence such as that models are good if they are parsimonious or fit with the b ody of evidence. Reliable epistemic processes are the meth ods that are accepted by the community to achieve t heir epistemic aims, such as using appropriate statistic al tests or systematically reviewing new evidence to determine whether and how it supports or contradicts existing models. Norms associated with epistemic aims, ideals, and reliabl e processes are established and refined by the community (Longino, 2002), and scientists use them as they co nstruct, evaluate, and revise epistemic products, such as models or arguments. This gradual change ha ppens through epistemic discourse—the varied discussions that epistemic communities, like science or classro om communities, have as they develop and refine the ir engagement with, and understanding of, their practices and norms (Longino, 2002; vandijk, 2014). Broadly, epistemic discourse is the process that the scienti fic community uses to establish norms that go beyon d the ideas and beliefs of individual scientists, and that are explicitly or implicitly taken-as-shared by the community. Engaging in epistemic discourse is valuable for  students in science classrooms  as well. Students can engage in authentic knowledge building through deve loping—constructing, using, and holding themselves and each other accountable to —epistemic norms (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014). Recent policy documents  (e.g., NGSS Lead States, 2013)  echo this call, and advocate that students, like scientists, should eng age in epistemic discourse with and about scientific norms as they learn science. A number of science interventions include opportuni ties for students to develop and discuss scientific norms, such as the use of criteria for evaluating epistemic products (Cheng & Brown, 2015; Rinehart et al., 2016). Other interventions also support students’ engagement with a variety of scientific norms around what methods or processes are reliable for generating science knowledge, such how to const ruct reliable experiments, and how to use evidence to evaluate and revise models (Barth-Cohen et al., 2021; Manz & Renga, 2017). Further, several studies have found that elementary and middle schoo l students are able to identify and use scientific norms and that, with support, students develop greater facili ty reasoning with and about scientific norms over t ime (AvShalom et al., 2018; Manz & Renga, 2017). However, students do not always develop only canonical scientific norms, and do not necessa rily prioritize or discuss norms in the same way as scie ntists (Hogan & Maglienti, 2001; Pierson et al., 2022). For example, Hogan and Maglienti (2001) found that when looking at scientific arguments, scientists prioritized being careful not to presume conclusions without sufficie nt evidence, whereas students prioritized the impor tance of clearly stating likely causal connections, even if the body of evidence was not robust.  The epistemic ideals that the scientists and students raised are both scienti fic norms. However, in science, the ideal that that  arguments should be supported by a sufficient body of evidence often takes precedence over other criteria (Longino, 1990). Further, when a scientific norm is raised during classroom di scourse, the class community may  or may not have a shared understanding of what that norm means, or whether and when it is appropriate to use it. Pierson et al. (2022) highlight examples of students in the same class al l valuing the norm of connecting models to the “real world,” but the same norm meant different things. Some students focus ed on the importance of a realistic appearance, whereas others focused on the importanc e of a realistic mechanism.  To engage with norms in their science practice, students need to have opportunities to authentically negotiate the meanings and appropriate usage of those norms, and their relevance to their science practice—just as scientists do. Drawing from Yackel and Cobb’s (1996) idea of sociomathematical norms and Ryu and Sandova l’s

(2012) similar epistemic norms, I propose the construct of socio-epistemic disciplinary norms (SED norms) as a

tool to understand the norms that are negotiated in  disciplinary classroom practice.  SED norms are epistemic in that they are about knowledge and how to engage wit h knowledge. They are disciplinary in that they are related to the discipline. In science, these can include th at we should aim to create accurate models of pheno mena, that models should be supported by the best available evidence, or that certain methodologies or instruments are more reliable than others. In mathematics they may inclu de wha t explanations count as “different” or “acceptable” (Yackel & Cobb, 1996). Finally, the term socio-in SED norms signifies that they are developed socially, by the classroom community—they are not handed to the students by the teacher or curriculum, but rather developed and revised over time, and come to be taken-as-shared by the class community. The social element of SED norms entails a process o f becoming—SED norms are not simply handed to the students by the teacher or curriculum, but rath er are dynamically developed and revised over time, and come to be taken-as-shared by the class community. I refer to the paths of change that SED norms go th rough in becoming taken-as-shared as their epistemic trajectories. The epistemic trajectories in this paper highlight how epistemic aims, ideals, and reliable processes are discussed, shaped, and come to be taken-as-shared as socio-epistemic disciplinary norms. For example, in a pivotal discussion or overall several months, a class might discuss or change the definition, scope, or relative importance of an ideal. The process of negotiating and abiding by SED norms in the class may influence the class’s episte mic discourse and broader engagement with the disci pline (Yackel & Cobb, 1996). To support class communities  in developing norms that support their science pra ctice, we need to understand the mechanism of change that norms go through as the class negotiates their mean ing. Towards that end, this paper explores: What might epistemic trajectories of SED norms look  like: what kinds of epistemic trajectories are seen in two middle schoo l science classes?  and Which AIR components are taken up and become SED norms in classroom discourse, and how are they negotiated?

## Theoretical framework

While researching  classroom social norms, Yackel and colleagues noticed discussions in which a classroom community negotiated norms that were specific to mathematics  practice, which they deemed sociomath ematical norms (diSessa & Cobb, 2004). Classroom social norms might include that students should explain their thinking to the class or that they should listen their peers —norms that might apply to many different subjects o r contexts. Sociomathematical norms, on the other hand, are unique to mathematica l practice, such as what counts as mathematically different, or what are acceptable ways of explaining mathematical solutions in math class (Yackel & Cobb, 1996). Extending this construct, socio-epistemic disciplinary  (SED) norms are the taken-as-shared understandings of what are appropriate contributions to classroom discussions in a discipline, such as science. In the intervention explored in this study, the res earch team and the teachers tried to facilitate the discussion of norms that have to do with ideals, re liable processes, and—to a lesser extent —aims, as construed by the AIR model. Thinking through these three comp onents, a) epistemic Aims are the goals that someone has with respect to epistemic matters. Consider an SED norm, “explaining.” As an aim, a student might aim to explain a phenomenon. b) epistemic Ideals are the criteria used to determine whether epistemic aims have been met. The same students could understand the same SED norm as an ideal and say that that a model is b ad because it’s not explained well. c) Reliable epistemic processes are the processes  used to achieve epistemic aims. As a reliable process, students might say that they make a good representation of a phenomenon by explaining all their ideas. When epistemic aims, ideals, or reliable processes are ra ised in science classroom discourse —through the curriculum or brought up by the teacher or students—there may be considerable disagreement among the class community about the definition of these norms, their relationship to one another, or their relevance to the practice of the discipline. Over time, as the class community discusses one or a family of related aims, ideals, or reliabl e processes (like “explaining” above) they shape the definitions of the norms and their appropriate usage, and may come to take them as part of the taken-as-shared understanding of the classroom science practice. This process of building a taken-as-shared understanding is the epistemic trajectory that aims, ideals, and reliable processes take in classroom discourse as they become SED norms —or, in some cases, do not become SED norms, through  the classroom community not ultimately arriving at shared understandings. SED norms can have many different aspects that are part of the taken-as-shared understanding of the class community. For example, over several months, the class community in this study negotiated the importance of details to modeling and argumentation practice. As these questions were raised by the curriculum, the teacher, or the students, the community discussed what const itutes good details, which details are relevant to artists but not to scientists, and how to choose appropriate de tails. They also considered which was more importan t, good detail or more detail, and how important are those compared to fitting with evidence or answering the question. To identify the aspects of SED norms that are discussed by the class community I will be using the AptAIR framework (Barzilai & Chinn, 2018). This framework outlines f ive aspects of competent engagement with epistemic matters that specify the three components of the AIR model of epistemic cognition discussed earlier (Chinn et al., 2011, 2014). In the context of this research, these five aspects highlight additional elements of what can be discussed and come to be taken-as-shared about SED norms. The five aspects are:

• Cognitive engagement in epistemic performance. In addition to discussing the meaning of SED norm s,

members of the class community alsoneed to use the m in similar ways. Evidence of a shared cognitive understanding of an SED norm could involve students  giving examples of where the SED norm is instantiated in a model, or the teacher modeling how to use an SED norm.

• Epistemic metacognitive knowledge and skills for understanding and regulating epistemic performance.

For SED norms to be meaningful, the class community  needs to have a shared understanding of what they are. An example of the metacognitive aspect co uld include the class community discussing and refining the definition of an SED norm.

• Adapting epistemic performance. Different situations may call for different uses,  definitions, and

prioritizations of SED norms. The class community could discuss different situations where an SED norm is applicable or discuss how to adapt an SED norm to different situations.

• Caring about and enjoying epistemic performance. SED norms can guide students’ engagement with

science and other disciplines, but only if students  agree that they are valuable. To support this aspect, a teacher could guide the class to consider when and why it might be important to use an SED norm.

• Participating in epistemic performance with others. The development of scientific knowledge and norms

is achieved in varied social configurations and settings. To develop this aspect, the class community could discuss which SED norms are used by different epistemic communities, and how they use them. Barzilai and Chinn (2018) argued that supporting students to engage with all five aspects will promote their epistemic growth. With that in mind, in this paper the Apt-AIR framework can provide a lens to explore the emergence and renegotiation of SED norms by classro om communities  towards gaining insight about the development of students’ epistemic thinking.

## Research context and participants

The data in this study come from the Promoting Reas oning and Conceptual Change in Science (PRACCIS) project. Participating teachers implemented a life-science, model-based inquiry curriculum in their middle school classrooms over several months, in public schools i n Northeastern USA. The curricula were collaboratively developed by the project team and teachers, and inc luded topics such as cell membranes, mitosis, and e volution. The curriculum included several scaffolds to support students’ modeling practice, including a class list of criteria for good models that each class collaboratively dev eloped. Their lists were publicly displayed in the classroom and referred to during discussions, and the class revised their criteria lists several times during the intervention. In one typical unit which was about cell membranes,  the classes were exploring the guiding question, “How do lead particles get into our cells?” The students developed initial models having only seen minimal evidence. Throughout the unit, students evaluated and revised their class models based on new evidence that they received and collected. They also evaluated the evidence, and wrote and evaluated arguments (e.g., “I think model A is better because…”). Students worked together as a class, as well as in small groups, pairs, and individually. I will be focusing on the classroom discourse in two of Ms. Pisano’s (all names are pseudonyms) seventh grade classrooms, classes A and B (students were 12-13 years old; ~25 students per class). Ms. Pisano taught at a suburban school in the United States and maintained a high fidelity to the PRACCIS curriculum. Data sources: Classroom discourse Evaluation of epistemic products and norms is a key part of epistemic discourse in science (Longino, 2002). It is through epistemic discourse around evaluation that epistemic products are ratified into knowledge and the aims, ideals, and processes of individual scientists become gro up norms. Facility with evaluation practices is als o important “outside of the laboratory” —laypeople need to be able to evaluate the informati on they encounter as they engage with science in their daily lives (McIntyre, 2018). Given its importance in scientific and lay practice, in this study I focus on class discussions pertaining to evaluation—I will refer to these as the focal tasks. The focal tasks had several different evaluation-related aims. These included evaluating, ranking, or comparing the quality of models, pre-written arguments, or evidence, as well as interpreting evidence towards evaluating models.  For example, several focal tasks involved the class  discussing how they ranked several competing arguments. For this task, students needed  to evaluate the relative quality of various argume nts and articulate their reasoning. In another focal task, the class evaluated and discussed their reasoning about whether a piece of evidence was of high quality. I also included class discussi ons or short teacher lectures that were in the same lesson as an evaluation-related class discussion and were aimed at explaini ng why or how the class should engage in the evaluation task or in scientific inquiry more broadly.  For example, in one task the teacher guided the class to discuss how they can evaluate whether a model is good (in theory—not focusing on a specific model). In another, the teacher spent several minutes explaining to students which scientific practices they had been using, and which they would be practicing in rest of the y ear. In total, I identified 22 focal tasks from Cla ss A and 17 focal tasks from Class B. The focal tasks ranged from 2-26 minutes (average: 9 minutes). Identifying and analyzing epistemic trajectories I operationalize an epistemic trajectory as engagement with a potent ial SED norm that took place over at least five focal tasks throughout the six-month intervention. The number emerged out of the data—examples that arose in fewer focal tasks often did not exemplify meanin gful change and it was not possible to characterize  the trajectory of that change when it arose in a smaller number of focal tasks. Each epistemic trajectory only included episodes from either Class A or B (not both). From this, I identified several 13 potential epistemic trajectories. To identify and analyze the focal epistemic trajectories I draw from discourse analysis (Gee & Green, 1998), and on Cobb & Whitenack’s (1996) method for conducting longitudinal analyses  of classroom data. I iteratively added process codes and wrote analytic memos characterizing the main changes  in the trajectories of the potential SED norms as a whole and about individual episodes to identify themes (Saldaña, 2016). I ruled out the epistemic trajectories of potential SED norms that were designed as central features of the curriculum. Fit with evidence and the importance and process of writing a good explanation, for example, were both central to the curriculum and were the focus of several lessons. It was thus impossible to determine whether these potential SED norms were actually adopted and refined by the class or simply required by the curriculum.

## Results and discussion

In this paper, I present two epistemic trajectories, one about answering the question and the other about including details. In each epistemic trajectory an SED norm gets neg otiated and refined. However, the two epistemic trajectories highlight several interesting contrast s, including differences in how the norms were rais ed in the classroom discourse, which AIR components and  Apt-AIR aspects were discussed, and different paths and ultimate outcomes they had —as in, different conclusions that the class commu nity came to regarding their appropriateness to their classroom science practice.

Following this discussion, they cognitively engaged with the ideal through identifying extraneous details in the model. As they were considering which detail s they could remove, the class then discussed the appropriateness of different representations: altho ugh an artist might choose to personify a n image of a cell, to a scientist that would be an unnecessary detail, and an inaccurate representation. This engagement with the adaptive and participatory Apt-AIR aspects highlights the disciplinary part of SED norms. Including and attending to details is important in many domains, and this  discussion about what kinds of details are appropri ate or inappropriate for scientific products was part of the process of shifting form a more general norm, which perhaps the students were familiar with from other classes or domains, to a scientific norm. As the students refined and renegotiated the ideal of including details, they began to be faced with i ts limitations. Often, two competing epistemic products would have the same level of detail. Other times, a n epistemic product with less detail might still be b etter for other reasons, such as when it aligned better with the evidence. As the class community developed an SED n orm around including details through progressively renegotiating and agreeing on refined definitions, they also saw its limitations, and began to deprioritize it. By the beginning of February including details had mostly fallen out of explicit use. This does not mean that the class decided it was irrelevant: the students still identified and attended to det ails. However, the students mostly stopped stating that they evaluated an epistemic product as being b etter or worse based on whether it included details (or relevant or necessary details,  or other refinements). Instead, students focused on other SED norms which they seemed to have decided were more u seful for discerning between the competing epistemi c products that they were engaging with. When including details briefly resurfaced later in the year, the teacher connected it to support the discussion of different  aims of modeling more broadly: the importance of s upporting one model vs. contradicting another. The students t hen further refined the SED norm with that issue in mind. Looking at this epistemic trajectory we can see that in negotiating SED norms, one possibility is that students will share an understanding of a norm, and based on that  shared understanding decide that it is not appropr iate or useful for their classroom science practice. This highligh ts the importance of looking closely at what norms or ideas are not present classroom discourse, as some of these may reflect an active choice by the community.

## Conclusion and contribution

The epistemic trajectories highlighted in this paper shed light on some of the very different paths that aims, ideals, and reliable processes can take as classroom communities negotiate and renegotiate their meaning and application to their classroom. In b oth cases, we can see that, with teacher support, t he students considered sophisticated issues about the relationship between aims, ideals,  and reliable processes, in terms of how they inter twined towards an SED norm, and in contrast with different  ones. As the SED norms emerged, students discussed  their scope and applicability to different contexts, with  a particular focus on their applicability to the a ims of their science class practices. In both cases, the negotia tion of socio-epistemic disciplinary was an active and ongoing process that involved expanding the scope and use of SED norms and understanding their limitations. Inquiry in science class often attempts to mirror a uthentic science inquiry (Minner et al., 2010), but the students come in with their own understanding of sc ience and its norms. In an inquiry context, the stu dents are enculturating into an existing epistemic community —the science community —through the guidance of the teacher and the curriculum. At th e same time, through negotiating SED norms  they become their own epistemic community, similar to but distinct from that of sci entists. By exploring epistemic trajectories, this study expands our understanding of how classroom science communities negotiate and develop their science practice.
