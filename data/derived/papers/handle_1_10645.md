# Designing equity policies for specific school subjects: District leaders’ sensemaking between equity and school mathematics

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this qualitative case study of policymaking in one school district, I found that leaders’ sensemaking about equity interacted with meanings about school mathematics, constraining policy solutions for equity in mathematics to access and achievement gaps while also rendering issues of identity peripheral to mathematics. Findings highlight a need for policy designers to attend to and address the learning demands posed by the subject.

## Introduction

Policy has been increasingly important to the learning sciences (e.g., Stein et al., 2016). Taking policymaking as design work (e.g., Lewis et al., 2020), learning scientists have focused on the learning demands of policy (Cobb & Jackson, 2012), since policy enactment requires that individuals  develop new knowledge and practices. I n schools, educational leaders are especially important in policy design, as their sensemaking—the meanings they make (Weick, 1995) —about educational issues shape teachers’ access to policy messages and how they learn about policy (e.g., Coburn, 2005). With equity as a central issue in education policy,  researchers have examined how policymakers make sense of equity-related ideas (e.g., Bertrand et al., 2015; Evans, 2007). This scholarship has mostly ignored the school subject, however, despite research suggesting that policy design is informed by leaders’ meaning-making about subject-matter (Burch & Spillane, 2005; Grossman et al., 2004).  For instance, mathematics is oftentimes considered “cold” and “ultra-rational” (Ernest, 2004, p. 11), which undoubtedly shapes how policymakers make sense of issues like identity and cultural relevance in mathematics education.

In this paper, I report the results of a qualitative case study of one school district’s policymaking for equity. Specifically, this paper sought to answer two research questions: (1) How do school district leaders make sense of equity, especially in relation to school m athematics? (2) How are their sensemakings reflecte d in and enacted through their policy solutions for equity ? My findings reveal that  leaders’ sensemaking about equity interacted with meanings about the school subject t o constrain equity in mathematics to access and achievement (gaps), and render issues of identity as irrelevant to mathematics. Based on this, I discuss implications for the design of equity policies, particularly the learning demands for enacting such policy in specific subjects. Conceptual framings Policy design I take policy design as both the solution to policy problems (policy as problem-solving), as well as the production of cultural meanings attached to policy issues  (policy as sensemaking). Taking policy implementation as a problem of individual learning, Cobb and Jackson (2 012) propose a learning design perspective  that views policies as  designs for supporting individual’s change in practice. By highlighting  the learning demands of changing practice, the learning design perspective supports policymakers in anticipating limitations of policy and informs why policy enactment unfolded the ways it did. For Oakes (1992), supporting (individuals’ learning about) equity requires policy that addresses not just technical aspects of change (e.g., tools, practices, structures) but also normative (deeply held norms) and political (redistribution of power and resources) dimensions. From the sensemaking perspective, policy design is socially constructed as educational leaders interpret policy issues through their existing worldviews (e.g., Donaldson et al., 2021; Evans, 2007). Meaning-making is situated, so the multiple overlapping contexts in which leaders (inter)act provide interpretive resources, including shared understandings, frameworks for action, and organizational practices. In particular, scho lars have argued that the school subject acts as one context for policymaking (Grossman et al,. 2004), providing subject-specific meanings that individuals draw upon in their sensemaking and enactment of polic y. For example, Burch and Spillane (2005) found that district leaders’ sensem aking about mathematics and English as core school subjects were reflected in their prioritization of these subjects in instructional reform efforts. To provide context for how leaders might make sense of the subject in relation to equity, I review relevant literature next. Equity in school mathematics For Gutiérrez (2012), equity in mathematics education encompasses four  dimensions: access, achievement, identity, and power.  Despite decades of efforts focused on access, inequ ities in achievement—in terms of standardized test scores and participation in mathe matics-related college major and career —continue to persist. Though attention to access and achievement has been  the focus of accountability policies  internationally (e.g., Smith, 2014), some scholars have argued for broader notions of equity, namely, issues of identity and power. This includes, for example, providing opportunities for students to “see themselves in the curriculum” (Gutiérrez, 2012, p. 20) and use mathematics to make sense of and address social and political issues important to them.

## Methods

Located in a micropolitan U.S. community, RockCity School District (pseudonym) served almost 18,000 students in 2022. Its student population was fairly diverse: 57% white, 21% Black, 10% multiracial, 7% Hispani c, 5% Asian. 44% of students received free or reduced-price lunch. Like other education systems around the world, RockCity was under accountability policies to meet adequate perf ormance on standardized tests. Students were tested in grades 3-8 for mathematics and English language arts (ELA), and in science for grades 5 and 8. Students were also tested in Algebra I, English II, Biology, and Government, typically during grades 9-12. Occurring January 2022-January 2023,  data collection included 15 interviews with eight district leaders, 100+ observation hours of  policymaking, and collection of artifact s (e.g., district strategic plans; curricular documents; internal newsletters). Interviews elicited participant’s (a) perspectives on perceived challenges and current policies in the district, including those specific to equity and mathematics, (b) their sensemaking about the subject in relation to those challenges and policie s, and (c) their views and perceptions of others’ views about mathematics. I recorded detailed field notes for each observation, and audio-recorded and transcribed interviews. A nalysis began with reviewing and selecting excerpts of interview transcripts, field notes, and artifact s that gave insight into the meanings RockCity leaders constructed about equity, especial ly in mathematics. Then, I employed discourse analysis, which helped me attend to the language and assumptions associated with specific ways of understanding equity and/or mathematics  (Anderson & Holloway, 2018). Informed by a review of the literature on equity, the epistemology of school mathematics, and its intersections, I wrote memos on emerging patterns in sensemakings between equity and mathematics, and relationships b etween these meanings and the organizational and subject-matter contexts that shape sensemaking. Then, I connected those meanings to district’s policy solutions, examining for policies that addressed technical, normative, or political aspects of change.

## RockCity sensemaking context

RockCity had been engaging in equity work since at least 2003 with the creation of the Achievement Gap Task Force, whose goal was to eliminate the achievement gap between white students and students of color (artif act: RockCity website). Of course, this  lofty goal is still being addressed, with continued disparities between RockCity’s white and Asian students, and other stud ents of color. For 2021 (time of case selection), RockCity performed below the state average overall in mathematics and for all students groups, except Asian students. RockCity’s central office was departmentalized, wit h specialized offices  for equity, curriculum and instruction (C&I), and assessment. The C&I departme nt housed subject-specific coordinators, including two (elementary and secondary) for mathematics. Though departmentalization facilitated attention to specific subjects and issues of schooling, several leaders explained that workflow was siloed, with little coordination among units. As reported in Nguyen (forthcoming), RockCity was characterized by shared meanings  about school mathematics. First, mathematics was a core subject, in part, because it was heavily tested on state standardized assessments. Second, mathematics was understood as a sequential discipline, where mastery of prior learning was necessary for future learning. This sequentiality was reflected in ability group ing practices, as well as sequential curriculum coverage and curricular alignment  among teachers. Finally, mathematics was seen as well-defined, with agreement over the content because mathematics is objective. Mathematics’ definition narrowly constructed mathematical activity and competence around accurac y and performance on standardized assessments. Thes e deeply-ingrained meanings about the subject were shared by  leaders, written in formal policy texts, and enact ed in district-wide practices. Together, they acted as a context in which leaders’ sensemaking about equity interacted.

## Findings

In the following, I  first describe how RockCity leaders  made sense of equity in mathematics as access and achievement, which was translated to technical policy solutions. Then, I explain how leaders made sense of normative policy solutions addressing issues of identity as peripheral to and incompatible with mathematics. Sensemaking about and policy solutions for equity All interviewed leaders made sense of  equity problems as disparities among student groups, primarily between white and Asian students, and other students of color. For example, the chief equity officer explained that her role was created  “ to look at those areas in our district that were ty pically overrepresented, behavior-wise, and disciplinary issue-wise and achievement-wise, by our underrepresented populations” (interview). With respect to achievement, RockCity was specifically concerned with student performance on state standardized assessments for mathematics and English Language Arts. Given the district’s historical efforts to address achievement gaps, it is perhaps expected that RockCity leaders made sense of  and defined (in)equity through accountability metrics, especially for the “core” subjects. Moreover, given high-stakes testing and the district’s assessment department, data-based decision making was an important aspect of the RockCity sensemaking context. In mathematics, the coordinators similarly made sen se of equity in terms of achievement. Both leaders saw their efforts support ing inquiry-based instruction and curriculum as equity-minded, as such approaches are more effective for students of color. For the secon dary mathematics coordinator, problem-based curricular materials “is more engaging for students. African A merican students learn better in small groups, with  more discourse” (observation: April 2022 mathematics department meeting). With this sensem aking, equity in mathematics centered around supporting achievement (especially for the district’s underrepresented populations) via access to high-quality mathematics  instruction. Reflective of meanings about equity as access and achievement, RockCity pursued technical policy solutions, such as tutoring, instructional c oaching, and intervention and progress monitoring, with mathematics and ELA receiving the most attention as the core subjects. RockCity also engaged in normative policy solutions to support  staff’s learning about identity. This primarily occurred through professional development, what RockCity leaders called “equity trainings.” Facilitated by the equity department, equity trainings centered on how one’s cultural values may be different from oth ers’, how people’s intersectional identities shape their experiences, and how privilege operate s in schools and society (e.g., observation: December 2022 equity training f or C&I department). In the following, I argue that RockCity leaders made sense of such identity-related equity issues as peripheral to and incompatible with mathematics. Sensemaking between equity trainings, identity, and school mathematics Leaders made sense of equity trainings as subject-neutral, as it was the same training for everyone, regardless of position, grade, or subject. Equity trainings supported teachers’ learning abo ut identity and cultural competence, but, according to the elementary mathematics coordinator, “I don't think there's ever an intentional connection to a subject” (interview). Departmentalization at RockCity served as an important context for leaders’ sensemaking about equity trainings as separate from  issues of (mathematics) instruction. Four RockCity district leaders explicitly shared that equity trainings were not the responsibilities of the C&I department because “it's not specific to instruction, and the coordinators aren't really tasked with it” (director of school improvement, interview). In particular, RockCity leaders made sense of the identity-related issues promoted in equity trainings as peripheral to school mathematics. First, mathematics was one of the most  core subjects, in par t, because it was heavily tested. So, mathematics received the burden of accountability pressure, which constrained  equity policymaking in mathematics to “gap-gazing” (Gutiérrez, 2008) over other, more critical issues. Second, sensemakings about mathematics as a sequential discipline (where mastery of prior learning is necessary for future learning) created pressure for linear content coverage. The secondary mathematics coordinator explained that the mathematics “curriculum is so linear. Something cool in the media might come up that has a statistical bent, but the stat istics unit was one. They're already past it. Teachers are reluctant to d o that lesson now because they got other stuff they got to do” (interview). Here, the need for sequential content coverage was made sense of as incompatible with the development of culturally relevant instruction in mathematics. This similarly played out with meanings about mathematics as well-defined. For example, the elementary mathematics coordinator shared that, in contrast to science instruction in the outdoor classroom or soc ial studies instruction with the city’s African American Heritage Trail, “in math and ELA, we're so focused on-we're working on addition. We're not thinking necessarily about within your pla ce” (interview). Instead of seeing mathematics as cultural,  the mathematics coordinator  made sense of mathematics as abstract and theoretical, where mathematical activity is narrowly constrained to “working on addition” in the classroom, separated from context.

## Discussing implications for the design of equity policies

Taking policy design as problem-solving and sensemaking, this case study illustrates the interaction between educational leaders’ policy solutions for addressin g inequity and the meanings they constructed about equity in school mathematics. Specifically, I found that RockCity  leaders made sense of equity problems in mathematics as access and achievement, which manifested in technical policy solutions for addressing the “achievement gap.” In addition, RockCity was engaged in normative policies addressing issues of identity  (e.g., through equity trainings), which leaders made sense of in subject-neutral ways. However, meanings about school mathematics— mathematics as a tested, core, sequential, and well-defined subject—still interacted with leaders’ sensemaking about equity, rendering such policies as peripheral to and incompatible with mathematics. Here, the organizational context was especially important for sensemaking, as departmentalization c onstructed meanings of equity trainings as subject-neutral, and not the responsibility of C&I and the mathematics coordinators. That sensemaking about equity interacted with meanings a bout the school subject suggests that educational leaders should intentionally examine and plan for these interactio ns when designing equity policies. The learning design perspective suggests  attending to the learning demands required for  implementing equity policies in specific subjects, and how to support t hat learning. The case of RockCity, for example, reveals that learning demands include supporting individuals (e.g., leaders, teachers) to make sense of identity in mathematics, which could include seeing (the learning of) mathematics as a human activity, related to students’ identities and experiences in the places they live. Doing so  likely requires interrogating deeply-held meanings  about mathematics as a sequential and well-defined subject, as RockCity leaders made sense of these epistemol ogical dimensions of mathematics as incompatible with culturally-relevant curriculum and instruction. For districts like RockCity with leaders explicitly charged with supporting education in specific subjects, content coordinators might be well positioned to su pport policy designs that facilitate subject-specific learning. This would include  leaders from different offices negotiating the ways in which their respective sensemakings about equity and the school subject align or misalign, and work ing to connect ideas about equity to the specific pedagogical practices and epistemological assumptions of the subject.
