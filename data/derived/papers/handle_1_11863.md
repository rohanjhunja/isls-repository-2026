# Supporting Productive Dialogue with Collaborative Conversational Agents: Frequency and Sequence of Learner Behavior

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study presents the impact of a collaborative conversational agent on the chat dialogues of 17 dyads. Specifically, participants were asked to study two cases of climate change using the chat tool of a learning platform to communicate. The agent was available only in the second case and was able to monitor the participants’ discussion and intervene using the talk moves of the academically productive talk framework (e.g., prompting for elaboration, examples, etc.). Analysis of the chat logs showed higher levels of elaboration for the second case, while the agent acted as a catalyst increasing the chances for elaboration and transactivity.

Conversational agents and productivity goals in collaborative learning Studies in Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) have shown that students can be more engaged during the lessons in online learning environments structured for intense exchange of ideas (Eshuis et  al., 2019; Nguyen, 2023). Particularly in small groups, students have more time to verbalize these ideas, and therefore more opportunities to engage in productive student dialo gue (Hu & Chen, 2023). Yet, productive dialogue do es not happen spontaneously (Michaels & O’Connor, 2015). C onsequently, students spend most of the time with t heir peers addressing each other’s confusion and progres sing on their own. While this can encourage autonomy, the lack of guidance can lead to slower progress or gaming-the-system behaviors that hinder learning (de Jong et al., 2023). Therefore, guidance is needed, and teachers must follow groups to provide adequate guidance (Howe, 2014). The Academically Productive Talk (APT) framework aims to help  teachers support their students in reaching four key goals of productive dialogue (Michaels & O’Connor, 2015):

• G1: Helping students share their reasoning;

• G2: Helping students orient and listen to one another;

• G3: Helping students deepen their reasoning; and

• G4: Helping students engage with each other’s reasoning.

Supporting multiple groups simultaneously can be challenging f or the teacher. However, when the dialogue happens online,  collaborative conversational agents (CCAs) can offer guidance by playing the role of the teacher. CCAs are usually implemented to rely on specific types of intervention (aka talk moves) triggered at certain moments that foster student reflection base d on productivity standards, such as the four goals  G1-4 (Adamson et al., 2014; Tegos et al., 2016). When de signing CCA interventions, the APT framework has been extensively used because of its content-independent talk moves and its ability to prompt for elaboration and reflection rather than check student correctness (see Table 1 later on). While prior studies reported benefits of CCAs in explicit reasoning, transactive  exchanges, and productivity goals (e.g., Demetriadis et al., 2021;  Tegos et al., 2016), there is still a need for further studies that evaluate the impact of CCAs on the dialogue. Specifically, a crucial step for a comprehensive CCA evaluation is identifying the effectiveness of the type of interventions used. In this study, we argue that understanding the CCA’s impact on the frequency and sequence of behav iors related to the four productivity goals may provide a more nuanced picture of how a CCA can affect student dialogue. Study motivation and research question Collaborative conversational agents have been used to support productive dialogue among learners (e.g., de Araujo et al., 2024 a, 2024b; Tegos et al., 2016). However, to our knowledge, little is known about how the different types of agent intervention s affect productivity as measured through goal-related behavioral shift s. To analyze the impact that CCAs would have on the prod uctivity of a dialogue, we focused on the frequency of the goals, the sequence in which the goals appear with and without the agent, and the types of agent intervention that support each goal. Therefore in this study, we explored the following two research questions:

• RQ1: How do the interventions of a CCA affect participants’ productive dialogue in terms of frequency

and sequence of goal-related behaviors?

• RQ2: What goal-related participant behaviors are exhibited after different CCA intervention types?

## Participants

A total of 34 participants (male  = 12, female  = 22; age  = 22.7 years, SD  = 2.11; German  = 31, Dutch  = 2, Greek = 1) volunteered to participate in the study that took place online in October 2024. Seven participants had a high school diploma, 26 a Bachelor's degree, and one a Master’s degree. The participants were grouped randomly into 17 dyads. No reward was given for participation and all dyads studied under the same conditions. To take part in the study, fluency in English was r equired. Participants were recruited by advertising  the study through flyers and the online recruitment system of the hosting university. Material The study was conducted on the Go-Lab learning platform (de Jong et al., 2021). The platform allows the teacher to create learning material, organize it into sections, manage access, and integrate a range of tools such as quizzes, videos, and digital labs. The domain of the study was climate change, a topic  that was purposefully selected because of its popularity and coverage in media and  curricula, thus ensuring that the participants wou ld be knowledgeable enough to participate in a meaningful  dialogue, ev en with limited preparation.  The learning material included six sections: a consent form, two pages with preparation material, two discussion tasks, and a demographic questionnaire. Following the suggestion of the SWISH model (split-when-interaction-shouldhappen) for collaboration scripts (Dillenbourg & Ho ng, 2008), the preparation material was split into two pages, one focusing on food production and the other on re newable energy – each assigned to a different dyad member. The preparation pages included information on how these issues are linked to climate change, along with links to external sources and a four-minute video about the respective topic.  After reading individually their preparation material, the dyad members had to discuss two cases on climate change and agree on a joint answer, for example: Case 1: Sustainable Food Strategy for a Nation Facing Climate Change A mid-sized country is facing increased climate-related challenges, such as prolonged droughts and floods, which are affecting its food production and  overall sustainability. The government wants to reduce the nation's carbon footprint while ensuring  food security. In particular, they are exploring how dietary habits, agricultural practices, and foo d production could be transformed to reduce emissions and climate impact.

Task:   Discuss the case with your partner. Share your ideas on the steps the country should take, justify your thoughts by presenting your arguments, and think of at least 3 ideas of what the country should change to become more sustainable. O f course, you can have different opinions, but please consider multiple sides of the issue in your discussion. Write your final answer in the chat (e.g., "FINAL ANSWER:...").

A chat tool was available during the two discussion  tasks. In the first one, the dyad members discussed without the presence of Clair, our collaborative conversational agent, while in the second case study, Clair was present in the chat to follow the discussion of the two participants, apply the principles of the academically productive talk  framework, and intervene when necessary to prompt the particip ants into more productive dialogue. Clair is based on machine learning and trained on previously coded dialogue transcripts. Clair analyzes the dialogue taking into account more than 20 conte xtual, lexical, and setting variables to identify opportune moments and the appropriate type of intervention (de Araujo et al., 2023, 2024b). The interventions are based on a pre-defined set of eight utterances, aligned with the talk moves of the APT framework (Table 1). Clair has been used in several countries for research and teaching activities, while an extension was developed into MAI, a speech agent aimed at enhancing metacognitive awareness in collaborative learning settings (Edwards et al., 2024). Table 1 Clair’s Talk Moves Novelty level Description Recapping “Can someone give a brief summary of what we've covered so far?” Add-on “<discussant>, could you add some new perspective to what <speaker> just said?” Rephrasing “<discussant>, can you rephrase what <speaker> said?” Agree/ Disagree “<discussant>, can you explain to <speaker> if there is something you disagree with?” Link contributions “<discussant>, can you link your ideas to what <speaker> said?” Build on prior contributions “<speaker>, can you explain to <discussant> how this fits into the bigger picture?” Example “<speaker>, can you give an example or a real-life scenario for <discussant> that can help illustrate the concept better?”

## Design and procedure

The study applied a within-subject research design with the dyads discussing t he first case without Clair and the second one with Clair. The study duration was one h our with each phase lasting 15 minutes. During the first 15 minutes, the participants provi ded their consent and studied the topics assigned t o them (food production or renewable energy). After 15 minutes, access to the first case study was granted and the participants had to use the chat tool to discuss with their dyad partners and write a joint answer. Next, access to the second case study was granted in which, as mentioned, Clair was also present in the chat tool. Finally, during the remaining 15 minutes, the participants filled in the demographic question naire and asked any follow-up question they wanted to the research team. The study design was approved by the ethics committee of the hosting university.

## Data analysis

Each chat message (unit of analysis for the study) was coded deductively by two independent raters using the four goals (i.e., G1-G4) as codes (κ =.81). Paired-samples t-tests were used to compare how frequently the dyads exhibited the behaviors of the four goals for produ ctive dialogue with and without Clair’s presence in  the chat, while Markov chain diagrams were used to analyze how the dialogues transitioned between goals  in the two discussion tasks. A transition matrix was used to analyze how each talk move type affected the dialogu e. No test assumption was violated, while for all statistical tests α =.05 was used.

## Discussion and conclusions

Regarding RQ1 and Clair’s impact on the dialogue in terms of the frequency and sequence of goal-related behaviors, the chat logs showed that Clair intervened on average 4.12 times during the 15 minutes allocated to the second discussion task. Analysis of the second discussion task showed a sig nificant increase in the number of times the participants deepened their reasoning (G3), while there was also an increase in the number of times that participants engaged with each other’s reasoning (G4), but this increase was not statistically significant. Comparing the two Markov chains, we see a dominant sequence G1 G3G2G1 for the first discussion task, with participants reaching G4 primarily after G3 (28.57%). Once the dialogue reached G4, it was very likely to either remain at G4 (36.92%) or move to G2 (39.13%) and back to the dominant sequence. So, it seems that having peers engaging with each other’s reasoning (G4) was challenging. Once the dialogue reached G4, a sequence of transactive messages was posted before the dialogue “moved back” to G2 and the dominant sequence. On the contrary, during the second discussion task,  where Clair was present, the dialogue was more likely to reach G3 and G4, with Clair intervening more often  after the participants deepened their reasoning (G3Clair: 30.77%) or engaged with each other’s reasoni ng (G4Clair: 41.67%), while the dialogue returned to these states 82.86% of the times after Clair intervened (ClairG3: 58.57%; ClairG4: 24.29%). So, it seems that Clair acted like a buffer keeping the dialogue in G3 and G4 states, with deepening one’s reasoning appearing to be, by a huge margin, the main reaction to Clair’s interventions. Finally, Clair seems unlikely to intervene after G2, while the transition G2G1 remains the most likely one for G2 in both discussion tasks. Summing up, the addition of Clair into the second d iscussion task resulted in dialogues that included more elaboration. There was no increase in the freq uency the participants shared their thoughts (G1) or oriented and listened to one another (G2). As previous studies showed, it is common for CCAs to have a significant impact only on some of the four goals (e.g., de Araujo et al., 2024a, 2024b; Tegos et al., 2016). Nevertheless, while G1 and G2 are crucial for externalizing thoughts and b ringing participants on the same page, we could arg ue that it is G3 and G4 that push the dialogue forward by making the participants deepen the discussion and be transactive. As such, we maintain that Clair’s role in the discussion is still valuable for the participants. Regarding RQ2 and the goals the participants reache d after each intervention type, interventions that prompted participants to build upon prior contributions and recap were the most frequent ones (21 and 16, out of 70 in total). While the former were followed primarily by further elaboration (G3), the latter were the interventions that were most often followed by engaging with each other’s reasoning (G4). Asking for an example was always followed by G3, while asking students to denote their agreement/disagreement with their partners was the type of intervention that was followed by G2 (orienting and listening to one another) more than any other type. There was not a particular pattern between intervention types  and sharing one’s understanding (G1), while th e remaining three types (i.e., add-on, rephrase, and link) appeared only a few times without any pattern emerging. The talk moves used are meant to cover all four goals of productive dialogue without a specific mirroring between a talk move and a goal. I ndeed, in previous studies on CCAs and the APT framework, a subset of talk moves was used (usually including build on prior contributions) targeting elaboration and transactivity within the dialogue (e.g., Demetriadis et al., 2021; Tegos et al., 2016). It makes sense, though, that for example, building on prior contributions would prompt participants to el aborate, while asking for a recap would nudge peopl e into taking into account their partner’s reasoning.

The study provided a preliminary analysis of the im pact of a CCA on a collaborative learning setting, through the lens of frequency and sequence of learner behavior. Markov chain analysis showed that the impact of a CCA can be evident both on the quality characteristics of the dialogue (e.g., frequency of goals) and the evolution of the dialogue over time (e.g., sequence of goals). In addition, the type of talk moves seemed to cover particular areas. Future studies could also examine how different talk moves could better serve the four goals of productive dialogue, while a control group and a more complex design could address a potential order effect.
