# Gestures Can Speak: Supporting Heritage Language Literacy Through Movement

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learning a heritage language in English-dominant contexts poses challenges for children with limited exposure to formal instruction. This study explores how gesture-based supports embedded in a mobile Persian-learning app enhanced letter recall among seco nd-generation Iranian-American children. Each letter in the app was introduced with a hand gesture reinforced through mini-games. During interviews, children were shown letter visuals and prompted with these gestures. Results showed that gesture p rompting increased correct recognition from 48% to 80%. Qualitative responses revealed that gestures served as memory hooks and cross-linguistic bridges, linking Persian letters to familiar English symbols. Findings contribute to embodied learning research, suggesting that gesture-integrated digital tools can support heritage language literacy in bilingual contexts.

Learning a family or heritage language in an English-dominant context like the US is challenging for many children of immigrant families. Formal schools rarely offer systematic opportunities to develop literacy in the home language, and everyday exposure i s often limited to informal interactions with parents or relatives. As a result, children may develop some familiarity with the language but struggle to acquire reading and writing skills. This creates a need for instructional methods that are both engaging and efficient in helping young learners acquire foundational literacy skills in their family language.

Prior research has highlighted the promise of embodied approaches, particularly gestures, in supporting second-language acquisition. Studies of embodied cognition argue that learning is grounded in bodily action and that gestures, as one form of embodiment, can serve as integral components of meaning-making (Barsalou, 2008; Goldin-Meadow, 2014; Nathan & Alibali, 2011), and even in a collaborative context (Garg et al., 2025). Empirical work has shown that pairing new vocabulary with meaningful gestures enhances memory, recall, and engagement (e.g., Macedonia & Klimesch, 2014; Goldin-Meadow, 2014). For young learners beginning with letter recognition, embodied strategies can help bridge the gap between abstract symbols and memorable actions. The current study examines how gestures can support the learning of the Persian alphabet. We developed a mobile app in which each Persian letter has a specific hand gesture (often related to the action of the word starting with the letter), reinforced thro ugh various interactions as mini-games. During data collection, children first saw a visual of a letter and tried to identify it; if they could not, we cued them with the same gesture used in the app. This design lets us track both initial recall and recognition after the gesture prompt. During follow-up interviews, children were first shown visuals of letters and asked to identify them; if they could not, the corresponding gesture was shown as a prompt.

The analysis revealed that gesture prompting increased correct recall from 48% to 80%,  demonstrating a strong effect on recognition. Qualitative comments from students further indicated that gestures served as memory hooks, cross-linguistic bridges, and anchors for meaning—helping learners connect Persian letters to familiar English sounds or contexts. These findings contribute to the growing evidence that embodied learning can effectively support bilingual and heritage language education. Integrating gestures into mobile learning environments not only enhances recall but also strengthens engagement and cultural connection, offering a scalable pathway for preserving family languages in English-dominant contexts.

## Theoretical background

Embodied cognition emphasizes that learning is grounded in bodily action and sensorimotor experience (Barsalou, 2008; Nathan & Alibali, 2011). Within language learning, gestures are not merely communicative add-ons but integral components of meaning-making. Recent research has shown how learners’ spontaneous gestures often reveal conceptual understanding and can be leveraged pedagogically (Goldin-Meadow, 2014; Nathan, Walkington, & Alibali, 2017). For heritage language learners, associating a letter with a hand gesture creates an embodied trace that connects physical action with abstract orthographic representation. Dual coding theory 1962 (Paivio, 1991) further explains why gestures are effective: information encoded through both verbal and sensorimotor channels strengthens memory. When children perform a gesture while viewing a Persian letter, they generate a multimodal representation that  is more robust than visual exposure alone. Neurocognitive evidence supports this account, showing that gesture-enriched word learning activates motor as well as linguistic regions of the brain, leading to deeper encoding and longer retention (Macedonia & Mueller, 2016). Together, these frameworks position gestures as tools that transform letter recognition from a purely visual-symbolic task into an embodied practice. In doing so, gestures may reduce cognitive load, enhance recall, and foster positive engagement with the family language. This study contributes to the field by providing empirical evidence on how gesture-based strategies can be designed to support heritage language learning in Englishdominant contexts.

## Participants

Participants for this study are first or second-generation Iranian-American children enrolled in pre-or elementary grades in an English-dominant school. As the participant inclusion criteria required that at least one parent know Persian, all participants had some exposure to Persian at home but limited opportunities for formal instruction in the language. Parental consent and child assent, both in writing and via video conferencing, were obtained prior to participation and recording.

Tools and materials The educational approach employed in the app's design is based on the method developed by Ali Sedaghaty (http://amookhayat.com/), which emphasizes simplifying the learning of Persian. The app includes a progressive curriculum that covers vowels, letter-vowel combinations, sentence building, and reading comprehension (see Hadinezhad et al., 2024; Shahi et al., 2024). It comprises videos, mini-games, and other activities focused on vocabulary expansion, learning the Persian alphabet, reading, and comprehension. Each letter is first introduced in a video of the instructor  making a gesture related to its context. For example (see Figure 1a, 1b), to teach the letter مMe), which is introduced with the act of gesturing writing. Later, many of the minigames utilized gestures as a hook to reinforce letter learning. For example, Letter Matching (Figure 1c), Guess the Letter (Figure 1d), and Scratch and Discover (Figure 1e) all prompt them towards the letter and help them succeed  in the game. Each gesture was consistently modeled during interactions in the app and later used as prompts during the interview. Other study materials consisted of visuals of Persian letters, which were used in the interviews to assess their learning.

## Procedure

1963 Each participant has three months of interaction with the learning materials, and the research team meets virtually in video conferencing with them and their parent/s weekly. The weekly interactions have three sections: (1) feedback from the parent/s, (2) feedback from the child, and (3) learning assessment interview. The data from this study comes from the third part of these weekly meetings. To assess their learning, we first displayed the letter-visual and asked, “Do you know this letter?” Depending on the child’s response, we followed a structured decision tree:

(1) If the child recalled the letter correctly, we asked them to describe or demonstrate the gesture associated

with it.

(2) If the child responded incorrectly or could not recall, we demonstrated the gesture and noted whether it

improved the letter recognition.

We employed this approach to capture both spontaneous recall and recognition (if any) after gesture prompts. We analyzed about 6 hours of video data using the coding scheme summarized in Table 1. Three dimensions were coded for each letter: initial recall, gesture description, and recognition after prompt. Overall, the findings show that gestures provided an effective scaffold for recall. While only 53% of letters were correctly recalled initially, gesture prompts increased recognition rates to 89%, highlighting the potential of using gestures for reinforcing letter recognition for language learning. The results from this study suggest that gestures played a meaningful role in helping children recognize Persian letters. Recognition rates increased substantially when gestures were introduced, though a small subset of children still did not recognize cer tain letters even after seeing the gestures. Several factors could explain these cases: learning traces may not have been strong enough, children’s attention may have drifted, or engagement issues may have led them to switch focus away from the target letter. Even with these limitations, the overall effect was a clear increase in recognition, illuminating gestures as potential effective supports in heritage language learning. These findings are consistent with broader theories of embodied learning, which highlight the ways in which bodily actions ground cognitive processes across disciplines. By pairing abstract Persian symbols with physical movements, the children were able to  anchor their memory in embodied traces, echoing prior research on how gesture strengthens recall and meaning-making.

An important observation from this study is how quickly children made cross-linguistic connections, often linking Persian gestures and letters to familiar English sounds or symbols. This finding suggests future work on examining how cross-linguistic associations shape the way children interpret and repurpose gestures, and how the meaning of context-specific gestures shifts across languages. Understanding these dynamics could help refine embodied approaches, ensuring that gestures not only aid recall but also deepen bilingual and cultural connections. This study examined how gesture-based prompts, designed within a mobile app for Persian letter learning, supported letter recall among first and second-generation Iranian-American children. The findings show that gestures meaningfully enhanced learning: while only about half of the letters were recalled correctly at first exposure, an additional third were recognized once gesture prompts were provided. Qualitative responses further demonstrated that gestures served as memory hooks, bridges between languages, and anchors for meaning even when Persian words were not remembered.
