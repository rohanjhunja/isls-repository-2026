# What Factors do Students Appreciate in a Math Class?

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Post-secondary students often have negative experiences and attitudes towards math. Alternatives to traditional algebra courses have been developed, which may increase student success. This study uses post-course reflections from a non-traditional math course that incorporates programming and art, to investigate what features students appreciated. The course improved student attitudes towards math. The aspects of math classes that students find supportive may lead to increased resilience in learning mathematics.

Similar to many post-secondary settings, students at our university have struggled to pass the algebra course which meets general education requirements. In response, an interdisciplinary team (mathematics, computer science & education) designed an alternative course, titled Mathematical and Computational Thinking (MCT) (Friend et al., 2023). Given that most students will not regularly use the mathematics they learn in school (Lave, 1988; Sfard, 2014), a major goal of the course was to increase students’ confidence and interest in math, i.e., to increase their resilience towards engaging with math through learning in a different context than they had previously experienced. This course embraces the visual arts and uses computer programming as a tool to support mathematics learning. Visual patterns form the basis for explorations in arithmetic and geometric sequences, from which algebraic functions and corresponding functions in computer programs are rea soned. The course is taught by mathematics faculty assigned to teach gen ed math courses. Instructors use active learning strategies: students spend class working on assignments. Assessment is through quizzes and tests, small homework assignments, and open-ended “art show” projects shared with the class.

With support from the U.S. National Science Foundation (Award 1712080), researchers collected data to investigate the effectiveness of the course. Although the percentage of students who pass the class mirrors that of college algebra (A, B, C grades ~66%), survey results demonstrate that students’ attitudes towards mathematics

– primarily confidence – significantly increased from the beginning to end of the semester  (Friend et al., 2023).

T his is important because the students who enroll in this course have extremely negative attitudes towards math, experiencing it as a barrier to graduation (Holm & Saxe, 2016). In structured reflections on their life histories with mathematics before the course (based on Drake, 2006), students describe negative experiences of repeated failure. The course succeeded as an intervention to improve students’ attitudes towards math. We theorize the classroom as a figured world of cultural practices (Holland et al., 1998; Nasir & Cooks, 2009). That is, students understand classroom practices through their identity and prior experiences, and their experiences further inform identity development and new understandings of the culture of the class.   In this study, we focus on students’ identification of positive aspects of this math class. By u nderstanding the aspects of the class students found beneficial, we can understand ways in which the course supported students’ attitude changes.  Thus, this study addresses the research question, “What aspects of the MCT course did students appreciate?”

## Method

As a homework assignment at the end of the semester, for completion credit, students were asked to write a structured reflection about their experience in the class. Prompts asked students to describe a “peak” moment in the class, a low moment, what they learned and got out of the class, things that were good about the class and things that should be changed (Drake, 2006). A total of 262 students completed these reflections across six semesters between Spring 2018 and Fall 2020. Reflections were anonymized for analysis. Qualitative data analysis was conducted by the first two authors. The researchers used affect coding to identify comments that referred to a positive aspect of the class (Saldaña, 2013). The researchers worked together to inductively code a subset of data, then used a collaborative coding process to refine the codes and code the rest of the data (Syed & Nelson, 2015).
