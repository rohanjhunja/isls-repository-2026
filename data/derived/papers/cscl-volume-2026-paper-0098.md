# AI as a Collaborative Partner: EMPA’s Role in Supporting Reflective Teamwork Learning

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teamwork requires students to reflect on their practices, yet individualized mentoring is often limited. While AI tutoring systems increasingly support learning, most position AI as external evaluator rather than collaborative partner. We present EMPA, an AI-based collaborative partner that scaffolds teamwork learning through case studies, activities, and videos. Grounded in Schön’s reflection-in-action and reflection-on-action, EMPA encourages students to adapt strategies during tasks and analyze experiences afterwards. Students engage with scenarios and activities while EMPA prompts real-time reflection, then receive personalized summaries and complete surveys that consolidate learning. Early deployment with undergraduates shows EMPA’s potential to help learners examine assumptions, recognize cultural dynamics, and develop strategies for improving collaboration. This study operationalizes Schön’s framework via AI-supported mentoring, positioning AI as a reflective partner to scale practice and strengthen teamwork.

Teamwork is a cornerstone of higher education because it prepares students to solve complex problems and collaborate across diverse perspectives (Sridharan et al., 2023). Yet students often struggle with communication breakdowns, unequal participation, and cultural misunderstandings (Hussein, 2021). Although instructors can support collaboration through mentoring, such guidance is difficult to provide consistently in large classes (Navisotschnig, 2024). Recent advances in AI offer new possibilities. Rather than serving only as tools, AI systems can function as collaborative partners that engage learners through prompts, feedback, and reflective dialogue (Ashish et al., 2025). In this paper, we introduce EMPA, an AI-based collaborative partner grounded in Schön’s reflective practice framework. Our research question is: How can an AI collaborative partner operationalize Schön’s reflective practice framework to improve teamwork?  EMPA supports both in-activity interpretation and retrospective reflection to help students develop adaptive teamwork strategies. Theoretical grounding and operationalization of Schön’s framework The Schön’s theory of reflective practice emphasizes reflection as central to professional learning in complex and uncertain contexts (Schön, 1991). He distinguishes between reflection-in-action, which occurs as learners adapt strategies during an activity, and reflection-on-action, which occurs afterwards as they analyze actions, outcomes, and assumptions. Teamwork provides an important domain for applying this framework because group work often involves conflict, miscommunication, and differing cultural norms (Jaiswal et al, 2024). Reflection-in-action helps learners respond flexibly in the moment, while reflection-on-action supports evaluation and future improvement. Prior research shows that scaffolding reflection candeepen collaboration, enhance metacognitive skills, and improve learning outcomes (Coulson & Harvey, 2013; Jia et al., 2025). However, these supports often remain external rather than participatory. EMPA  extends this work by positioning AI as a collaborative partner in reflective cycles.  EMPA op erationalizes Schön’s framework through interactional sequencing: reflection-inaction occurs during scenario analysis through real-time prompts, while reflection-on-action occurs after interaction through structured synthesis and personal strategy planning. Reflection-in-action is supported through real-time prompts embedded during engagement with case materials. As learners analyze unfolding teamwork scenarios, EMPA poses interpretive and metacognitive questions (e.g., “What assumptions might be shaping your interpretation?” or “How might this interaction be perceived differently across cultural contexts?”). These prompts intervene during engagement to support in-situ interpretation, prompting learners to reconsider assumptions and adapt reasoning while the scenario is unfolding. Reflection-on-action is supported through structured post-activity dialogue. Following scenario engagement, EMPA shifts roles to facilitate retrospective analysis focused on how learners would respond in their own teamwork contexts  (e.g., “How would you respond differently in your own team?” or “What strategies would you carry into future collaborations?”). Here, EMPA shifts from moment-tomoment prompting to facilitating consolidation, abstraction, and transfer. Importantly, this distinction is operationalized through interaction timing, prompt structure, and conversational roles rather than through separate 615 model training pipelines. The same LLM supports both reflective modes, but its behavior is shaped through system prompts, activity sequencing, and interface design that differentiate in-situ reflection from retrospective synthesis. System architecture overview EMPA combines an efficient LLM, modular interface, and scalable backend to support AI-mediated teamwork reflection. It is built on fine-tuned LLaMA-3 3B Instruct using QLoRA, enabling lightweight deployment while preserving performance. The browser-based interface (React/Tailwind, FastAPI, Supabase) guides learners through six modules with videos, quizzes, reflection prompts, and chat-based dialogue. FastAPI manages registration, conversations, and chat history, while Supabase stores user profiles and logs.  Together, these components allow EMPA to function as a collaborative mentor that scaffolds reflection-in-action and reflectionon-action and helps learners transfer teamwork strategies to future contexts.

## Pilot study and preliminary findings

To explore EMPA’s potential as a collaborative partner, we conducted an initial pilot study with five undergraduate students. The study was designed to investigate how students engage with an AI partner during a reflective learning activity focused on teamwork.  Five undergraduate students were recruited from an undergraduate course at a large public university. Participants engaged in a single, unmoderated session with the EMPA system. They were instructed to watch an embedded case study video depicting a m ulticultural project team facing common collaborative challenges. After the video, they engaged in a text-based dialogue with EMPA to interpret the team’s dynamics and translate those observations into strategies for their own teamwork. We collected the full text logs of the conversations between each participant and EMPA for qualitative analysis.  Our practices, positioning the AI as a collaborative partner in the learning process. Finding 1: Reflection-in-Action: Students engaged in reflection-in-action by analyzing unfolding team behaviors while interacting with EMPA. One student noted, “The team dynamic broke down by Jonas interrupting the others and not letting them finish their ideas.” EMPA responded by contextualizing the comment: “Some cultures value directness and assertiveness overactive listening. Reflecting on these  potential biases can add depth to your analysis.”  This exchange illustrates EMPA’s ability to prompt reflection-in-action, encouraging learners to question assumptions as they engaged with the scenario.  Finding 2:  Scaffolding Collaboration Strategies:  Students frequently proposed ways to improve the team dynamic s within the case study. For example, one suggested, “I would have told Jonas (character name) to wait a second and to hear the others out.”  EMPA affirmed this and extended the idea: “Consider implementing a round-robin approach, where each participant has an equal opportunity to contribute.” Similarly, another student concluded, “If they gave each other time to hear each other out it would have worked better.”  EMPA reinforced this by introducing strategies such as active paraphrasing and the StopThink-Share technique. These exchanges illustrate EMPA’s role as a collaborative partner, supporting learners in co-constructing strategies they could apply within their own teamwork contexts. Finding 3: Fostering Reflectionon-Action: After the interaction, EMPA prompted reflection-on-action, guiding learners to reinterpret the scenario and formulate strategies for their own future teamwork. This is exemplified when a student proposed a sophisticated, actionable plan for future teamwork:  “I’d start with an empathy check: ask teammates early how they prefer to plan and what motivates them. I’d explicitly discuss expectations around deadlines and collaboration. By agreeing on a shared process at the start, we could prevent misunderstandings and respect everyone’s working style.” This demonstrates a clear instance of reflection-on-action. Crucially, EMPA acts as a collaborative partner by not just validating the response, but by connecting it to theoretical concepts:  “Your proposed strategy is a great starting point. Your focus on active listening and prioritizing teamwork values aligns with collectivist cultures that emphasize harmony and cooperation. ” By validating the student ’s idea (“great starting point”) and co-constructing a deeper understanding by linking it to a theoretical concept (“collectivist cultures”), the AI moves beyond the role of a simple feedback tool into that of a true partner in learning. To conclude, this study introduced EMPA, an AI collaborative partner designed to operationalize Schön’s reflective practice framework. Its main contribution is a proof-of-concept design that shifts AI from external evaluator to active participant in reflection. Although pilot findings (N=5) do not support claims of improved teamwork, they illustrate how EMPA’s architecture and interaction sequencing can scaffold reflection-in-action and reflection-on-action. These formative results provide a foundation for larger studies of teamwork outcomes and adaptive mentoring.
