# Integrating ChatGPT into Writing for a Doctoral STEM Course

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Empirical research on how generative AI (genAI) is transforming academic writing in doctoral education remains scarce. This participatory study examined a STEM doctoral course where students used ChatGPT-4 at various stages. Students found genAI helpful with mechanics and structure, sensemaking, and literature review. This "hybrid AI-human writing process" enhanced both writing and AI literacy. Pedagogical insights are provided.

Pedagogical insights are provided. Generative AI (genAI) is producing a paradigm shift in academic writing (Raitskaya & Tikhonova, 2024). Known difficulties doctoral students experience with academic writing include developing a distinctive voice, learning the writing conventions, and learning to respond to feedback (Parker et al., 2024), but there is little research on how genAI use might impact those student difficulties. While “hybrid writing co-created by human and artificial intelligence is becoming prevalent and will soon become the norm.” (Eaton, 2023, p. 3), there is little empirical research on hybrid writing in doctoral education (Parker et al., 2024). Research on a doctoral writing class that purposefully integrated genAI found that hybrid writing followed an iterative process characterized by brainstorming and ideation, feedback, meaning negotiation, and refinement (Parker et al., 2024), and called for  replication in different disciplines. We report on a participatory research study conducted on a doctoral course on contemporary issues in STEM education research at an R1 public university in the US that featured extensive, integrated use of AI for class assignments. The authors include the instructor (first author), an expert on AI in education who was a guest speaker at one class session (second author), and five of the six students. Data sources include various stages of a position paper including draf ts, critiques, and final paper; instructor observations; and student participants’ autoethnographic written reflections after the end of the course. Students’ writing began with ChatGPT 4o-generated drafts of position papers, which the students critiqued, modified, and drew from when creating their own initial draft. This genAI-first approach contrasts with previous studies that used genAI for brainstorming and feedback rather than initial draft generation.

The study is guided by the following research questions: RQ1. How do doctoral students use AI in their writing process? RQ2. How do doctoral students perceive the effects of using AI in their writing?

## Context, setting, and data analysis

The major assignment was a position paper on a STEM education research topic. Students first submitted a brief plan including the topic, title, rationale for the choice of the topic, and their position on the topic. Then the professor used ChatGPT 4o and 4o mini (the state-of-the-art versions at the time) to generate initial drafts for each topic using the prompt “Write a position paper for a doctoral class in STEM education arguing that [position]. The position paper must include at least 10 citations to scholarly journal papers.” This approach  legitimized the use of genAI and provided an entryway for AI-novice students. Students analyzed and discussed these genAI drafts in class. Next, they each generated a draft on Scholar GPT (a ChatGPT-powered plug-in designed to provide “reliable research and data analysis capabilities…to streamline the research process” (Scholar GPT, n.d.) and compared the output to the ChatGPT 4o drafts on a discussion board post. Students then wrote their own draft position paper and received feedback from ChatGPT, a peer, and the professor. T hey revised accordingly and submitted their final position paper with all genAI drafts, genAI critique, and peer critique as appendi ces along with a half-page description of whether and how they used AI. IRB authorization was obtained. The students were invited to participate in the writing of this paper and five of six participated.  Students wrote reflections on their use of GenAI in the course guided by prompts about their professional and doctoral identity, prior AI experience, AI use in  the class, what they had learned about AI for academic writing, challenges  they experienced, and any other not eworthy aspects they wanted to share. Recruitment of co-authors was done after the semester ended. 2839 Class materials and autoethnographic reflections were qualitatively analyzed using a hybrid inductive and deductive thematic approach (Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006). Deductive analysis was based on identified functions of genAI for academic writing (Raitskaya & Tikhonova, 2024) and stages in the hybrid human-AI writing process (Parker et al., 2024). Themes were allowed to inductively emerge from the data during coding. The data were analyzed using a hybrid thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006; Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006; Xu & Zammit, 2020). We then merged codes (mechanics & structure, literature review, and sensemaking). Mechanics and structure We identified three “grain sizes” of genAI use, starting with spelling and grammar: “I ran my final assignments through ChatGPT to catch any mistakes, misspellings, or unclear sentences in my work.” (Student #4). Students used AI for higher-level edits, such as structuring or organizing their manuscripts: “Using ScholarGPT allowed me to reflect on how I could organize and structure my headings, giving me a more precise direction for my approach to my paper.” (Student #6). At the largest granularity, students mentioned polish and style: “AI can serve as a resource to refine, edit, and enhance writing, which is particularly helpful for students like me who may have strong ideas but struggle with formatting or articulating them clearly." (Student #4)

## AI for literature review

Literature reviews are an essential part of academic writing and are highly labor-intensive. ScholarGPT references were usually accurate and included a link to a pdf, while ChatGPT included many hallucinated papers. One student noted that AI located useful papers even while they had struggled to find relevant literature. AI for sensemaking Students also found AI helpful for sensemaking: “For brainstorming, I used ChatGPT Scholar to help generate main points for assignments and to come up with ideas I might not have thought of on my own. ” (Student #4). Another student noted: “There are some interesting perspectives that appeared that I had not thought of that can be used as a jumping-off point.” (Student #1). Students found AI particularly useful for strengthening arguments: “I might use it to help edit my work, or go back and forth on strengthening arguments, and maybe even critique my own work.” (Student #2, Week 10).

Although AI research has grown exponentially, limited research has explored how AI is transforming student interaction and creating new pedagogical practices (Diaz & Nussbaum, 2025), as well as the role of AI within those practices (Diaz & Delgado, 2025). To address this gap, we examine AI integration in a graduate-level course. Students described AI usage as a means of enhancing their self-confidence in writing, as AI provided reinforcement and helped them make decisions about the writing process. While students used genAI for sensemaking, there is a risk that generative AI may homogenize texts, posing a potential threat to students' development of their unique authorial voice (Parker et al., 2024). A hybrid approach, in which students incorporate AI-generated text but revise it in their own words, is therefore crucial. In our conceptualization, we aim to understand how students can use AI to start drafting their ideas, shifting their initial role from writer to editor. We align our conceptualization more cl osely with AI-human hybrid writing rather than the human-AI interactions described by Eaton (2023). However, we concur with Eaton’s (2023) perspective that "humans remain accountable for fact-checking, verification procedures, and truth-telling" (p. 20). Indeed, we believe that seamless AI integration should involve humans playing a critical role in coproducing knowledge. AI should function as a collaborative tool that enhances students' writing processes rather than replacing them. This was supported by our findings, as students mentioned that when they experienced writer's block, they used AI to generate initial drafts, allowing them to take on the role of editor while AI acted as the writer. This dualistic role of AI was reflected in Student #3’s comment: "...it allowed me to go from 'writing a paper from scratch' to 'editing a paper that already existed.'" Becoming a skilled editor is an essential academic competency, and generative AI offers opportunities for students to develop this skill. AI usage statement Throughout this paper, we used generative AI to correct grammatical and typographical errors. We did not use AI to generate text; instead, we provided our own writing and explicitly instructed AI to correct grammar mistakes only. All output was reviewed to ensure accuracy.
