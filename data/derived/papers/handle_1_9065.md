# Children’s Navigational Map Reading Strategies and Implications for Educational Geogames

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Researchers emphasize the need for a deeper understanding of children’s navigational map reading and an emerging interest in digital geogames for educational purposes. However, there are only a few related studies. We conducted a navigational map reading study with children aged 6 to 13. The children were asked to navigate to a destination within a virtual environment by using a map and to explain their procedures thinking aloud. Some children described how they recognized the destination from its nearer environment, whereas others described their route and stopover locations towards the destination. Based on the children’s statements, we formulated educational implications for geogame features with the potential to represent successful navigational map reading strategies for training purposes.

## Introduction

Navigational map reading (NMR), map-aided navigation between locations in the environment (Lobben, 2007), is highly relevant in geoscience education (Hemmer et al., 2008; Ishikawa & Kastens, 2005). Children d iffer in their success in using maps for navigation. One reason for these individual differences is the amount of experience children have in reading a map which underlines the importance of NMR trainings (Hemmer et al., 2013). The literature mentions several “abilities” and “skills” related to NMR, e.g. being able to locate oneself on a map, to navigate to a certain destination, to recognize objects on the way by locating them on th e map, as well as to know the direction towards destination a nd start location (Arthurs et al., 2021; Hemmer et al., 2013; Lobben, 2007). High NMR performance is defined by short routes to the destination and correct turns as well as by little hesitations or spins around the own axis (Hergan & Umek, 2017; Hemmer et al., 2013; Lobben, 2007). Despite its relevance, research on children’s NMR strategies and processes is quite rare (Arthurs et al., 2021). Geoinformation technologies such as geogames are promising tools for spatial learning, but rese arch on the effectiveness of these tools and its features in educational contexts is sparse (Laato et al., 2019; Baker et al., 2015). This paper aims to fill the research gap by investigating children’s NMR strategies and suggesting geogame features with the potential to address these strategies within NMR trainings. Educational geogames Geogames are location-based games working on mobile  and geoinformation technologies (Bartoschek et al.,

2018) with a game activity that “evolves according to players’ location“ (Avouris & Yiannoutsou, 2012).

Numerous location-based games with educational and non-educational purposes exist. They were associated with positive learning outcomes in the context of naviga tion (Laato et al., 2019). This contrasts with stud ies that demonstrated negative short-and long-term effects of geoinformation technologies on spatial learning which is explained by the user’s passivity when using the technologies (Ishikawa, 2019; Field et al., 2011; Münzer et al., 2011). However, the technologies investigated in these studies were not designed for learning purposes. Therefore, we want to focus on geogames that were created with  an educational intention. One example is Biparcours (Actionbound) which allows its users to create map-based rallies for a chosen location on different ed ucational topics. It includes two types of navigation tasks (navigation following an arrow and navigation via a map) and a street map that is fixed to north and can be panned and zoomed. Navigation is supported by displaying the location, the destination, and a compass. Feedback can be tur ned on or off and shows the accuracy of the player’ s task solution as well as the correct answer. With its game idea as well as its limited customizability and availability of features, Biparcours represents one of several educ ational geogames that are currently available on th e market. Since geogames are adjustable to their application, research on educational geogames should focus on the variety of possible features and their potential in addressing NMR strategies for training purposes. Navigational map reading strategies Lobben (2004, p. 272) defines map reading strategy as a “specific method or tactic employed by a map user to complete navigational map-reading tasks”. With a sh ort review of related studies, Lobben highlights th at researchers often make use of think-aloud methods to investigate people’s strategies in reading a map. ICLS2022 Proceedings 639 © ISLS Adamina and Erzinger (2017) interviewed third to sixth graders on their procedures in map-related tasks and identified different strategies used. In map-ba sed navigation tasks many children used a kind of matching strategy: they tried to find already known representations based on their spatial experiences and used these f or orientation. Moreover, many children made use of a landmark strategy (i.e. orientation to prominent landmarks such as school buildings or playgrounds) and/or a route strategy (i.e. use of route knowledge, directions, and connections in space such as right, left, straight ahead) when solving tasks involving mental mapping, describing routes, or direction pointing or when explaining ho w to orientate in an unknown environment. Other chi ldren solved these tasks with an overview strategy (i.e. imagining being above the ground and looking  down on it by changing the own perspective).

The scientific and educational interest in children’s NMR strategies is high, but research in that field is still in its early stages and remains underrepresen ted (Arthurs et al., 2021). This paper answers to t his gap by exploring children’s strategies via think-aloud method. Additionally, it makes suggestions for geogame features that have the potential to address successful NMR strategies in educational trainings.

## Method

We conducted a study in forms of a digital map-based game that was played by children in a virtual environment. Participants had to solve NMR tasks by navigating through the virtual world and interacting with the digital map. We chose a virtual setting for conducting this stud y, since virtual reality is proven to offer realist ic simulations for spatial cognition research and full experimenta l control (Hardiess et al., 2015). As a sub-aspect of this overarching study, we investigated the children's procedures and strategies in NMR via think-aloud method which is the basis for this paper.

## Participants and materials

This study is a sub-study of an NMR study with 110 boys and girls (balanced) aged 6 to 13. For answeri ng the question of this paper, we reduced the data to 37 d atasets as explained in the Data analysis section. With regard to its socio demographic information, the sub-dataset represents the total population. The participants were locally recruited via advertisement. They participated one after another and were rewarded with certificates o f participation and science toys. The study took plac e during the schools’ summer holidays in our instit ute in Münster, Germany, in conformance with the local Covid-19 regulations.

## Experimental design und procedure

The NMR tasks were implemented in the location-based geogame GeoGami installed on a 10.2” tablet. The virtual environment was shown via a projector on a large screen. The participants were sitting on a chair in front of the screen with the tablet on a stand next to them and a controller in their hands (see Figure 1). Figure 1 Experimental design: navigational map reading in a virtual environment.

GeoGami presented the different NMR tasks in text a nd audio format as well as a map of the virtual environment. Participants had to solve the NMR task s by navigating through the virtual environment usi ng the game controller or by interacting with the map on the tablet. The game lasted 30 to 60 minutes and included eight ICLS2022 Proceedings 640 © ISLS navigation-to-a-flag tasks as well as eight self-location tasks. In the navigation-to-a-flag tasks, the children were asked to navigate to a flag marked on the map. In t he self-location tasks, the children were asked to locate themselves by tapping on the map. The map was fixed  to north and the children were not allowed to rota te the device, but free to zoom and pan the map. The virtual environment represented a park area with several paths and crossings, grass, trees, flowers, benches, trash cans, bridges, a river, and lakes as well as three unique landmarks (a playground with two swings, telephone booths, and a snack bar). The start location of the total gam e was the playground located in the south of the map.

In the first step, the participants took part in a tutorial that explained the game, the tasks, the map symbols, as well as the controller usage. They have been given around 10 or 15 minutes to practice. The game did not start until the participants felt confident in the controlling and tablet handling. During the game, after two specific tasks —a navigation-to-a-flag and a self-location task—we  asked the children to explain their solution to th e experimenter in forms of retrospective think-aloud statements. This procedure does not influence the task solution itself and is still representative because of its t emporal proximity to the task. Already at the begin ning of the experiment, we informed the children on that procedure and explained them that we would like to learn how they read and use maps for navigation. Directly after a participant solved one of these tasks, we asked: “How did you solve/do that?” or—if this question was unclear— “How did you know you had to go here?” or “How did you know you were in/tapped on the right place?” The experimenter transcribed the children’s answers directly in the experimental situation including notes on gestures if a child was pointing at objects in the virtual environment. In the following, we will focus on the statements of the navigation-to-a-flag task since it represents the navigational aspect of NMR which we aim to investigate. In this  task, the children were initially informed on their start location by presenting them a location marker on the map next to the telephone booths (see Figure 2). They started this task with the view direction fixed eastwards. Afterwards, the location marker disappeared and a destination flag was presented. The flag was locate d at a three-paths-crossing with a trash can and a tree. The children were asked to move to the flag in the virt ual world and to press an OK-button on the tablet w hen they thought they have reached the destination. They had to move in southern direction with a map aligned to the north. In the virtual world, there were also other similar locations: some with same objects but a different arrangement and some with the same arrangement of these objects. This design of the virtual environment ensured that children could not find the destination just by randomly searching for it. In order to solve the task, the children had to think spatially by using the objects, paths, and landmarks around. ## Data analysis

In the first step, we identified participants who fully completed the game and who solved the navigation-to-a-flag task correctly. This way, the “successful” NMR proc edures and strategies were accessed. They were of m ajor interest for this sub-study since our implications for GeoGame implementations focus on the training of children in successful NMR strategies. The think-aloud statements were filtered for expl anations with details on the procedure. Generic or general comments such as “I walked on the paths” or “I looked for benches and br idges” were ignored since they were not useful to understand the children’s learning. With the remaining 37 think-aloud statements, a con tent analysis was done by identifying and coding aspects describing the children’s procedures or strategies in solving the navigation-to-a-flag task. The statements were coded by two experimenters with the software MAXQDA. In line with the literature, the focus of the analysis was on routes, crossings, objects, and landmarks as  well as on mental rotation or perspective taking p rocesses. Based on the children’s statements, these categorie s were refined as described in the Results section.  After comparing the first analysis’ results among the exp erimenters, the codes were redefined to become clea rer and more distinctive. Both experimenters rechecked thei r coding with regard to these definitions and overl ooked aspects. Finally, the intercoder matching as well a s the number of participants assigned to each code-category were determined with the use of MAXQDA. In order to learn more about possible strategies behind the children’s statements, the aspects included in each statement were visualized with colors.

## Results

By analyzing the think-aloud statements, we found 1 3 different aspects that were mentioned by the chil dren in order to describe their NMR procedures or strategies in the navigation-to-a-flag task (see Figure 3). The intercoder matching (agreement) with regard to the existence of these aspects within the statements is 96.95 %. We distinguished between objects and crossings repr esenting the start location, the destination, as we ll as “stopover” locations (i.e. objects and crossings that were visited while navigating to the destination). Additionally, we coded the snack bar and the playground as “landm arks” since they were unique in the whole environme nt (instead, the telephone booths were coded as “start location”). However, some children mentioned that they used the playground as an orientation point from where t hey restarted the navigation to the flag. We coded this procedure with “new start location”. Objects that w ere near to the destination and mentioned for expla ining the solution, but not explicitly described as stopover locations, were coded as “adjacent objects”. Most children (23 of 37) used the destination objects “tree” and/or “trash can” for their explanation (1), 6 children mentioned the destination crossing. 13 to 14 participants listed stopover objects like flowers, bridges, ICLS2022 Proceedings 642 © ISLS trees, and benches and 5 used stopover crossings (“ I have oriented myself a bit on the crossings”). Adjacent objects like flowers, bridges, trees, and benches were mentioned by 17 and the landmark “playground” or “swing” was included in the statement of 6 children. 5 chil dren described that they restarted orientation from  the playground (“I went to the swing first, from there it was easier”). This way, they changed the view and movement direction from south to north which simplified the original task. Only 3 children mentioned the start location when describing their procedure.

13 children described how they followed paths and route sections towards the destination (“Then I looked at how I would have to run through the curves”). Some children described the relative arrangements and shapes as well as relative distances of objects and paths to each other: 17 to 19 children mentioned how obje cts, paths, and stopover locations were arranged with words lik e “left”, “right”, “behind”, and “between” as well as with terms describing shapes like “branching” or “edge”. 11 to 12 participants also mentioned the relative distance of objects to each other or to themselves with terms like “near” and “far” (“I had to go to the trash can and it was the only one near the bridge”).

5 children explained that they excluded routes or locations (“Then, I saw that there was a bench on th e bridge, so I didn't have to cross that bridge.”). 3 children were able to describe how he or she mentally rotated the map (“[I] turned it in the head to my perspective”) or changed the own perspective (“If I were in there”). The color-visualization (see Figure 4) gives insight in the combination of aspects within the statements. Individual colors and pattern were chosen for each aspect. The visualization shows no distinct groups in the way aspects were combined. Rather, the results show com binations that were highly individual. However, two main tendencies can be observed in the data. A first group (14 children) described their procedure in a dynamic way by mentioning stopover objects, crossings, and/or the routes they took without explaining how the destina tion is located with regard to adjacent objects or landmarks. The 3 children that were able to explain how they mentally rotated or changed their perspective belong to this dynamic group. A second group (12 children) explained their solution in a static way by describing objects and landmarks that are adjac ent to the destination. However, also explanations combining these two ways were observed. What additionally can be seen is that 3 of the 5 children that used the playground as their new start location mentioned neither the dynamic, nor the static way. One further child of these 5 children mentioned the travelled r oute (dynamic way) only very vague: “I then go to t he playground, then I know where I am, because the gam e started there. ## Discussion and suggestion for the design of a geogame

Children’s navigational map reading strategies Children differ in their procedure or strategy in how they solve navigation-to-a-flag tasks. In the specific task of this study, the children started at a landmark (telephone booths) and had to move in the southern direction to the destination. The destination was surrounded by objects that appeared also in many further places on the map. The nearest landmark was a playground which was several crossings away from the destination. Since it was no unique location, it was not possible for the children to find the destination just by accidently stumbling upon it. Therefore, we assume that the children’s statements on the cor rectly solved task inform us on successful procedures or strategies in navigating with a map towards a desti nation. However, we suppose that the statements rep resent elaborated and less elaborated ways in doing so.

ICLS2022 Proceedings 643 © ISLS For each procedure or strategy aspect, we determine d the number of participants mentioning it. The number gives us an idea on how aware a child was ab out the individual aspect as a part of his or her p rocedure. However, we cannot deduce from the statements whether the individual child did not consult further aspects. The number does not tell us how often children made use of individual aspects or if they were able to do so. Moreover, it is also conceivable that children noticed some a spects only afterwards, i.e. in retrospect. For fut ure work, we suggest to ask for explanations also directly in the situation in order to prevent that children forget or add aspects in retrospective. This concurrent think-aloud method could also clarify which aspects are relevant to children at different decision points. However, it is possible that this method induces strategic procedures that would not have arisen with a retrospective think-aloud method.

The results show that children’s successful NMR strategies incorporated the consideration of destination objects as well as adjacent objects and landmarks. The children included shapes, relative arrangements, and distances (closeness and farness) in their spatial analysis of the destination as well as the route towards it. Although the destination was distinguishable from other locations in the way the destination objects “tree” and “trash can” were arranged in relation to each other, the childr en did not explicitly mention this arrangement. The  children's statements rather give the impression that the exis tence of and relative distance to other objects as well as their own way towards the destination played a greater role in their decision-making process: whereas some children predominantly analyzed the destination on site (static way) by mentioning adjacent objects and landmarks, others remembered how they planned and repeatedly checked the route towards the destination (dynamic way). So me children described that they mentally rotated the map or mentally changed their own perspective to support their route planning. Other children mentioned that they crossed decision points on which they decided on or excluded routes. These decisions were based on the existence, arrangement, and shape of objects, landmarks, and paths. It would be interesting to investigate if the “static” group took longer routes towards the destination (and therefore was less efficient in solving the task) than the “d ynamic” group: could it be that the “static” group scanned the area in a trial-and-error way until they recognized the destination objects “tree” and “trash can” and verified their assumption with adjacent objects? Future work could  also focus on self-locations tasks that are create d to be solved on site: do children solve such tasks predominantly by foc using on adjacent objects or do some of them make use of their knowledge about their route towards the destination? In addition to the so far mentioned procedures and strategies, there was another successful way in solving the navigation-to-a-flag task: some children looked for another, already known orientation point at the bottom of the map to prevent a non-alignment of view direction and map. They reorientated from the playground which was the start location of the total game. Although the children were successful in solving the task (and m ay have reduced their error probability this way), such a procedure causes significantly longer routes and is therefore less efficient than other strategies. As shown above, it  occurred mainly isolated from the other procedures. Future work with a focus on children’s statements on incor rectly solved tasks could possibly further illumina te such inefficient strategies.

Suggestions for the design and conception of a geogame Based on our empirical findings described above, we developed a new concept for a geogame design. The focus is on geogame features that have the potential to represent or address successful procedures and strategies in NMR. Developing successful learning methods demands to understand the thinking of successful learners. The geogame as one possible methodic tool in geoscience education offer the potential to be adjusted to these educational needs. Educational geogames are often map-based games using classical street maps that are fixed to north and can be panned and zoomed. Panning and zooming are useful in NMR situations because they allow the user to get an overview of the environment as well as to plan the route based on start location, destination, and possible stopover locations. A north-oriented map activates the spatial thinking of the map reader when navigation is orientated to west, east, or south. But what is often missing in geogames is a customizability of the map in forms of a choice between automatic, manual, and no panning, zooming,  and rotation. Automatically or manually rotating maps instead of fixed maps, for example, are helpful to address mental rotation and perspective taking or to facilitate the spatial perception of object arrangements (see Figure 5a). As an alternative to map rotations, view direction markers – either presented initially or during the whole N MR process – can visualize mental rotations, perspectives, and spatial arrangements without changing the map view (see Figure 5b). Several educational geogames visualize the user’s l ocation and view direction. What is missing are customizable map marker settings. Location markers (see Figure 5c) can visualize start locations, dest inations, and routes towards the destination. The absence of a marker activates the map reader in his or her spatial thinking. Initial location marker can support in the initial self-location and can then handover this process back to the map reader by disappearing during navigation. Tracks in forms of lines marking the route walked (see Fi gure 5d) or ICLS2022 Proceedings 644 © ISLS highlighted streets representing the approximate location of the map re ader (see Figure 5e) are further features with the potential to address route planning and self-location during navigation. Point, line, or polygon markers  are useful features to emphasize objects, landmark s, routes, start locations, and destinations, as well as the shape of landmarks for orientation (see Figure 5f). A draw functionality (see Figure 5g) allowing the map reader to mark objects, structures, and routes during the navigation process on his or her own is a helpful feature for making use of map details that are relevant to the individual map reader. In order to draw attention to relative distances and arrangements between objects or objects and map reader, a buffer (see Figure 5h) can be used that draws a circle around a certain location with a given diameter. All these features are no technical innovations, but missing in most educational location-based games. Several educational geogames do not offer the option to change the map layer, but satellite pictures (see Figure 5i) and 3D maps (see Figure 5j) can help the  map reader to recognize objects and special shapes  of landmarks as well as to understand map visualizatio ns. A swipe functionality (see Figure 5i) allowing the map reader to successively change the street map into a satellite picture and back is a further supportive feature for this kind of applications.

Already existing geogames give rather simple feedba ck on task solutions, but location-based technologies offer the possibility to give detailed  feedback on task solution by highlighting deviatio n distances and angles (see Figure 5k), marking correct locations, view directions, and objects on the map (see Figure 5l) or giving custom messages on pre-defined errors. This is also missing in most educational geogames. Although a huge variety of educational geogames exist, the customizability and availability of features like markers and layers, feedback, and map draw functionalities is very uncommon. Therefore, we are currently ICLS2022 Proceedings 645 © ISLS equipping our location-based geogame GeoGami with the above-mentioned features: some features are alre ady implemented, others under development. Being intere sted in geoinformation technologies for school educ ation requires rethinking these tools from an educational perspective. NMR training studies must consider tools that are adjusted for its special use. In this paper, we sug gested enhancements for such tools based on an anal ysis of children’s successful NMR strategies.
