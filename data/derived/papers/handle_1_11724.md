# Playing at the Crossroads: Exploring Intersections of Research, Education, and Design in a Case of Musical Improvisation

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While research, education, and design involve distinct priorities and practices, they frequently intersect. Scholars and practitioners have called for further study of how to navigate these roles. This study presents a case of playtesting a game that supports musicians developing improvision skills. Through qualitative analysis based in design research and autoethnography, I elaborate the priorities and practices involved in navigating simultaneous roles of researcher, designer, and educator and highlight areas of confluence and tension.

## Background

The aims and activities of research, education, and design often intersect  and interact in complex and nonlinear ways (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Coordinating and navigating these roles requires strategies for coordinating the relevant activities within a common context (Edelson, 2002; Norman, 2013; Vakil et al., 2016). To examine those challenges, this paper presents a case that explores the process of designing a game for musicians to develop their improvisation skills. Through a series of playtesting sessions, I occupied the simultaneous roles of designer, music educator, and r esearcher as I facilitated gameplay and discussion. Using autoethnographic  and design-based methods, I explore how the practices and priorities of these roles combined and interacted in the course of activity. The practices of improvising are challenging to def ine and describe, and learning to improvise  is a complex, multidimensional, and highly variable  process (Ng, 2019).  These aspects create related challenges for music researchers, designers, and educators. Improvisation is difficult to analyze, as the practices of improvising cannot easily be examined either in advance or afte r the fact  (Sawyer, 2000). Designing for an activity that is intrinsically undetermined is far from straightforward (Biasutti, 2017), and music educators must guide novices in developing their own improvisational skills (Kra tus, 199 5). Moreover, these roles are often occupied simultaneously by a single practitioner  (Edelson, 2002; Ito et al., 2012). How practitioners balance and navigate these roles therefore merits further study.  By examining my own involvement in designing and facilitating the game, this study asks: how did these roles of educator, research, and design intersect, complement, or conflict? What approaches or strategies helped with navigating these simultaneous roles?

## Methods

This study focuses on the playtesting phase of game development, which addressed three broad aims: user testing the design, providing a workshop for musicians, and collecting data for research. This phase thereby acts as a case of balancing simultaneous roles as designer, educator, and researcher. In playtesting sessions, players alternated between gameplay and semi-structured focus groups (Parker & Tritter, 2007). Data consist of videos and transcripts from these sessions, along with  notes, documents, and memos. This analysis builds on design-based methods that emphasize documenting decision-making and tracing changes in activity  (Howard, 2011), as well as autoethnographic methods (Chang, 2016). Through this approach, I enumerate the practices and priorities of each role, with particular attention to areas of overlap or tension.

## Findings

These findings elaborate the priorities and practices associated with each role. I discuss how these priorities and practices complemented or conflicted and how I navigated interactions between them. Finally, I consider the priorities and practices involved in deliberately taking on and navigating all three roles simultaneously. As research, this project aimed to understand the in-the-moment mechanics of collaborative improvising and relate them to t he influence s of personal, social, and cultural histories. In the researcher role, I aimed to position musicians to demonstrate and articulate how they improvise, making the relevant practices and processes visible. In the improvisation segments, this involved noticing and documenting details that could offer insight into the two research aims. For instance, I wrote notes and marked the timestamps of moments that could constitute openings to raise areas of interest during the disc ussion. In the focus groups, I highlighted these moments as discussion points, prompting explanations or clarifications and confirming (or disconfirming) my interpretations. The designer role focused on  developing and refining a pedagogical structure for deve loping improvisational abilities. Key questions in this process included: how does this design shape users’ activity? Does it have the effects we expect ed, and why or why not? How can it be adapted to achi eve those effects more consistently? These conjectures can be understood as hypotheses a bout how users will respond to the design; in this sense, design aims and research questions inte rsected throughout the user testing process. During  the improvisations, this entailed noticing how musicians were responding to  the design. In the focus groups, these moments of interest served as  focal points for users to articulate the affordances and constraints  they perceived and their strategies for navigating them. Through this combination of prompting, iterating, and documenting, the design role complemented the research aspect by helping theorize how improvisation is learned-what Edelson

(2002) calls  “ knitting together a coherent theory ” (p. 106). Design f eedback, including participants’ verbal

accounts and the improvisations themselves, indicated how improvisation functions (e.g., core practices) and how tools and structures afford improvisational development (e.g., the effects of using a designed structure). From the educator perspective, this game aims to help musicians develop their ability to improvise. This goal entails having a corresponding theory of learning, which dovetails with the  design conjectures described above. For instance, the game as pedagogy builds from the idea that musicians learn from working within generative constraints; that theory of learning is embodied in the design and informs facilitation practices during gameplay. Similarly, the game operates on the premise that ma king tacit practices visible affords opportunities for further learning and reflection. The improvisation segments served to make practices  of improvising visible, which in turn enabled discussion moves such as orienting participants to specific details and drawing attention to themes and patterns (Coles, 2019; van Es et al., 2014). Each focus group discussion then transitioned into another round of gameplay, providing an opportunity to introduce recommendations or challenges. This iterative approach to the educator role aligned with the design principles of making iterative adjustments and improvements, as both teaching and design aims were served by engaging in  rapid cycles of planning, doing, and evaluating  (Louie et al., 2021; Norman, 2013). The educator role did evince some tensions, however, with the researcher role. As an educator, my aim was to support a generative experience for participants, i.e., to ensure that they learned from the game, which prompted interventions as a facilitator.  In contrast, a  research orientation often evokes principles like objectivity and non-interference to produce unbiased data. Navigating and balancing the roles  entailed an additional layer of priorities and practices, creating an additional “meta-role” around managing priorities.  While o rganizing prior to the sessions was essential for navigating these three roles, grounding the focus group  discussion in the concrete details of the improvised segments required the protocol to  adapt to emergent events. In one example,  I asked a question about the mechanics of setting a tempo, and the musician responded with a reference to genr e. That connection led into a conversation about childhood experiences listening to music with his family. Following this tangent provided an opening for discussing the role of intuition in improvising. Exchanges like these led me to reorganize my materials in a non-linear format to support shaping discussions in response to  the improvisation s. This example also highlights the importance of c apitalizing on opportunities to combine roles. Discussions of genre proved especially apropos for addressing multiple areas, as it  provided an opening to discuss how musicians’ past experiences and their social and cultural identitie s manifest in their improvising. In some interactions, the roles took on complementary or synergistic relationships,  with the activities of one role affording expanded opportunities for insight in others. For instance, introducing more challenging constraints during gam eplay illustrated the extent of musicians’ capabilities and made a wider range of practices visible, creating an opportunity for the research ele ment. At the same time, w orking through these adaptations also identified po tential design changes and helped demonstrate how the game was shaping musicians’ learning.

## Discussion

This case reveals several key practices that overlap or bridg e across the three roles. First, making intuitive and implicit processes visible  was an  essential practice that supported priorities in all areas. R esearch required musicians to actively improvise to access their obj ect of study, design needed to observe gameplay directly, and pedagogy built on  seeing and hearing learners’ practices in real time. Reifying and documenting  participants’ practices further supported participants’ learning  and furthered research and design goals. Capturing improvisations on video provided participants with access to their own in-the-moment practice in a form that afforded reflection and analysis. This reifying and documenting also allowed for closer study after the fact, advancing the design process (Cao & Bank, 2015) and acting as a key data source for research  (Coles, 2019). Finally, noticing also emerged as a central practice across areas. The construct of noticing, which encompasses what a practitioner attends to, how they interpret details, and how they decide to respond, is primarily associated with teaching (Mason, 2001), but is equally important in research (Spradley, 1979) and in design (Oxman, 2002). One central claim in the notici ng literature is that noticing is an active practic e that emerges from one’s professional vision (Goodwin, 1994).  In this case, each of the three roles entails its o wn variety of professional vision. This case also highlights the importance of noticing in integrating the three roles, interpreting moments of convergence as opportunities to combine roles and leverage synergies among them.
