# Vulnerability and Expertise in Intergenerational STEM: Teachers Negotiating Learner Identities in EMPWER

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This short paper examines how two high school science teachers responded to invitations to position themselves as learners within an intergenerational program focused on environmental justice and data literacy, called E MPWER. Drawing on Holland et al.’s (1998) notion of figured worlds and Goodwin’s (1997) concept of interactional expertise, we traced how teachers negotiated identity and expertise within EMPWER as science educators, learners, and community members. A comparative case analysis showed that both teachers struggled with and resisted learner identities in different ways throughout the year-long project; however, one teacher was able to author a learn er identity more consistently, while the other resisted learner positioning. Our findings speak to how teacher learning identities are shaped by prior figured worlds of school science and social expectations associated with expertise. We argue that intergenerational designs should support teacher vulnerability, share authority, and create conditions for purposeful partnership that build collective agency across generations.

Decades worth of research in the Learning Sciences have highlighted the importance of working in partnership with educators toward more equitable teaching and learning in science education (Penuel et al., 2022). However, very few projects engage teachers and students as co-learners, working toward complex community challenges (see Basu & Barton, 2010 for one example). In intergenerational STEM programs, teachers are invited to learn alongside youth, yet many bring with them school-based expectations of guiding and structuring inquiry. This creates a tension between authority and vulnerability that shapes who gets to participate as a knower or “expert” (Allen & Hill, 2024). This tension between leading and learning invites a closer look at how teachers navigate identity while co-creating knowledge with youth. We explore these tensions and identity navigations within a design-based participatory research project (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016), called EMPWER, which brought together high school science teachers and students as co-learners in a design-based, participatory program focused on air quality, health, and environmental equity. In particular, we explored the question: How do high school science teachers author identities in an intergenerational design-based learning environment? We drew on Holland et al.’s

(1998) concept of figured worlds and Goodwin’s (1997) notion of interactional expertise to trace how two science

teachers-Elizabeth and Joanna-positioned themselves, and were positioned by others, as learners and experts within the context of EMPWER. Both teachers navigated tensions between teaching and learning roles. Elizabeth appeared to author herself more frequently as a learner within EMPWER, while Joanna more often drew on familiar positioning from the figured world of school science. These patterns illustrate how teachers’ participation was shaped by prior figured worlds of schooling and the interactional norms that define who may claim expertise or learner status.

## Conceptual framework

Understanding how teachers’ author identities as learners requires attention to the social and cultural worlds in which those identities take shape. Drawing on Holland et al.’s (2001) concept of figured worlds, we view identity not as fixed but as produced through participation in culturally and historically situated spaces where people negotiate roles, values, and authority. Within these worlds, individuals position themselves and are positioned by others as experts, novices, or somewhere in between. We al so draw on Goodwin’s (1997) notion of interactional expertise to examine how knowing and not knowing are accomplished in practice. Expertise is not something people have, but something negotiated in interaction. What counts as “knowledgeable” emerges through participation and shared activity. Figured worlds help explain how teachers bring prior histories of participation in schooling into EMPWER while interactional expertise allows us to examine how authority and knowledge are negotiated in moment-to-moment interaction. Together, these perspectives frame identity and expertise as co-constructed in interaction and guide our analysis of how teachers navigated invitations to learn within EMPWER’s intergenerational design.

## Research design

We draw on ethnographic and case study methodologies situated within a participatory design research project (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Ethnographic methods allowed us to examine how participants’ interactions and ways of communicating shaped how they made sense of their experiences during E MPWER sessions and how they authored identities within this context.

## Research context

EMPWER was a year-long participatory design research program focused on environmental justice and data literacy, engaging high-school youth, teachers, and university researchers as co-learners. The program included an initial fall kickoff weekend, four additiona l day-long sessions (two in fall, two in spring), and a concluding community symposium. Across sessions, participants collaborated to build data-literacy and STEM-research practices connected to local issues of air quality, health, and social and environmental equity. The project was codesigned and facilitated by a university-based research team in partnership with community educators. The initial design was developed around shared concerns related to air quality, health, and environmental justice, and participant interviews and feedback informed ongoing iteration of the program. The broader study examined how EMPWER’s design cultivated community engagement and positioned participants as agentic, historical actors (Gutiérrez et al., 2019).

## Participants

Participants in EMPWER included teachers and students from two racially and economically diverse high schools located in areas affected by poor air quality and heavy traffic (see Allen & Hill, 2024). This paper focuses on two high-school science teachers-pseudonyms Elizabeth and Joanna-who participated across both semesters of the program. These teachers were selected as comparative cases because they illustrate variation in how learner identities were taken up within the same intergenerational program context. Data sources Data for this analysis include pre-program interviews (Spring 2023) and winter check-ins (Fall 2023-Winter 2024), each lasting 45-60 minutes. Interviews were conducted, audio-recorded, and transcribed verbatim. While the broader EMPWER  dataset also includes video recordings and fieldnotes from program sessions, this comparative case study draws primarily on teacher interview data to examine identity authoring over time.

## Analytic approach

Consistent with our theoretical framework, we treated discourse as a site where participants position themselves and are positioned by others (Holland et al., 1998; Goodwin, 1997). We began with low-inference codes identifying role language (teacher, learner, expert) that reflected how participants described themselves and others. We then coded for positioning (how authority and knowledge were distributed in talk), self-authoring (how teachers narrated participation), and interactional moves that displayed or deferred expertise within “role” moments. Two researchers independently coded all transcripts and reached consensus through discussion to refine category definitions. Analytic vignettes (Erickson, 1986) were then constructed to foreground moments when teachers’ positional identities shifted or were unsettled by what we term moments of vulnerability within ongoing interaction. These vignettes illustrate how epistemic authority was negotiated and how teachers authored identities across contexts. This comparative design allowed us to see how teachers’ negotiations of vulnerability and authority revealed the cultural and relational conditions that make authentic co-learning possible. The following analytic vignettes illustrate how two teachers navigated tensions between authority and learning within EMPWER’s intergenerational design.

To examine how teachers navigated tensions between expertise and learning within EMPWER, we present two analytic vignettes. These vignettes trace distinct yet connected identity paths: Elizabeth’s re-authoring of herself as both mentor and learner, and Joanna’s continued reliance on familiar school-based patterns of authority in her self-authoring.

Case 1: Elizabeth re-authoring learner identity through care and vulnerability Elizabeth appeared to take up a learner stance through the same care and advocacy she routinely brought to her teaching. From the start of E MPWER, she positioned herself as “not the expert.” In her pre-program interview, she anticipated the tension of shifting from teacher to learner, noting that EMPWER was not “her classroom” and 1608 that she was “not the default expert here.” Later, in a mid-winter check-in, she echoed this stance while learning new mapping software: “I don’t know how to use QGIS myself, but I did download it.” These moments suggest how she navigated her role between two figured worlds: the world of school, where teachers hold authority, and EMPWER’s collaborative world, where expertise was built through interaction (Goodwin, 1997). Her phrasing carried both discomfort and determination. Naming what she didn’t know and staying in that beginner space showed a willingness to participate without entitlement to authority. Moments like these suggested what Holland et al. (1998) describe as the ongoing re-positioning of self within and across figured worlds, where identity is continually authored through participation and recognition. Elizabeth approached learning as an extension of care, modeling persistence and humility through her willingness to “not know” publicly. When she said, “I don’t know how to use QGIS myself, but I did download it,” she suggested a readiness to keep engaging despite uncertainty. She also connected learning to her students, reflecting on wanting more time to “sit down with students and continue with it.” Across interviews, Elizabeth’s talk returned to care for her students: “Some of my kids are on the spectrum, or English learners, and people don’t want to help,” she explained. Later, she reflected, “If I just had the time to sit down with them and continue with it, that would be awesome.” In these moments, Elizabeth authored herself as a caring educator committed to supporting the students who, as she put it, others “don’t want  to help.” Her learner identity and teacher identity appeared closely connected. She approached learning as an extension of care, modeling persistence and humility. Even when she was unsure, she persisted in downloading new tools, experimenting on her own, and returning to sessions ready to keep learning. Her comments about wanting more time to “sit down with students and continue with it” showed a commitment to staying with the work, not just for her students  but for her own growth. In this way, Elizabeth authored herself as someone who chose to learn, treating uncertainty as part of participation rather than a barrier to it. Her participation suggests how care became a resource for negotiating expertise and belonging within EMPWER’s co-learning design.

Case 2: Joanna maintaining authority within the figured world of school science Joanna seemed most comfortable drawing on her established teacher identity and less frequently took up a learner stance during EMPWER. In her early winter interview, Joanna described E MPWER as “a useful experience for the kids,” adding that she would learn new data tools so she “could show the students how to do that.” Her talk suggested she was most comfortable learning when it was directly tied to her teaching practice and her students’ needs. She spoke about EMPWER as something she could bring back to her classroom rather than as a space for her own inquiry, keeping her focus on helping students rather than  experimenting herself. Joanna appeared to maintain a traditional teacher-student hierarchy, maintaining intellectual distance from the youth in the program. For example, while the interviewer used “we” when discussing next steps for the group, Joanna referred to “the Prairie kids” and “they,” keeping herself separate from the learner role. Within E MPWER’s figured world, her participation continued to center students as the primary learners, reflecting traditional power dynamics of school science.

Joanna also shared her discomfort with parts of E MPWER’s inquiry process. She recalled “hating” writing policy briefs for her own research, yet described that same work as “a useful experience for the Prairie kids.” This contrast suggests that she connected struggle and uncertainty to student learning, but not to her own growth. Within E MPWER, this stance appeared to maintain the boundaries of teacher expertise rather than reauthor them. As Goodwin (1997) reminds us, expertise is not something a person sim ply has, it is negotiated in the moment through shared practice. Joanna’s participation suggested that her sense of expertise remained rooted in the figured world of schooling, where competence and control define good teaching. At the same time, Joanna’s reflections revealed a deep care for her students and a commitment to their success. Her focus on what would be “useful for the kids” al igns with the moral commitments of teaching, even if it limits her opportunities to see herself as a learner. Rather than  resistance, her stance reflects a tension between worlds: between the accountability and expectations of school and the open, inquiry-based culture of EMPWER. Taken together, Joanna’s discourse shows how the social expectations of teaching shaped her participation and constrained how she could author herself as a learner. Within E MPWER, her identity remained anchored in the authority and responsibility of school-based expertise. Her case suggests how the cultural narratives of teaching about knowing, guiding, and protecting students can make vulnerability feel risky, even in intentionally co-designed, intergenerational spaces.

Cross case insight Both Elizabeth and Joanna participated in E MPWER as experienced science educators stepping into a design intentionally structured for shared learning across generations. Each encountered tensions between the figured world of school, where teachers are positioned as experts responsible for guiding others, and E MPWER’s 1609 collaborative figured world, where expertise was built through interaction (Goodwin, 1997) and distributed among youth, teachers, and researchers. Elizabeth appeared more willing to take up learner positioning within this new context. Her talk about “not being the default expert” and her openness to uncertainty illustrated a readiness to reauthor expertise through participation and care. Joanna, in contrast, often positioned herself through the discourse of facilitation and responsibility, aligning her participation with the authority and moral commitments of teaching. These patterns do not suggest that one teacher embraced learning while the other resisted it; rather, they reflect different ways of navigating the tension between authority and vulnerability within the cultural narratives of teaching. Both teachers’ participation suggests how deeply histories of schooling shape what counts as legitimate expertise in intergenerational spaces. For Elizabeth, care and empathy provided entry points for taking up learner roles. For Joanna, care was enacted through guidance and protection of students’ learning, which appeared to limit opportunities to author herself as a co-learner. In both cases, authority and vulnerability coexisted but were expressed through different discursive and relational practices. These insights suggest that supporting teachers’ participation as learners may require more than simply inviting them intonew roles. It requires designs that intentionally make space for teacher vulnerability as a valued part of participation and that reframe authority as a shared, relational practice. Within E MPWER, opportunities for teachers to engage as learners depended on how the design created safety for “not knowing” to be seen as productive rather than risky. This finding raises design questions central to the 2026 ICLS theme, Partnering with Purpose: How can intergenerational programs create the cultural and relational conditions for teachers and youth to share epistemic authority? What kinds of structures, discourse, and care practices make vulnerability a resource for collective inquiry rather than a threat to credibility? Together, these cases illustrate that teacher learning in intergenerational programs is not only cognitive but cultural, relational, and deeply tied to histories of practice and recognition. Teacher learning in intergenerational STEM programs is not just about acquiring new skills or tools; it’s about authoring new identities within cultural worlds that redefine who can know and how knowledge is shared. Within EMPWER, both teachers navigated tensions between the figured world of school, where authority and accountability shape participation, and a collaborative world where expertise was built through interaction (Goodwin, 1997). How each teacher positioned herself across these worlds highlights  the social and emotional complexity of learning to participate differently. Elizabeth’s willingness to take up a learner stance suggests how care and relational trust can become resources for re-authoring expertise. Naming “not knowing” did not necessarily diminish her authority; it created space for shared inquiry and co-learning. Joanna’s discourse, in contrast, reflected how school-based expectations of responsibility and guidance shaped her participation. She maintained a facilitator stance that preser ved authority but limited opportunities to author herself as a learner. Together, these cases  suggest that vulnerability and authority are not opposites but can be co-produced through interaction and recognition.

From a figured worlds perspective, participation in EMPWER required teachers to navigate overlapping identities as educators, learners, and community members, each with distinct norms and values. What counted as legitimate participation depended on how the program design created space for teachers to take up learner roles without losing credibility. From an interactional standpoint, moments of learning depended on how authority was negotiated in practice, including who asked questions, modeled uncertainty, and responded to others. Supporting teachers as learners means paying attention to the relationships, routines, and talk that make it feel safe to take risks. When programs build in time for shared inquiry, model what it looks like to not have all the answers, and recognize teachers as both educators and learners, they create space for authority to be shared across generations. Teachers’ histories in the figured world of school are not obstacles but resources; these histories carry commitments to care, responsibility, and student success that can anchor authentic co-learning. Ultimately, EMPWER shows how intergenerational programs can cultivate purposeful partnerships that honor teacher expertise while making space for shared uncertainty. When learning and authority are co-authored through participation, teachers and youth engage in the world-making work of redefining who is recognized as a knower in STEM. These insights connect to the 2026 ICLS theme, Partnering with Purpose, by showing that purposeful collaboration depends on trust, care, and a willingness to learn together. When programs center those values, teachers and youth can share expertise and build know ledge in more connected ways. While this paper focuses on comparative insights, future analyses will follow each teacher’s identity journey across EMPWER to see how vulnerability and authority change over time, a step toward the larger “story-arc” study envisioned for future ICLS work.
