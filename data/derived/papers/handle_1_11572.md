# Principles and Practices of Building Teacher Learning Communities

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster examines the design and structure of teacher learning communities as an alternative to traditional professional development (PD) models. Anchored in socio cultural theory and situated cognition, it draws insights from expert interviews to outline key principles of a teacher learning community: rootedness in practice, integration in school culture with flexibility, reliable support structures and sustainability mechanisms. This study contributes to the discourse on teacher learning highlighting the transformative potential of teacher learning communities.

## Introduction

With dynamic integration and growing discussion on the role and impact of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in teaching, educators must adapt to ever-changing demands to prepare students for rapidly evolving societies (OECD, 2011). In India, PD programs often follow a one-size-fits-all, lecture-based approach (Das et al., 2013), leaving teachers feeling unsupported or disengaged (Ministry of Huma n Resource Development, 2018).  PD programs should encourage collaborative problem solving for teachers and engage them in active learning over sustained durations of time (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017; Desimone, 2009). One such model that aligns with this purpose is learning communities. This poster provides insights into principles of shaping learning communities to promote continuous learning.

## Theoretical framework

With respect to teacher learning, learning sciences research falls into two broad categories: learning as social and distributed (Nathan & Sawyer, 2022; Sawyer, 2005) a nd learning as effective when situated in practice and authentic contexts (Engeström, 2022). The first cat egory draws from sociocultural theory and the theor y of distributed cognition. Based on Lev Vygotsky’s (1978) theories, knowledge begins as a distributed, situated social practice before becoming internalized as individual  mental thought (Nathan & Sawyer, 2022). In the con text of teachers, Shulman (1987) argued that teachers are members of communities of practice who develop shar ed understandings of teaching. This translates into either Communities of Practice  (CoPs) or Professional Learning Communities (PLCs). Compared to CoPs, PLCs are more  specific to educational settings, have stronger ex ternal leadership, and are more structured and deliberatel y arranged (Vangrieken, Grosemans, et al., 2017), whereas CoPs are more organic, interest-driven, and loosely coupled (Imants, 2003). The second category draws on theories of situated c ognition, which examine how learning is deeply influenced by the physical and social environment. These theories are heavily shaped by phenomenologic al philosophy, particularly its insight that knowledge  arises through direct perception of actionable pos sibilities in interaction with  the environment (Gibson, 1977). Aligning with this view, the term “situativity” suggests that knowledge is not a static mental construct but a dynamic process involving individuals, tools, social interactions, and the activities in which knowledge is applied (S awyer, 2022). Situative perspectives focus on the “ joint interactional accomplishments of individuals in rel ation to broader communities, processes, and struct ures” (Hand, 2010), such as how teachers make sense of lo calized problems of practice while  considering broader systems like schools and sociopolitical contexts that shape their meaning-making (Nasir & McKinney de Royston, 2013).

## Context and methodology

The study explores four learning communities: Learning community #1 is an online CoP supporting 400 high school mathematics teachers across 11 districts in a southern Indian state, integrating ed-tech modules with weekly online discussions, classroom visits, and expert we binars to nurture pedagogical discourse. Learning community

#2 is a face-to-face PLC of 50 teachers in four rural schools, structured ar ound term-based learning camps,

monthly discussions, school visits, and WhatsApp practice-sharing groups, making participation a mandatory part of the school calendar. Learning community #3 started with primary teachers in a single school and expanded to 12 schools at the block level, transitioning from f ace-to-face to virtual PLCs, with weekly learning circles, classroom observations, and monthly surveys, aiming  to empower teachers towards  autonomous learning. Learning community #4 focuses on a single school, using class room observations to identify challenges, facilitating structured PLC discussions during scho ol hours, encouraging one-on-one teacher interactions, maintaining a dedicated learning space and reflecti ve journals. Each learning community has a unique structure which has been designed with respect to the local context, teacher needs, time and nature of the facilitation team. This poster presents the findings of the first phase of the qualitative research study. Data for this phase is collected through semi-structured interviews  with four experts who h ave led and manag ed learning communities. Each interview is for 45-60 minutes and was administered online. A thematic analysis was conducted based on the research question “What are the guiding principles to build a teacher learning community?”

## Findings and discussion

The thematic analysis of the data led to the emergence of four principles essential to build a teacher learning community.

Principle 1: Teacher learning communities should be rooted in practice The experts emphasized the importance of grounding discussions in practice, either by providing evidence during discussions or by observing and reflecting on practices after community meetings and workshops. This connection makes the learning community relevant and meaningful. This principle directly aligns with situated cognition that is related to developing knowledge in relation to practice enunciating the importance of aligning all the learning activities, discussions and reflections to happenings in the classroom to amplify impact. Principle 2: Teacher learning communities should be integrated as a part of the school culture and be flexible The crucial aspect for teachers to be invested in l earning communities is the  integration of the community in school culture and schedule. The experts highlighted that while face to face PLCs is ideal, it is alsonecessary for the community to operate in a hybrid mode to give c omfort to the teachers. Flexibility should also be in terms of the facilitation where there is a balance of structured and open-ended conversations that accommodates space for teacher fundamentals, challenges and queries. This principle is related to the social and distrib uted nature of learning where the knowledge is not perceived as a separate entity to be provided at a special time. It is rather a process where it is jointly co-constituted with the community. Principle 3: Teacher learning communities should have reliable support structures and resources The experts note that their communities had support systems such as resource repository, one to one conversation, classroom observations, expert talks  and constructive feedback on teaching practices.  Apart from th ese, they established formal communication channels which served as a space to clarify doubts and share solut ions. This equipped teachers to explore independently, experim ent with the resources available at their own pace and share it with the peers. The situativity in this principl e exposes the importance of contextual resources essential for the learning community to thrive and flourish.  It emphasizes on the concept that kno wing is a process that involves tools, environment and other people.

Principle 4: Teacher learning communities should include mechanisms for sustainability The experts highlighted that sustaining a learning community requires either careful planning from the  outset or the integration of the facilitation team within the  internal community.  However, when facilitators are external, identifying key resource persons to lead the commun ity can be challenging. In learning community #3, teachers took initiative through autonomous projects, creating a sense of ownership that allowed the community to thrive even after the facilitation team exited. In contras t, in learning community #2 and #4, the facilitation team was already embedded within the school, eliminating the need for additional sustainability planning. With the concept of PLCs drawing from learning organization theory, it is crucial for the external stakeholders to build a learning culture embedded with the principles and people as they transition out of the school.

## Conclusion and way forward

The four principles presented serve as a beginner’s  guide and cannot be treated as an exhaustive list as more principles might evolve when other learning communities are explored.  In conclusion, the structure of the learning community needs to be relevant, flexible, supportive and sustainable. Moving forward, the next phase of the study aims to establish a PLC at school level to analyze the impact and effectiveness of learning communities on teaching practices and teacher beliefs on literacy development.
