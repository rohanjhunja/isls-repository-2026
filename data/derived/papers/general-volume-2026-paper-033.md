Collaboration is multimodal in nature, and members’ engagement is embodied in multimodal interactions. Therefore, although verbal interaction has remained a primary focus, nonverbal interactions should be considered in analyzing collaboration (Enyedy & Stevens, 2022; Ochoa, 2022). Building on this background, Multimodal collaboration analytics (MMCA) has been attracting more attention in Learning Analytics and CSCL (Esterhazy et al., 2025; Schneider, 2023), with advancements in pedagogical/practical, technological, methodological, and theoretical aspects (Esterhazy et al., 2025). Esterhazy et al. (2025) categorized multimodality into five types: namely, auditory, visual, tactile, physiological, and kinesthetic. According to Esterhazy et al. (2025), auditory and visual modalities have often been analyzed; however, the kinesthetic modality has received relatively less attention. Kinesthetic modality represents body movements like gesturing or interacting with material artifacts. It potentially sheds light on embodied engagement in collaboration, fostering deeper understandings of classroom orchestration, social regulation, and problem-solving (Prieto et al., 2016; Li et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2023). Previous studies have measured the amount of kinesthetic activity (body movements) to capture the embodied engagement in co-located collaboration. Several studies utilized accelerometer sensors to capture physical engagement with material artifacts in nursing simulation by calculating the intensity of body movement; in other words, the amount of activity (Buckingham Shum et al., 2019; Echeverria et al., 2019; MartinezMaldonado et al., 2020). CPR activity performed on a manikin was detected from acceleration data collected by a wristband. Studies suggest that it is possible to estimate the embodied engagement based on acceleration data. However, previous studies have mostly focused on nursing simulation settings, and theoretical and methodological challenges remain. Within co-located collaboration, members may interact with various material artifacts, such as computers and whiteboards. Theoretically, accelerometer data are potentially capable of capturing diverse forms of embodied engagement in collaboration, not only in nursing simulation. Moreover, from a methodological perspective, the pattern mining technique might enable us to gain further understanding of embodied engagement in collaboration. Previous studies have shown that pattern mining of the accelerometer data is useful for human activity recognition (Bijalwan et al., 2022; Matsubara et al., 2014). Examining not only the amount of activity but also the patterns of activity may reveal nuanced differences in embodied engagement. In this demo, we explore the potential of pattern mining of accelerometer data in co-located collaborative problem-solving. In the following section, we first describe the multimodal sensor badge that we developed. We then present our analysis pipeline for examining the patterns of kinesthetic activity. Finally, through a case study, we demonstrate that the proposed approach can capture nuanced differences in embodied engagement. Embodied engagement analysis with kinesthetic modality data We developed a multimodal sensor badge, named Sensor-based Regulation Profiler (SRP) badge (Yamaguchi et al., 2022), to capture embodied process in co-located collaboration (Figure 1). The SRP badge is designed to c apture the embodied engagement, equipped with a triaxial accelerometer (ACC) that collects ACC data in 12 bits at 100 Hz. It also incorporates an infrared LED to detect face-to-face conditions and a sound pressure sensor to capture speech activity, but they are not explained in this demo. Figure 2 illustrates our analysis pipeline. Based on the frameworks of Esterhazy et al. (2025) and Ochoa (2022), we explain the analysis targets, modalities, sensors, and analytical procedures. To capture the digital traces of embodied engagement, SRP badges are attached to participants’ chests and arms, and kinesthetic modality data are collected through the ACC sensors on the badges. From the ACC data, we extract two indicators: the amount and the patterns of kinesthetic activity. The amount of activity is analyzed using moving-average smoothing, and the patterns of activity are analyzed using a pattern-mining algorithm. The following sections describe each of these in detail.

The amount of activity is analyzed using moving-average smoothing, which is a commonly used conventional approach. The ACC collects triaxial (x-, y-, and z-axis) data (i.e., 3D vectors) at 100 Hz, and the instant amount of activity is calculated by the L2 norm (i.e., magnitude of the vector). Here, applying a moving average to the ACC data can reduce instantaneous noise and enable more2 appro2priat2e capture of embodied engagement (Echeverria et al., 2019). The patterns of activity are analyzed using the AutoPlait pattern-mining algorithm (Matsubara et al., 2014). AutoPlait discovers temporal patterns through unsupervised learning, enabling pattern detection and segmentation within large-scale time-series data (e.g., ACC-based motion-capture data). While the L2 norm (i.e., the amount of activity) captures only the magnitude of the ACC data, pattern mining considers both the magnitude and the direction of the ACC data. This potentially enables the detection of nuanced differences in embodied engagement by capturing subtle changes in body posture and movement. ## Contexts and participants

Eight groups of 3 undergraduates (9 female, 15 male) participated in a 60-minute collaborative problem-solving (CPS) activity. Participants were asked to design a creative system or application to improve the user experience of university students. During the discussion, scaffolding for productive CPS was provided as needed. Participants were provided a computer and a whiteboard (Figure 3). Participants were free to access any generative AI tools. ## Method

CPS sessions of eight groups were recorded and transcribed. CPS performance was assessed with the creativity framework (Runco & Jaeger, 2012). The SRP badges were attached to participants’ chests and arms to capture kinesthetic modality data (i.e., ACC data). After preprocessing the collected ACC data to remove missing values and outliers, we extracted the amount and the patterns of activity: the amount of activity was calculated through moving-average smoothing, and the patterns of activity through pattern mining. In this demo, we present two cases from high-and low-performing groups, focusing on the interpretation of the indicators and video analysis. The results suggest that in the high-performing group, the relatively calm member engaged in small but important collective activities, whereas the inactive member in the low-performing group barely engaged in the activities. The results of the moving-average smoothing analysis suggest that, in both the high-and low-performing groups, one member had a stable amount of activity, while the others had a large variance. Figure 4 illustrates the amount of activity based on the ACC data of participants’ chest, right arm, and left arm. Figure 4(a) suggests that member A was more stable than the others. Video analysis tells us that member A mostly engaged in computer operations, while members B and C were more actively writing on the whiteboard and moving their arms for gestures. Thus, the amount of activity suggests that Member A engaged less in dynamic kinesthetic activities such as writing or gesturing. As well as member A in the high-performing group, member D in the low-performing group also demonstrated stable kinesthetic activity, indicating less dynamic movement during the CPS session. The video recording suggests that member D mostly remained standing and did not engage in activities such as writing, gesturing, or computer operations. However, the amount of activity, one of the conventional indicators, cannot distinguish the different types of engagement exhibited by Members A and D by itself. These activities are small enough to be overlooked, yet pattern mining candetect these nuanced differences. Member D, on the other hand, had only one segment of kinesthetic activity. This result aligns with the video analysis that member D was simply standing and watching the whiteboard or the screen, which implies less engagement by member D. Although member C also had only one segment, the amount of activity and video recording suggests consistent and dynamic activity, such as pointing and gesturing. In this way, combining the information of the patterns of activity with the conventional indicator, the amount of activity, enables the extraction of nuanced but meaningful insights that cannot be identified solely from activity intensity.

Through the case study, the combination of two indicators, the amount and patterns of kinesthetic activity, revealed nuanced differences in embodied engagement. Although both members A and D showed low amounts of activity, member A engaged in calm but important activities, whereas the inactive engagement of member D. Our analysis pipeline provides both theoretical and methodological contributions to MMCA research. From a theoretical perspective, it extends the understanding of embodied engagement beyond nursing simulation. Further analysis of different kinesthetic patterns will deepen the insights of cognitive/socio-emotional interactions, potentially opening the discussion on actionable analytics for orchestration (Amarasinghe et al., 2024) and social regulation (Li et al., 2024). From a methodological perspective, it offers an interpretable MMCA approach based on pattern mining that can capture nuanced differences in kinesthetic activity. We argue that the proposed approach can indicate cues of learners’ embodied engagement and can support further in-depth qualitative analysis. A limitation remains. Despite recent attention to the integration of multimodal datasets (e.g., Buckingham Shum, 2019), our analysis focused solely on the kinesthetic modality. Further research is required to combine the kinesthetic modality with other modalities, such as visual, auditory, and tactile, in collaboration. Interactive tools and demos In this interactive demo session, we demonstrate the entire analysis pipeline, from data collection to data analysis. Participants will first experience data collection wearing the multimodal sensor badge, the SRP badge. We will then provide the case-study datasets and explain how to interpret the plots of the amount and patterns of kinesthetic activity, including video analysis. Through this session, participants will learn how to extract meaningful insights from kinesthetic modality data using wearable sensors, by iterative analysis of quantitative plots and qualitative video recording: differences in embodied engagement within and between members and groups will be explored.
