# Exploring the Role of Boundary Infrastructures in Facilitating Learning in a Research-Practice Partnership

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how boundary infrastructures in Research-Practice Partnerships (RPPs) facilitate learning and collaboration between a rural school district and two universities. Using the Learning at the Boundaries framework, we analyze design team meetings, teacher feedback, and co-designed curricular materials. Our findings highlight the roles of boundary spanners, practices, and objects in bridging gaps among stakeholders and fostering sustainable collaboration. This research contributes to the learning sciences by documenting effective processes for organizational learning, emphasizing the importance of structured co-design in educational improvement.

## Introduction

Research-Practice Partnerships (RPPs) are increasingly recog nized by the learning sciences community as an important part of the educational ecosystem for linking research, policy, and practice while addressing persistent problems of practice (Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Henrick et al., 2017). Yet despite the growing research on the promise of RPPs, the mechanisms that enable successful partnerships often remains unclear (Farrell et al., 2022). In response to the need for shared understanding of the working structures of RPPs, Farrell et al. (2022) developed a conceptual framework for partnerships to identify these processes. The authors call for future research that applies and tests this framework in order to contribute to the learning sciences community’s collective knowledge on whether and under what conditions RPPs can foster organizational learning for those involved, and with what consequences for longer-term impact (Farrell et al., 2022).

In this paper, we respond to this call by applying Farrell et al.’s framework (2022) to an RPP between two universities and a rural school district in the U.S. to investigate the processes and mechanisms that led to educational improvement; in other words, how we mad e it happen. Specifically, we focus on the boundary infrastructure and ask, what role does the boundary infrastructure play in creating o pportunities for learning ? Our analysis contributes to the learning sciences c ommunity's understanding of sustainable research-practice collaboration by documenting specific processes tha t support knowledge sharing and learning across and within institutional boundaries. This work advances the fi eld's collective knowledge about the conditions und er which RPPs can foster organizational learning and achieve  longer-term impact, while directly addressing the conference's focus on building meaningful partnerships between researchers and practitioners. Theoretical framing Our work is situated  within the Learning at the Boundaries of Research and Practice  framework (Farrell et al., 2022). This framework leverages both sociocultural and organizational theories of learning to propose a theory of action for RPPs to share fundamental information about how the partnerships work and the processes that underly it to foster organizational learning  (Farrell et al., 2022). The framework posits that learning can occur at the boundaries where research and practice intersect, facilitated by both the existing boundary infrastructure and preexisting organizational capacities, leading to the integrati on of new knowledge into organizational practices (Farrell et al., 2022). The boundary infrastructure is made up of three components: boundary spanners (individuals who move across boundaries), boundary practices (partnership activities bringing together multiple participants), and boundary objects (artifacts and tools created by the partnership to support joint activity). In the context of this design RPP, boundary spanners include researchers who facilitate co-design sessions or district personnel who advocate for the project. Boundary practices include activities such as co-design meetings. Boundary o bjects consist of artifacts such as co-designed curricular unit or tools, that help align different perspectives and practices (Farrell et al., 2022). Boundary objects play a crucial role in RPPs by facilitating collaboration between educators and researchers.

The effectiveness of this boundary infrastructure is influenced by organizati onal conditions including relevant prior knowledge, internal communication pa thways, resource mobilization, and strategic knowle dge leadership (Farrell et al., 2022).  Evidence of organizational learning includes seeing  changes in the RPPs collective knowledge, policies, and routines. ## Methods and data

This project is situated within the context of an RPP between a rural serving school district and two  universities, called the Design Code Math RPP  (see Table 1), centered around the district’s problem of practice: the need to provide high quality, equitable  computer science  (CS) instruction at the elementary school level  (Beck et al., 2024). The school district context, like many other public-school districts, has limited resources and instructional time, with few elementary educators who have the requisite backgrounds to teach CS. In this district, CS is taught by paraprofessionals, referred to as computer lab specialists, as an additional class students attend in the computer lab. Students attend the CS class once per week for roughly 40 minutes of instruction, which includes  learning both keyboarding and programming skills.

To address the problem of practice, the project leaders assembled a design team consisting of the District Elementary Math Specialist, the District CS Specialist, two fifth grade math teachers, three  computer lab specialists (CLS), four PIs, and three graduate research  assistants (GRA).  The design team me ets five times a year to design math lessons that integrated CS and CS lessons that integrated math at the fifth-grade level and discuss feedback (Shehzad et al., 2023). At the start of the project, the math teachers identified topics that their fifth-grade students struggle with, including exponents, fractions, and quadrilaterals. Based on the curricular pacing, exponents were selected as a focus for the first Math-CS integrated lesson. The present study focuses on the development and subsequent revisions of the integrated lessons focused on Exponents. ## Results

Boundary infrastructure is the network of people (spanners), practices, and objects that are necessary for the RPP to make prog ress. In the present study, we identified the D istrict Math specialist as a key boundary spanner who helped facilitate the co-design and iterative refinement of the integrated lessons. The design team identified “math routines”  (short mathematics lessons) as a key and familiar classroom routine that could serve as boundary infrastructure for the math integrated CS lessons. In the context of the math routine, the design team developed visualizations in Scratch (see Figure 2), which served as boundary objects that crossed the CS and math classroom. In this way, the  boundary objects and processes helped make design team members’ practice and prior knowledge visible, cont ributing to shifts in the groups collective knowled ge of how math was conceived in terms of CS integration.

Figure 2 Scratch Visualization of Repeated Addition Versus Repeated Multiplication The visualizations in Scratch of repeated addition and repeated multiplication in the same program became boundary objects. The co-design of the visualizations required collaboration  across all stakeholders and served as a coordination function across the design  team and mediation within the design team (Farrell et al., 2022). The math teachers and District math specialist brought their expertise of the math content, the terminology, and the best way to integrate them into existing le ssons. The CLS and District CS specialist brought t heir knowledge and expertise of teaching Scratch in fift h grade. The university partners brought their know ledge of programming and design.  Commenting on the design p rocess, one of the math teachers said: “ when we came together, we would bounce things off each other. And there would be different ideas from and then they  would spark ideas …what we're coming up with is a fantastic tool.” The boundary practices  created routines for the design team to engage in design, share feedback, and discuss modifications to the lessons and visualizations. The visualizations served different functions for different stakeholders. For the math teachers, the visualizations in Scratch represented a new way to teach exponents. One math teacher said, “w e don't say remember; exponents are just repeated multiplication. At least we didn't. But now we do.” They provided language to talk about multiplication as repeated addition and exponents as repeated multiplication. Further, they are tools to better support student learning of a challenging topic. For the CLS, they helped them make connections between math and coding. For the District Math specialist, they were a resource the teachers were comfortable using, saw value in, and found easy to integrate into existing  lessons. For the District CS specialist, they were  a t ool for integrating CS more deeply.  For the researchers, the visualizations were a way to engage with educator practice. Finally, for teachers, the integrated exponent lesson required them to “look at how we teach exponents.” Reflecting on their experiences at the boundaries, they expressed that they would never teach exponent s in the same way again. In terms of the Design Team, the vi sualizations served as shared reference points, facilitating communication, encouraging collaboration, and foste ring a shared understanding of the integration of math and CS (Farrell et al., 2022).

## Conclusion

This study illustrates how boundary infrastructures, framed by the Learning at the Boundaries framewor k, facilitate collaboration and learning in Research-Practice Partnerships (RPPs). Our findings reveal that boundary spanners, practices, and objects play pivotal roles  in bridging the gaps between research and practice, thereby fostering educational improvements. By documenting these processes, we contribute to a deeper understa nding of how RPPs can effectively support learning across  diverse stakeholders. This research not only reinforces the theoretical underpinnings of the Learning at the Bo undaries framework but also provides actionable ins ights for educators and policymakers aiming to enhance collaborative efforts in education. Future studies should investigate the application of these principles in various contexts to further enrich the discourse on effective research-practice collaboration.
