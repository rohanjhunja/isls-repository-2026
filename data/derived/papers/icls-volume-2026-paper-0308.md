# Performing sustainability in construction through 3D printing with clay

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The construction industry produces around 21% of global greenhouse gas emissions and faces major decarbonization challenges. Digital fabrication methods such as robotic clay extrusion offer opportunities to support sustainable construction and learning. However, little is known about how young people engage with sustainability through such technologies. This qualitative video-based study, grounded in constructionist and socio-material perspectives, examined how youth aged 11–17 explored sustainability in construction using clay and 3D pen extrusion during an after-school course. Findings show that clay enabled learners to engage with multiple aspects of sustainability, supported by its material properties, particularly the smooth clay-water mixtures central to digital design and extrusion. In contrast, 3D pen extrusion mainly fostered rapid prototyping and early planning. The performative, material qualities of clay encouraged deep physical exploration, suggesting that introducing digital clay extrusion can cultivate a material-driven understanding of sustainability in construction from an early age. The need for learning about sustainable construction The construction industry, which accounts for approximately 21% of global greenhouse gas emissions, faces a significant challenge in meeting the 2030 decarbonization targets. This highlights a critical disparity between current emission rates and the neces sary reductions required to mitigate climate change (United Nations Environment Programme, 2024). Encouragingly, emerging digital construction technologies, such as robotic 3D printing with innovative earth and clay materials and optimized architectural design, offer promise for enhancing sustainability within the sector. These advancements offer solutions to reduce environmental footprints and can reshape climate impacts (Dörfler et al., 2024; Agnusdei & Del Prete, 2022). However, technology innovation alone is not sufficient. To lead the sustainability charge, there is a need to prepare children and young people to tackle the pressing challenges of climate change (UN DESA, 2024) and to provide young people with early experiences to build with the innovative technologies and techniques that promise to advance sustainability in construction (Construction Blueprint, 2023). However, there is a substantial knowledge gap about how young people can learn about how new construction technologies (e.g., digitally controlled robotic extrusion with clay and earth materials) advance sustainable building processes, effective digital designs, and efficient material mixtures. Thus, we asked: What sustainability in construction concepts do young people perform through robotic 3D printing, and how? To address this research question, we adopted a constructionist approach to learning (Papert, 1993) and posthumanist perspectives (Keune, 2022) and were guided by architectural theory (Bredella, 2022) and 3D printing construction approaches (Dörfler et al., 2024). Building on these perspectives, we designed and facilitated a 9-week course for young people to observe and control a robotic clay print and create digital 3D models that they could then physically extrude using plastic (PLA) materials with 3D pens and clay through hand-coiling clay. We analyzed video-recorded interactions among youth regarding sustainability in construction concepts directly driven by 3D printing (e.g., geometry optimization, planning labor use, planning construction sequences, on-site fabrication, and material use optimization). The findings show that different materials shape how young learners perform sustainability in construction. Clay, with its adaptable and reusable properties, enabled engagement with all sustainability in c onstruction concepts. In contrast, PLA filament used in 3D pens primarily supported rapid prototyping and early-stage planning. These differences highlight how clay fosters deeper material exploration and understanding of sustainability in construction compared to PLA-based fabrication. These findings have implications both for the design of educational activities in sustainable construction and for understanding how learning processes can be structured to foster conceptual understanding in architecture and construction. ## Theoretical background

Innovative technologies in construction, such as robotic clay printing, depend on closely linked material and technical processes, often requiring custom-developed material mixtures to achieve consistent extrusion (Dörfler et al., 2024). 3D printing construction technologies are expected to impact the construction lifecycle and promote 2031 sustainability through approaches like on-site printing and optimized labor planning (Agnusdei & Del Prete, 2022). Facilitating aspects of a sustainable construction future, made possible through digital and robotic printing, in educational settings, has implications for equipping young people with core knowledge to tackle global crises. Empirical research on 3D printing in construction informed the design of learning opportunities introducing youth to its processes and implications using age-appropriate, safe materials (i.e., clay instead of cement extrusion). Socio-material approaches to learning about sustainability in construction Our approach to learning is grounded in constructionist theories (Papert, 1993) and socio-material perspectives (Keune & Peppler, 2019). Constructionism asserts that learners internalize complex concepts through the active manipulation of digital and tangible materials toward sharable designs (Papert, 1993). This perspective led us to focus on how conceptual knowledge related to sustainable construction is performed through design with construction materials. Sustainability is enacted as learners organize, plan, and adapt their design decisions. We also built on socio-material approaches to learning, foregrounding how knowledge emerges from entanglements among people, tools, materials, and environments (Keune & Peppler, 2019; Kumpulainen & Kajamaa, 2019; Peppler & Thompson, 2024; Seitamaa-Hakkarainen et al., 2010). These perspectives highlight that learning is coproduced through the properties of the materials. Their weight, resistance, and unpredictability play an active role in learning. Guided by material syntonicity (Keune, 2022), which suggests that attuning to the different properties of materials directs what can be learned, we attend to the particular properties of two construction materials, clay as a promising sustainable resource and PLA as prolifically available throughout educational settings, wondering whether some materials highlight different aspects of performing sustainability over others. Following this trajectory, we were also inspired by socio-material perspectives in architecture and design (Bredella, 2022; Dörfler et al., 2024), which highlight that digital and physical processes are inseparable and produce new material processes that, together, can advance sustainable construction. This perspective directed us to consider construction sites, whether physical, digital, or hybrid, as spaces where learners engage directly with materials, robotic processes, and design tools, experiencing how material properties, temporal rhythms, and collaborative interactions co-produce knowledge about susta inable construction practices. This outlook sees material engagement as the means through which sustainability concepts emerge in 3D printing.

To address the research question, we designed and facilitated a 9-week after-school course at an international school in southern Germany (90 minutes per session) for 15 students (3 girls, 12 boys), aged 11– 17, organized into five groups of three. The course focused on sustainable construction through digital clay PLA extrusion. The course combined robotics, architecture, and engineering through hands-on material explorations using digital fabrication techniques, while participants designed and 3D-printed projects. Activities were structured to integrate digital and tangible aspects of construction to perform key sustainability practices that emerge from the interplay of clay materiality, extrusion processes, and the design of digital geometry for 3D printing (e.g., mixing clay to ensure extrudable structures that stand up). Fabrication tasks included modeling in TinkerCAD, working with 3D pens, hand-coiling, and using piping bags for extrusion, exploring both low-and high-tech extrusion tools to create school buildings. Approximately 65 hours of video data were collected, with each group recorded by a dedicated camera that captured detailed interactions with materials, the iterative design of group projects, and participants’ verbal utterances about their design process and understanding of sustainability, often prompted by facilitators. For analysis, we applied a coding scheme of 16 sustainability in construction learning outcomes (Keune et al., 2025). The coding scheme was developed through iterative cycles, including interviews and two workshops with architects and construction engineers (Keune & Simšič, in progress). It was used to examine how robotic clay extrusion fosters sustainability in construction practices. The codes capture aspects such asgeometry optimization (designing structures that minimize material use while maintaining stability), planning labor use (assigning roles and tasks to maximize efficiency and effectiveness in the construction process), planning construction sequences (arranging construction tasks in a logical order to improve efficiency), on-site fabrication (adjusting and building directly on-site in response to real-time needs), and material use optimization (minimizing waste and maximizing material efficiency during planning). Two raters iteratively coded the video data to identify moments when these outcomes were performed in design. The analysis showed how participants’ experimentation (e.g., adjusting extrusion, refining geometry) linked fabrication to understanding sustainability in construction. All participants engaged with both PLA and clay across the workshop activities, and similar learning processes appeared across groups. However, across all groups, both materials elicited markedly different performance of sustainability in construction. Cla y drove participants to perform and talk about all sixteen sustainability in 2032 construction outcomes, while PLA produced six. This difference was related to the materials’ distinct properties and the kinds of fabrication processes they required, making certain sustainability outcomes performable, discussable, and learnable or not. For this paper, we present excerpts from two representative groups to illustrate the material differences in relation to five sustainability in construction outcomes: Two supported by both materials (i.e., geometry optimization, planning labor use), two supported by clay only (i.e., planning construction sequences, on-site fabrication), and one for which PLA demonstrated potential when additional material diversity was provided (i.e., material use optimization). The first group (Gemma, Sadie, Stella) learned with PLA and 3D pens, while the second (Steven, Dylan, Adam) explored sustainability with clay-based fabrication. Geometry optimization, which aims to design structures that minimize material use while maintaining stability, was performed using PLA and clay. With PLA extrusion using 3D pens, Gemma, Sadie, and Stella engaged in rapid, iterative prototyping, enabled by  the material’s lightweight, quick-drying properties. In Figure 1, Gemma sketched while Sadie managed the printing and Stella adjusted the connections. Moving between sketching, printing, and testing transformed their abstract reasoning about efficiency into tangible experimentation. As they discussed how to improve connections and shapes, Stella suggested, “You have to make four triangles and connect them together,”  reflecting an emerging understanding of geometry optimization. The shift from PLA to clay revealed how different materials afford distinct ways of understanding geometry optimization. Steven, Dylan, and Adam molded forms such as a pyramid, a cylinder, and a sphere (Figure 2), testing the objects’ balance and load-bearing capacity by hand, for example, by pressing down on the shapes to see how the clay deformed or by throwing them onto the board to test their limits. The weight and malleability of clay turned stability into a physical problem, something to feel, adjust, and negotiate with the material. With PLA, youth distributed roles related to the digital design workflow. For instance, Gemma sketched, Sadie printed, and Stella coordinated (Figure 1). Their labor planning reflected a digital production logic where parallel processes and coordination are central to efficiency. With clay, labor planning emerged dynamically and responsively. For instance, Steven, Dylan, and Adam negotiated responsibilities in real time as material demands emerged. While kneading and preparing clay, Adam suggested, “Let’s make a plan. What do we want to make first of all?”  Later, he proposed, “I suggest we make a base structure and someone designs a little bit of a roof...”  Their coordination followed the rhythm of the clay process (i.e., kneading, coiling, drying). This approach reflected how construction teams often respond to material behavior and environmental conditions rather than adhering to rigid plans. 2033 Planning construction sequences, which refers to arranging construction tasks in a logical order to improve efficiency and reduce labor, material, and time, was only supported by clay. The slow, stepwise process of coil building required participants to anticipate, adjust, and plan transitions carefully. Adam’s question, “Around that thickness?”  initiated a discussion about the base’s dimensions, setting a reference for subsequent coils (Figure 3). The group’s awareness of sequence and consistency arose from clay’s drying times and layering, making construction planning necessary, performable, and learnable.  In contrast, PLA hardened instantly, removing sequencing and making construction planning less visible. For instance, Steven and Adam compared coil thickness, debating whether to add reinforcement: “Just compare it, if it’s in similar thickness… Or are we layering it inside, double layering it?”  (Figure 3 Their fabrication decisions were guided by the clay’s state. Its variability demanded situational judgment, closely resembling full-scale 3D printing where builders adapt to changing material or  environmental conditions. PLA’s precision enabled clean outcomes but limited improvisation and learning through adaptation, needing less sensory input.

Material use optimization, which refers to minimizing waste and maximizing material efficiency during planning, revealed that some sustainability concepts performed with clay could also be produced with PLA when we introduced additional material diversity. For instance, Gemma, Sadie, and Stella had two different 3D pens with different PLA types: One rigid and one flexible. To decide which would best support their structure, Sadie reasoned, “This one is hard material. And mine is softer, so we can see which one we should use. ‘Cause we can’t use both of them.”  This demonstrated an understanding that material compatibility and efficiency are integral to sustainable design, as they selected the filament type that met structural needs without wasting resources. However, this reflection only happened when multiple PLA types were available. In contrast, clay made optimization self-evident. Material use optimization emerged directly through the process. For example, when Dylan asked, “Do we need that much clay here ?” and later noted, “Because it is coiled, so it is hollow on the inside.” The group recognized that their method (i.e., coiling rather than slab construction) minimized material use. This realization showed how embodied interaction with clay fostered intuitive reflection on material efficiency, as participants adjusted their plans to prevent overproduction and waste. The coiling method physically drove sustainability in construction by producing hollow, structurally efficient forms. PLA supported reflection only when prompted to compare densities, nozzle sizes, or settings, making it taught rather than material-driven. The research showed that learning sustainability in construction is inseparable from the materials. Materials do not simply illustrate sustainability outcomes; they shape what can be learned and how. PLA enabled engagement with six sustainability in construction concepts, primarily those related to design efficiency and coordination. Its predictability and immediacy supported rapid prototyping.  Clay enabled all sustainability outcomes. Its tactile, variable properties required decisions about use, sequencin g, and structural stability, making sustainability a feelable experience. Working with clay emphasized efficiency, waste, and planning, and its deformable nature revealed the complex links between adaptability, resource use, and making. These messy qualities prompt learners to think with materials, respond, and reflect in ways that digital models or ready-made PLA cannot. The findings suggest that learning about sustainability in construction benefits from deliberately including materials.
