# Taking Up Opportunities to Practice in a Social Studies-Themed Digital Simulation Game: A Quantitative Ethnography

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We use quantitative ethnography and Epistemic Network Analysis to examine how the social studies-themed digital simulation game Offworld Trading Company generates opportunities for players to practice using disciplinary knowledge, skills, and concepts (DKSCs) as tools for taking goal-mediated action. Our study design places particular emphasis on establishing inferences regarding specific game mechanics as generative of such opportunities to practice, as well as the DKSCs they invite and require of players.

## Introduction

This poster proposal describes work currently in pr ogress. Preliminary results will be ready for repor ting at the time of the conference. Previous research indicates that Offworld Trading Company (OTC), a digital simulation game thematically aligned to social studies--partic ularly economics and geography--invites players to practice using disciplinary knowledge, skills, and concepts (DKSCs) associated with social studies to plan, take, and reflect on goal-mediated actions (Kessner, 2021). The goal of the present research is to explore in greater de pth the structural design elements that afford these opport unities to practice (OTPs). We define OTPs as embod ied interactions in which players are positioned within  identities and with access to tools to solve probl ems; and in which the relevant identities, tools, and problems are disciplinarily integrated. Good games are excellent learning environments. The y privilege experience before text, performance before competence, identity play, and systems thinking; and they present players with interesting, well-structured, and well-sequenced problems players solve by master ing and applying game-valued skills (Gee, 2003). Go od games often align better than formal education environments to long-standing claims of situativity theorists that what we learn cannot be disentangled from how and why we learn it (Gresalfi et al., 2009). For these and other reasons, games have maintained a level of popularity among both educators and researchers. In the context of social studies education, however, a mismatch exists between this interest and the available evidence of effectiveness, as well as conceptual guidance around what (kinds of) games are useful and why. Use of games in social studies education contexts may indeed represent a unique challenge. Done well, social studies education should prepare students to don the mantle of citizenship, with particular emphasis on active, critical participation in making choices that (re)design the world to align as tightly as possible to shared visions of a just and equitab le pluralist democracy (Banks, 2006; Barton & Levstik, 2004; Kessner et al., 2020). This form of civic preparati on requires opportunities to practice using discipl inary knowledge, skills, and concepts as tools for taking  action. Good games are particularly good at provid ing such opportunities generally, though what design element s (e.g., game mechanics) generate these opportuniti es specifically remains unclear in the context of social studies education; far more attention has been p aid to these design issues in the contexts of math and science (Sengupta & Clark, 2016). We use quantitative ethnography (QE) (Shaffer, 2017) to examine small-group play of OTC to explore the following research questions:

1. To what extent do learners take up OTPs to practice using DKSCs to take goal-mediated action in OTC?

2. What DKSCs do learners leverage while planning, taking, and evaluating goal-mediated actions in OTC?

3. What game mechanics generate these OTPs in OTC, and which DKSCs do they invite students to use?

## Theoretical framework

Humans learn best through embodied interactions with the world. These interactions are further mediated by tools. In the context of games, we frame these embodied interactions as conversations with the world, cycles of thinking and action in which people identify goals, plan actions, and gather feedback on those actions from the world (Gee & Gee, 2017). These actions are probes into the world, a kind of question put to it, and the world “talks back” (p. 2). In videogames, players are embodied in the game world as avatars, which come with toolkits (Gee, 2015). It is with these tools and through these avatars that players (are able to) take actions in games. In OTC, players’ avatars, their associated toolkits, and the problem  spaces presented to players, align conceptually to  those of economists and geographers (Kessner, 2021); we contend OTPs emerge at their intersection. ## Methods

QE leverages quantitative designs and analyses to m ake sense of complex qualitative discourse data (Sh affer, 2017). Recent games scholarship leveraging QE has explored how an online game community forum facilitated participants’ identity exploration processes (Barany & Foster, 2019), when and why students quit learning-focused games (Karumbaiah et al., 2019), the correlation between students’ facial expressions and their understanding of biological and complex systems concepts (Martin et al., 2019), and the connection between multimodal observable behavior and collaborative problem-solving skills (Andrade et al., 2019). As with the above applications of the method to games research, QE represents articular affordances for the present study. Games are immensely complex syst ems, making purely qualitative accountings of the connections between many diverse variables difficult if not altogether untenable. Additionally, at least two games take place concurrently during gameplay: the game on the screen, and the game in players’ heads. Our r esearch questions are additionally concerned with a third s pace: the game that takes place behind the screen, which includes game mechanics.

Participants for this study are a class of high school students enrolled in an Academic Decathlon elective course in a public school in the American South. Students came from diverse backgrounds, and their varied levels of academic achievement, from “low” to “high,” as measured by school-calculated grade point averages, indicates a wide and potentially representative sample of academic interest and engagement with school. We are collecting discourse data by recording students’ conversations as they plan and deliberate which actions to take as they play the game in small groups. We will then transcribe this data for a priori coding based on prior analysis of DKSCs implicated in the C3 Framework. The results of this coding will guide our segment ation of the gameplay data (screencaptured) and accompanying video data in OTPs. Using Epistemic Network Analysis, we will then identify game mechanics that generated OTPs, as well as which (kinds of) mechanics generated stronger or weaker OTPs and stronger or weaker DKSC use.
