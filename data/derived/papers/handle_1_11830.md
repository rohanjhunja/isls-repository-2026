# “Yes, And” or “Yes, Let’s Reflect”: Children and Adult Perceptions of Journaling in Cooperative Inquiry Co-design

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Within the learning sciences, reflection is portrayed as an important component of learning. Written reflection, in the form of journaling, has been shown to improve learners’ self-awareness, development, intentionality, and agency; however, it can be difficult to foster in out-of-classroom informal learning contexts. In this paper, we explore how adults and children in one such context—Cooperative Inquiry adult-child co-design teams—conceptualized the goals, opportunities, and challenges associated with reflective journaling. We interviewed six children and eleven adults with Cooperative Inquiry expertise about their perspectives and experiences with journaling in co-design. Our findings indicate tensions between children’s and adults’ perceptions of journaling objectives and practical and ideological challenges that complicated the implementation of journaling routines. Based on these findings, we highlight four opportunities to enhance reflective journaling in co-design and other informal learning contexts.

## Introduction

The learning sciences have long documented the impo rtance of reflection for a range of learning proces ses (Dewey, 1986; Schön, 1987; 2017; Vygotsky, 1978). C lassrooms have traditionally served as important and effective spaces for promoting children’s reflection due to the enforcing structures they typically include through mandatory curriculum-driven assignments, assessments, and teacher feedba ck (Black & Wiliam, 1998). Though often less structured and systematic, informal learning contexts like out-of-school STEM and design programs can also offer rich environments for children’s reflection and identity development. However, it can be challenging to inspire children to pause and reflect in these informal contexts, given the absence of conventional classroom norms and children’s reluctance to disengage from dynamic hands-on learning (Garner et al., 2006). Moreover, children may only participate in a given program fo r a handful of hours over the course of a single su mmer or semester, which can further limit o pportunities for the types of meaningful sustained reflection known to foster learning and personal growth (Dewey, 1986; Fleck & Fitzpatrick, 2010; Schön, 1987; 2017).

In this paper, we explore a less common informal le arning context—that of Cooperative Inquiry (CI) intergenerational co-design teams —where adults and children (typically between the ag es of 7 and 11) work together as equal partners across multiple years to design real world child-facing technologies (Druin, 1999; Guha et al., 2013). We consider how one reflective practice, that of journaling, has been conceptualized, implemented, and envisioned by child and adult members in these teams. Specifically, we aimed to answer the followi ng research question: How do children and adult de sign partners characterize the goals, opportunities, and challenges associated with journaling in CI co-design? Understanding how journaling has been experienced  and leveraged for reflection and adjacent goals in auth entic co-design contexts is an important first step toward a broader research agenda aimed at clarifying, developing, evaluating, and expanding the potential of this co-design technique to promote deep reflection (Fleck & Fitzpatrick, 2010; Moon, 2013) in and on children’s design practice and developing identities. This paper thus extends prior work on learning in adult-child co-design by: (1) unpacking children’s and adults’ perspectives relat ed to the reflective practice of journaling in CI; (2) surfacing challenges team members face in feasib ly and meaningfully implementing this technique; and (3) providing recommendations for how to enhance and scaffold exi sting reflective journaling approaches to better su pport children’s design learning and personal development.

## Theoretical background

Though journaling can take a variety of forms, pers onal reflection is often described as a central goa l (Moon, 2006). Scholars across the learning sciences and Hu man-Computer Interaction (HCI) have employed numerous theoretical models and personal definitions of refl ection, leading to what some have characterized as an overall ambiguity regarding what constitutes ‘reflection’ and how to design for it (Bakyal et al., 2021; Fleck & Fitzpatrick, 2010; Kori et al., 2014). In education literature, conceptualizations of reflection have drawn from Dewey's (1986;

2024) perspective that reflecting upon lived experi ences helps learners gain awareness of their action s and

viewpoints, which, in turn, enables them to make intentional decisions when applying these insights to solve real world problems. Learning sciences literature also d raws upon Schön’s (1987; 2017) work, which builds o n Dewey’s concept of experiential learning to identify two distinct, but interrelated forms of reflection theorized to support professional learning: reflection-in-action and reflection-on-action. Reflection-in-action occurs amid enacting a particular course of action in response to a surprising problem or situation that must be a ddressed inthe-moment. In contrast, reflection-on-action involves looking back at and analyzing past experie nces to reconsider one’s decisions and develop a subsequent course of action. Scholars have also sought to understand the differe nt ways reflection can manifest, and the types of guidance needed to support such efforts. Building o n Moon’s work regarding the different purposes of reflection (e.g., learning, theory-building, self-development, empowerment, etc.) and conditions required to facilitate it (e.g., time, structured guidance, and motivation), Fleck a nd Fitzpatrick (2010)  identify five levels of reflection based on a synthesis of transdisciplinary literature—R0 Description: Revisiting (characterized as ‘not reflection’ insofar as it is primarily a description of events); R1 Reflective Description: Revisiting with Explanation; R2 Dialogic Reflection: Exploring Relationships; R3 Transformative Reflection: Fundamental Change; and  R4: Critical Reflection: Wider Implications —with higher levels proving difficult to foster in c hildren’s design-learning contexts (Iivari & Kinnula, 2018). Moon (2006; 2013), in her  work on learning journals, similarly distinguishes between descriptive and truly reflective writing and highlights how the act of journaling can enhance learning and metacognition by encouraging learners to slow down and organize their thoughts, take ownership over their learning process, and attend to the affective dimensions of learning. Though these theoretically grounded frameworks primarily focus on adults’ reflective practices, the case has also been made that reflective journaling is be neficial for younger learners’ cognitive and social  emotional development (Noyes 2004; Regan et al.,  2005). A body of literature addresses the use of reflective journaling as a classroom learning approach; however, in this stu dy, we focus on the informal learning context of CI, where children and adults collaborate to explore and desi gn new technologies. Though traditionally less crit ical in its orientation than other participatory design and co-design models (Iversen et al., 2017), CI is neverth eless characterized by a commitment to positioning children as equal partners throughout t he design process and reducing adult-child power dynamics through relationship-building and an avoidance of formal classroom norms (Druin, 1999; 2002; Fails et al., 2013; Guha et al., 2013). Therefore, the writing expectations commonly associated with the classroom do not apply in CI, with adults writing and drawing on the behalf of children who r equire or desire these forms of assistance.

Within the literature on CI, journaling has been de scribed as a technique that can serve a variety of functions, with journals portrayed as “a place where design partners can individually ske tch ideas for new technology, reflect on a session, or draw or write new ideas,” (Guha et al. 2013, p. 147);  and where adult design partners can gain useful data to inform research go als and future design sessions (Fails et al., 2013). In our CI team, journals are often used as a tool for childre n to document their design ideas during a session a nd—time permitting—to respond to reflection prompts about relevant des ign topics and activities at the conclusion of a session. As adult design partners in our own university-based co-design team, we have observed children’s lack of intrinsic motivation to engage in reflective journaling (echoing Begnaud et al., 2020; McNally et al., 2017; and Tucker, 2004) and have struggled to communicate the goals and usef ulness of this technique in a manner that resonates with children’s personal interests and va lues. Unlike other CI design techniques which have been described in greater detail and supported by concre te examples in the literature, information about jo urnaling in this context —its specific goals, practices, and outcomes —is scant, with descriptions of the t echnique rarely encompassing more than a paragraph.

Given the broad range of definitions and goals asso ciated with the term ‘reflection’, and the fact tha t meaningful reflection is notoriously difficult to f oster even among adults (Fleck & Fitzpatrick, 2010;  Kori et al. 2014), our study was motivated by a desire to explo re the types of reflection this CI journaling techn ique can aspire to engender and the scaffolds and routines through which this can be accomplished with children. Therefore, though we entered this research with knowledge of t he theoretically grounded conceptualizations of reflection discussed above, we were reticent to preemptively impose these frameworks in our research design and analysis. Rather, in keeping with the spirit of CI, we aimed to foreground how the informal learning context of co-design and associated CI processes could influence how children and adult s approach journaling and reflection. Consequently, our goal was to first situate our par ticipants’ perspectives more authentically within messy CI contexts as a stepping stone to ward formulating theory that is more broadly applicable across multi ple types of design and learning contexts.

Analysis Interview recordings were transcribed and iteratively inducti vely and deductively coded (Saldaña, 2013) using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006; 2019). The lead author began by reviewing two adult and two child transcripts to inductively develop a set of i nitial codes. She then applied, refined, and expanded upon th ese codes while analyzing the remaining interviews, resulting in a codebook consisting of 33 codes and corresponding definitions that was developed in consultation with the broader rese arch team. Using MAX QDA software, she then deductively coded all the transcripts using this codebook to identify themes within and across the two groups of participants. These high-level themes were then further differentiated by the application of four deductive codes based on our research question: children’s goals and opportunities; adults’ goals a nd opportunities; children’s challenges; and adults’ challenges. In the final stage of analysis, the lead author e xported excerpts for each code to write detailed code summaries, which included any emergent sub-themes and representative participant quotes, which were then discussed and refined with the second and third authors.

## Findings

In this section, we share six salient themes which surfaced through our analysis of the data. Theme 1: Journals as a tool for documenting and remembering Though children and adults attributed various purposes to journaling, children typically approached the technique as a tool for documenting and remembering their co-design ideas and experiences. They frequently portrayed the act of remembering as valuable in and of itself, with C6 dramatically expressing a fear that his paper journal may “someday turn to ash.” Indeed, several children envisioned looking back through their journals when older, with C2 likening his to a “time capsule where you keep all your i deas.” Many children also enjoyed reviewing their earlier journal entries in the here-and-now and indicated that they engaged in this process on a semi-regular basis. Indeed, during their interviews, nearly all childre n flipped through their journals —often witho ut adult prompting—while reminiscing about prior experiences that influenced specific entries. Adults, though to a lesser extent than children, al so considered child journals as a documentation too l capable of supporting their research goals and desire to better understand their child design partners. Additionally, some emphasized journaling as an opportunity to ens ure that all child perspectives in a session were captured, both for the purposes of data collection and in the interest of promoting an equitable co-design environment, with A11 noting, “Everyone communicates, thinks, and designs differe ntly. Often, whoever's loudest or constantly talking—it’s those ideas that make it to the sticky notes. Journaling was the quieter moment where all kids got to say what they were thinking and feeling.” Others similarly observed how the journaling format  could inspire children to express ideas that they hadn’t fully co nsidered during more collaborative activities and d iscussions. For example, A2 suggested that, in contrast to more  invasive data collection methods like audio and vi deo recording, journaling afforded a more comfortable way for children to “talk freely, without limiting it to a specific topic or goal, and to share something very personal.” Theme 2: Journals as a tool for engaging in “reflection” In contrast to children’s strong emphasis on documenting and remembering, all but one of the adults interviewed explicitly called out “reflection” as a primary goal of journaling in co-design, though many observed a disjuncture between the ways they had ac tually implemented this co-design technique and the greater opportunities they  felt it could afford. Many suggested that quiet independ ent reflection could serve as a useful complement t o the frenetic pace and energy that characterized more in teractive hands-on group activities and be particularly empowering to quieter, more introverted children.

With respect to the types of reflection adults hope d to engender, many emphasized the importance of engaging children in investigating their design pro cess as opposed to merely evaluating more visible d esign artifacts like prototypes. Several also discussed the utility of this approach when children were in the early stages of “becoming design partners” (A7), during which they were often prompted to reflect on their feelings about the new design techniques to which they were being introduced. Others highlighted the authentic nature of reflecting on design processes, as practiced by design profess ionals.  For example, A3 considered the differences between how she tended to approach journaling with children  (i.e., “asking them to journal about a prompt rela ted to the session’s topic) as compared to her own journaling process: “What I write down and reflect on isn’t the thing I'm designing—it's the process of designing. We didn’t often ask kids: ‘How did design go? Where was I affirmed or stuck? What do I still want to think about?’”  Additionally, many adults discussed how journals co uld be used as a tool to help children recognize their learning and growth as designers over time, with A6 characterizing journals as “less of a technique”  and more of an “overarching tool”  used across sessions, and A4 advocating for “reflecting, not only on a single design situation,  but looking across and thinking about it in identi ty-oriented ways so kids can see, ‘Hey, I've got this whole repertoire. Look how my design has shifted.’” Children did not explicitly talk about ‘reflection’, though several did express that journaling could help them as designers. For C1, the oldest member of the team, this took the form of combining different features from ideas he’d previously documented to  develop new ideas, while C3 mentioned that he would  occasionally iterate upon earlier entries by adding additional details t hat hadn’t occurred to him previously. Two younger members of the team similarly shared that looking back at old entries helped them to “keep our progress” (C6), and “do it better next time” (C5). Even C2, who initially asserted that journaling had no impact on his design work, conceded that, “if you’re not sure about something, you could go over your previous ideas on the topic and give your opinion on it in the future,” though he expressed that he, himself, felt disinclined to do so. Theme 3: Journals as a tool for private pursuits and identity development With respect to developing journal content, many ch ildren used their journals to pursue personal inter ests, with several describing their entries as “random doodles.” The strongest example of this came from C4, who had filled her journal with elaborate illustrations of fiction al characters, stories, songs she was composing, and abstract explorations of colors, textures, and page layouts, all of which she declared as entirely “unrelated” to co-design. Her conceptualization of her design journal as a space for personal exploration was further reinforced by her desire to keep its contents private, as indicated by a page declaring her journal as ‘top secret’ alongside illustrations of a ‘fingerprint recognition button,’ and an ‘inside lo ckdown button,’ —an elaborate technology design in its own right. Though less protective of their journals tha n C4, all children —except for C1 who was the oldest in the group—generally portrayed their journals as personal scra pbooks rather than as tools for sharing and communicating their ideas and experiences.

As reflected in A4’s quote above, identity developm ent and child agency were perceived as a desired outcome of reflective journaling among adults. A1 s poke of her desire to “get kids to see that they have some aspect of their identity in this, that what they're doing is important. That it's not just this afterschool program, but that they’re contributing to this design process.” To this end, several carved out time for children to decorate and personalize their journals, which —more than a fun arts and craft s activity —served as “an important act of ownership to set the stage. It's an opportunity for  them to sort of say, yeah, let me put on this jour nal-designer costume” (A1). Several adults also saw journals as a space w here children could reflect upon their collective identities as members of a design team, with some h ighlighting journaling as an opportunity to nudge c hildren toward thinking more critically about the underlyin g power dynamics and everyday life implications ass ociated with the technologies and visiting industry and university collaborators with whom they engaged. Theme 4: A lack of clear guidelines Several of the adults with whom we spoke expressed a lack of clarity regarding the overarching goals o f child journaling, how this CI technique  should be implemented, and the specific roles adult s should play to fully reap its benefits. Unlike other co-design techniques, which are perhaps more transpare nt due to direct adult involvement, journaling, for many, proved more elus ive: “I don't think we built-up journaling as a design methodology or showed why it's important and how to journal. It ’s not as intuit ive as building something with materials. Kids aren't collaborating like in other techniques where adults can ask questions”  (A3). In addition, some adults highlighted how their lack of experienc e implementing a reflective journaling practice in their own lives could leave them confused about how to best g uide children in similar endeavors. (A2) explained,  “It was hard because I don't know that my design perspective is necessarily aligned with reflecting about design. So how could I direct the kids if I don't even know what t o reflect on as an adult?”  Thus, adult reluctance to engage children in journaling struck some as a sort of self-fulfilling prophecy, where the benefits of journaling were never truly made visible due to its infrequent use and overall de-prioritization. This left some adults, as well as children, unable to “really see what the point is”  (A9). Children’s journaling motivations —or lack thereof —similarly reflected uncertainty about the technique’s overarc hing purpose, with C2 contending, “I already know my ideas. I don't need to write them,” and C4 indicating that she journaled primarily to appease her adult design partners.

## Theme 5: Practical challenges with journaling in the CI context

The challenge raised most often by both adults and children was time constraints, with adults describing journaling as “the forgotten component of a session” (A11) and “hard to do right when there are so many things competing for your time and attention”  (A7). Several adults lamented that, as the final el ement of CI co-design routines, journaling was often the first thing to get dropped  or rushed, with some surmising that this must inva riably influence children’s impression of, and response to, the practice. Indeed, children also observed that “we don’t do journaling a lot” (C1), with some expressing a desire for “a few more minutes to finish doing our journals” (C5). Additionally, though adult design partners would often scribe for children who did not want to write, A8 recalled times when a child really wanted to write out their  thoughts independently, but that the amount of tim e required for this far exceeded that which was allotted to journaling. Another related issue was tied to accessibility con cerns—both the physical accessibility of children’s journals and challenges children experienced using journaling tools themselves. With respect to the la tter, C6 expressed frustration that pages kept tearing out of his jour nal and markers kept drying out, while C2 felt that existing journaling routines and tools were inefficient, explaining, “If I could change one thing about journaling, I’d want to be able to type. Having adults help wit h writing is OK because I can just think of it and don't have to waste time writing. But typing’s faster.”  Adults commented less on journaling tools themselve s, though several mentioned how writing—and even drawing— could be difficult for younger children. Several also discussed how and when children on their teams accessed their journals, expressing reluctance about letting children bring them home, for fear they might get forgotten or lost. Wh ile, in many instances, children’s journals were ke pt in a consistent and easy-to-access location during sessions, others observed th at journals were stored elsewhere and typically only taken out when they were needed for an activity or requested by a child. Thus, some surmised that, despite adult desires to increase child agency and ownership of their journals, their lack of consistent availability could signal that it was ultimately adults who served as the gatekeepers of this design technique.

## Theme 6: Ideological challenges with journaling in the CI context

Several adults struggled to reconcile their perception of journaling as a school-like task with their commitment to CI’s goal of equalizing adult-child power dynamics. For example, A7 observed how some children would apply standards associated with school toward their co-design journaling practice, either by anticipating a grade or specific amount of writing they were expected to complete. Though not a broad theme among the children in this study, this was reflected to some extent in childre n’s expressed desire to avoid spelling mistakes and  two children’s use of the word “boring” in reference to journaling. Thus, several adults we re fearful that they could alienate their child design partners by asking them  to engage in written reflection, observing that so me children approached journaling with resistance, a lack of focus, or a desire to finish as quickly as possible. A5 recalled that some of the children with whom he worked explicitly complained that journaling felt like ‘homework’ or ‘school’, a factor that ultimately led him to eliminate the technique from his co-design practice: “Kids, all they do is write. I don't need them to d o that here. We're trying to get them to be creative and share their thoughts and be the designer. When you put them into a journaling role, suddenly, they're no longer the designer, especially if you are prompting them and want to read what they write. They're now producing work for you as opposed to with you. So, it just felt like journaling reinforced a power structure that I wasn't really interested in reinforcing.” Even those adults who expressed a strong commitment  to journaling could still struggle to implement the technique in a manner that didn’t feel like a top-down directive. A1 described this tension aptly: “I think the improv ‘yes and’ philosophy,” (a n improv comedy concept that emphasizes accepting and building upon scene partners’ ideas (Talks at Google, 2011)), “ that helps in participatory design can also hamper the reflection needed for journaling. Journals are not really a ‘yes, and’. They're more like a ‘yes, let's reflect’. So, trying tonegotiate that tension of wanting to respond to a child's whims—because that is participatory design and inclusion—is sometimes at odds with the request to take a moment to reflect. There's a formal scaffolding that is required of that kind of action that is a bit in conflict wi th the way that Cooperative Inquiry has been implemented.” Adults also shared that they had misgivings about a sking children to engage in an independent activity or simply dictate their thoughts to an adult, given the emphasis placed upon collaboration in CI. A3 observed how the absence of authentic collaboration during journ aling could result in a “change in the energy”  on her team, which, while beneficial for some children, could fu nction as a deterrent for others. At the same time,  C5 shared that he enjoyed journaling because “I like adults helping me and it's fun collaboratin g,” suggesting that some children viewed journaling as an opportunity for a different type of collaboration. Nevertheless, it w as acknowledged that simply providing writing support or enjoying a “short respite” (A5) during journaling was perhaps at odds with CI’s goal of equalizing adult-child power dynamics. When asked if they could reca ll a time when they or other adults journaled alongside children, several adults were surprised that this was something they had not previously considered, noting that journaling was perhaps the only part of a session during which children did one thing (reflection) while adults did another (supporting children’s reflection).

## Discussion

In this section, we synthesize our findings as they  related to Schön’s (2017) and Fleck and Fitzpatrick’s (2010) reflection frameworks in the context of adult-child co-design. We contribute four design opportunities for researchers and practitioners to consider for integration into their collaborative processes with children. Opportunity 1: Expanding design documentation approaches Themes 1 and 2 in this study indicate a tension bet ween adults’ and children’s primary conceptualizati ons of the journaling co-design technique, with adults placing greater empha sis on reflection about design processes and personal development, while children focused on the documentation and revisitation of design ideas. In mapping these goals to Fleck & Fitzpatrick’s (2010) Levels of Reflection, it appears that children generally aspired to level R0 Description (i.e., “not reflective” (p. 217)), whereas adults’ professed goals were typ ically more akin to R3 Transformative Reflection (i.e., “leading to a change in practice or understanding”  (p. 218)), which, along with R4 Critical Reflection, has been characterized as very challenging to design for (Fleck & Fitzpatrick, 2010; Moon, 2006, 2013; Iivari & Kinnula, 2018). We argue that this seeming incongruity raises an opportunity to e xplore journaling approaches capable of synthesizing these two perspectives in order to intrinsically motivate children’s engagement in the more reflective aspects of journaling in CI and other co-design contexts. In considering Schön’s (2017) dual-pronged approach to reflection, we propose that chi ldren’s and adult’s perspectives in this study are, in fact, co mplementary. Documentation is viewed as an importan t precondition for reflection-on-action insofar as it facilitates one’s ability to recall p ast experiences to accurately analyze and contemplate their broader impacts (Dewe y, 1986; Fleck & Fitzpatrick, 2010; Schön, 2017). T hus, it serves as a crucial first step toward being able to “revisit an event or knowledge with intent to re-organize and/or do something differently” (Fleck & Fitzpatrick, 2010, p. 218). Rather thandiscouraging children’s commitment to capturing and reminiscing about their ideas, co-design practitioners can instead build upon these inclinations by introducing a broader palette of documentation tools. In capitalizing upon this interest, it may also be beneficial to introduce other self-report modalities and technologies demonstrated to support documentation and higher levels of reflection, such as digital video, photos, audio recording, wearab les, and online portfolios (Fleck & Fitzpatrick, 2010), to help children examine their design ideas and exp eriences from multiple perspectives —a crucial step for R3 Transformative Reflection. Additionally, such an approach could foster reflection-in-action by nudging children toward documenting and communicati ng—not only their final products —but also the development of their ideas and prototypes.

Opportunity 2: Developing reflection-on-action routines The literature demonstrates that children’s reflection in design contexts is difficult to facilitate (Iivari & Kinnula,

2018) and requires more than mere documentation of events and access to intelligible information. Thou gh one

of Moon’s (2013) three preconditions for reflection is the availability of “structured guidance”, there was minimal discussion among participants about how to scaffold children’s reflective journaling beyond offering adult writing support. These findings point to an opportunity to explore and develop scaffolded routines aimed at making reflective journaling more developmentally accessib le and meaningful to younger children in this  and other informal learning contexts.

As indicated by the participants in this study and the CI literature, reflective journaling is often approached as a self-contained activity, in which children are asked to respond to a specific prompt at the conclusion of a session. However, in building on Sc hön’s conceptualization of the different temporal c onditions required to promote reflection-in-and on-action, design researchers might also consider expanding journaling routines to encourage the retrospective and prospective thinking required for transformative reflection (Baykal et al., 2021). For example, designers could periodically encourage children to look acros s a body of their journal entries (as recommended by A4 and explored by Begna ud et al., 2020) with a specific reflection-oriented goal in mind. This could complement and deepen children’s p rocess of “remembering” by motivating them to identify and interrogate patterns in their work as is characteristic of R2 Dialogic Reflection (e.g., noticing and considering a personal preference for certain activities and technologies, observing similar and different characteristics across one’s designs, etc.). A child like C4, for example, might look back across all her self-declared “unrelated” journal entries with the goal of identifying connections between them and her other co-design work. In addition to better guiding reflection-on-action, such an approach could carve out dedicat ed time for reflection to ensure that journaling is not relegated to the “forgotten component” of more typical, jam-packed sessions. Opportunity 3: Directly involving children in the design of journaling techniques Among the adults interviewed in this study, there w as a lack of consensus regarding whether journaling, as a technique, was inclusive (i.e., by empowering and c apturing the perspectives of more introverted child ren) or isolating and alienating (i.e., due t o an absence of dynamic collaboration) (in contrast to Moon’s (2006,

2013) assertion that the self-directed nature of reflective writing can inspire a  sense of ownership over one’s

learning). We suggest that these tensions highlight  an opportunity to expand how we think about and pr omote child agency within the context of co-design, by inviting children to directly participate in the design of journaling tools and routines that resonate with their own and their broader team’s needs and values. Within this approach, children and adults could co-design reflection prompts, which, in addition to potentially yielding more developmentally appropriate and engaging scaffolds, could enable them to see their own voices reflected in the parameters of this design technique. Moreover, such an approach could facilitate greater choice and agency by giving children the option to select the type of reflection in which they wish to engage  by choosing from a menu of scaffolded co-designed prompts. This menu might include a selection of design processoriented prompts (such as those recommended by A3), critical reflection-oriented prompts about technology (such as those employed in child-as-protagonist participatory design approaches (Iversen et al., 2017)  and at the higher levels of Fleck and Fitzpatrick’s and Moon’s framew orks, emotion-oriented prompts about a design topic or technique, and/or action-oriented prompts that ask children to explore next-steps or shifts in their perspective, as is characteristic of transformative reflection (Baykal et al., 2021; Fleck & Fitzpatrick, 2010). Opportunity 4: Reconsidering and expanding upon adult roles in co-design Adults’ reluctance about journaling as a co-design technique was often tied to their sense of discomfort regarding their own roles (or lack thereof) in this process, leading to ideological concerns pertaining to adult-child power dynamics and the collaborative nature of co-design. While scribing is often characterized as a tool through which adults can scaffold children’s journaling process a nd better distinguish the technique from school-based tasks (Guha et al. 2013), we might consider, as indicated  by C2’s desire to type his journal entries, employ ing digital technology like speech-to-text or audiovisual recordings to make the practice  more independently accessible to children. This could, in turn, free up adults to jo urnal alongside children or take a mo re participatory approach toward the implementation of the journaling technique in this and other co-design contexts with children. Adult co-design partners, for example, might consider taking on the additional role of reflection partners. On the most basic level, this could take the form o f adults more frequently modeling and sharing their  own journaling practice with children, both in accordan ce with CI’s goal of striving for equal partnership s (Druin, 1999; Guha et al., 2013) and with the literature on  effective scaffolding in learning environments (Vy gotsky, 1978; Wood et al., 1976). Alternatively, adults might approach journaling in a similar manner to ot her CI design techniques, where adult-child discussion and elaboration takes center stage  (Druin, 1999; Guha et al., 2013), through intergenerational asynchronous dialogue jou rnaling—a slower-paced approach which, in the absence of authoritative power dynamics, “can have an effect of deepening thinking and learn ing and of allowing time for unexpected thoughts to contribute to the dialogue” (Moon, 2006, p.55), while prompting attitude change (Baykal et al., 2021), and intrinsic motivation for reluctant journalers (McNally et al., 2017).

## Limitations and future work

One potential limitation of this study lies in the greater sample of adults (represented by eleven 30–45-minute interviews) as compared to children (represented by  six 10 –15-minute interviews), resulting in a possible overrepresentation of adult perspectives in the findings presented in this paper. This may have been compounded by children’s shorter attention span s, as compared to most adults, which arguably result ed in a less detailed and smaller body of self-reported child data. Future work might therefore consider engaging children in focus groups and co-design activities around these topics to gain more detailed insight into their perspectives on journaling. An additional limitation is that the sample in this re search was limited to a small number of children fr om one intergenerational co-design team and adults with experience practicing C I. Thus, further research is needed to understand how the findings in this study play out across other intergenerational teams and co-design approaches. Such work could be fruitful in identifying convergences and divergences across different reflection and reflective journaling approaches in design contexts. Finally, future research should include analyses of the cont ent of children’s journals to identify and build upon existing instances of reflection.

## Conclusion

The findings and insights discussed in this paper lead us to conclude that journaling—if intentionally enacted and accompanied by carefully designed structures and scaffolds that resonate with both child and adult stakeholders— has the potential to foster the development of  reflective child practitioners poised to learn and grow through co-design. We look forward to pursuing these opportunities in our own CI practice and discovering how they may be explored in other co-design and informal learning contexts with children. Though further research is needed, we anticipate that these approaches have the potential  to nudge children toward contemplating the relationships between topics, ideas, techniques, and technologies  they encounter (e.g., R2 Relationships), while we, as adult designers, researchers, and educators  can leverage deliberate and systematic approaches t oward reflection-onaction to encourage children to revisit their prior design  documentations in a manner that leads to changes in practice, understanding, and personal growth (e.g., R3 Transformation).
