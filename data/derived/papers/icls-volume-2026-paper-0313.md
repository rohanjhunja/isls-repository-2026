# Analyzing Pre-Service Teachers’ TPACK Development Trajectories through SRL-Supportive Artifacts Production Using SSNA

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how the relationships among the knowledge domains of Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK) change over time by comparing those who produce higher-and lower-quality self-regulated learning (SRL)-supportive artifacts using Social Semantic Network Analysis. We analyzed the artifacts and reflection journals of 46 pre-service teachers. The results indicated that artifact quality was primarily associated with the SRL support scores. Although not statistically significant, the improvement group tended to show a higher total degree centrality and stronger connections between technological (TK) and pedagogical knowledge (PK). This paper presents an approach for capturing the temporal development of linking knowledge domains within the TPACK development process.

In contemporary society, where digital technologies are embedded in everyday life, pre-service teachers are expected to develop technological pedagogical content knowledge (TPACK)(Mishra & Koehler, 2006). TPACK is conceptualized as teachers’ knowledge that emerges from the integration of technological (TK), pedagogical (PK), and content knowledge (CK). Prior research emphasizes the importance of interactions and integration among these knowledge domains. However, what does it mean for teachers’ knowledge to be “integrated”? Angeli and Valanides (2009) distinguished between two epistemological perspectives o f the TPCK: the integrative and the transformative view. In the former, TPCK emerges through the context-dependent integration of TK, PK, and CK, whereas the latter  conceptualizes it as a distinct body of knowledge constructed through their interactions, rather than mere aggregation.

These two perspectives may appear to be mutually exclusive. In contrast, Tschönhens et al. (2024) argue that the integrative and transformative views are complementary. They propose d that teachers dynamically integrate knowledge domains (TK, PK, and CK) in response to instructional situations and that these processes contribute to the development of transformative TPACK.  Their Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA)  results showed that patterns of co-occurrence among TK, PK, and CK  (e.g., TK-PK) vary across instructional contexts. From this perspective, examining how the knowledge domains of TPACK  are associated over time provides a lens for understanding teacher learning processes.

However, many existing approaches to assessing TPACK rely on self-reports, questionnaires, and classroom observations (Koehler et al., 2012), and tend to focus on measuring knowledge domains as outcomes of integration. To examine developmental processes, previous studies have analyzed  weekly reflection journals and ENA. Nevertheless, these analytical methods require extensive coding, which makes the analysis timeconsuming and resource-intensive. Consequently, this study employs  the Knowledge Building Discourse eXplorer (KBDeX; Oshima et al., 2012). KBDeX is a type of Social Semantic Network Analysis (SSNA) (Shaffer, 2017). This enables the analysis of co-occurrence relationships among knowledge domains  over time while reducing the need for manual coding. This differs from ENA, which typically relies on fine-grained coding. In this study, the co-occurrence of the knowledge domain is treated as an indicator for describing the process of linking through which knowledge domains become associated over time. This perspective enables the visualization of changes in the relationships among knowledge domains that are difficult to capture through outcome-oriented assessments.

We applied this analytical approach  to a 14-week pre-service teacher education course based on two complementary frameworks: Learning by Design (LBD) (Kolodner, 2002) and self-regulated learning (SRL) (Zimmerman, 2002). LBD promotes the integrated acquisition of knowledge and skills through iterative cycles of inquiry and design/redesign (Kolodner, 2002)  and has been applied to pre-service teacher education to foster TPACK development (e.g., Lu, 2014). In SRL environments, learners are expected to regulate their learning processes, which involve forethought, performance, and self-reflection phases (Zimmerman, 2002). Accordingly, the course was designed for  pre-service teachers to create 2–3-minute SRL-supportive self-instructional videos and accompanying post-tests (“SRL-supportive artifacts”), and to iteratively refine them. Supporting SRL through self-instruction videos requires integrating technical knowledge (TK) with PK and CK, for example, using 2056 technological features to support motivation and monitoring. By engaging in this task, pre-service teachers are expected to develop TPACK.

Therefore, this study examines how the relationships among the knowledge domains of TPACK change gradually through the production of SRL-supportive artifacts  by comparing  pre-service teachers who produce higher-quality SRL-supportive instructional videos with those who do not.

## Method

This course was conducted in Japan in 2023 among second-year university students. Of the enrolled students, 46 provided consent and were included in the analysis. The 14-week course devoted eight weeks to instructional video development. Following LBD’s key elements (iterative process, authentic practice, modeling, collaboration, and reflection) (Kolodner, 2002), students learned PK through the planning and modeling  of artifacts (Weeks 4– 6), acquired TK for video production (Weeks 7– 8), and submitted their first artifacts (Week 9). Peer evaluations of the first artifacts were conducted in Week 11, followed by revisions based on feedback, and the final artifacts were peer-reviewed in Week 14.  As the course focused on teaching methods and technology use rather thandisciplinary expertise, students selected familiar topics (e.g., sign language). Therefore, CK was not emphasized. Reflection journal Each week, pre-service teachers submitted reflection journals to the course learning management system. After weekly lessons, they answered the question, “What did you learn in today’s class?” A fter submitting the first version in Week 9, they reflected on difficulties and insights, and after the final version, they were asked both what they had learned and how they revised their artifacts from the first to the final version. Evaluation of artifacts Artifacts (videos and post-tests) were assessed using a rubric with three dimensions and six items: (A) alignment between learning objectives, videos, and tests; (B) SRL support for self-assessment, self-correction, and review; and (C) engaging use of visuals to promote understanding and motivation. Each item was rated on a scale  of 0–2 (0 = not achieved, 1 = partially achieved, 2 = achieved). Dimensions (A), (B), and (C) were related to PK, strategies for supporting self-monitoring in SRL, and the integrat ion aspect of TPACK, respectively. Artifacts were independently evaluated by two instructors, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. The interrater reliability (Fleiss’ kappa) was 0.70, which indicated substantial agreement. To examine whether students improved the quality of their artifacts through an iterative process, we classified 92 videos (46 first versions and 46 final versions) based on standardized rubric scores and analyzed them using Ward’s hierarchical clustering method. Analysis of the combined data from both versions allowed us to determine whether the pre-service teachers had produced high-quality videos from the outset, improved over time, or exhibited little change in quality.

We examined how pre-service teachers’ TPACK evolved during SRL-supportive artifact production using KBDeX. A list of nouns related to PK (e.g., post-test), CK (e.g., canon chord), and TK (e.g., subtitles) was developed by the first and second authors based on students’ reflection data. The first author extracted candidate terms, which were reviewed by the second author, standardized (e.g., orthographic variations and synonyms), and classified into PK, CK, and TK categories. The second author then confirmed the final list.  T o trace the developmental trajectories, the total degree centrality for PK-, CK-, and TK-related terms was calculated weekly for each student. Degree centrality indicates how frequently a term co-occurs with other terms and is operationalized as the number of edges, where an edge represents the co-occurrence of two terms within the same reflection. The total degree centrality is obtained by summing these values within each domain, capturing the extent to which each knowledge domain is associated with other s. To compare developmental patterns between students whose artifact quality improved and those who did not, we conducted Mann –Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction for each domain (PK, CK, and TK), with group (Improvement vs. Non-Improvement) and week as factors. Interdomain connections (e.g., TK-PK) were explored by comparing the number of edges linking the domains in the SSNA networks. Because TPACK is context-dependent, integrative domains such as TPK or 2057 TCK cannot reliably be identified at the noun level. Accordingly, we  focused on PK, CK, and TK, which can be directly captured, thereby providing an exploratory yet scalable method for tracing temporal development, while reducing the coding burden.

Cluster analysis identified three types of pre-service teachers’ artifacts (Figure 1, Table 1).  Furthermore, 3 (clusters) × 6 (rubric items) Kruskal–Wallis tests revealed significant differences among clusters across all items (p <.05). Based on these differences in rubric scores, the clusters were labeled Cluster 1: Non-Learning Support, Cluster 2: Knowledge Transmission-Oriented, and  Cluster 3: SRL Support.  Cluster 1 was characterized by significantly lower mean scores than the other two on all rubric it ems, except SRL support for self-assessment, which indicates a lack of basic alignment in instructional design. Cluster 2 exhibited significantly lower scores than Cluster 3 on the two SRL support items, while performing comparably well on alignment-related items. Cluster 3 was distinguished by significantly higher scores on the SRL support items than the other clusters.  As illustrated in Figure 1, both the Non-Learning Support and Knowledge Transmission-Oriented groups scored particularly low on “SRL support for self-correction and review.”  This aspect appeared to be a key factor in distinguishing higher-quality artifacts from others, ultimately highlighting that many pre-service teachers struggled to incorporate SRL-supportive perspectives.

Cluster transitions from the first to the final version s revealed that many pre-service teachers remained in this category (Figure 1). Of those initially on Non-Learning Support (N = 23), nearly half (N = 11) stayed, and only three moved to SRL Support. In the Knowledge Transmission-Oriented group (N = 25), 11 remained, while another 11 shifted to SRL Support. In the final version, 45.7% of the pre-service teachers produced SRL Support videos, while 47.8% stayed in the same cluster as in the first submission. This suggests that the course design did not necessarily enable all participants to substantially improve their artifacts. These results suggest the following key findings: First, within the iterative LBD cycle, the quality of reflection based on the initial artifact may serve as a turning point for subsequent outcomes. In this study, differences in artifact quality were associated with SRL support scores and, although non-significant, with the strength of TK-PK connections in reflections after the first and final submissions. These findings suggest that providing reflection prompts that encourage students to consider whether they have effectively supported SRL by leveraging technological features after producing the first version may help improve the artifact quality. Second, by employing SSNA, this study introduces a new evaluative perspective to capture the temporal development of linking among knowledge domains within the TPACK development process. A key feature of this approach is its ability to trace relationships among knowledge domains over time using the total degree centrality and edge counts derived from KBDeX. This approach serves as a methodological contribution toward a complementary understanding of the integrative and transformative perspectives on TPACK (Tschönhens et al., 2024). It also provides a lens through which to examine how interactions among TPACK components relate to instructional design. As Saubern et al. (2020)  note, prior TPACK research has primarily focused on measuring or comparing individual components and has paid limited attention to how te achers’ professional knowledge relates to effective instructional practice. This study offers an approach for bridging this gap.

However, this study had several limitations. While co-occurrence among knowledge domains was treated as linking, it remains unclear whether these  relationships represent integrated knowledge such as TPK. No statistically significant differences in linking were observed during this process. Future research should refine the theoretical and empirical foundations of this approach.
