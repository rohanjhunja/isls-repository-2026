# Responsive Teaching in the Context of Theory-Building Activities

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Theory-building activities are a powerful context for eliciting and building on student ideas. We present an approach to structuring responsive teaching in the context of theory-building activities, which is designed to help students articulate, evaluate, and refine their ideas through generating, testing, and revising theoretical knowledge artifacts, such as models and explanations. Our approach focuses on the design of theory-building activities and teacher moves to support student knowledge construction, in alignment with constructivist ideals.

In recent years, science education has shifted from teaching students the content and skills of science separately, to helping them learn both in an integrated way, through participation in science practices (Ford & Foreman, 2006). The U.S. Next Generation Science Standards include theory-building practices such as modeling and explanation in their learning objectives for K-12 science education (NRC, 2012). Many instructional designers have used theory-building activities as a way of eliciting and building on students’ ideas, in alignment with constructivist ideals and anti-deficit perspectives (Adiredja, 2019). With the goal of helping teachers build on student ideas, researchers have studied responsive teaching pedagogies. These are approaches through which teachers deliberately pursue student thinking (Robertson et al., 2015). Rooted in the assumption that students have a wealth of productive prior knowledge  resources, responsive teaching is a family of practices that seek to 1) understand the substance of  students’ ideas, 2) identify connections between students’ ideas and the knowledge and practices of a discipline, and 3) adjust instruction to pursue students’ ideas (Robertson et al., 2015). Research on responsive teaching has found that it can help students develop conceptual understanding and engage in disciplinary practices, while  promoting student agency and equitable participation (Carpenter et al., 1989; Hammer et al., 2012). Despite its benefits, responsive teaching presents teachers with a number of challenges (Prawat & Floden, 1994). In light of these and other challenges, teachers need support implementing responsive teaching. To support teachers’ engagement in responsive teaching practices, we propose an approach to structuring responsive teaching in the context of theory-building activities. Theoretical foundations Our approach is grounded in a theory of knowledge and learning called knowledge in pieces (KiP; diSessa, 1993). KiP is a constructivist theory of knowledge and learning, which views knowledge as a complex system of elements drawn intonetworks in response to the sense-making demands of a given context. Learning is viewed as a “gradual tuning to expertise” through a piecemeal process of reorganization and refinement of the knowledge system. Because more expert knowledge is built of many of the same pieces th at comprise the novice system, novice knowledge is viewed as rich with resources for the construction of more sophisticated knowledge. KiP pedagogy therefore seeks to elicit and build on the intellectual resources that students bring to their learning. In this way, KiP pedagogy aligns with the general goals and methods of responsive teaching.

## Responsive teaching in the context of theory-building activities

Figure 1 illustrates the core elements of our approach to structuring responsive teaching in the context of theorybuilding activities. Our approach pursues the broader goals of responsive teaching, which are to elicit and build on student ideas, by helping students articulate, evaluate, and refine their thinking. Students are guided through these steps as they generate, test, and revise their theoretical knowledge artifacts. When students generate their artifact, they articulate their thinking, as their artifact embodies their thinking. When they test their artifact, they evaluate their thinking, as testing the artifact surfaces unexpected findings that conflict with their ideas or reveals where their thinking may have been black-boxed. Their revised artifact embodies their refined thinking. Students’ generation, testing, and revision of artifacts are scaffolded by the design of theory-building activities and teachers’ responsive teaching moves.

For example, an activity might be designed to prompt students to generate a particular kind of artifact, which explains or m odels a phenomenon under investigation. It might then prompt students to test their artifacts through a particular process, such as experimentation, simulation, or argumentation. Finally, it might invite students to generate a new artifact to represent the ir refined thinking. The teacher moves layer builds on the design of instruction and is concerned with how teachers’ moves might further support students in engaging in each of these actions. For example, the teacher might frame the theory-building activity as aligned with the knowledge-building activity of scientists and elaborate the activity instructions to help students understand how to generate a model of a phenomenon and articulate their initial thinking with regards to it. They might help students test their artifact and evaluate their thinking by asking them to clarify, elaborate, or evaluate the ideas represented in their artifact, or to compare it with observations or other data. Finally, they might ask students to consider how their ideas have changed as a result of testing their artifact and invite them to revise their artifact to represent their refined thinking.

In this paper, we presented an approach to structuring responsive teaching in the context of theory-building activities. In this approach, the broader goals of responsive teaching (to elicit and build on student ideas) are specifically pursued by helping s tudents articulate, evaluate, and refine  their ideas. These actions are naturally supported by theory-building activities, which structure students’ generat ion, testing, and revision of theoretical knowledge artifacts. The paper makes a contribution to literature on responsive teaching, by expanding the characterization of responsive teaching practices and by describing how they might be structured in the context of theory-building activities, which are viewed as a critical component of science education by learning scientists, educators, and U.S. national policy. The paper makes a contribution to literature concerned with engaging students in theory-building activities, by presenting a method through which these activities might be leveraged as a powerful context for responsive teaching.
