# Shifting from Cooperation to Collaboration: A Case Study of Medical Students' Collaborative Learning Experiences

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates barriers to collaborative learning among medical students in a medical humanities course. Data were collected from 74 second-year medical students in Taiwan, with 23 having received collaboration training. Using grounded theory, we analyzed online discussions, learning portfolios, and interviews. Findings reveal distinct patterns in discussion timing, interaction dynamics, and outcomes between trained and untrained groups. Students' prior success with division-of-labor approaches emerges as an inhibitor to collaborative learning adoption. Notably, consistent implementation of assessment criteria becomes the critical catalyst for shifting students from cooperation to collaboration. This research deepens understanding: learning outcomes alone cannot reveal learning processes; learners' agency must be reconceptualized; curriculum, activities, materials, and assessment require structurally transparent integration.

This study's problematic awareness is rooted in the "observed yet unrecognized" phenomenon prevalent in medical education settings. Following Bamberger & Schön's (1983) advocacy for reflective practice, the researcher identified several noteworthy pedagogical dilemmas through systematic teaching observation and postclass interaction documentation: First, when groups present orally on stage, learners in the audience often exhibit dispersed attention and participation absence. Second, formative feedback provided by the instructor to specific groups, including questions, suggestions, and reminders, frequently requires repetition to different groups in subsequent classes, revealing difficulties in transfer of learning (Perkins & Salomon, 1992). Deeper problematic awareness emerges from discourse episodes of post-class teacher-student interactions documented by the researcher. These dialogues reveal a core phenomenon: when learners are queried about the overall argumentative context of team presen tations, they frequently respond with "that's not my responsibility," reflecting that learners are accustomed to cooperation rather than collaboration. According to Johnson and Johnson's (1999) classification of cooperative learning, cooperation emphasizes  task division and individual accountability, whereas collaboration stresses shared goals, mutual interdependence, and collective accountability.

More concerningly, learners often reduce the necessity of collaboration to an instrumental rationality consideration "related to grades" rather than genuinely understanding its substantive significance for future clinical practice. When asked "Will discuss ing with classmates be useful in the future?" learners' confusion highlights their inability to establish meaningful connections between classroom learning and future clinical applications, constituting what Barnett (2004) termed the "knowing-doing gap." The significance of these phenomena lies in the fact that if medical students fail to develop collaborative capabilities during their studies, they will likely struggle to collaborate effectively with interprofessional healthcare teams in clinical settings, thereby affecting the quality of patient-centered holistic care they provide. According to the core competency frameworks of the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC, 2014) and Taiwan Medical Accreditation Council (TMAC), teamwork and interprof essional communication are listed as essential core competencies for medical students.

However, how can learners' attention be shifted from individual performance to collective efficacy? What constitutes the specific substance of collaboration? When and how should instructors implement pedagogical interventions? Although these questions are critical issues in medical humanities education settings, they have rarely received systematic attention from teaching practice researchers. This may be because transforming the abstract concept of collaboration into concrete, operationalizable teaching activities presents considerable difficulty.

Nevertheless, adhering to the spirit of medical humanities education and Scholarship of Teaching and Learning (SoTL), as Kennedy (1962) stated: "We do these things not because they are easy, but because they are hard." This research is initiated in this sp irit. Based on the aforementioned problematic awareness, this study distills two core research issues through grounded analysis: First, learners are accustomed to cooperation rather 484 than practicing collaboration. Second, learners cannot understand, imagine, or connect collaboration to its application value in future clinical practice, presenting what may be termed a "situated learning barrier." Therefore, this study adopts the "backward design" philosophy (Wiggins & McTighe, 2005), taking Team Resource Management (TRM) capabilities required in clinical practice as the end goal to recalibrate the medical ethics curriculum. TRM originates from Crew  Resource Management in the aviation industry and has been adapted for healthcare, emphasizing effective communication, situational awareness, team leadership, and mutual support to enhance team performance and patient safety (Salas et al., 2008). Specifically, this study aims to deconstruct TRM's operational principles and concretize them through reconstruction in curriculum arrangements, learning activity designs, and material development. By engaging learners in these redesigned authentic learnin g tasks, the study seeks to enable them to gradually develop conceptual understanding and procedural knowledge of collaboration through participatory processes, thereby cultivating teamwork capabilities needed for future clinical practice and ultimately ac hieving the professional goal of providing patient-centered holistic care.

## Literature review and theoretical perspectives

This study aims to enhance medical students' collaborative capabilities. The following sections review relevant literature from three dimensions: teamwork, assessment of teamwork, and situated learning theory. It is noteworthy that when transforming theore tical concepts into teaching practice tools, appropriate adjustments according to context are necessary for effective application (Farnsworth, Kleanthous & Wenger, 2016; O'Brien & Battista, 2020). Therefore, this section not only introduces core concepts but emphasizes dialogue and translational application between theory and teaching practice. Teamwork: From cooperation to collaboration Teamwork refers to learner-centered interactive processes where team members collectively engage in thinking, inquiry, reasoning, and clarification to solve specific problems. Learning here encompasses not merely professional knowledge acquisition but social learning through interpersonal interaction toward specific goals (Stahl & Suthers, 2006). Substantial empirical research demonstrates that teamwork not only promotes learning effectiveness but positively impacts interpersonal relationships and social skills (Hong, Chai, Tsai, & Lin, 2020; Chang, Chen, & Tseng, 2012).

However, qualitative differences exist in teamwork. Dillenbourg (1999) explicitly distinguishes between "cooperation" and "collaboration": the former involves dividing work tasks into independent parts that members individually complete before assembling into final products; the latter emphasizes members continuously "confirming individual and mutual status," "communicating," and "providing mutual support" to jointly make decisions and clarify issues when solving complex problems. As Johnson and Johnson's (1994) early research indicated, although cooperation offers advantages in rapidly addressing specific problems, complex issues in the real world often require collaborative approaches. Regrettably, without deliberate instructor guidance, most learners' habitus (Bourdieu, 1977) tends toward cooperative modes for handling complex problems, echoing the "that's not my responsibility" phenomenon observed in this study's problematic awareness. Team Resource Management (TRM) provides a concrete operational framework for promoting collaboration. TRM originated from Crew Resource Management in the aviation industry and was later adapted for healthcare. Taiwan's Joint Commission on Hospital Accredit ation introduced the American TeamSTEPPS system (Team Strategies and Tools to Enhance Performance and Patient Safety) in 2008, naming it Team Resource Management. The TRM framework encompasses five core elements: leadership and team structure, situation monitoring, communication, mutual support, and decision making (Salas, DiazGranados, Klein, Burke, Stagl, Goodwin, & Halpin, 2008).

Given that medical education accreditation standards (ACGME: Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education) list "collaborating with healthcare teams to provide patient-centered care" as one of six core competencies, the TRM framework is particularly suitable as a pedagogical approach for promoting collaboration in medical education. This study transforms TRM' s five elements into concrete, operationalizable role and task designs: for instance, establishing roles such as "core knowledge presenter," "empirical case analyst," "data verifier," and "report and design integrator" in team presentations. Each role must  proactively confirm task progress among members, provide mutual support, and jointly use assessment tools for decision-making, thereby concretizing TRM's abstract principles.

Assessment of teamwork: From recording to promoting Traditional assessment mechanisms often fail to effectively promote learner growth. Benjamin Bloom identified two ineffective assessment patterns: first, when assessment is disconnected from learner achievement; second, 485 when assessment serves merely as a record of learning (cited in Wiliam & Thompson, 2008). Under this paradigm, assessment often becomes a restrictive concept for learners, actually diminishing learning motivation and depth. Not until the late 1980s was the  concept of "appropriately using assessment to improve learning" widely recognized (Wiliam & Thompson, 2008), gradually transforming assessment into a tool for promoting learning. Assessment that promotes learning requires embedded learning guidance mechanisms in tool design. In the 1990s, "formative assessment" gradually gave way to "assessment for learning" due to definitional ambiguity (Black & Wiliam, 1998). The core of this paradigm shift lies in effective assessment tools being actually usable by learners and leaving traceable learning trajectories and intervention traces (Wiliam, 2011). According to Sadler's (1989) assessment theory, assessment that promotes learning must satisfy three conditions: learners must understand standards of quality performance, be able to evaluate their current performance, and take action to close the gap between the two.

This study develops a "TRM Team Collaboration Content Assessment Table" integrating TRM capability frameworks with assessment-for-learning principles. This assessment table functions not only as a summative assessment tool but more importantly as scaffolding (Wood, Bruner, & Ross, 1976) during the learning process, guiding learners in self-monitoring and peer assessment during collaborative processes. By explicating specific indicators of collaboration (such as "proactively providing conceptual tools," "confirming content consistency," "mutual support"), the assessment table transforms abstract collaborative concepts into observable, operationalizable concrete behaviors, aligning with Wiggins' (1998) concept of "educative assessment." Situated learning: Legitimate Peripheral Participation This study adopts "Legitimate Peripheral Participation" (LPP) from situated learning theory as its primary theoretical sensitivity (Glaser, 1978). This theoretical framework differs from the cognitive paradigm using individuals as the unit of analysis and viewing knowledge as objects internalized in the brain. Situated learning theory adopts the participation paradigm, approaching from the perspective of social practice and viewing learning as a process of gradually participating in communities of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Situated learning emphasizes learning's essence as a situated participatory process. From this theoretical perspective, learning occurs within everyday practice contexts, and no one can learn disconnected from context. The essence of learning activities li es in participation in specific situations, continuously engaging in meaningmaking and understanding through participation. Therefore, the participation paradigm concerns learners' holistic actions in the world, portraying learning as a relational phenomenon—a dynamic process of continuous evolution and renewal involving people, actions, and the world. "Legitimate Peripheral Participation" does not merely mean "learning in situations" or "situations in learning" but represents a holistic concept: "Situated Learning: Legitimate Peripheral Participation." The core of this concept lies in providing "learning opportunities," namely opportunities for learners to access learning resources. Using African tailor apprentices as an example, Lave illustrates that unlike schools concentrating knowledge authority in teachers' hands, in communities of practice, learning resources are distributed among community members and various operational elements, with learners learning by approaching learning resources with specific structures (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Making structural resources transparent is key to promoting successful learning. Structural resources refer to learning resources ubiquitous in practice fields that form specific structures. However, these resources are sometimes deliberately segregated through instructional design or fail to become transparent due to management factors, leading to learning failure. Lave and Wenger (1991) use supermarket butcher apprentices as a classic case: the apprentice, due to management considerations, was assigned to a separate room and could only access processing of specific parts, resulting in learning only that part while remaining ignorant of others. In contrast, traditional market butchers work in open spaces where apprentices can observe other butchers' tool se lection, operational sequences, force control, etc., naturally acquiring comprehensive skills. Translating situated learning theory to this study's context requires identifying factors that may hinder learning resource transparency in medical education. Phenomena presented in this study's problematic awareness such as "that's not my responsibility" and "unrelated to other groups' presentations" reflect structural blockages of learning resources: when learners limit attention to their individual responsibilities, other members' presentation content, instructor feedback to other groups, and concrete TR M collaborative operations all fail to become transparent due to this "habitus." Therefore, this study's instructional design must deliberately promote accessibility and transparency of learning resources, for instance, through role designs requiring proac tive confirmation of mutual status and using common assessment tables for decision-making, thereby making previously implicit collaborative knowledge explicit and accessible to learners. 486

## Participants and setting

Participants in this study include medical students enrolled in target courses during the 2024 academic year (113 academic year in Taiwan) and the researcher. The research setting spans two semesters: in response to Taiwan Medical Accreditation Council (TMAC) course equivalency requirements, student numbers increased from 40 in the fall semester (1131, "Medicine, Culture, and Systems Perspectives," second-year fall) to 74 in the spring semester (1132, "From Patient Behavior to Doctor-Patient Relationships,"  second-year spring). Notably, 23 students completed both semesters of this teaching practice research project, providing a longitudinal sample for observing learning developmental trajectories.

The research setting encompasses multiple learning spaces, including physical classrooms, the online learning platform I'm@TMU, and students' commonly used interactive applications (such as Facebook Messenger, LINE, Instagram). This multi-site configuration serves dual research purposes: first, providing bases for adjustment decisions in action research cycles; second, offering rich data foundations for research analysis and writing. As Lave and Wenger (1991) emphasized, learning occurs in authentic practice fields; thus, capturing cross-site learning patterns is crucial for understanding collaborative development. This study employed action research methodology, cyclically conducting "planning-action-observationreflection" phases (Kemmis & McTaggart, 2013). Data collection strictly followed research ethics protocols, with explicit invitations in the first class emphasizing voluntary participation without affecting course rights or grades. Data sources included: (1) online learning trajectories (I'm@TMU interaction records, Google Document collaboration processes); (2) completion processes of the "TRM Team Collaboration Content Assessment Table";

(3) field notes from informal pre/post-class conversations; (4) in-depth interviews following IRB

recommendations after final grades, with 3 interviewees per semester using purposive sampling representing different learning patterns (high/medium/low grades and engagement), including one extreme case showing transformation from "low grades, low engagement" (1131) to "high grades, high engagement" (1132); (5) researcher reflective notes employing reflexivity strategies (Mannheim, 1993).

## Implications of grade-driven engagement

These findings appear to highlight that while most medical students possess strong intellectual capabilities, they rarely engage in dialogue to reduce conflict, completely avoiding situations rather than listening to and understanding others. Although avoi dance represents one conflict-reduction strategy, it eliminates opportunities for continuous refinement of thinking. Conversely, listening to or understanding others' perspectives and leaving behind expressions of disagreement can initiate opportunities for discourse consistency and continuous improvement of ideas. Remarkably, this awareness emerged from teachers' final grade assignments. While gratifying that learners began actively engaging in dialogue, concerns arose about whether "passive requirements for posting and interaction" driven by grades might undermine authentic engagement. 490 This study focuses on collaborative learning practices in medical education settings. Through curriculum implementation and observation, it reveals multiple challenges learners face and potential directions for transformation. The following converges research findings into three core dimensions with concrete suggestions and reflections.

From superficial cooperation to authentic collaboration The primary finding reveals learners in medical education collaborative settings often present inconsistent arguments and lack genuine mutual verification. They focus on their own tasks during peer presentations rather than engaging as a learning community, viewing "division of labor" as collaboration rather than true teamwork— a distinction Dillenbourg (1999) clarifies.

While division of labor completes tasks efficiently, it neglects interpersonal negotiation and collective decision-making. This habit may impair learners' future clinical teamwork and healthcare quality, contradicting ACGME's teamwork competency. Educators must redesign collaborative activities to emphasize mutual exchange and co-construction over task completion.

This study recommends educators prioritize "collaboration" over "division of labor" and implement process-quality assessments like peer feedback and interaction records to promote viewpoint integration (Wiliam, 2011). Instructors should create interdependent situations requiring authentic collaboration for complete learning, consistent with Johnson and Johnson's (1999) positive interdependence principle. Reconciling assessment with collaborative learning goals Relying solely on learning outcomes to assess collaborative capability is insufficient in medical education. Learners' past successes often stem from individual effort or division-of-labor task completion, which becomes resistance tonew collaborative appr oaches—reflecting Bourdieu's (1977) habitus concept. Problematically, learning becomes focused on "obtaining high scores," causing learners to view collaboration as a means to meet assessment standards rather than core professional competence, distorting m otivation and educational purpose. Cultivating genuine collaborative capabilities requires "quadruple integration" of curriculum, activities, materials, and assessment —embedding collaboration throughout the learning process rather than as an external form, consistent with Wiggins and McTighe's (2005) backward design. This helps learners recognize collaboration as an indispensable professional capability, not an additional burden. This study recommends educators integrate collaboration into all curriculum structures through quadruple-integrated design. For example, incorporating collaborative processes into formal assessment or using cross-case comparisons in materials to demonstrat e collaboration's necessity, aligned with Sadler's (1989) assessment-for-learning principles. Only when collaboration is institutionally embedded in curriculum objectives can learners understand that "learning to collaborate" is itself an important medical  professional capability. Repositioning learner agency From learning theory perspectives, this study offers new insights. Traditionally, educators attribute learning deficits to insufficient resources —instructors, clinicians, or administrators —echoing Lave and Wenger's (1991) concerns about resource transparency.

Contemporary learners demonstrate significant agency—not as passive recipients but as active decision-makers shaped by experience and judgment, consistent with Holland et al.'s (1998) agency concept. Learning insufficiency cannot be attributed solely to re source deficiency; learners' subjective roles must be examined. Learners may selectively engage with new knowledge or adjust strategies in response to assessment, reflecting Zimmerman's (2002) self-regulated learning. Learning theory must therefore reposit ion learners beyond knowledge constructors, emphasizing their agency, judgment, and value choices. Effective educational design requires attention to both resource provision and learner action to comprehensively explain learning phenomena and enhance outcomes.

This study suggests future curriculum design and medical education research must increasingly emphasize learners' subjective roles, viewing them as co-designers in learning processes. Instructors can attempt to provide more open-ended learning tasks, allow ing learners to exercise agency in self-planning, problem-solving, and value choices. Simultaneously, educational researchers should develop analytical methods capturing learner agency to more comprehensively understand multiple causes of learning success and failure. This shift will help establish teaching theories and practice contexts more aligned with medical professional needs, advancing medical education toward more subjective and professional directions. 491 Bamberger, J., & Schön, D.
