# Reframing Design in Education: Proposing a Framework to Support Pre-Service Teachers in Adopting Designerly Stances

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Guided by contemporary theory and research from design fields and education, we propose a framework for conceptualizing K-12 pre-service teachers’ stances toward designerly thinking. We developed the framework following Crismond and Adams’ (2012) scholarship of integration approach combined with an inductive content analysis of 43 pre-service teachers’ conceptualizations of design across two versions of a design course. The resulting framework focuses on five dimensions including: (a) co-evolution of problem frames and possible solutions, (b) problem complexity and structure, (c) idea fluency and scope, (d) inquiry and stakeholder engagement, and (e) conceptualization of the design process. We elaborate each dimension, proposing anchor points based on the literature and an inductive content analysis of the data. Detailed examples will be presented in the conference session, and a full version of the paper is under review and is available by email from the first author.

## Introduction

Traditional classroom practices in North America re sult in poor outcomes and engagement for many stude nts (Gallup, 2016; Harry & Klingner, 2014; Hooks, 1994;  Meier, 2004; Neckerman, 2019; Peske & Haycock, 200 6; Willms, Friesen, & Milton, 2009). K12 teachers need  support in developing their practices and identiti es as designers to better serve students beyond basic con tent mandates. This study proposes a framework for conceptualizing and supporting K12 pre-service teachers in developing mindsets and practices as designers that draw on perspectives from the design fields. As wit h Goldman and colleagues (2020) and others, our goa l is not to support K12 teachers in developing the full prac tices of professional designers from traditional de sign fields, but rather to support the potential for fundamental changes in the experiences and classroom structures they design for their students. We view this framework as a fir st step toward developing and validating a more dee ply articulated learning progression (cf. Duncan & Hmelo-Silver, 2009) of core design competencies for K12 teachers.

## Background

While Schön (1987) advocated for the integration of designerly thinking (which might promote such efforts) into teacher education programs over thirty years ago, this call has only recently gained a degree of traction within the wider field of education (Androutsos & Brinia, 2019; Authors, in press; Henriksen & Richardson, 2017; Norton & Hathaway, 2015; Razzouk & Shute, 2012). Research has increasingly highlighted the ways that stakehol dercentered approaches to design can help K12 teachers  become more adap tive, flexible, and innovative designers of learning for their students (e.g., Boloudakis et  al., 2018; Wu et al., 2019) as well as support the ir students in design (e.g., Borge et al., 2020; Carroll et al., 2 010; diSalvo et al., 2017; Goldman et al., 2014; Goldman et al., 2016; Gravel & Svihla, 2021; Smirnov et al., 2018).  Paralleling the situation in other fields, however, implementations in education often fail to leverage  and build upon the research from the design fields  on designerly thinking (Johansson‐Sköldberg et al., 2013; Kimbell, 2011; Sosa, 2015). We propose that the f ield of education could draw more deeply across the design literature in conceptualizing a framework to suppor t K12 teachers in design. The goal of this manuscript is therefore to propose a framework as an initial step  toward a more fully articulated learning progression around designerly thinking for K12 teachers.

## Process and methods

Paralleling Crismond and Adams’ (2012) scholarship of integration approach, we begandeveloping the framework by reviewing the research on designerly t hinking from the design fields to define the concept “where the intended outcome is a set of key performance dimensions pertinent to the selected learning targets (Duncan & Hmelo-Silver, 2009)” (Crismond & Adams, 2012, p. 742). We next began bounding design performances within baseline and upper anchors that are appropriate lea rning targets for pre-service teachers based on research from the design fields and education fields  and an inductive analysis of our data. We follow Cr ismond and Adams

(2012) in selecting a baseline in design capability  for our framework as “the behavior and thinking of  students

with little or no experience and no formal training in designing – the beginning designer” (p. 743). Also paralleling Crismond and Adams, we set the upper anchor of the framework as “the ‘informed designer,’ whose level of competence lies somewhere between that of the novice and expert designer. This middle stage of design expertise is appropriate for pre-service and earl y in-service teachers considering that (a) deep expertis e in most fields requires many years of extensive deliberate practic e and (b) the goals, practices, and responsibilitie s of K12 teachers are not identical to those of designers in  traditional design fields. The mandatory design course for pre-service teachers that provides the context for this manuscript has an enrollment of approximately 450 students per year across approximately 15 sections at a universi ty in western Canada. All students whose work w as analyzed consented to participate in the research. Analyses from both versions of the course focused on three data sources: pre-reflections, post-reflections, and design projects. Defining designerly thinking for education: Proposed framework Current perspectives on design in the design fields  are strongly influenced by Schön’s (1984) construc tionist theory of design as a reflective practice. Schön’s (1984) perspectives on reflective practice addressed limitations he saw in prevailing technical  rational problem-solving perspectives on design (e.g., Simon, 1969).  Whereas technical rational problem-solving perspectives on design assume a fairly well-defined problem, Schön’s perspectives focus on the structuring role of the d esigner wherein the definition of the design challe nge and the exploration of possible solutions co-evolve through consideration of different ways to “ frame” the design challenge and possible solutions. As Dorst (2003) explains, if we view problems as well-structured, then designers can engage deductively in design as a technical rat ional process, but if we view problems as ill-structured, then design requires an abductive process (Cross, 1982; Dorst, 2010) that involves developing frames to co-evolve our understanding of both pr oblem and solution (Dorst & Cross, 2001). In fact, even when problems appear well-structured, designers should approach and engage with them as though they are ill-structured (Cross, 2006). Design integrates these abductive framing processes  with a heavy emphasis on synthesis wherein information is collected, evaluated for potential s ignificance, and explored in terms of key relations hips within the larger context of the design challenge (Kolko, 2010). These synthetic processes provide the founda tion for framing and reframing the design challenge and poss ible solutions. Furthermore, from a designerly thin king perspective, these synthetic processes should highl ight and leverage stakeholder pers pectives as central in identifying focal elements and relationships to des ign with and around stakeholders (Krippendorff, 200 4; Luck, 2018; Sanders & Stappers, 2008).

If one were to point to key distinctions between these views on designerly thinking and those popularized by IDEO, the focus on abductive reframing is minimi zed in IDEO’s process template, which highlights th e importance of defining a design challenge after exp loring the design context but does not highlight th e centrality of abductively exploring new frames to co-evolve the problem definition and possible solution s. Because it does not explicitly explore and co-evolve frames and solutions, the simpler IDEO template increases the likelihood of maintaining the original frame, which in turn can l imit design to incremental change. As Dorst (2015) explains, challenging problems cannot be solved within the frame in which they arise. Thus, for the purposes of this manuscript, we defin e designerly thinking as: an iterative process of synthesis and abduction wherein the framing of a de sign challenge and possible solutions co-evolve through extensive reflective conversation and exploration a mongst contextual elements and relationships. The s ynthetic processes shou ld highlight and leverage stakeholder perspectives,  and optimally their participation and co-design, as central in identifying focal elements and relationships.  We further integrate into our definition the perspectives of Buchanan (2001) that design focuses  not only on usability but also on supporting the d ignity and goals of the stakeholders. Although Buchanan’s pers pectives are not universally adopted by resear chers of designerly thinking, we propose that teachers should enact frames with this focus on dig nity and equity to better serve students who traditionally have not been well served. Based on the definition of designerly thinking outl ined above, we propose that a designerly thinking framework for K12 teachers might be conceptualized in terms of six dimensions. Three of the dimensions emphasize problem space and framing. The first dime nsion, co-evolution of problem frames and possible solutions, focuses on the degree to which K12 teachers assum e problems are fixed versus assuming that the definition of problems should co-evolve as current and new frames and solutions are considered and explored. The second dimension, problem complexity and structure, focuses on the degree to which K12 teachers recognize the emergent and fluid nature of challenges in clas srooms as well as the complex causal factors implic ated in those challenges. The third dimension, idea fluency and scope, focuses on the degree to which K12 teachers engage with a broad range of ideas and frames inclu ding ideas and frames beyond traditional discourses  in education. The fourth dimension, inquiry and stakeholder engagement, focuses on the degree to which K12 teachers engage in active inquiry into the needs and perspectives of students and other stakeholders, with a particular emphasis on students who have traditiona lly not been well served by school, and actively so licit their perspectives and participation in design where feasible. The final two dimensions emphasize design process. The fifth, conceptualization of the design process, focuses on engaging in a managed and systematic a pproach to design where ideas are productively iterated upon and strategies are productively employed as needed. The sixth, attitude toward ambiguity, focuses on the degree of acceptance and embrace o f the inherent ambiguity of the design process. Table 1 presents the dimensions and  anchors. Table 1 Dimensions and Anchors Beginning Designers Informed Designers Dimension 1 Co-Evolution of Problem Frames and Possible Solutions Beginning designers treat design challenges as well-defined and determined problems (usually focused on efficient delivery of content objectives or behavior management) and prematurely attempt to solve without considering opportunities to reconceptualize aspects of a design challenge in ways that might present feasible approaches to better serve students. Beginning designers thus often assume that design challenges are predefined in their entirety by either curricular mandates or traditional classroom framings of those challenges.

Informed designers treat design challenges as underdetermined. Definition of educational challenges co-evolve with possible solutions as alternative frames are explored or at least considered from new framings or positionings (e.g., considering the root or underlying problems that might suggest potential alternative frames) even if the original framing/positioning is eventually adopted. The goal of this co-evolution focuses on exploring reframings of the challenge that might provide approaches that can be feasibly implemented by the teacher to better serve students within the curricular mandates and other pragmatic demands of the classroom.

Dimension 2 Problem Complexity and Structure Beginning designers focus on routine and narrow issues in education such as content delivery or behavior management as though they are isolated issues with no consideration of how challenges in these areas might be surfacelevel manifestations of underlying ill-structured or complex factors (e.g., considering selfregulation only in terms of behavior management or considering teaching math only from rationalist academic disciplinary lenses).

Informed designers consider and explore potential causal and complex factors and connections to larger structural, contextual, systemic, cultural, historical, and societal factors (e.g., considering how questions of selfregulation are actually embedded in complex issues of gender norms of society, disproportional penalization of minoritized youth, and other complex factors that move curricular design in the classroom beyond a rationalist, individualist, and academic perspective).

Dimension 3 Idea Fluency and Scope Beginning designers work with few ideas and frames, or even just one, and become fixated or stuck on these ideas and frames. They may exhibit resistance to changing or discarding these ideas. Ideas are usually drawn from traditional content-focused discourses or typical progressive student-centered discourses (e.g., shallow focus on student interests as a veneer that does not change some aspect of traditional goals, structures, or roles of classroom activities or management).

Dimension 5 Design Process Beginning designers either follow a template of design steps once in a linear order (often in the case of backward design processes or an IDEOinspired approach that overly formalizes sequences of steps) or design in a haphazard manner without systematic or cumulative exploration.

Informed designers design in a managed and systematic approach where ideas are productively iterated upon and strategies are productively employed as needed toward the goal of developing feasible approaches to restructure aspects of curriculum and practice. A focus on design ideally continues beyond initial development across implementation and practice (e.g., ongoing iteration and reflection across sections and years to improve).

Dimension 6 Attitude Toward Ambiguity Beginning designers either do not acknowledge messiness and ambiguity in the design process or they express substantial aversion to it. They may view challenges, dead ends, frustrations, and questions that emerge during the design process as evidence of their failure or insufficiency as teachers.

Informed designers can clearly articulate the messiness and ambiguity inherent in the design process along with some level of embracing it. They view the ambiguity of such encounters not as failures on their part but rather as a healthy and inherent part of the design process.

Teachers take on a growth mindset and view failure and iteration as part of learning.
