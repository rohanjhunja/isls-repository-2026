# Static and Changing Roles in Transdisciplinary Co-Design

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Transdisciplinary computing arts experiences provide potential to merge practices and cultures from across disciplines to create an inclusive learning environment. However, this can be hard to do, especially when instructors within these spaces feel their expertise lies mainly within either computing or the arts. In our work, we analyzed facilitator reflections on the codesign and implementation of a 14-week online computing-arts experience designed to integrate computing, poetry, and visual arts. We found that focusing on the static and changing roles assumed by the co-designers provided a lens to understand how to build knowledge across one another’s disciplines in the pursuit of creating new practices within transdisciplinary education.

## Introduction

Co-design has been defined as a “team-based process in which teachers, researchers, and developers work together in defined roles to design an educational innovatio n, realize the design in one or more prototypes, and evaluate each prototype’s significance for addressing a concrete educational need,” (Roschelle et al., 2006). The practice emphasizes collaborative meaning-making, connecting  design to a real context for learning, and integra ting reflective practices for developing an understandin g of the design. In transdisciplinary education, on ce siloed disciplines have the opportunity to become something new as the practices, norms, and cultures merge (Nicolescu, 1999). Much of the work in artistic computing education emphasizes how learners can explore CS concepts in a discipline that they care about as they leverage their prior knowledge to develop computing-artwork that becomes meaningful through the expressivity of learners’ id entities and cultures and ability to be shared beyo nd the environment. Few works examine how arts educational  practices can change processes within the learning environment outside of the artifacts. Our work expl ores how a co-design team composed of three profess ional teaching artists and three CS education researchers  from New York University and Georgia Tech reflecte d on roles within the design and implementation of an online physical computing, visual, and literary arts experience. We analyze teaching activities, individual interviews, an instructor focus group, and learners’ final presentations examining the research question:  How do roles emerge and change within a co-designe d computing-arts experience among its facilitators?

## Methods

This research took place within Community-Word Project, a non-profit arts organization that provides K-12 arts education within New York City public schools. During the 2020-21 school year, the facilitation team co-designed a remote curriculum in which students incorporated elements of their identities and cultures in conjunction with art activities and physical computing “tech” kits to create artifacts belonging to socio-futurist communities. The tech kits contained introductory programmable digital components, such as a BBC micro:bit, servos and sensors. We collected semi-structured interviews and a one h our focus group with all facilitators to capture th e facilitators’ thoughts, feelings, and personal gains from the co-design experience. We also obtained a  recording of the curriculum’s 2-hour finale in which students  presented their final projects to each other, pare nts, and community members. The data was analyzed using thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2012). We identified how facilitators reflected on various elements of the p rogram and their role in supporting learners and me diating between disciplines. Through multiple rounds of cod ing transcriptions of the collected data, we genera ted a codebook composed of themes of exercising roles within the planning and execution of the program.

## Findings and discussion

Assumed, avoided, and adopted roles Each facilitator approached the collaboration with a strong sense of identity and confidence in their own skills and contributions, therefore assuming a specific role within the co-design process. Among the co-designers, the ICLS2022 Proceedings 2204 © ISLS roles of tech-person, art-person, and researcher were used to describe themselves and each other. W hile the facilitators were interested in crossing the disciplinary content, they still connected primarily to these roles. Facilitators also distanced themselves from specific activities and disciplines. For example, two teaching artists felt disconnected from the research efforts of the program and deferred research-oriented role s to the CS ed researchers. Avoided roles became apparent when discussing facilitators’ varying levels of comfort with computing technologies, as one of the teaching arti sts candidly said referred to herself as “not a tec h person” despite a history in making digital art. In contrast, one CS ed researcher distanced herself from literary arts, saying she does not consider herself someone who practices  poetry. We observed that facilitators openly expre ssed uncertainty and concerns around how the different roles would contribute to the curricular goals and shared vision for the program. Some facilitators found it challen ging to envision how the tangible art making and ph ysical computing practices would be integrated throughout the curriculum, both in students’ artifacts and in the literary activities preceding them. One teaching artist was most concerned with being unable to support the stu dents in the computing tech components, while a CS ed researcher was concerned with overshadowing the artistic learning goals with computing in the curriculum.

Facilitators discussed acquiring knowledge and skil ls that had the potential to contribute to their ow n identities, empowering them to adopt new roles duri ng the program and after. One teaching artist who a t first struggled with the writing and poetry activities and self-identified as a “visual person” was really m otivated to start her own journal (an activity students practiced in the program) and write more. The teaching artists each had their own tech kit so that we could test out the in structional resources and enable them to play with,  build, and program a circuit. One of the teaching artists shared how she found the electronics challenging at first, but that it led to valuable learning that she was happy to receive. The CS ed researchers did not experiment with the a rts activities since there was less uncertainty of failure of those activities. However, they did reflect on learning from teaching artists’ disciplinary practices. For example, one researcher reflected on a writing exer cise led by a teaching artist after the verdict of the George Floyd police violence case was announced. They were  impressed with the “ways [the teaching artists] ha ndle different situations,” and how it led to insightful discussion with the students. Another CS ed researcher echoed this sentiment and saw new purpose in writing and talking about one’s personal community. Pushing beyond static roles We found that the facilitators’ instructional and disciplinary practices aligned with the roles they assumed in the co-design process. Further, as they distanced themselves from certain roles, they also distanced themselves from certain disciplinary practices—i.e., not practicing  poetry. Despite building new knowledge through an understanding of each other’s roles, a majority of the co-design led to recognition rather than implem entation, such as the CS ed facilitators recognizing the powe r of the poetry activities but never leading any. O ur work suggests that embodying roles within an interdiscip linary environment provides opportunities for identity work that can help instructors bridge disciplinary boundaries in order to push beyond them. While the teaching artists identified the ways they learned about computing as a discipline, the CS ed researchers did not identify learning art practices. We hypothesize that this may have been because the CS ed researchers did not engage in enacting roles of the teaching artists in the arts activities while the teaching artists did so with the computing activities. Disciplinary and instructional roles also carried implications for facilitating greater engagement of the co-design team with the role of researcher. The role of researcher was dominated by the CS ed researchers, which created an imbalance in the focus and practices enacted within the research. Prior to assisting with data analysis, the teaching artists felt disconnected from that aspect of the co-design. This is importan t because researchers in these spaces are more often coming from computing d isciplines than the arts. Drawing on participatory action research approaches could present useful tools to e ngage teaching artists in research framing and inve stigation design in order to center the role of researcher in the co-design process and address power imbalances.
