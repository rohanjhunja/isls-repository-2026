# Responsiveness: A Feature for Predicting the Productivity of Non-Convergent Discussions

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This work focuses on estimating the impact of produced arguments on the discussion's upcoming development. We sought to define a feature upon which the productivity of non-convergent discussions can be predicted. We rely on the Bakhtinian notion of responsiveness, the degree to which a speaker embeds or builds on the arguments of an interlocutor. We demonstrate the potential of this feature, using a corpus of 10,000 threads extracted from Reddit's 'Change My View' forum. Utilizing Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique, we experimented with several supervised machine learning algorithms, each of which drew on a different set of selected features. The performance of the obtained models was evaluated through repeated stratified 10-fold cross-validation. Our preliminary results are encouraging: responsiveness contributes to accurate prediction of discussion productivity.

## Introduction

Non-Convergent Discussion (NCD) is a discussion that is neither pre-framed by nor aimed at converge nce, i.e. reaching a shared conclusion, solution, or consensual decision. However, despite the lack of convergence and even because of it, such discussions can be productive; they can serve as a f ruitful venue for the development of dialogic agency, providing the opportunity to examine ideas in light of peers’ dis agreements, critiques, and alternative perspectives (Bakhtin, 1981; Matusov & von Duyke, 2009). Therefore, NCDs are of great importance in many educational settings (e.g., civics, literature, and history classes). The work in progress presented here is part of an endeavor aimed at monitorin g and scaffolding online NCDs. We focus on one main objective of this endeavor: to estimate the impac t of produced arguments on the discussion's upcoming development. Such assessment could be then utilized to denote, in real-time, the potential productivity of NCDs. Indeed, many studies in the field of Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) provide frameworks and tools for measuring the quality of an argument in order to indicate productivity. Yet, for the most part, these measurements miss out on how the argument may shape the content (and the quality) of the unfolding discussion (Spatariu et al., 2004).

Our guiding principle is that the quality of arguments impacts the quality of the r est of the discussion. We rely on Bakhtin’s (1981) dialogical theory, in particular, the concept of polyphony-the inter-animation of previously-uttered ideas and arguments in the speaker’s voice. When interlocutors ec ho (re-voice) each other preceding thoughts — converging or diverging from them — they realize essential dialogicity, which in turn promotes productivity. Our approach is in line with the empirical work by Wise et al. (2014). Investigating the relationship between ways of reading a post (online listening) and the quality of writ ing (speaking) a new one, they found that spending time on or re-reading previous peers' posts was a positive predictor of students’ responsiveness (i.e. being attuned to those uttered voices), what increased the quality of following posts. Bakhtin’s ideas of polyphony and dialogicity have inspired works, in which tools of Machine Learning (ML) and Natural Language Processing  (NLP) were employed to enable tutors/participants to evaluate participants’ contribution or involvement in a discussion. For example, Trausa n-Matu et al. (2014) developed PolyCAFe, an automatic system for a polyphonic analysis of e-discussions; Dascalu et a l. (2022) harnessed Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT), a state-of-the-art model of NLP, to pinpoint points of view (voices), which can be operationalized as semantic chains that contain related words or concepts. The abovementioned works focused on the individuals’ contribution or involvement in th e discussion and sought to compute responsiveness  (previous speakers, current speaker), that is, the degree to which one embeds or builds on the arguments of (all) interlocutors that spoke before. In this work, we e mploy the concepts of polyphony and dialogicity to infer about discussion productivity as a whole. We def ine the responsiveness of the discussion as the average responsiveness of its participants, namely Average (∑ responsiveness [previous speakers, speaker]). Our theoretical premise is that the more responsive the interlocutors are to previously-uttered ideas, the more potentially productive the discussion. The feature of respons iveness celebrates the theoretical stance that a productive NCD is an arena for a continuous exchange of new ide as and arguments, where interlocutors listen to and build on each other. Non-responsive exchange —even if wonderful ideas are uttered— CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 198 is a monologic discourse, which can be characterized by zero listening or full adoption of voices with no novel (let alone challenging) thought.

First, we explain and justify how we decided to compute the feature of r esponsiveness. Then, using Reddit's 'ChangeMyView' (CMV) forum, we examine the contribution of this f eature to predict discussion productivity. Finally, we discuss the possibilities and limitations of responsiveness. Rationale As mentioned above, our main objective is to estimate the impact of produced ar guments on the discussion's upcoming development. We hypothesized that responsiveness — as part of a set of selected features — could be exploited in a supervised manner to train ML algorithms to be capable of classifying, with high accuracy, potential (un)productive discussions. We examined the accuracy of the obtained models using NCDs extracted from Reddit's CMV, which we now turn to describe.

Reddit’s ChangeMyView (r/CMV) Reddit is an American social media, news-sharing, and discussion website tha t serves as a platform for millions of anonymous users within hundreds of thousands of forums termed 'Subreddits'. Change My View (CMV) is a 'Subreddit' (1) containing tens of thousands of asynchronous, argumentative NCDs on diverse topics. It is selfdescribed as a place to post an opinion you believe might be flawed, in an effort to understand other  perspectives; thus fostering a mindset for conversation rather thandebate. Each discuss ion evolves around the topic presented in the submission by the Original Poster (OP). CMV is heavily moderated to  maintain proper conversations. Also, a gamification mechanism is used to encourage users to respond to each other; whoever succeeded to shift or expand (i.e. change) the view of the OP can be rewarded with a badge termed Delta (∆). Owing to the high quality of discussions in CMV, they are commonly used for various NLP and social science research, ranging from argument mining to the study of the effec ts of forum norms and moderation. In particular, efforts have been made to gain insights into argumentation and pers uasion strategies, as well as the broader influence on people's engagement in a discussion (Dutta et al., 2020; Na  & DeDeo, 2022; Nguyen & Young, 2022). The Delta reward is perceived in those studies as ground truth for  carrying out their analysis. Correspondingly, rewarding a Delta in our case serves as an indicator for a productive discussion, since it is a declaration of change of view, hence for learning that probably occurred. Our decision to rely on the Delta reward as an indication of productivity is in line with bayesian argumentation; the idea of evaluating (the productivity of) an argument based on the magnitude of belief change it brings about (Eva & Hartmann, 2018; Hahn, 2020). Furthermore, the work by Xiao and Me nsah (2022) strengthens our hypothesis that high responsiveness promotes productivity. They have noticed the following process: the first post in a CMV discussion brings up a new idea or perspective, which is matured through a fusion of new information or elaboration in the next posts, thereby each following post is more likely to receive a Delta than the previous one. Based on these studies, we decided that a CMV dataset is ideal for our purpose. Corpus We extracted 10,000 threads (e-discussions) that took place between April 2017 and January 2018 on CMV. Due to the nature of CMV, the overall available discussions (regardless of a specific time period) encompassed diverse subjects (e.g., social, ethical, philosophical, fictional, scientific, political, religious, and so forth). To compensate for the highly unbalanced ratio between Delta/Non-Delta threads during data extraction, we decided to randomly sample 3000 threads that ended with delta and 7000 that did not. The threads were delimited to contain at least 3 posts (including the OP's), written by different users with no deleted posts or users. In addition, we removed the quoted content in each post (<quote>content</quote>), to prevent biased comparison of semantic similarity between posts.

## Methods

Selecting and generating features Pre-processing the corpus, we have generated the following four features from each thread: (I) Users_Count (the total number of different users in a thread), (II) Posts_Count (the total number of posts in a thread), (III) Avg_Words_Per_Post (the average number of words per post in a thread), and (IV) Responsiveness. The target variable was (Non-)Delta (binary class). We chose these features because they represent important characteristics of e-discussions, which potentially influence discussion's responsiveness, and thus should be taken into account CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 199 as part of our effort to predict productivity. For example, it is reasonable to assume that discussions with many posts may provide more opportunities to inter-animate previous posts compared to discussions with just a few. The responsiveness feature was a product of harnessing 'stsb-roberta-bas e-v2' (2), an NLP pre-trained model capable of mapping sentences and paragraphs to a 768-dimensional dense vector space; fine-tuned for the task of semantic textual similarity  (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019). Leveraging the unique architecture of BERT, such a model fits our goal as it can pick up context; drawing power also from  capturing the important meaning the order of words has in a sentence. We utilized this sentence-transformer model to compute a Cosine Similarity Score (CSS) for each pair of posts (a post and each post that follows, except f or those created by the speaker); denoting the degree to which their content is close in terms of meaning. The CSS ranges from 0 (indicating no similarity at all) to 1 (completely identical). After assigning a corresponding C SS for each pair of posts, we generated an Average Cosine Similarity Score  (Avg_CSS) from all the CSSs of the same thread. As a result, discussions’ responsiveness was obtained; reflecting the average polyphonic degree caused by the impact of interanimating arguments (towards divergence or convergence) as the discussion evolved. Training and testing supervised machine learning algorithms Out of the corpus, 3000 threads ended with a Delta, while 7000 did not. Namely, the dataset was unbalanced. Therefore, in order to use the above set of selected features in a super vised manner, Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was also applied (Chawla et al., 2002). SMOTE is an effective tec hnique to deal with an imbalanced dataset, by generating synthetic samples of the minority class to balance the data. We allocated 70% to training and 30% to testing (hold-out), proportionally to the distribution (stratified) of the binary (Non-)Delta class (0 or 1, respectively). The quality of the obtained mode ls was evaluated through Repeated Stratified Cross-Validation with 10 folds and 3 repeats, while the performance metrics are Weighted Average (W.Avg) Precision (Prec), Recall (Rec), and F1-Score (F1-S). Using the sklearn package (Pedregosa et al., 2011), we experimented with the following classifiers: Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), XGBoost (XGB), and Feedforward Neural Network  with Multilayer Perceptron  (FNN-MLP). Three combinations of the features were examined, each time generating a different model, to explore how responsiveness contributes to the prediction of Delta: (1) features with Responsiveness (F+R), (2) features without Responsiveness (F─R), and (3) only Responsiveness (R). To provide a strong baseline, we compared both the average performance  of SMOTE+Classifier (S+C) and the Weighted-Classifier (W-C); in case it was already implemented in sklearn. We report the best results per classifier, as well as the Average Feature Importances (Avg.FI, when available), and the model's performance on unseen real-world data (Test Set).

## Results

Table 1 shows the models' performance in terms of predicting the probabi lity that a discussion would (not) end with Delta. The results support our hypothesis. While W-XGB-F+R (W denotes w eighted) has achieved the highest prediction accuracy, the combination of all four features (F+R) consistently yielded the best outcomes, or at least the same as the models where responsiveness was omitted (F-R). Furthermore, feeding the models only with responsiveness (R) resulted in W.Avg F1-Score above 50%. The Avg.FI of the following models underscores the role responsiveness played (as a feature) within predicting productivity: W-RF-F+R (Avg_Words_Per_Post: 0.289, Responsiveness: 0.260, Users_Count: 0.238, Posts_Count: 0.214), W-XGB-F+R (Users_Count: 0.669, Posts_Count: 0.224, Avg_Words_Per_Post: 0.055, Responsiveness: 0.052). Also, the average responsiveness was found to be higher in Delta threads compared to Non-Delta threads (0.486 and 0.433, respectively). A two-sided permutation test on the responsiveness of  Delta/Non-D elta threads yielded a statistically significant result (p < 0.01; 10,000 rounds of permutations). We  thus conclude that responsiveness can serve as a feature for predicting discussion productivity.

## Discussion and conclusions

Our preliminary results are encouraging. Specifically, we found that responsiv eness combined with other three features predicts (Non-)Delta, i.e. discussion productivity, with good accuracy. Yet, more work is required to shed light on how responsiveness could be better modeled. Here, we used semantic similarities. However, we believe that other measurable variables can refine the modeling. The current fe ature should be further investigated, too: What is the optimum value of responsiveness, if at all exists? What other fe atures interact with responsiveness and how do they influence discussion productivity?

In the future, we strive to predict the potential productivity of NCDs outsid e CMV, and produce an automated tool for noninvasive assessment — focused on content rather than on interlocutors — which in the hands of teachers and moderators will enable intervention (when needed) before  it is too late. Later studies may CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 200 extend our work by testing responsiveness (among related features) with other ML algorithms, in order to achieve better results or different goals. Here, we only focused on providing initial p roof of concept, both theoretically and empirically; laying the ground for continuous research in this direction.
