# Action Research as a Professional Development: Fostering a Professional Learning Community in Brazil

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We exposed Brazilian teachers to a professional development (PD) program incorporating action research into teacher education and documented it in this study, evaluating how the program influenced educators' pedagogy in the school setting. We engaged teachers in a mentorship activity fostering reflection on classroom practice, engagement with theory, and academic dissemination. Results highlight growth in teachers' conscious awareness over classroom practices and the role of connecting theory and practical applications in education with action research.

Teacher education has been identified as a barrier to educational reform and has received broad investments in different countries so teachers can participate in professional development (PD) projects, such as action research, in which their teaching actio ns can become an object of reflection and investigation (Darling-Hammond, 2017; Lewin, 1946). In action research projects, individuals actively participate in understanding what constitutes improvement (Corey, 1954). The theoretical framework expands on conscious awareness (Vygotsky, 2001) practice by offering educators tools to support their disciplines and new skills to enhance the teaching-learning process, such as conceptual maps, readings, and discussions about didactic sequences. From a constructivist perspective, PD programs with action research are fundamentally based on classroom practice and the construction of professional learning communities (Borko et al., 2010). To this end, we developed and analyzed a PD program in Brazil that sought to prepar e teachers to act as researchers and users of research (Corey, 1954), realigning practice and theory through action research. We used teacher journals collected during the project as a main data source. Ultimately, this research aims to seed investigative communities so that teachers can experience and propagate the effects of the action research practices included in the program. Our findings converge with existing research on teacher education that demonstrates the potential of school-based professional development models as a device capable of creating continuous and collective professional learning communities (Borko et al., 2010).

Our team developed a mentoring program for five early childhood and elementary education teachers for these educators to discuss their projects and relate practice and theory. Teachers were selected based on pre-existing relationships, including four educators from a private school in southeastern Brazil, all with postgraduate degrees and extensive teaching experience, and one educator from a public school in midwestern Brazil, with five years of elementary childhood teaching experience.

The mentoring program had five repeating stages, adapted from Kemmis et al. (2014), using the spiral of self-reflection model. In the initial phase, educators shared their sources for evidence-gathering through various artifacts and documentation. Concurrently, they revisited their research and investigated the progressive development of each experiential aspect. Researchers encouraged teachers to think about research questions to pursue based on their evidence gathering. Subsequently, mentees did a guided literature review on pedagogy theory. Collaboration was encouraged to develop strategies for integrating theory into their classroom experiences. Then, we focused on writing and submitting conference papers. Mentorship and writing allowed educators to reflect on meeting discussions. Mentors reviewed each written piece and facilitated group-wide reflection. Meanwhile, academic experts provided feedback to the groups and translated their texts from Portuguese to English. Educators engaged in feedback discussions and revised their papers accordingly. In preparation for the 2351 prese ntation, they selected images and student quotes, and edited a video. Finally, teachers shared their conference experiences with their school peers, catalyzing learning communities within their educational institutions. The sources analyzed by the research team were (1) audio and video recordings, (2) transcripts of s essions between mentors and mentees, and (3) individual and group semi-structured interviews about their learning outcomes and self-reflection on the changes in their practice after taking part in the project. The primary objective was comprehending teachers’ challenges, fostering self-reflection, and establishing theoretical connections. From this, we observed the emergence of two levels of PD processes, informed by conscious awareness and mastery (Vygotsky, 2001), which are types of psych ological neoformations. Neoformations characterize changes that occur for the first time during someone's development.  These particularities of thought begin to operate from a previously undeveloped zone of development, enabling a person to understand the world from a more complex degree of development of conceptual thinking (Vygotsky, 2001). In the first level, teachers began viewing their teaching as an intentional and conscious activity for the first time. They recognized the need for meticulous planning that organizes its stages in a complementary manner and aimed at reaching greater levels of complexity regarding the subject addressed. Attaining this conscious awareness triggered substantial changes in the organization and planning of their teaching work. It enabled teachers to engage in a reflective examination of past practices and to project future teaching activities that are more systematic and personally meaningful.

In the second level, teachers reached even higher levels of conscious awareness and mastery regarding their practices. This occurred as mentoring activities offered the opportunity for in-depth analysis of selected didactic sequences through peer discussions and interactions with experts well-versed in relevant theories. Consequently, a more complex and refined framework was needed to achieve conceptual mastery and generalization. This enhanced teachers' capacity for intentional and voluntary management of their teaching practices. As a transformative outcome, upon the con clusion of the mentoring activities, teachers initiated the practice of fostering reflective discussions within their respective schools regarding ongoing pedagogical projects, starting a research-based learning community.

Action research is designed to create a professional environment for investigation, driven by the desire to improve personal practices and school community development (Corey, 1954; Hine, 2013). This research highlighted four main takeaways from the teacher's PD experience as described in the findings: (i) improve the conscious awareness ability through the appropriation of scientific concepts (Vygotsky, 2001), (ii) changes in the organization and planning, (iii) in-depth analysis of selected didactic seque nces and (iv) fostering reflective commitment of time beyond the classroom setting, which poses challenges for engagement.   Thus, it is essential that schools support PD programs and reduce teachers’ workload during their implementation as a form of achieving an investigation environment.
