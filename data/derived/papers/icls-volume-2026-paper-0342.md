# Hybridity, Equity, and Conference Design

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This hybrid symposium serves as a conversation and data gathering effort in ongoing iterative design of accommodations through hybrid conferencing in the International Society of the Learning Sciences (ISLS). We examine recent ISLS conferencing history borrowing the concept of “curb cuts” from disability studies to describe the diffusion of accommodation options. ISLS stakeholder communities (disabled scholars, caregivers, scholars from Majority World, conference organizers), rooted in relevant theory and research, will share perspectives and experiences of accommodation processes and/or the impact of multiple modalities for conference access. We will then engage attendees in discussion breakout groups, facilitated by volunteers across in-person and virtual modalities using Padlet, to generate knowledge and recommendations. We conclude the symposium with a discussion synthesis. The results of this discussion, artifact-generation, and synthesis will inform ongoing iterations of ISLS hybrid designs and conceptualizations of anticipatory design in academic conference spaces, within and beyond ISLS Annual Meetings.

In spring of 2020, during what we now know was the early  phase of the COVID-19 crisis, acclaimed novelist Arundhati Roy wrote, “Historically, pandemics have forced humans to break with the past and imagine their world anew. This one is no different. It is a portal, a gateway between one world and the next.” She  argued that we can move through this portal by dragging with us everything problematic about our pasts, or we can step through lightly, without baggage, opening ourselves tonew possibilities. While academic conferences like the annual meeting of the International Society of the Learning Sciences (ISLS) may seem like a small consideration in the face of the global economic inequalities Roy was describing, we too have an opening for new ways forward. The forced-virtual conferencing that characterized the quarantine years of the pandemic has, in some cases, posed opportunities for academic fields to reconsider how conferencing happens, while others have been determined to re-establish in-person normalcy (Beemer & Richards, 2025). It is perhaps not surprising that, while some incremental change has happened, ISLS has until now not sought opportunities to collectively, explicitly, and strategically re-think our approach, given three decades of conferencing as not just a means of gathering and sharing research, but as a form of identification and self-determination for an emerging field of study. Conferencing has been good to us, some might argue, so why would we change it? One answer to that question is to consider those for whom in-person conferencing has never been “good,” for whom a return to normal represents a return to inequality and a lack of access. Gloria Ladson-Billings

(2021) reminds us that post-pandemic transformations must be framed with equity in mind, not only for those

2214 who would benefit directly, but for all of us. “The point of the hard reset,” she argues, “is to reconsider what kind of human beings/citizens we are trying to produce” (p. 72). This innovative, hybrid symposium brings together several stakeholders to examine new possibilities and open dialogue across the ISLS community as part of multi-year Design-Based Research (DBR Collective,

While they spoke of disciplines such as science learning, we see their charge to disentangle received knowledge about what conferences have been  from what might be  when we take up “expansive and insurgent” ways of learning and growing our community through ISLS Annual Meetings (AMs). We wonder together, as members of several stakeholder communities, what designs for heterogeneity, centering marginalized experience and voice, might allow us to forge new futures as we innovate AM designs together. To prepare attendees to understand the context of this work, we begin with a brief review of ISLS Hybrid History and then frame our symposium through the lens of curb cuts to explore how the efforts of the founding coalition of hybrid innovators served the broader community’s emergent needs. ISLS Hybrid History As a field, the International Society of the Learning Sciences is committed to the interdisciplinary study of learning “as it exists in real-world settings and to how learning may be facilitated both with and without technology” (ISLS). ISLS has hosted Annual Meetings (AMs) that include two  conferences (International Conference of the Learning Sciences, International Conference of Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning) since 2021. Prior to 2021, separate bi-annual meetings had been held for each conference since the mid-1990s. Our history of gathering for conferences is rich. These Annual Meetings are international events which cover “timely and important issues and report research findings across the entire field of the Learning Sciences” (ISLS). Like many conferences, the ISLS Annual Meeting moved to fully virtual in 2020-2022 as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic. Despite the ongoing nature of that pandemic, ISLS AMs have shifted back to a fully in-person design since 2023. Unlike conferences that have refused disability access or other hybrid design features (AERA) or have relegated a virtual-day separate from in-person conference events (NARST), ISLS has chosen to explore how to support broad and rich virtual access via an accommodations process while maintaining traditional in-person commitments. ISLS has also founded a new Hybrid Engagement subCommittee (HEsC chair Keifert, member Ching) which liaises with the Annual Meeting Committee and each year’s Local Organizing Committee to slowly expand our hybrid competencies and imagination. Thus while ISLS AMs are in-person meetings, there are a variety of supports for those with access needs that are deserving of consideration. Of interest in the pres ent symposium is an exploration of the supplemental hybrid designs that have shaped ISLS AMs in 2024 (Buffalo) and 2025 (Helsinki), and that will continue to shape the present AM in 2026 (Irvine). Hybrid supplemental designs at ISLS were launched when a group of ISLS members authored a letter to the Equity & Justice Committee articulating concerns after AERA 2022. Their letter included the following: We hope to persuade our community to resist the draw to “return to normal” when “normal” has always been deeply inequitable for people with disabilities, marginalized identities, and those navigating persistent circumstances at the intersection of structural racism and homophobia/transphobia. We take seriously that attending to the most marginalized members of our community is integral to the mission of ISLS and serves all of us. Attendees to AERA 2022 learned quickly that virtual attendance, without an attention to design for equity and inclusion, resulted in second-class participation and status; hybrid sessions limited engagement for virtual attendees to listening only (no chat functions in Zoom webinars), and even specially approved “disabled” access often times did not allow conference presenters to speak (microphones were not set up before sessions started) and all special access required “approval” of individuals’ disabilities by AERA administrators. We believe that ISLS cando and must do better in supporting equitable virtual engagement, and that this will require deliberate attention and effort in collaboration with our full diverse community of scholars. 2215 The authors of this letter included disabled, chronically ill, queer, trans, environmentally-informed scholars and allies. In response to their letter, the Equity & Justice Committee and ISLS Board along with board member Yotam Hod, supported Keifert to establish a new charter for the HEsC. Launching in 2023, Ching, Scipio, D’Angelo, Danish and soon others joined to found hybrid efforts. The HEsC has taken a coalition building approach from the start to center marginalized voices in iterative and expansive hybrid AM designs. Of most interest to the present symposium is the way that centering one set of marginalized scholars’ needs led to the benefit of newly emergent challenges at the Helsinki 2025 AM. The curb cut effect as a lens on isls hybrid efforts Historically, curb cuts were part of a guerilla movement in Berkeley CA as wheelchair users  added concrete to curbs to make their neighborhoods more accessible. Over time, curb cuts —a small ramp at intersections —began to be a common feature of many sidewalks. These designs allow not only wheelchair users, but many others including people with baby strollers, shopping carts, or children on bikes to more easily navigate getting on and off sidewalks. Thus, curb cuts were designed for wheelchair users but  benefit a much broader range of people in unplanned ways. This phenomenon, of designing for a marginalized group of people and benefiting untold numbers of other people, has come to be known as the curb cut effect (Blackwell, 2017; Gorney, 2021). There is a direct parallel between the curb cut movement and the ISLS hybrid AM movement. Just as the original curb cut movement began when a small group of people came together to meet the needs of the marginalized disabled community of wheelchair users,  the ISLS hybrid movement began centered on the needs of disabled and chronically ill scholars. And just as the curb cut movement came to benefit a wide variety of people in society, so too has the hybrid movement benefited a range of scholars and collabor ators. For instance, although the initial coalition was focused on the needs of disabled, queer/trans, caregiving, and environmentallyinformed scholars, their efforts provided benefits to numerous members of the broader society. For instance, scholars wit h practitioner, youth, and family collaborators have been able to bring their co-designer/coresearcher/participant partners to the conference through hybrid features, allowing all attendees to benefit from their experience and expertise. Or take Helsinki 2025, when the abrupt cancellation of U.S. federal grants left hundreds of LS scholars without planned funds to cover costs. Or the fact that international scholars based in the US with visas were warned by their universities that they would likely not be able to get back into the US if they left, leaving many scholars stranded at distance from the in-person Helsinki events. These emergent marginalized communities did not, however, miss out on the 2025 AM because of the pre-existing hybrid offerings includi ng accommodations for remote presentation, virtual access to all sessions with in-room hybrid ambassadors to facilitate cross-modality engagement, and virtual socials, keynotes, and poster sessions designed specifically for online attendees. HEsC and the i nnovation of ISLS members created our own curb cut; designing for one set of marginalized scholars benefited scholars newly marginalized by shifting geopolitics. ## Four perspectives on hybrid designs

Co-designing our way beyond compliance and toward relational, onto-axiological approaches to disability access Anemoi Clean Air Group: Dylan Paré, Ali R. Blake, Vanessa Svihla, Enrique  Suárez, Alexis Papak, Teo Keifert Across disability studies, feminist philosophy, and design justice, scholars remind us that infrastructures are sites where values are enacted. Hamraie (2016) emphasizes that access is a relational and epistemic practice, where meaningful access is a form of relational accountability, materialized through iterative commitments to the questions of who belongs, where, and how. This requires both accommodation and anticipatory design, and it demands that we continually redesign our strategies for access rather  than treating inclusion as a one-time fix. Evans et. al. (2017) similarly frame higher education as a system structured by ableism, arguing for a social justice approach that shifts responsibility from individuals seeking accommodation to institutions cre ating more accessible environments. Lajoie (2022) critiques legalistic compliance models that collapse access into bureaucratic checklists, obscuring relational dimensions. Costanza-Chock (2020) extends these critiques through the lens of design justice, centering the voices and leadership of those most marginalized so that design becomes a collective practice of liberation.

In the learning sciences, methodologies such as social design experiments (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016), equity conjectures (Lee et al., 2022), and utopian methodology (Rajala et al., 2023) offer complementary insights. They position design as a collaborative,  future-oriented project of transforming inequitable systems while attending to power, temporality, and possibility. Given the shift in the last decade toward equity in the learning sciences, it is an opportune time to draw attention to critical disability  approaches in both our work and the ways 2217 we convene as a community. To guide this shift, we pose the following questions: What are the epistemic and relational goals of academic conferences, and how do our design decisions privilege one over the other? Who gets to share their work, and under what  conditions of belonging? Sharing scholarly work is important, but so too is relationality. Disability access must become an ongoing, iterative, and co-designed project that actively desires disabled presence and cultivates belonging. Building on ISLS’s re cent efforts to hybridize aspects of its conferences, we argue that infrastructure decisions —such as air quality, hybrid participation, and conference access design—are not merely logistical but ontological and axiological commitments that shape who belongs and how knowledge circulates. Seeing them as such, a design justice framework demands going further by approaching access not as accommodation after the fact but as a shared commitment to infrastructures that make participation, relation-making, and collective flourishing possible. In moving away from frameworks of accommodation, which, however widely “approved,” require disabled scholars to make ourselves known to conference organizers, we propose a framework instead organized around desire (Slovin, 2024). From this position, we invite the society to organize conference activities from a stance of actively desiring the presence and contributions of disabled scholars, rather than retrofitting a conference format that is inaccessible to many. Scholars thinking across queer theory and critical disability studies have put these theories to work to characterize a desire-based orientation to disability, often through queercrip futuring (McRuer & Wilkerson, 2003; Kafer, 2013). As part of this collective theory-toward-worldbuilding effort, Kafer

(2013) has described ways disability is often framed as undesirable, “bear[ing] too many traces of the ills of the

present to be desirable.” (p. 2). This constructed undesirability is at the core of eugenics movements (Ordover,

2003) that seek to eradicate ‘undesirable’ populations—movements that are gaining momentum in the global rise

of nationalisms in their overt forms and in their pernicious forms of socially-accepted abandonment. There are so many ills of our present. When asking for continued mitigation measures, disabled peoples who recognize the gravity of increased viral transmission and immune dysregulation since the beginning of the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic insist that our social structures and institutions remember this ongoing metaphorical and literal ill—and act accordingly in a way that actively values the lives and contributions of disabled peoples. From a political/relational stance (Kafer, 2013), disablement is a collective and structural accomplishment, one that we can—and we should—a pproach differently.

Through the transformation of inaccessible infrastructures that render some bodies as ‘normative,’ facilitating their actions, while rendering others as undesirable and stifling or foreclosing their actions, we, as a field, can move toward desiring disabled presence and contributions. And as with curb-cuts, such moves will enable a field that’s better for all of us. When we consider design decisions, such as clean air infrastructure, and recognize entangled social systems that differentially distribute the mobilities required for conference travel and physical presence, a coalitional politics becomes necessary. Desiring the presence and contributions of disabled folks necessarily involves desiring trans and gender non-conforming folks, folks rooted in the Majority Countries, poor folks, caregivers of many sorts, im/migrant communities, and more and more of us as vital members of our society—both in the sense of ISLS as a society and of committing to collective well-being. Critical disabilities studies has implications for our research, not only in shaping who participates in conferences, but also in our research. To illustrate what progress toward a critical disability stance in the learning sciences might become, we share illustrative cases about infrastructuring for hybrid epistemic engagement at our conferences and designing for relational engagement. The first case introduces and builds on the Hybrid Engagement subCommittee’s recent efforts to bolster access to virtual participation in response to calls from a coalition of disabled, chronically ill, queer, trans, and environmentally-conscious scholars. The next case highlights how a queer and trans communal clothing making space with clean air infrastructure (Blake, 2025) designed for learning in ways that actively desired disabled, queer, and trans peoples without expecting participants make themselves known in relation with these communities. Expanding from society efforts to maintain opportunities for socializing and connection in early pandemic years, the final case focuses on the design of relational and life spaces of conference participation, such as hybrid social spaces and energy-saving and-restoring spaces, which make conference participation more possible for more scholars. Family, feminism, and hybridity as an ethic of care Cynthia Carter Ching In 2006, in my first faculty role, I was invited to be on a campus panel called “Do Babies Matter at the University of Illinois?” This was billed as a local version of a national conversation in academia, spurred by the early years of what would come to be  a decade-long, nationwide study in the United States, examining the impact of birth and childcare on career advancement or “gender and family in the ivory tower,” (Mason, Wolfinger, & Goulden, 2019). At the panel event, each member was asked to share how we balanced caring for young children and our 2218 faculty duties, and what some of the most helpful and difficult factors were. Since I had given birth to my second child the previous year, I imagine that the panel organizers expected me to talk about how family-friendly campus policies, such as clock-stopping and parental leave, had facilitated my advancement. What I described instead was the daily juggling act my academic husband and I engaged in, not just in birth years but every year, as we navigated childcare, teaching, research, meetings, and conference travel. Conferences, I said, were important in my field, critical to establishing visibility, networks, and reputation as a junior scholar, and probably the hardest part, because of the long absences required and the increased burden on the remaining parent. No amount of campus-level accommodation was going to solve this problem.

That was twenty years ago, and in the Learning Sciences we are just now developing viable alternatives to the conference travel that forced many parents or other caregivers of earlier generations to choose between field networking and family responsibilities. There is some evidence that the virtual conferences that characterized the COVID quarantine allowed more equitable attendance and participation by women and young parents in many academic fields (Jarvis, et al, 2023; Black, et al, 2022), although gendered caregiving expectations during conferencing from home still existed (Standaert & Thunus, 2022). Hybridity in conference design is not a matter of internal family politics, however, or of institutional policy for the universities, research centers, or o ther organizations where scholars are employed. Rather, designing conference hybridity and accommodation is an opportunity for defining our own academic field expectations and norms. Field norms matter, not only for the participation and advancement of women, parents, and other caregivers currently in our field, but also for how our field is perceived by young people considering future careers and observing the challenges experienced by their professors and mentors (Seymour, Hunter, & Weston, 2019). In this lens on ISLS hybrid accommodations, I use an ethics of care  feminist and relational framework (Noddings, 2013) to argue that the Learning Sciences has a responsibility to support caregivers through offering conference hybrid participation as an accommodation. Moreover, and in accordance with Noddings, I argue that those of us who occupy senior positions of influence and authority in the field must act, as caring individuals and as a collective, in support of these colleagues via inclusive and thoughtful hybrid conference and session designs, rather than relying on organizational policy to care on our behalf. Organizations and policies, Noddings asserts, do not care; in fact, they cannot care. Caring is personal and relational, and it requires direct and immediate action, not merely the passive existence of a policy, program, or accommodation. In fact, if we return to the “Do Babies Matter?” study, several of the bombshell findings to come out of that work describe the inefficacy or unintended consequences of caregiving accommodation policies (Mason, Wolfinger, & Goulden, 2019). Nationwide data revealed that while parental-leave quarters or semesters were a boon to productivity for fathers, mothers who took leave still experienced downturns or stalls in their career progress and, in many cases, were expected to do more  upon returning from leave. Clock-stopping options that added time to the pre-tenure period, while well-intentioned, did not help parents in departments or fields whose colleagues and external reviewers increased their expectations for year-over-year publication output in response. Clearly, as these examples demonstrate, policy alone is not sufficient to create equity, and it is the responsibility of individuals to act in accordance with the values a policy or accommodation describes. Models exist of inclusive designs for hybridity and virtual participation as caregiving accommodations. Many scholars have argued that virtual conference access can ameliorate inequities in who attends and presents, including across gender and caregiver groups that had lower pre-pandemic participation (e.g., Niner, et al, 2020; Johnson, 2022). In addition to the fact of virtual access itself, however, there are important features of conferences and sessions that are more likely to facilitate equita ble engagement, including time-and-date-shifted events and consideration is particularly relevant to caregiving accommodation, since the family responsibilities that require scholars to remain at home rarely align with distant conference schedules (Kelly, Yu, & Vanner, 2020). Other scholars have argued that home videoconferencing poses both advantages and challenges for gender and caregiver equity, including privacy for attendees and family members/children, appearance-focused engagement or comments, and use of screen text to indicate lived names and pronouns (Dhawan, et al, 2021). In ISLS efforts over the past several years, we have checked a number of these boxes. We have hosted time-and-date-shifted events such as pre-conference socials and keynote watch parties. We deployed “hybrid ambassadors” whose sole purpose in sessions was to ensure access and discursive participation for virtual viewers and presenters. We have hosted virtual-only poster sessions and “born hybrid” symposia, where the intent and design of the latter is to seamlessly integrate virtual and in-person participants. These innovations have yet to become norms, however, or widespread in terms of how most members envision conferencing. In the knowledgebuilding portion of this symposium, we can hopefully include not only a focus on what features and policies benefit caregivers, but also move further to imagine how we can encourage caring and inclusive actions by society 2219 members who propose and attend sessions, invite panelists, and create the scholarly conversations and networking that caregivers need to advance and participate equitably in our field. Inclusiveness across borders: Amplifying voices from the Majority Countries Sameer Honwad, Raúl Alberto Mora There is an urgent need to understand how people learn within the diverse spaces and communities of the Majority World. Introduced by Alam (2008), the term Majority World challenges labels such as “Global South” or “developing world” by emphasizing that these countries are in fact home to the majority of humankind. Unlike “Third World,” which carries historical baggage, or “Global South,” which is both geographically inaccurate and often hierarchical, Majority World reframes these contexts as central rather  than peripheral. It highlights both their numerical significance and their crucial role in shaping global knowledge production, in contrast to the socalled “Minority World” of wealthy, industrialized nations (Oopong & Dromboski, 2024). Yet most Learning Sciences research continues to be conducted within the Minority World. The International Society of the Learning Sciences (ISLS) has long recognized this imbalance and has taken important steps to address it. Still, much more needs to be done to deepen our understanding of how the majority of humanity learns. Hybrid sessions offer a powerful way to broaden participation, enabling more researchers and practitioners from the Majority World to engage in conferences and other activities organized by ISLS We begin by outlining the challenges that scholars and practitioners from the Majority World face when attempting to participate in face-to-face activities organized by ISLS. The most immediate barrier is economics. Traveling across the world to attend a 3– 5 day conference is expensive, and for many scholars and practitioners in Majority World countries, it is difficult to justify allocating scarce financial resources to such short events. Although ISLS provides funding to support scholars and practitioners from Majority World countries in attending its conferences, these opportunities remain limited and highly competitive. While generous, the limited funding levels inevitably cap participation and prevent broader inclusion. Another major challenge is securing visas. Traveling to European or North Americandestinations from the Majority World countries often involves a lengthy and uncertain process that can take months, if not years. For example, before the COVID-19 pandemic, obtaining a visa from India to th e United States typically required about 3 –4 months. Since the pandemic, however, processing times have exceeded 12 months, creating significant barriers to participation. The risk of rejection further compounds the challenge of obtaining visas. Even when applicants can secure the necessary financial resources and meet time requirements, visas may still be denied by embassies for socio-political reasons, creating an additional layer of uncertainty and exclusion. As a result, the significant investment of time and money required for visa applications can ultimately prove futile.

Finally, one cannot overlook the issue of language. A great deal of participants from the Majority World rely on languages other than English for everyday communication and scholarship. Some may also use English as a medium for scholarly communication, wit h varying degrees of competence and using multiple varieties of said language in their repertoire. However, academic spaces tend to operate under certain forms of Anglonormativity that favor or expect only specific variants and registers (Chang-Bacon, 2021; Leung et al., 2009), which sometimes places speakers from the Majority World at a disadvantage, as reflected in their higher proposal rejection rates, unrealistic demands about the written discourse they should use in their proposals. Another troubling issue is the paucity of academic events that provide spaces for multilingual presentations; these spaces do not consider featuring different languages or options for translanguaging in the event (e.g., the use of multilingual slide decks or other supporting tools). For scholars and practitioners from the Majority World, this linguistic barrier adds yet another layer of challenge to meaningful participation. Hybrid spaces offer a powerful and practical response to many of the barriers that scholars and practitioners from the Majority World face when engaging in international academic communities. By enabling participation both online and in person, hybrid formats significantly reduce the financial burden associated with traveling to conferences across the globe, allowing individuals to allocate scarce resources toward research and local priorities rather than airfare and accommodation. They also bypass the lengthy and uncertain visa processes that often prevent talented researchers from contributing, ensuring that inclusion is not determined by bureaucratic or socio-political hurdles. Furthermore, hybrid spaces can provide more flexible linguistic environments, where translation tools, captioning technologies, and asynchronous engagement options help bridge language divides in ways that face-to-face events alone rarely achieve. This model creates opportunities for participants to join landscapes, while still fostering cross-border dialogue. In doing so, hybrid spaces not only increase accessibility but also create more equitable platforms for knowledge exchange, ensuring that the voices and perspectives of the Majority World are centered in global conversations rather than excluded by systemic barriers. 2220 While hybrid spaces represent an important step toward greater inclusiveness and equity, some challenges, both technological and logistical, remain, particularly for participants from the Majority World. Regarding technology, we cannot ignore the reality that reliable broadband internet and video conferencing tools are often taken for granted in the Minority World but remain unevenly distributed elsewhere. For example, in a five-year research –practice partnership in rural India, connectivity barriers have remained a constant issue. Although roughly 82% of India’s population has access to some form of internet (mobile or satellite connections), only about 3% of the population has access to reliable fixed broadband service (Mandal, 2025). This digital divide limits the extent to which hybrid participation can truly level the playing field and underscores the need for more creative and context-sensitive solutions. Finally, regarding logistics, hybrid conferences are still wrestling with how to set up the virtual  component in their events. It is concerning to see how often the virtual portion of an academic event is limited to either posting the presentation on a platform or just delivering the presentation after the on-site conference has taken place. This denies  virtual participants the possibility to engage in extended networking both with on-site attendants and others participating in the virtual option. If we are not conceiving hybrid events as integrative, truly interactive experiences, that may also further exacerbate socio-economic inequities, where those who cannot afford to travel are subjected to a subpar experience.

## Discussion & Implications

In conclusion, while the inequities limiting the participation of scholars and practitioners from the Majority World demand innovative solutions, hybrid conference formats are an important step forward toward inclusivity. When designed intentionally and tr eated as an integral rather than peripheral part of the conference, hybrid spaces can create powerful opportunities to engage with learning sciences research from diverse contexts. Such inclusivity not only enriches the field’s theoretical and practical foundations but also affirms its responsibility to reflect and serve the diversity of global communities.

## Perspectives on hybridity from conference chairs

X Christine Wang, Chris Hoadley Over the past several years, ISLS has been grappling with the promise and limits of hybrid conferencing. The 2024 Annual Meeting in Buffalo was framed around learning as a cornerstone of healing, resilience, and community. That theme was not only about what our scholarship should address in the world, but also about how we design our own gatherings as scholars. As we wrote then, “Our goal for this annual meeting is, and always has been, to disrupt and expand limited notions of how we practice scholarship in the learning sciences.” (Note: During ISLS 2024, Chris took on the role of writing and circulating the “A Note from the Chair.” While those announcements carried his name, they reflected our joint deliberations and decisions as co-chairs. In this paper, we draw on them as a record of our shared perspective.) In other words, hybridity was not simply a logistical question of what technology to use—it was bound up with our values as a community: inclusion, care, resilience, and creativity.

The Buffalo meeting became our first large-scale experiment in what we began to call slow conferencing. After years of pandemic-era virtual events followed by a rush back to in-person, we wanted to avoid simply reproducing the hyper-scheduled, “firehose” style of large academic conferences. We shortened sessions, added longer breaks, and built in reflective pauses like the daily walk between venues. As we reflected in the notes, “What if… we could slow things down?”. For us, slowing down was a design choice  to prioritize depth over breadth, connection over coverage. Hybridity in this sense meant not just mixing online and in-person formats, but rethinking the tempo of scholarly life together.

Our 2024 online participation plan reflected that same pragmatism. We knew we could not realistically deliver a full “HyFlex” model, where every session was equally accessible in both modalities. Instead, we treated hybrid as an iterative experiment. Resource constraints —conference site, facilities, technology availability, and even room design—forced our hand, but rather than see those limits as failures, we turned them into an intentional design challenge. We experimented cautiously, setting up a born hybrid  symposium track for sessions that could benefit from it, piloting hybrid ambassadors to support inclusion, and making targeted accommodations for colleagues facing disability, caregiving, visa, or travel crises. These were not perfect solutions, but they allowed us to move forward without overpromising. As we put it at the time, “a well-executed but modest strategy is likely to produce more progress than something more ambitious and more untried.” Looking back now, with the vantage of time, we are glad we started this experiment. The push for hybrid conferencing did not arise in the abstract but from our colleagues —disabled and chronically ill scholars who demanded alternatives to exclusionary “return to normal” practices. Their advocacy helped us see that accessibility could not be an afterthought. Drawing on the curb cut effect, we came to realize that designs created for one group ultimately benefit everyone. The Helsinki 2025 Annual Meeting made this strikingly clear: when 2221 funding collapsed and visas tightened, pre-existing hybrid supports meant that many scholars who would otherwise have been stranded still participated meaningfully. Hybrid features that had once seemed like “special accommodations” turned out to be vital i nfrastructure for unexpected crises. We are grateful to Teo, whose thoughtful and dedicated leadership, in close collaboration with us, helped make these early hybrid experiments possible.

At the same time, hybridity has a personal side. As conference chairs, we found ourselves navigating competing demands—how to balance inclusion with feasibility, sustainability with connection, and ideals with very real resource limits. We also recognized the toll of academic culture itself. In one note, we wrote, “Travel has an outsized impact on our planetary ecosystem,” and challenged ourselves and our colleagues to make our carbon footprint count by linking travel with deeper community engagement. In another, we spoke directly to the pervasive fear of missing out: “There is an alternative to FOMO… we can celebrate the joy of missing out”. These were not just slogans but reminders to ourselves: that conference design is as much about care for people and planet as it is about papers and keynotes.

From this perspective, hybridity becomes an ethical and relational project. It is about more than streaming a plenary or setting up a Zoom room. It is about asking: who is present, who is absent, and what choices we make to expand or narrow the circle of participation? It is about acknowledging the infrastructures —air quality, travel policies, childcare supports, room configurations —that shape not only whether people can attend, but how knowledge circulates and communities form. It is about recognizing that design constraints will always exist, but we can treat them as opportunities for collective problem-solving rather than excuses for exclusion. Looking ahead, we see hybridity as an ongoing design challenge. It is not a question of if we hybridize, but how we do so in ways that keep people, care, and inclusion at the center. We do not yet have all the answers, but we know the questions are worth asking. As we reminded ourselves in 2024, “People first, learning second, everything else third.” We hope this mantra can remain our compass as we continue to experiment, together, with what hybrid conferencing can become.

Synthesis and Future Conversations Teo Keifert, Cynthia Carter Ching (co-chairs) We begin here our ongoing process to synthesize diverse perspectives in the continuous, iterative design of virtual and hybrid features at ISLS Annual Meetings. This process will continue during the symposium through breakout room discussions with all attendees. Here, we begin our synthesis based on the preliminary writing offered by our authorship teams.

First, we recognize how the curb cut effect shaped multiple authorship teams’ contributions. The Conference Host authors emphasized that “designs created for one group ultimately benefited everyone.” Both Caregiver and Majority World authors indicated that hybrid designs, centered on the needs of disabled/chronically ill scholars first and foremost, met the long-standing needs of Majority World and Caregiving scholars in innovative ways (ways that were at risk in the rush to “return to normal” but that ISLS has chosen not to abandon). These traces of the curb cut effect in ISLS hybrid AM designs are signs of success. Yet, we wish to return to the definition of the phenomena to propose a slight shift in how we take up this idea. The curb cut effect  is a phenomenon of designing for one marginalized group of people and benefiting other, unexpected people (Blackwell, 2017; Gorney, 2021). Yet, we should not as a society (ISLS, broader society) rely on benefits to dominant communities as a justification to meet th e needs of marginalized members of our community. Rather, we hope to highlight that members of our society that belong to dominant communities should choose to act in solidarity to support marginalized members of their communities, first because it is righ t and just to do so, and second because anyone can end up with temporary (or permanent) marginalized status at any time. For instance, a common refrain in the disability community is that “anyone can become disabled at any time” (and in fact, anyone lucky enough to have a long life will become disabled). Perhaps easier to grasp for non-disabled members of our society is the example from Helsinki; the sudden need for hybrid features of many established U.S. -based scholars, after the federal government cancel ed grants at an alarming rate, illustrates how members of dominant society may become marginalized by geopolitical systems at any time. Thus, we choose a solidarity lens that then raises new questions as we (co-chairs) identify thematics across contributions in this symposium. First, we as members of the International Society of the Learning Sciences must choose, and actively embody who we want to be as a community through our routine actions and designs. The Caregiver perspective asks us to consider how “field norms matter” for the participation and advancement of marginalized communities but also in the ways we set the stage for how new members of our society perceive membership based on their own and their mentors’ experiences. Responsibility as a field also means laying desi gn challenges at the feet of those “who occupy senior positions of influence and authority” to act as caring individuals and as a collective. 2222 This challenge to consider who we are, and how we present ourselves through AM design was also present in other perspectives. The Anemoi Clean Air group offering disabled and trans perspectives challenged us to “continually redesign our strategies for acce ss rather than treating inclusion as a one-time fix.” Instead, these scholars frame inclusion as an ethical choice, making accommodations not about individuals seeking their own solutions or hacks, but about our society choosing to foreground flexibility in design to anticipate the needs of marginalized scholars. They invite us to “organize conference activities from a stance of actively desiring the presence and contributions of disabled scholars.” Thus, across Caregiving and Disabled and Trans perspective s, we see challenges to design, as institutions and as individuals, in ways that enthusiastically welcome the presence of marginalized scholars and their scholarship. Prior Conference Hosts echo this desire: “Our goal for this annual meeting is, and always  has been, to disrupt and expand limited notions of how we practice scholarship in the learning sciences.” The Conference Hosts also contributed to this understanding by exploring the intersection of care, desire, hybridity and logistics asking “what technology to use” as that question was, and always is “bound up with our values as a community: inclusion, care, resilience, and creativity.” Second, we recognize that recent ISLS efforts to address the diversity of needs in AMs is long-awaited for many members of our society. Caregivers have long wrestled with challenges of conferencing, and “just now” are we “developing viable alternatives to the conference travel that forced many parents or other caregivers of earlier generations to choose between field networking and family responsibilities.”  Similarly, Majority World scholars have always struggled with the challenges of in-person only conferencing. New hybrid designs “not only increase accessibility but also create more equitable platforms for knowledge exchange, ensuring that the voices and perspectives of the Majority World are centered in global conversations rather than excluded by syste mic barriers.” In resisting the “return to normal” that other conferences embraced (i.e. AERA), or the segregation of virtual days from in-person events (i.e. NARST), ISLS is refusing to leave behind the COVID-quarantine advancements that allowed for more equitable participation by these and other communities. Thus as the Conference Hosts emphasize, “hybrid conferencing did not arise in the abstract but from our colleagues—disabled and chronically ill scholars who demanded alternatives to exclusionary ‘return to normal’ practices. Their advocacy helped us see that accessibility could not be an afterthought.” And these design shifts continue to meet needs, but also offer new challenges as we ensure that, as Majority World authors explained, designs are “treated as an integral rather than peripheral part of the conference.” Authorship teams present many lingering challenges. Some of these include needs that have not been met: clean air tools and discussion, time-and-date-shifted events to attend to timezone challenges, visas, financial challenges, broadband and internet accessibility, and challenges with limited language norms. All of these should be seen in relation to who we are as a society. We start from conversations that center solidarity and move towards collectively embracing care for and the desire to center scholars and scholarship from marginalized communities. We also see connections between emphasis on onto-epistemic heterogeneity and translanguaging within our scholarship (e.g., Warren et al., 2020; Pierson et al., 2021), and calls from our Majority World authors naming the troubling lack of “academic events that provide spaces for multilingual presentations; these spaces do not consider featuring different languages or options for translanguaging in the event (e.g., the use of multilingual slide decks or other supporting tools).” As Scipio (2023, June) tweeted: Yes! Hybrid Ambassador is a new volunteer role (introduced in Helsinki 2025). HAs focus on the Zoom room, first by ensuring before the start of the session that virtual attendees have video/audio access, and that in-person attendees have audio-free access to the room (to avoid audio feedback) both for visual access to slides and for access to the Zoom chat. During the session, HAs serve as a bridge between virtual and in-person attendees, primarily through the chat. For instance, if a virtual attendee has a clarifying question during one of the presentations that requires immediate attention, the HA can gain a turn for the attendee to ask their question.

Thus, we believe our hybrid plan meets the standard for a creative, innovative approach to hybrid symposium design.
