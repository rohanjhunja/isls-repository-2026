# Reflective Use v. Rote Use: Engaging Students in Computational Thinking for Science With Computational Tools

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As computational tools are becoming increasingly common in scientific practice, developing students’ computational thinking for science (CT-S) skills is a priority in science education. To support educators in meeting this need, we present a theoretical construct that distinguishes between science activities that engage students in CT-S through interactions with computational tools and science activities in which students use computational tools but do not engage in CT-S. We discuss how this theoretical construct can support educators in developing or modifying science learning experiences to promote CT-S.

## Introduction

Contemporary science is becoming increasingly computational. Scientists employ computational tools to conduct investigations and contribute to the design of new computational tools for their specific research. To  learn authentic science practices, students must have opp ortunities to think deeply about how to leverage th e computational nature of tools for scientific purpos es (Denning, 2017; Kaczmarczyk & Dopplick, 2014). T his necessity is reflected in new science standards in the United States—most notably, the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS), which mandate that computational thinking be taught as a practice of science (NGSS L ead States, 2013). Although it is becoming more common for science lessons to incorporate digital models, simulations, and other computational tools, the use  of these tools alone does not guarantee that stude nts will engage in computational thinking (Li et al., 2020; Yadav et al., 2016). We offer a theoretical constru ct that specifies whether a student using a computational tool is engaged in Computational Thinking for Science (CT-S). Methodological and theoretical approaches The theoretical construct presented in this paper contributes to an ongoing multiyear study investigating the role of computational thinking in middle school science learning. The project aims to create an instrument that can measure middle school students’ CT-S abilities. At the commencement of this project, we convened a pan el of experts in relevant fields—including educational research, learning design, computer science, and computational thinking—for a two-day retreat at the University of  California, Berkeley. Participants reviewed a seri es of activities involving computational tools in the Amplify Science Middle School curriculum. Discussion centered on the potential of each activity to engage students in CT-S—defined as the cognitive processes involved in building or modifying a mental model of a computational tool’s functionality within a given science activity. Participants concluded that for CT-S to occur, students would ne ed to attend to the functionality and positionality  of the computational tool within the given science activity. This was consistent with ideas about computational tool use in the literature (e.g., Grover & Pea, 2013; Sengupta et al., 2013; Weintrop et al., 2016). We understand computational thinking as situated in activity and distributed across the actors and tools involved in an activity (Engeström, 1987; Greeno, 1998). These intrapersonal cognitive processes are only a piece of broader goal-directed activities, but are critic al to understand nevertheless (Greeno, 2015). With this perspective, our team reexamined a set of science a ctivities, employing Activity Theory as a framework  for analyzi ng students’ use of computational tools and cognitive processes in each situation (Vygotsky, 2012; Leont’ev, 1978; Engeström, 1987). This approach builds on an established tradition of using Activity Theory to analyze outcomes of technology-centered learning ex periences (e.g., Brine & Franken, 2006; Murphy & Rodríguez-Manzanares, 2008). Activity Theory is a f ramework that considers an entire activity system t hat extends beyond the individual, or subject, engaged in activity to include the components tha t contribute to the outcome of the activity—like tools (Engeström, 1987). In an activity system, the subject’s efforts are motivated by and directed toward an object, or goal of the activity (Kuutti, 1996; Engeström, 1987). Through the subject’s employment of tools, an outcome of the activity is reached (Engeström, 1987). Tools in an activity system can be either physical or immaterial. Activity systems are  dialectically structured, such that components mut ually influence one another. Through using a tool, the su bject may come tonew understandings about the tool  or the activity’s goal—informing the subject’s subsequent actions. We examine d how the shift in goal would impact the students’ use of the computational tool, their subsequent cognitive processes, and activity outcomes. Across lessons, we determined that computational thinking was most likely to be an outcome of activity systems in which the goal was to understand the functionality of the computational tool itself. This goal would arise, for instance, when students would tink er with a computational tool that they had never pr eviously used, when students would investigate how to employ  a computational tool in a novel way, or when stude nts would encounter a glitch or an unexpected outcome during tool use and would need to reinvestigate an aspect of the tool’s functionality accordingly. Aggregating examples resulted in a generalized Reflective Use construct. We analyzed student interactions with computational  tools without explicit attention to the culturalhistorical mediators of activity characteristic of modern Activity Theory (Engeström, 1999). With measurement development as the primary motivator of our work, w e made this choice in order to focus on the individual contributions of the subject within each activity: what are the cognitive resources brought to bear in tool-mediated, goal-oriented activity, and how might those resources and those mental models, become visible and get revised through activity? However, a complete understanding  of CT-S within an activity system must attend to t he complex situativity of learning represented in the “bottom row” of the modern Activity Theory triangle: the rules, community, and divisions of labor within activity systems (Engeström, 1999). We will address this in future work. Reflective use construct Activity Theory positions intelligence as being dis tributed throughout an activity system such that kn owledge emerges from a subject’s interactions with tools (Engeström, 1987; Greeno, 1998; Pea, 1993). It follows that when students use a computational tool, their activity could engender critical thinking about the functionality of the tool itself (Kaptelinin & Nardi, 2006; Kuutti, 1996; Jon assen & Rohrer-Murphy, 1999). However, students do not necessarily engage in CT-S every time they use a computational tool. Our construct distinguishes between science activities in which students engage in CT-S as a result of using computational tools, Reflective Use, and science activities in which students use computational tools but do not engage in CT-S, Rote Use. Reflective use A Reflective Use activity system is one where the s ubject builds a mental model of a computational too l’s functionality by interacting with the tool. Imagine a student is investigating bacterial growth to better understand how bacterial populations change over time. This student has a graphing calculator (the computational tool) and data on a bacterial population’s size at different timepoints. However, this student has never used a graphing calculator. Before this student could employ the calculator to investigate population growth, they would need to determine what computations this tool can perform t hat may support their science investigation. To do this, the student could engage in Reflective Use. The student  might begin by manipulating the calculator, select ively pressing certain buttons, and observing the results of those actions. The student could then reflect on the outcomes of these actions and form an initial mental model o f the graphing calculator’s functionality. As the s tudent continues to interact with this computational tool,  their discoveries may reinforce, revise, or supple ment their developing mental model. In this way, Reflective Use is bi-directional in terms of information transfer: the student takes actions, the student reflects on what the com putational tool does, and the student takes new act ions based on their reflection. Through this process of Reflec tive Use, the student builds a mental model of the graphing calculator’s functionality with respect to their goal of graphing population growth: the student has engaged in CTS. (Figure 1). Rote use Reflective Use stands in contrast to Rote Use  of a computational tool. When a student is engaged in Rote Use, they do not build or modify a mental model of the c omputational tool’s functionality. Building on the previous example, once the student has formed a mental model of the graphing calculator’s functionality through Reflective Use, the student can now employ that mental model alongside the graphing calculator in a new activity system to create a graph of the bacterial growth data. (Figure 2). Since the student already knows what the calculator cando in this context, they do not need to engage in CT-S. As demonstrated, it is possible for Rote Use to immediately follow Reflective Use of a computational tool. Howe ver, these do not need to occur at the same point i n time. ## Significance

Attending to the distinction between Reflective Use  and Rote Use can support the development and implementation of science learning experiences that effectively engage students in CT-S. Imagine, for instance, a science lesson in which students use a computer-based simulation to investigate the Guiding Question: how are the Moon’s phases related to the Moon’s orbit around Earth? The instructor could choose to begin the lesson by teaching students how to manipulate the simulation. The students might then employ the simulation, in accordance with what they have learned from the instructor, to  make observations. Through using the simulation to  make observations, students may arrive at an answer to the Guiding Question. In this Rote Use scenario, students would engage in thinking about the moon’s phases and orbi t; however, students would not need to engage in CT-S because the activity did not require students to co nstruct a mental model of the simulation’s function ality in relation to their science goal. To promote CT-S more effectively, the instructor could instead structure the lesson to begin with Reflective Use of the computational tool. For example, the instructor could start the lesson by asking students to consider: how could this computer-based simulation support us in answering our Guiding Question? Students would need to investigate the functionalit y of the simulation in relation to their goal of an swering the Guiding Question—in other words, students would reflectively use the tool. After this Reflective Use, students could proceed to investigate the relationship between the moon’s phases and orbit and formulate a response to the lesson’s Guiding Question. When the lesson is structured in this way, using the computational tool could promote students’ computational thinking in addition to their thinking about the moon’s phases and orbit. More over, we posit that designing lessons to engage students in Reflective Use could motivate additional science le arning opportunities. For instance, perhaps during their R eflective Use, the student observes that the moon s ometimes enters into an eclipse but does not do so every mon th; this observation could inspire students to mani pulate the simulation further to figure out how a 2-D model of the sun-earth-moon system is insufficient for understanding the 3-D interactions between the three objects. Overall, attending to the distinction between Reflective and Rote Use of computational tools could enable educators t o design learning experiences that more effectively  meet expectations for contemporary science education and that may lead students to engage with scientific phenomena more deeply.
