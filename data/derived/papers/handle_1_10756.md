# Towards an Intelligent Augmented Reality Tutoring System for Precision Manufacturing

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
With an aging workforce, the manufacturing sector faces an increasing demand for skilled workers. To address this challenge, we introduce an innovative training approach that seeks to couple Augmented Reality (AR) and Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS). Focused on complex psychomotor skills required in precision inspection tasks common to aerospace, medical, and defense industries, our prototype AR tutoring system delivers adaptive and real-time instructions and feedback. Through a Wizard of Oz (WOZ) study, we investigate transitioning from conventional AR systems to an intelligent AR tutoring system that adapts to individual learner needs. This study addresses the benefits and challenges posed by this transition and proposes solutions and recommendations. The study contributes to our understanding of how AR and ITS can jointly tailor instruction to learner needs in complex psychomotor tasks. Results from the WOZ study argue adaptive scaffolding improves training outcomes and helps identify technical challenges in automating the scaffolding.

## Introduction

The manufacturing industry faces several challenges  including an aging workforce, advancing technologi es like AI and automation, and a lack of interest in manufa cturing jobs among younger generations. Despite sig nificant job losses in the past (Houseman, 2018), the industry still expects a stable or growing demand for skilled workers. However, this integration of technology raises the skill requirement, exacerbating a skills gap in are as like assembly, maintenance, and inspection. Augmented Re ality (AR) is increasingly recognized as a potent t ool for training and upskilling manufacturing workers (Mogh addam et al., 2021). Various studies confirm its effectiveness in improving performance and reducing  errors (Lai et al. 2020). Current methods often la ck personalized interactions (Yoo et al. 2023) and provide generic instructions, potentially leading to overdependence on technology or cognitive overload.

Motivation: Intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) are computer env ironments that provide learners with tailored instruction and feedback to facilitate kno wledge and skill acquisition (Graesser et al.  2018). The ITS literature has often focused on teaching cognitive skills and knowledge (Nye, 2015). In our work, we specifically leverage the integration of AR and ITS technologies  to train psychomotor skills in manufacturing domai ns. This paper is motivated by the lack of thorough explorations of adaptive and intelligent instructions for manufacturing tasks in current industrial AR systems.

Problem Statement:  In this paper, we explore training that involves th e coupling of AR with an ITS, whereby a pedagogical agent would scaffold learners  by providing feedback as they perform the task usi ng AR. Our goal is to create a baseline of an immersive ITS in AR that supports the development of the complex cognitive and motor skills, which often require workers to interact with sophisticated tools, which involves precise physical manipulation, as well as cognitive reasoning and decision-making. Use Case: As an initial evaluation of our approach, we leverage an example related to  precision manufacturing. Inspection process is a crucial part of advanced pr ecision manufacturing in industries such as aerospace, medical, and defense. It involves carefu lly measuring parts using specialized tools, referr ed to as gauges. While machining processes in precision man ufacturing are increasingly automated, inspection t asks remain predominantly unautomated, requiring skilled  workers to comprehend complex technical drawin gs, measure part characteristics, and adjust process parameters. Proposed AR Tutoring System:  Our prototype AR tutoring system designed to enhanc e learning by providing real-time instructions overlaid on the physical workspace (Cao et al, 2020; Huang et al, 2021). Learners interact with the system through hand gestures and dialogue, receiving feedback and accessing informat ion as needed. The system offers textual instructions, feedback on mistakes, and immersive 3D animations as suggested by (Herbert et al. 2018). It enables visualization of setups, calibration techniques, and gauge movements overlaid on physical equipment. The focus is on evaluating t he benefits and challenges of integrating an ITS in to AR, exploring how AR features and tutoring feedback can  enhance learning outcomes. The current system does  not include ITS but aims to assess the synergy between AR and tutoring feedback. Wizard-of-Oz Study: We conducted a Wizard-of-Oz study to assess how the AR system can transition from a passive one-size-fits-all delivery of task information and instructions to an intelligent AR tutoring system that can proactively “coach” a learner. The study contributes to two key goals: (a) explore and evaluate alternative ways to incorporate tutoring that leverages the affordan ces of AR, and (b) identify opportunities and chall enges in replacing the current human wizard with a pedagogical agent powered with machine vision and gaze detection.

The study addressed the following research questions (RQs): ● RQ1: What types of scaffolding or interventions prove most effective in improving performance during training? Specifically, we explored three approache s: (a) obligatory stepwise AR guidance (defined as “full scaffolding”), (b) AR guidance that is reduced from obligatory to learners’ demand (defined as “ondemand scaffolding”), and (c) transition from obligatory to intelligen t interventions triggered when the system detects specific mistakes (we define as “adaptive scaffolding”). ● RQ2: What aspects of this system can benefit from adding intelligence? Specifically, what technology is needed to bridge gaps between our current system and the targeted Intelligent system?

## Methods

For this study, 26 participants (6 Females, 18 Males; mechanical/industrial engineering students) were randomly chosen, among which, 24 were able to complete the study. There were eight participants in the bachelor's program, 14 in master's and 2 in PhD program. University IRB  approved this human-subject experiment. In this section, we elaborate on the study design, task description, and the procedure for answering research questions.

## Study design

We designed a between-subjects study with three conditions to explore the  research questions outlined in the introduction. We evaluated a central hypothesis: The adaptive scaffolding condition in the AR tutoring system will result in a significantly greater performance increase compared to the control conditions, full scaffolding and ondemand scaffolding. The three study conditions are as follows: ● Condition 1: Full scaffolding. Participants were required to follow a detailed set of step-by-step multimodal instructions consisting of text, audio, and animation. ● Condition 2: On-demand scaffolding. Participants initially experienced full scaffolding then performed tasks on their own, but could request instructions for any step of the task. ● Condition 3: Adaptive scaffolding. Again, participants initially experienced full scaffolding  and then performed tasks on their own, unless the system audio notified them of a mistake and referred them to a particular step, where they could receive detailed instructions. This scaffolding condition was carried out through a Wizard of Oz study methodology.

The experiment consists of three sessions happening  over two consecutive days. The first two sessions happened on the first day and the third session on the second day. On the first day, participants were  shown an introductory video on the basics of tasks,  and the AR training (Hololens 2 Tips app); they the n proceeded to do the tasks. In the first session, the pre-intervention session, participants in all three con ditions received the full scaffolding. In the second session, the interventio n session, based on the random allocation that occu rred before the experiment, the participants received either of  the scaffolding conditions. In the third session, the post-intervention session, participants did not have acc ess to the AR application or any scaffolded instruc tions and were thus required to complete the tasks completely  on their own. In this paper, participants in Condi tion 1 are referred to as Group 1, those in Condition 2 as Group 2, and those in Condition 3 as Group 3. Task description Participants were presented with a setup table containing gauges and sample parts (see Figure 1). Their objective was to select appropriate gauges based on tolerance s and configurations, prepare the chosen gauges, lo cate characteristics on sample parts, and measure them. They reported measurement values and decided whether to accept or reject sample parts for use in a jet engine. The accuracy of their decisions was evaluated through binary scoring for analysis.

Wizard of Oz design In the adaptive scaffolding condition, participants  were informed of being monitored by an intelligent  vision system capable of detecting errors. Upon detection, the system would intervene by directing the learner to relevant instructions in an AR app for assistance. A Wizard of Oz study was conducted to emulate this system, whereby a researcher remotely observed participants via video and acted as the system. An automated script was employed, outlining task steps, possible mistakes, and corresponding corrective actions. ## Results

According to RQ1, we are interested in examining pe rformance differences in the inspection task depend ing on the session (i.e., 1-3) and the scaffolding condition (i.e., full, on-demand, adaptive). We aim to test whether the difference between performance in Session 3 and Session 1 can be explained by the scaffolding condition, in terms of the number of correct answers on the inspection rep ort. According to our central hypothesis, we expect the group that received adaptive scaffolding to hav e a significantly higher performance in Session 3 r elative to Session 1 compared with the other conditions. This would suggest that the change in performance between Session 1 and Session 3 is moderated by the scaffolding app roach. The distribution of the scores in each sessi on and the change in scores from Session 1 to Session 3 for each condition are depicted (see Figure 2). To answer the above questions, we fitted a logistic  mixed effect model (estimated using Maximum Likelihood and BOBYQA optimizer in R v4.3.1) to pre dict the binary score of whether the participant go t a question wrong or right using the lme4 package. We used session, condition and their interaction as pr edictor variables and included participants as a random eff ect to account for the variability of the individua l differences. The p-values were computed using a Wald z-distribution approximation. The results indicate a significance in our model, which is for the interaction between Group 3  and Session 3 (SE = 1.1085, pvalue < 0.05). It suggests that Group 3 had a higher change in performance from Session 1 to Session 3, compared to Group 2. The diffe rences between slopes for Group 2 and Group 3 are shown in  Figure 2. ## Discussions and future directions

Our findings pertaining to RQ1 suggest that adaptiv e scaffolding effectively enhanced the skills of th ese participants. However, in Session 3, the adaptive s caffolding group exhibited the highest performance but the lowest confidence, likely due to receiving stepwise  negative feedback during the intervention session.  This combination of high performance and low confidence is considered desirable for critical tasks, such as those with life-saving implications. The study concludes that adaptive scaffolding shows promise for enhancing performance and reducing errors in precision tasks and proposes  further exploration to refine interventions and ma ximize effectiveness for individual learners.

We realized that most of the mistakes made by participants are difficult to dete ct. This endorses RQ2, indicating that an advanced vision system capable o f detecting precise hand motions is beneficial in p reventing or timely correcting errors in such high-precision tasks. Additionally, as the disparities of the pre-intervention scores suggest, there were clear individual differences in performance. According to the individual interviews, we observed that participants had preferences for the timings of interventions, favoring instant interventions. Furthermore, some participants expressed a desire to receive affirmative feedback, whereas others held the belief that such feedback will not have a significant impa ct on them. These findings highlight the importance  of individual preferences and integrating them into an intelligent AR tutoring system.

In conclusion, our findings suggest a high level of overall satisfaction with using AR as a tutor and value in adding adaptive feedback to that tutor. Future directions should better leverage the affordances of AR, develop the advanced vision system, and study different mod es of feedback in terms of timings and content, and  their effects on individuals’ performance and emotions, such as engagement, boredom, and frustration.
