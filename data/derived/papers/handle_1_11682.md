# Queering Pre-college Engineering Education as a Method of Increasing Student Interest in Engineering

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite improvements in pre-college engineering education curricula over the past decade, student motivation to continue with engineering as a career path remains low. Queering these curricula is proposed as a method of enabling a wider variety of students to envision themselves as engineers.

## Introduction

Engineering is a set of iterative strategies and pr actices to address problems with no obvious solutio ns that have not already been solved (ABET, 2022). Engineering i nvolves the application of scientific and mathemati cal principles, design, and adherence to constraints to  solve such problems (Koen, 2003). As such, enginee ring has the potential to provide solutions to everyday issu es and global crises. Despite this, student interes t in Science, Technology, Engineering, and Math (STEM) fields remains low (Archer et al., 2010). In this paper, it is my intent to demonstrate how applying a queer approach to pre-college engineering education could help students w ith varied motivations reevaluate their view of enginee ring as a practice and, in turn, come to see the mselves as engineers.

The state of pre-college engineering education With both the potential of engineering as a problem-solving method and the accompanying increase in dem and for engineers in the labor force (U.S. Bureau of La bor Statistics, 2024), primary and secondary school s in the United States and around the world have been increasing the number of standalone engineering courses that they offer as well as the number of non-engineering courses that offer engineering elements  (Ekiz-Kiran & Aydin-Gunbatar, 2021; NASEM, 2020), but barriers remain. One such roadblock in offering more and higher qual ity engineering courses is that pre-college teachers across grade levels feel underprep ared to teach engineering (Banilower et al., 2018). Consequently, teachers ma y fail to adequately distinguish between science and engineering in their instruction (Antink-Meyer & Meyer, 2016). This has resulted in teachers who have low selfefficacy for teaching engineering and may even avoid doing so (Menon et al., 2024). Student interest in engineering Motivation and interest are two factors students re ly on to determine their education and career plans  after completing their primary and secondary education (NASEM, 2019), and research suggests that student interest in engineering begins to lag around middle school and never recovers (Archer et al., 2010). Students’ understanding of what engineering is and what engineers do is often largely shaped by representations of engineers in media, their experi ences with engineering, as well as their intuitive perceptions (Trotskovsky et al., 2013). These societal notions about and popular images of engineering and enginee rs often do not accurately reflect what engineering is for or how engineers work (Trotskovsky et al., 2013). Fo r example, students may have a perception that STEM subjects provide fewer opportunities for collaborative work, communal goal setting, and opportunities to help and receive help from others (Belanger et al., 2020). It should also be noted that female and minority-gendered students in middle and high school lose in terest in pursuing engineerin g in academics or career at an even higher rate than mal e students at that age (Wang & Degol, 2017). Girls who may be interested in STEM must push back against a vari ety of subtle and overt societal messaging: lack of  diverse role models, stereotypical behavior directed at them and expected of them, and “masculine” cultural norms leading to a lower sense of belonging, among others (Master et al., 2016). Current pre-college engineering frameworks for teaching engineering in pre-college contexts emphasize learning engineering tools and processes (Moore et al., 2014; Roehrig et al., 2021). This knowledge is  essential, but comparatively little focus is given  to correcting vague or incorrect notions of the nat ure of engineering or helping students envision themselves as engineers (Blotnicky et al., 2018). Towards a solution through queering engineering education “Queer” and its verb counterpart, “queering,” are words that, b y their very nature, defy definition (Browne & Nash, 2016). A full examination of queer scholarship is  beyond the scope of this paper, but suffice it to say that as an approach to inquiry and research, queer theories and queer methodologies have to do with reexamining and reconceptualizing previously-established power structures, especially as they relate to knowledge and knowledge development (Browne & Nash, 2016). Queer scholars look at how desires and ide ntities intersect to form established practices (Sheldon, 2019), and they may question the need for categorization (Wilchins, 2022). Queer theories tend to prioritize self-expression, transformation, community, empathy, and  creating new ways of being over rigid processes and competition (Heimlich, 2019).

Many students and teachers perceive the primary fun ction of studying engineering to be opportunities for job security, status, and high-paying careers (Caspi et al., 2019). Rather than merely being utilitarian, however, engineering is, at its core, a problem-solving approach that can be applied toward global and local transformation (Roehrig et al., 2021). It is likely that appealing  to long-term educational and career success in representing  the value of engineering may not appeal to students whose motivations orient more toward more-queer ideals such as care, empathy, creativity, or community, even when these principles are essential to the work of many engineers (Cropley, 2015; Gunckel & Tolbert, 2018). Queering pre-college engineering education, then, involves reframing the engineering curriculum to place more emphasis o n these qualities of engineering.  This is not to say that fundamental engineering procedures, tools, and know ledge should not be taught, but rather that these t hings should ideally be taught in a way that allows students to understand the broader purpose of the skills that they are learning.

This, in turn, could lead to more students having more motivation to learn engineering and stay in engineering as an academic and career path.
