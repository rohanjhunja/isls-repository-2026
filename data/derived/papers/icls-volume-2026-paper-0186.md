# From Complex to Humane Systems: Helping Educators to Engage with the Emergent Complexity of Immigration

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents initial findings of a case study of a five-day professional development workshop designed to help K-12 teachers engage with the emergent complexity of immigration in a more humanizing manner. Focusing on the experiences of two teachers, initial results show that even when teachers had ideological and political clarity, they felt disempowered by their lack of understanding the lived experiences of immigrant students and families. The inclusion of personal narratives in the workshop was a design commitment that helped teachers understand the emergent complexity of immigration and engage the full humanity of their students. Guest speakers, books, short stories, movies, art projects, and other narratives made the experiences of both immigrants and people seeking to support immigrants more tangible and also helped teachers make connections with their own lives. Teachers reported feeling more confident they could effectively support their students while being more strategic in their advocacy. Project background The authors are members of Boston University’s Center on Forced Displacement (CFD), an interdisciplinary research center that takes a complex systems theory approach to community-research partnerships. In 2024, we piloted a 6-week workshop for refugee resettlement staff after the leadership requested that we help staff understand the complexity of the refugee regime. While we do not report on this pilot project here, it helped us build our design commitments for a redesigned workshop for K-12 teachers in the surrounding metropolitan area. With this new iteration, we shifted from a lecture-oriented workshop to incorporating humanizing design elements (Bartolomé, 1994, 2004; Salazar, 2013), including sharing narratives, resisting binary answers, and inviting personal and professional reflection. These design commitments were intended to help educators resist the challenges of complex systems (Mitchell, 2009). The workshop took place across five full days, with each day being structured around a different topic. We made space for humanizing elements through art-based reflection activities, the inclusion of humanizing narratives (e.g. books, documentaries, poetry, art), planned critical discussions about topics without clear answers, and opportunities for teachers to collaborate and to create practical projects for their specific contexts. We designed a qualitative case study to understand how emergent complexity manifested for K-12 teachers, and to what extent our humanizing design commitments responded to these challenges. Complex systems and humane engagement In this paper, we combine two frameworks —humanizing pedagogies (Bartolomé, 1994, 2004; Freire, 1970) and complex systems theory (Mitchell, 2009)  to understand how the emergent complexity of immigration impacts teachers’ ability to engage humanely with their students. Dehumanization arises when schools take a decontextualized, one-size-fits-all approach to the education of marginalized students (Bartolomé, 1994; Del Carmen Salazar, 2013). These approaches ignore the current and historical realities that students bring with them, both their strengths as well as the ways that they have experienced oppression and discrimination. This results in deficit notions of learners and expectations to assimilate into mainstream norms in ways that deny students their ability to bring in their whole selves to the classroom (Bartolomé, 1994). A humanizing approach, in contrast, “builds on the sociocultural realities of students’ lives,  examines the sociohistorical and political dimensions of education, and casts students as critically engaged, active participants in the co-construction of knowledge” (Del Carmen Salazar, 2013, p. 128). A humanizing approach tasks teachers with building political and ideological clarity about the socio-cultural-historical realities marginalized students engage with in order to resist this dehumanization (Bartolomé, 2004).

1412 Complex systems theory is an interdisciplinary perspective on complexity that originally arose in the fields of mathematics, science, and computer sciences (Funtowicz & Ravetz, 1994; Manson, 2001; Mitchell, 2009; San Miguel, 2023). Complex systems theory does not refer to how complicated a system is but describes the emergent complexity within a system. Emergent complexity can be contrasted with ordinary complexity where ordinary complexity is stable, describable, and predictable while in emergent complexity, individual parts of the system operate individually and unpredictably, leading to a whole that does not resemble the sum of its parts (Funtowicz & Ravetz, 1994). The principle of emergence suggests that elements of the system are not managed by centrally organized decisions, but emerge from unpredictable interactions of separate and contrasting  parts, leading cause and effect across the system to not be linked in easily observable or linear fashions (Manson, 2001; San Miguel, 2023). This means that interventions on complex systems often do not have expected impact, making it difficult to predict how a change in a system will unfold or how to appropriately respond to this emergent complexity, and making it difficult to see how different parts of a system are interconnected. While we do not claim that emergent complexity is responsible for the discrimination and sociohistorical harms that immigrant students face, we suggest that the unpredictable and non-linear aspects of emergent complexity of immigration make it more difficult for teachers to learn about the sociohistorical realities their immigrant students have faced, to develop political clarity about their work with immigrant families, and to know how to act on these complex systems to bring about change. A complex systems approach has been used as a lens to understand how educational institutions resist reform and professional training efforts (Jacobson et al., 2016, 2019; Sutherland et al., 2023). In this study, we use these frameworks to specifically look at how characteristics of emergent complexity impact teachers’ abilities to take humanizing approaches to education and how addressing this emergent complexity can ease these obstacles.

Originally planned as a multi-stage, iterative design project,  this larger project was interrupted by recent government and university policy and funding restrictions. The design commitments that arose from our pilot project were to have been evaluated in this study and then iterated on in future projects. These design commitments are (a) shifting from lecturing about facts to sharing narratives representing human complexity and diversity, (b) not reducing complexity to binary or certain answers, (c) inviting participants to reflect on their own personal and professional experiences in complex systems, and (d) providing support for concrete actions. These commitments were designed to support our overarching goal to help partners move from complex to humanizing systems (Salazar, 2013). To evaluate how these design commitments helped to meet this goal, we decided to employ  a single qualitative case study (Yin, 2017) of the first stage of this project with attention to how to support teachers to engage humanely with complex systems.

The workshop was advertised to teachers in several districts in surrounding metropolitan areas  as a workshop to learn about the causes and drivers of forced displacement. 20 teachers completed the workshop and received professional development credits for attending in addition to a stipend provided by the CFD. The workshop took place a few weeks before the start of the 2024-2025 school year, and teachers attended for a total of 35 hours, from 9:30am to 4:30pm, Monday to Friday. Prior to attendance, teachers filled out an application to explain their interests, goals, and prior knowledge. Based on the se responses, we developed a syllabus that included narrative texts (creative nonfiction, essays, poetry, and film) and mixed art-based reflections (writing reflections and poetry, creating collages, and drawing), traditional lectures, and engagement with invited guests including a documentary filmmaker and staff from the local refugee resettlement agency. Topics of the workshop included discussion of the immigration system in the global, U.S., and local contexts, language in education, and critiques of political discourse surrounding immigration. Throughout the week, teachers also collaborated in small groups to develop a project tailored to their classes or local contexts that they could implement after the workshop. They presented these projects on the last day of the workshop. Primary forms of data collection included the teachers’ applications, field notes taken throughout the workshop, artifacts created by teachers (collages, poetry, reflections), and semi-structured interviews. 15 teachers agreed to be interviewed with interviews ranging from 30 minutes to an hour. Interviews focused on understanding to what extent teachers felt  supported by their schools  in working with immigrant students, soliciting feedback on the design of the workshop, and how the teachers navigated emergent complexity in their school.  T he findings presented here are initial findings primarily based on interviews. 1413 The authors coded the data collectively (Smagorinsky, 2008) making use of collaborative discussion to align our understanding of the data and resulting codes. Our organization of codes related to emergent complexity was guided by a top-down analysis (Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006) based on our theoretical framework. These categories included codes such as  lack of interconnectedness, lack of understanding of student experiences,  and system is resistant to teacher intervention. However, some codes arose from a bottom-up analysis based on teachers’ descriptions of how the complexity of immigration intersected with the school system. Examples include educational priorities in conflict with humane engagement  and challenges related to continued mobility. These bottom-up codes deepened our understanding of complex systems and how they manifest within schools in ways we had not predicted.

We used our codes to answer our research questions. To answer RQ1, we noted all of the challenges teachers faced, and then looked specifically at which ones contained elements of emergent complexity. To answer RQ2, we evaluated how emergent complexity  constrained teachers ’ humanizing approaches. This involved looking for when teachers made connections between an element of emergent complexity, such as unpredictability, and an aspect of humanizing pedagogies, such as understanding the lived experiences of students. We looked for moments when design elements resisted these elements of  emergent complexity, such as facilitating cross collaboration as resisting lack of interconnectedness, and to what extent this supported teachers in feeling more empowered or in taking specific actions in their schools.

## Initial findings

To describe our initial findings, we focus on two participants, Callie Newman and Johanna Porres  (pseudonyms) because they represent the concerns of many of the teachers. Callie is the primary science teacher for high school newcomer students classified as ELLs (English Language Learners). Johanna is a third grade ELL instructor. Callie is not an immigrant or bilingual but presented herself as an activist and advocate for “at risk” children. Johanna is a Latina immigrant who has also advocated for immigrant families, particularly through f amily engagement work. Both teachers work in the same district, where over a third of students are classified as English learners and over half speak a first language other than English. All quotes below are from their respective interviews.

Emergent challenges: Understanding the lived experiences of immigrant children Both Callie and Johanna sought a  more holistic approach that encompassed the full humanity of their students. This suggested to us that teachers were already committed to humanizing approaches prior to the workshop. For example, when asked what they would like other educators to recognize about  immigrant students, Johanna said to: Be open to the fact that they’re human and they’re also trying to figure things out... we’re focused on, standards, standards, standards,....  We’re not mindful that these are families, and they have stuff going on. Callie similarly responded that “unless you meet all those basic needs, there’s no point in teaching.... Your students are not just students, they are human beings.” Throughout the workshop, teachers’ concerns were rarely about teaching, as they were co nfident in their pedagogical expertise, but often about how to support immigrant students in the most humane and compassionate way.

While humanizing pedagogy typically focuses on helping build ideological and political clarity in teachers (Bartolomé, 2004), we considered  that Callie and Jo hanna had already done much of this critical reflection. However, critical consciousness was not enough for them to feel they could effectively  support their students. They linked their concerns to a difficulty understanding the emergent challenges students faced during experiences of displacement. For example, Callie heard that a student had spent three months in an Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) detention facility prior to arriving in class and wondered if this and similar experiences could help explain in-s chool behavioral issues. Johanna heard that the Haitian children, who had recently moved intonearby migrant shelters, had fled violence, often as unaccompanied minors, and wondered what supports these children might need. The students often behaved in unexpected ways in class, and typical approaches to supporting these students were not enough, reflecting elements of emergent complexity such as unpredictability and non-linearity.

This emergent complexity of immigration made gaining clarity about the lived realities of their  students more difficult. Callie was aware of prior immigration experiences but  felt that she did not have enough specific information to confidently make connections between students’ prior immigration experiences and their learning. Johanna noted that like many teachers, “we’re not really given much time to speak to our students, to really speak to them.” Teachers were reticent to talk with students about topics they did not understand, particularly when there 1414 was a risk of retraumatizing their students. Not feeling able to directly obtain this information from the students and families themselves, both Callie and Johanna sought out the workshop to fill in gaps in understanding the lived experiences of students.  As different elements of complex systems act individually (Funtowicz & Ravetz, 1994), we observed that efforts to learn about children were impacted by lack of interconnectedness between parts of the system, for example content teachers not being in contact with ESL teachers, and teachers in general  not being in contact with immigration and resettlement staff or with families and students. Workshop design: Responding to emergent complexity through the power of narrative One central design commitment was to help teachers cross borders (Bartolomé, 2004) into the lived experiences of students through the use of humanizing narratives. We shared works of creative nonfiction, short stories, graphic novels, film, poetry, and visual art. Teachers reported that, as opposed to learning about the legal immigration system or about statistics and facts, these narratives offered real stories that they could associate with the experiences of their students. Callie explained: It’s like a lived experience, so you’re able to kind of really connect to it.... like, I am not an immigrant. That will never be part of my story, so while reading a story or seeing the... video [it] is really powerful.... you can really connect t hat to the kids you’re supporting in the classroom.

Johanna also spoke of the need for “really getting to know people... getting to know immigrants at a deeper level,” and she recommended hearing migration stories, either from families directly, or from the movies and the books that we offered as something that would help other teachers as well. Beyond learning about the stories of immigrant children, the teachers also benefited from hearing the stories of people who, like them, worked to support children and families moving through the complex system of immigration. Teachers particularly reported valuing a non-fiction book about a translator helping chi ldren to fill out asylum applications. Another highlight was a documentary about Syrian refugees seeking asylum in Europe that was followed by a talk-back with the filmmaker who accompanied the asylum seekers  during their journey. They also appreciated discussions with refugee resettlement staff who helped them understand other parts of the complex system of support for immigrant families. These stories helped them to learn in real time how others had navigated the complexity inherent in their work with displaced persons. While their work was not the same, their experiences of navigating the unpredictable challenges of emergent complexity were similar. Researchers discussing how to prepare teachers to create humanizing learning environments often focus on getting teachers to build empathy and ideological and political clarity (Bartolomé, 2004; del Carmen Salazar, 2013). It was quickly clear to us that the teachers in this workshop already possessed this empathy and  broad political clarity, but elements of emergent complexity such as unpredictability and lack of interconnectedness impacted their ability to learn about their students and to tailor their classroom environments to their needs. For example, s tudents sometimes appeared in their classes in the middle of the year due tonearby migrant  shelters or due to time spent in immigration detention centers, and they did not feel equipped either due to language barriers or fear of traumatization to talk to them directly about their prior experiences with the immigration system. The teachers sought out this workshop and the humanizing narratives presented to them to help fill this gap. Overall, the teachers did feel that they learned more about student experiences and how to support families who moved through the immigration system  and that this led to more confidence and more agency in being able to respond to emergent challenges. Callie and Johanna reported feeling more confident and able to take strategic action to push back against inequality in their schools. T his study suggested that as a research organization, our role was not to help teachers with pedagogy, as they were already ex perts, or to help them be more caring and compassionate, but to help them develop more agency in knowing how to combat dehumanizing learning environments that often conflicted with their pedagogical demands. When it comes to the challenges of emergent complexity, this study suggests that narratives are potential tools in resisting this complexity, potentially because of their ability to center immigrants’ voices and for their ability to allow teachers to make connections with their own work and lives, and for their ability to help navigate emergence by helping teachers see where they fit within a larger complex system. While there are limitations to what personal stories can teach about comple x systems, these narratives helped to make experiences of immigrants more real and human, and to help teachers evaluate the decisions they make moment to moment.
