# Towards Dignity for Multilingual Learners in Science: A Framework to Examine Dignity- Conferring Classroom Interactions

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Resisting dignity-denying practices in education is urgent. This paper is guided by Poza’s (2021) call to center racialized multilingual students as rights-bearing individuals through a dignity frame grounded in meaningful participation, social agency, and social equality. Using secondary qualitative analysis of our own prior studies in science classrooms, we reexamine classroom moments to identify how dignity is conferred. Two cases illustrate this frame: bilingual students’ poetic talk during formative assessment, and a teacher’s curriculum design that leveraged multimodality as poetic “talk” and inclusive sensemaking. Findings highlight how discourse moves, translanguaging, and multimodality can confer dignity by affirming students’ humanity and agency. The dignity frame provides educators and researchers with an analytic and pedagogical tool to identify, enact, and design dignity-conferring classroom interactions.

Resisting the dignity-denying practices of education and educational research has never felt more urgent than in the current moment. Increased ICE raids and executive orders (i.e., EO 14224) are but a few new additions to the timeless track record of anti-immigrant violence and dignity-denying practices. As such, we offer a conceptual framework that takes up Poza’s (2021) invitation to construct a dignity frame that reorients our analytical lens toward foundational values of personhood, including social agency, equality, and meaningful participation. This secondary qualitative analysis examines data from our previous studies through a dignity frame to foreground how pedagogies confer dignity for multilingual (ML) students. Two pedagogical focal areas are prominent in our own prior studies in the field. First, teachers’ discourse moves matter when creating equitable sensemaking opportunities in science classrooms (González- Howard & McNeill, 2016). These moves can either open up or close students’ opportunities to engage in productive uncertainty (Manz & Suárez, 2018) and to demonstrate their science sensemaking (Haverly et al., 2020). In addition, teachers can use translanguaging pedagogies to sustain dynamic communication in their classrooms by engaging in translanguaging shifts-adjustments in discourse and facilitation that keep student meaning-making central while communicating across named languages  and semiotic repertoires  (García et al., 2017; Fine et al., 2023). These two pedagogical areas of focus have taken center stage in the field and our research. However, Poza’s (2021) call to “position racialized bilingual students as rights-bearing individuals first and language learners second” has motivated us to reorient our analytical stance toward our data through a dignity frame.

## Conceptual framework

Dignity is inherent in people, but the lived experience and sense of one’s own dignity is deeply dependent on relationships and interactions between educators and learners (Espinoza et al., 2020). Too often, educational experiences and interactions can be dignity-denying-stripping students of their sense of rights and worth when educational interactions fail to see and hear students as meaningful participants with worthy contributions (Chavous et al., 2023; Valenzuela, 1999). “Doing school differently” can fundamentally reshape learners' experiences of dignity by affirming and conferring dignity through interactions (Espinoza et al., 2020, p. 339). Poza’s (2021) insistence that dignity frames have power to demand interactional conditions that create full personhood and that do not “reduce students’ rich humanity to their English proficiency” motivates this project (pp. 483-484). This paper offers our preliminary effort to create and try out a dignity frame for analyzing and interpreting multilingual science classroom learning experiences. We draw directly from Poza’s (2021) dignity frame when conceptualizing the three facets that make up our triangular dignity frame. We envision our dignity frame as a triangle composed of three equally important facets (See Figure 1). One facet is meaningful participation when people exercise their voices in consequential learning environments structured by dialogic relationships (Espinoza & Vossoughi, 2014). A second facet is social agency as an actor and a contributor to the social interactions of a group (Poza, 2021). In classrooms, this can look like ML students having freedom and authority to speak up and be recognized as valuable participants. A third 1134 facet is social equality-a facet that invokes both educational policies and practices protecting students’ equal rights. Poza (2021) offers examples of schools ensuring that curricula, pedagogies, and grouping practices recognize ML students’ and communities’ knowledge, linguistic repertoire, and vital contribution to meaningful participation in classrooms. Taken together, these three facets create a frame through which we can look at classroom interactions to analyze and interpret how those interactions might be dignity-conferring or dignitydenying. To support our analytical efforts, we alsoneeded some tangible ways to enter the frame with specific ways of seeing and hearing how a moment of classroom interaction might be dignity-conferring. Espinoza et al., 2020 suggest three indicators, but we suspe ct that there are likely many more. One indicator, poetic or artful “talk,” includes informal interactions that generate a sense of communion and solidarity. For example, word play, gestures, and laughter as students share a common cultural reference durin g class time indicate artful “talk.” Another indicator, disagreements treated as invitations, occurs when a teacher or student marks disagreement as an opening for further interaction. This might occur through explicit talk moves, recording disagreements on postit notes, and/or through body language. A final indicator, recursive pedagogical looping, occurs as teachers return to students’ contributions for continued discussion and sensemaking. ## Method

We engaged in secondary analysis of qualitative data from our own previous research studies to build and refine our dignity framework (Heaton, 2008). Secondary analysis of qualitative data involves new analysis of pre-existing data gathered in prior studies that were organized around different research questions, often questions that emerged from the initial analysis of data or from literature and theorizing related to the data. This purpose is exactly what motivates our secondary analysis seeking a way to conceptualize and analyze dignity-conferring features of classroom science interactions among teachers and their ML students. Caitlin, an English/Spanish sequential bilingual, taught science at a dual language elementary school. Sam, a second-generation Korean/English multilingual from immigrant parents, taught middle and high school science. Melissa, an English monolingual, taught elementary and secondary science in multilingual communities. Melissa provided a triangulation lens as an outsider to the origi nal studies. We are all experienced teacher educators attending to science and culturally and linguistically diverse communities. Specifically, we analyzed two data sets derived from Caitlin and Sam’s previous studies. Case 1 took place within a larger participatory co-design project focused on integrating and reflecting on translanguaging in sixth grade science formative assessments  (Fine, 2022; Fine et al., 2023; Fine & Braaten, 2025). This analysis takes a 'microlook'  at interactions between two students, the teacher, and the researcher where students worked collaboratively to share what they understood about matter and energy in ecosystems. Case 2 took place within a professional learning community (PLC) focused on co-designing curriculum customizations to support MLs’ equitable sensemaking using a translanguaging lens (Lee et al., 2024; Lee & McNeill, In review; Lee et al., In review). The analysis looks at one seventh grade teacher’s stimulated recall interview (Geiger et al., 2016) where he reflects on the affordances and limitations of a customized lesson about contact forces. Across cases, we sought examples of linguistic and n onlinguistic semiotic communication where humor, emotion, or narrative (poetic/artful “talk”) represented opportunities to be positioned as valuable contributors. Case 1: Making space for dignity in-the-moment Case 1 follows William and Charlie’s poetic “talk” in whole group and small group settings over three days. This example highlights how pausing to make space for students’ poetic “talk” is, at its core, a dignity-conferring move 1135 enabling science sensemaking and translanguaging. William and Charlie identified as Spanish/ English bilingual youth, and they had attended the same suburban school district since Kindergarten-a district with a de facto English-language instructional policy. Emily, the monolingual English-speaking teacher with over 20 years of experience and a concentration in Culturally and Linguistically Diverse Education, regularly encouraged translanguaging. When we look at the extended example of poetic ‘talk’ from a dignity lens, we see dignity-conferring moves made by Emily (the teacher) and Caitlin (the researcher) that may have been consequential for students’ science sensemaking and languaging decisions. On Day 1, the class collectively co-created a Spanish /English bilingual anchor chart about biotic and abiotic elements of ecosystems. During this conversation, Emily built a sense of social equality when she encouraged Charlie to use Spanish to help a newcomer classmate meaningfully participate through expressing his idea about mushrooms-a word Charlie translated into Spanish as hongos. William and Charlie initiate a metalinguistic disagreement about hongos  as mushrooms versus hongos  as something that ‘grows on your feet.’ Poetic “talk” helped them build a sense of solidarity as both Emily and students giggled about a common television jingle on their local Spanish-language channel for athlete’s foot (See Figure 2). While the class finished cocreating the bilingual anchor chart, Charlie and William continued to discuss in Spanish about different types of hongos. William shared: ‘Esperate. Hay dos tipos de hongos, ¿verdad?’ Charlie responded by sharing ‘Yeah. Uno es de sus…en los pies.’ William added ‘Like, if sometimes if you wear tight shoes...then you could get it.’ Caitlin followed up quietly with William and Charlie, encouraging them to elevate their prior experiences and disagreement about hongos to the whole class, highlighting an opportunity for collective growth of students’ science sensemaking and linguistic repertoires. This led the entire class into a lengthy discussion in both Spanish and English about hongos  and champiñones, the differences between the two words, and whether this concept represented an abiotic or biotic element of ecosystems. Her attention moved students toward social equality, agency, and meaningful participation through poetic or artful “talk.” Case 2: Designing for dignity We think this framework can also help us understand how we might design for dignity in the materials we use with students. This example highlights how designing with a translanguaging lens, especially when attuning to non-linguistic modes or multimodality as poetic “talk”, opens classroom spaces for dignity-conferring moments. Mr. Bob was a seventh-grade science teacher with 11 years of teaching experience, certified to support students with disabilities, with an endorsement for sheltered English instruction (Echevarria et al., 2011). As a problem of practice (Horn & Little, 2010), Mr. Bob wanted to cultivate a linguistically inclusive classroom, so his MLs had more ownership in their learning. The school is a large urban middle school in the New England area, called Broad Middle School (BMS). Mr. Bob and Sam underwent three cycles of curriculum customization (Lee & McNeill, 1136 2024). This study focused on a class of 24 students where a third of the students were MLs, in which Portuguese, Spanish, and Farsi were the languages spoken other than English. The following paragraphs illustrate one stimulated recall interview examining MLs’ models of contact forces. In this illustrative case, within the co-design work, an unintended insight was how the design opened the learning environment as a dignity-conferring space by explicitly making space for poetic or artful ‘talk’ as multimodal meaning-making resources. The customization to the student worksheet encouraged MLs to develop a model of a related phenomenon to help explain a contact force. When asked to reflect on the design choice, Mr. Bob articulated why the design better supported his MLs’ equitable sensemaking and was a more generative assessment for student understanding rather than a “traditional” assessment. He said, It's supportive because it allows them to draw on related phenomenon that they've picked and wanna relate it to. So there's ownership there. Um, it allows them to, like, be creative with their thought and, um, expression of what they know, like, um, drawing a model versus, um, some other form of assessment.

In the quote, Mr. Bob described how the design provided students with an open format “to draw on related phenomenon that they’ve picked and wanna relate it to,” while providing MLs with the agency  to “be creative with their thought and, um, expression.” Mr. Bob asked his MLs to showcase what is happening when a moving object makes contact with a nonmoving object, which causes damage. When reflecting on the ML’s poetic “talk” within their models, Mr. Bob said, “Yeah, for some reason they got hooked on video games, games and, like, having these scenarios happen in a video game. But yeah, hers was, like, about buildi-like, mining in Minecraft, but then falling off a cliff as she was mining.” In the quote, Mr. Bob is tapping into the ML’s sensemaking by noticing and affirming Minecraft as a valid related phenomenon to explain collisions within a contact force. When reflecting on the student work and customization further, Mr. Bob specifically valued the MLs’ multimodal or non-linguistic modes as generative resources for sensemaking as a form of social equality. He said, She didn't draw the, like, anchoring phenomenon model. So they're getting, like, the process of, like, drawing models, using symbols, using colors to represent thinking. So I think there's a lot of good stuff like that in here to, like, express what they know, express their knowledge and application of what they're learning in class to, um, real world scenarios… In the quote, Mr. Bob highlighted how the models his MLs created didn’t reproduce a class consensus model (e.g., “anchoring phenomenon model”). Rather, the MLs used scenarios from their own examples and used visual modes to express their ideas through “color” and “symbols” (i.e., red to represent friction). When valuing visual modes or multimodal forms as rich meaning-making resources, Mr. Bob helped  to expand his ML’s meaningful participation but also tapped into his ML’s rich poetic and artful ‘talk’ for sensemaking. This case highlights how teachers can not only enact dignity-conferring classroom interactions for MLs in-the-moment, but also plan to make space for them.

The dignity frame presented here represents a new way for science teachers and researchers to plan for and analyze how classroom interactions can confer dignity in science for MLs through social agency, equality and meaningful participation. Two cases demonstrate strong examples of how teachers might create dignity-conferring spaces in science classrooms through poetic and artful ‘talk’ in ways that were consequential for honoring students’ full personhood and supporting their sensemaking. Ultimately this exploratory secondary qualitative analysis gives us reason to believe that a dignity frame offers strong potential both as a design framework useful for orienting teaching toward dignity-conferring pedagogies and as an analytical framework useful for teachers and educational researchers to see, hear, and interpret how classroom interactions can and should affirm dignity and rights of ML youth who are too often denied dignity.
