# Exploring the Significance of Explanation in the Construction of Conceptual Metaphors

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Previous research showed that embodied activities can facilitate learning irrespective of the learner’s awareness of the aim of the activity. In contrast, we present findings that suggest a need for this awareness under specific circumstances. We present the results of a study, in which the students received force feedback as a grounding opportunity for the concept of force in a quantum chemical context. We previously found that if the students were not aware of the mapping between their sensory experience and the learning content, the force feedback had a hindering effect on the learning outcome. In this work, we introduced the metaphor explicitly. The force feedback increased perceived relevance, curiosity, and positive affect, if, and only if, this explanation was added. We attribute this finding to an increase in cognitive salience and a removal of mismapping opportunities.

## Introduction

Learning of imperceptible concepts is often difficu lt due to a lack of prior experiences that the new information can be related to. Examples of such concepts are climate change or molecules and their behavior as they are either too big or small to be perceived. The grounded and embodied learning paradigm (GEL; Nathan, 2022) suggests grounding, mapping new information to meaningful pr ior experiences, as a relevant learning mechanism for learning in general and for imperceptible concepts specifically. Learners can understand a concept by means of familiar sensorimotor experiences. Importantly for this work,  grounding new information in prior experiences does not necessarily require the student to be aware of the learning goal (Abrahamson et al., 2020). However, this holds potential for misconceptions by mapping unintended characteristics (Niebert et al., 2012). In a recent study, we investigated the effect of fo rce feedback provided by a haptic device on student s’ learning of the concept of the quantum chemical notion of force as the negative derivative of the potential energy (F = -∇E, Müller et al., 2024). The instructions were identical except for the techn ical introduction to the device. In particular, the students were not introduced to the meaning of the feedback. We found a small hindering effect of the haptic feedback on the learning outcome.

Here, we present a study in an identical setting, with the students receiving additional explanation of the metaphor offered by the force feedback or the more literal visual representation of force, to investigate the effect of the additional explanations of the intended grounding opportunity on several learning mechanisms.

## Conceptual framework

We draw from the GEL framework insofar as we hypoth esize grounding to be an important mechanism  in the construction of abstract knowledge. We focus on one specific form of grounding, the constr uction of conceptual metaphors. Conceptual metaphor theory suggests that often, metaphors are not only linguistic expression s, but constituents of cognition  (Kövecses, 2020). Important for the present work, metaphors always highlight certain characteristics of the source domain and hide other s. If the students cannot correctly distinguish whi ch characteristics to integrate and which to obscure, they may construct potentially robust misconception s (Niebert et al., 2012). For example, if the learner understa nds the chemical bond as a stick, a physical object, they might not only map the structural, intended aspect of the  stick to the chemical bond but also that energy is  required to build such a physical object. Energy being required for bond formation is one of the most common misconceptions related to the chemical bond (Boo, 1998).

We hypothesize that grounding opportunities  in preparation for learning, such as offering a pulling and pushing sensation corresponding to the quantum chem ical force, support the activation of design-specific, in this case mechanisms specific to preparation for future learning (PFL; S chwartz & Bransford, 1998; Sinha & Kapur, 2021), such as knowledge gap awareness, affect, or state  curiosity, if successfully adopted  by the students. We previously found a small negative effect of haptic feedback on the learning outcome (Müller et al., 202 4) and argued that it might be due to the lack of explicit explanation of the metaphor.  In the present work, w e further hypothesize that successful grounding supports motivational variables, specifically perceived relevance, as found in a previous study of similar design  (Chatain et al., 2023). Consequently, we hypothesiz e performance to be higher for students receiving the grounding opportunity.

## Participants

The study was conducted with participants of a physical chemistry laboratory course at ETH Zurich, Switzerland, as part of a two-day computational experiment  (n = 48, female = 17, male = 30, non-binary/third gender = 0). Informed consent was sought and received  from all student s. The participants were assigned to one of the conditions depending on which week of the semester they participated in the experiment. The course marked their first encounter with computational chemistry. Notably, they were not yet aware of the multidimensional nature of the potential energy hypersurface and how the force  depends on the change in energy.  However, we expected them to be familiar with forces in a physics contex t and with the vector representation of forces.  The study was approved by the ethics committee of the university and was preregistered on OSF. Material Set-up The learning environment was an open-source graphical quantum chemistry environment (see Figure 1, Bensberg et al., 2022). While the learner selects and moves an atom across the screen, the program calculates the change in energy and hence also the force that would act on this atom  in real-time (Haag & Reiher, 2013; Marti & Reiher, 2009). Moreover, unselected atoms change their position spontaneously towards a structure with minimal energy. As a result, the other atoms might follow the movement of the learner to maintain a stable molecular structure. We followed a between-subjects preparatory problem-solving design (Loibl et al., 2016; Schwartz & Bransford, 1998). We compared students who received force feedback together with an explanation (HD w FF w E) to students who did not receive force feedback, but a visual representation of the force together w ith an explanation of it (HD w/o FF w E). The latter representation was introduced to create a fair comparison group and showed the force as arrows on the individual atoms with the length corresponding to the strength of the force. The problem that needed to be solved by students wa s finding the reaction mechanism of a Diels-Alder reaction. The set-up was highly similar to the study by Müller and co-workers (2024). However, the chemical reaction that the students performed was changed to correspond to the reaction of interest for the remaining of the course. This ensured a better incorporation of the study content into the course content  which enhanced the authenticity of the learning setting. Furthermore, the intended metaphor was explained  in the instructions. The students received the following explanations in writing (developed by two of the authors who are both chemists). The explanations were highlighted and were further mentioned verbally by the instructor. A bigger repulsion corresponds to a steeper ascend in energy (and similarly a bigger attraction to a steeper descend), similarly to when you are climbing a steeper mountain. The effort needed to reach the peak is higher than if it was a hill. You feel the force, which acts on the selected atom. (HD w FF w E) The arrows correspond to the forces, which act on the individual atoms. The longer the arrows, the steeper the ascend or descend of the energy. (HD w/o F w E)

## Measures

Grades of the first-year introductory course were taken as proxy for re levant prior content knowledge. Mechanisms relevant to problem-solving included positive affect (Total 끫븨II = 0.92, Watson et al., 1988), knowledge gap awareness (0.91, Glogger-Frey et al., 2017) and state curiosity (0.91, Naylor, 1981). We further measured perceived relevance (0.90, Keller, 2010) and extraneous load (0.93, Glogger-Frey et al., 2017). Towards the end of the semester, the learning outcome was m easured through an in-house developed posttest  (Müller et al., 2024). The multiple-choice posttest was validated by two chemists and was refined twice. The test measured multiple interconnected concepts  such as change of potential energy relate d to molecular structure, change of energy related to force, energy difference related to reactivity or energy extrema as critical points during reactions.

## Design and procedure

The students were first introduced to the environment by navigation tasks s uch as rotating, zooming or dragging and dropping of an atom. After self-proclaimed proficiency, they moved on to the problem-solving phase in which they performed a Diels-Alder reaction. After successfully creating the product, we gathere d data on the motivational and affective variables.  Finally, the students optimized the relevant structures of the Diels-Alder reaction with a common quantum chemical method. The study took 90 minutes out of the 8 hours of the course. The content knowledge posttest was sent out to all via e-mail towards the end of the semester. The time difference between the intervention and the posttes t was not a significant predictor for posttest perf ormance (F(1,11) = 0.36, p = 0.56). However, this led to a low response rate (n = 13). ## Discussion

In this work, we presented a study  that investigated how force feedback might facilita te the learning of the imperceptible concept force in a quantum chemical c ontext. We previously found that providing the experience without support led to a hindering effect of the force feedback in this  specific context  (Müller et al., 202 4). Providing the students with metaphorical explanation of their experience activated relevant learning mechanisms more than providing the students with explanation for a more traditional disembodied visual representation. This seems to contradict evidence suggesting that embodied activities support learning processes even if the learners are not aware “that or what they are learning” (Abrahamson et al., 2020). We offer two explanations for our findings which should be investigated further. Firs t, we argue that the grounding opportunity needs to  be conceptually salient (Rau & Herder, 2021). The students  in Müller and co-workers (2024) navigated a complex three-dimensional molecular simulation while receiving in formation on the energy change on a graph and being pulled by the haptic device. The connection between energy change and force might have not been obvious to the students without proper introduction. This is further supported by the short-lived experience of force. Second, the conceptual mapping of the students migh t be incorrect without proper support. Niebert and co-workes (2012) suggested that many misconceptions of imperceptible concepts can be explained by comparing expert to novice metaphors. By offering an explicit metaphor to the students together with the experience potentially prevented the construction of unintended mappings. Concerning the PFL-specific mechanisms, we found that students who receive force feedback together with an explanation showed higher state curiosity and positive affect immediately after the intervention, suggesting that the intervention met the affective draw criterion (Sinha & Kapur, 2021). The metaphor was made explicit by comparison to an experience that the students potentially could relate to, a hike to the top of a mountain. We further replicated findings from Chatain and co-workers (2023), indicating that students engaging in embodied activity perceive the intervention as more personally relevant. They argued that grounding opportunities activate among others modal priming, which then increases the perceived relevance of the target concept (Chatain et al., 2023; Körner et al., 2015). Modal priming d escribes how  embodied interaction can activate abstract concepts. The embodied experience allows the students to reconnect the content, which is not experiential in both their and our study, to prior experiences, which is a crucial part of relevance in learning. Finally, this work is limited by the lack of statistical powe r in the posttest measure  and the potential confusion due to the short introduction time to the new and unfamiliar device. Allowing the students to work with the haptic device over a longer timespan might incr ease their ability to benefit from it.  Furthermore, while the study showed high ecological validity due to its embedding into a university course, future studies should focus on increased internal validity by reproducing the findings in a more controlled setting.
