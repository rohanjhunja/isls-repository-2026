# Posthumanizing Creativity with GenAI: Assemblages in a Design Workshop

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We present a case study of a five day design workshop in India where middle school students produced linked artifacts: hero characters, songs, short videos, while working with GenAI tools. Grounded in posthuman-sociomaterial perspectives and current Learning Science conversations on hybrid intelligence, we treat creativity as emergent forms in human– AI– material assemblages. Using diffractive reading of observations, semi-structured interviews, we craft posthuman vignettes to make nonhuman participation visible. Findings suggest that students take relational stances toward the agent, entanglements “glow” around near-constraint extensions and hard-constraint violations, and boundaries of authorship blurs as students engage with GenAI tools in creative acts.

Recent research works related to AI are accelerating a shift from solely human‑centered collaboration toward hybrid, more‑than‑human constellations in which learners coordinate with conversational agents and creative generators (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023; Pöysä‑Tarhonen, 2025). This turn towards hybrid intelligence, asking how human and AI capabilities can be combined to augment learning processes while keeping collaborative activity at the center. In this framing, the empirical gap is less about whether AI improves outcomes, and more about how learner–AI interactions reorganize regulation, coordination, and judgments of actions in context (Cukurova, 2024; Dellermann et al., 2019; Järvelä, Nguyen, & Hadwin, 2023). A complementary shift in the research community calls for decentering the human, acknowledging the entangled agency of non‑human actors—interfaces, datasets, model priors, defaults, and material constraints —in shaping what becomes thinkable and doable in collaboration. This perspective, translated from the domain of Human Computer Interaction ‘more‑than‑human’ design discourse, invites empirical methods that render non‑human contributions visible such as ethnographies with non‑humans, thick observations of learner–AI sessions, and design approaches that focus on relations and ecologies rather than users alone (Barron, 2003; Cress & Kimmerle, 2023; Giaccardi, 2020).

These agendas are especially salient in creative learning, where youth now compose across modalities with AI systems that propose continuations, impose defaults, and introduce productive frictions. From a distributed-collective creativity view, possibilities emerge through webs of engagements rather than isolated units. The reframed question, then, is not whether Gen‑AI makes students ‘more creative,’ but how human–AI–material assemblages redistribute authorship, evaluation, and coordination over time, and with what mechanisms towards the ongoing creative acts.

We contribute to this conversation with an analysis of a creative technologies workshop in Mumbai, India organized at an alternative school. Students produced linked artifacts such as hero characters, theme songs (music), and short videos by using multiple GenAI systems (ChatGPT, Suno, Sora). We treat the design workshop as a sociomaterial ecology, human-tool-artifact trajectories alongside interactional engagements. With this article, we respond to the call from the research community for  qualitative accounts by crafting posthuman vignettes that make non‑human contributions inspectable and mapping assemblages that show how these actors reconfigure participation and authorship (Järvelä et al., 2025, keune, 2022; Pöysä‑Tarhonen, 2025). To that end we look at the research question: How do Indian middle‑school students engage with Gen‑AI tools to arrive at collaborative creative outcomes?

Theoretical grounding We approach creativity in youth GenAI studios through a posthuman-sociomaterial lens (Barad, 2007; Keune, 2022; Orlikowski, 2007). Rather than treating creativity as a property of individuals or as the output of a human wielding a neutral tool, we conceptualize it as an emergent effect of assemblages: configurations of learners, peers, AI models, prompts, datasets, interfaces, devices, and temporal rhythms (Orlikowski, 2007; Suchman, 2007). Posthuman scholarship argues that agency is distributed and co-constituted across human and nonhuman actants; what happens in activity is not simply caused by human intention but is intra-actively produced through 1702 relations among heterogeneous elements (Barad, 2007; Bennett, 2010). In this view, tools and materials do not merely mediate action; they participate in and reconfigure it (Ingold, 2013; Malafouris, 2013). Within this stance, creativity can be described at the level of relations, and surfaces when the assemblage rearranges constraints and possibilities so that different acts play out and become recognizable (Glăveanu, 2014; Sawyer & DeZutter, 2009). The “creative move” is not located in one student’s mind but in the reconfiguration of the assemblage. This framing resists two unhelpful reductions: attributing creative acts solely to human ideation, or attributing it to “the AI.” Instead, it asks what configuration of human–AI–material elements made a new trajectory viable, and how that trajectory took shape via collective acts (Csikszentmihalyi, 1996; Glăveanu, 2014).

Recasting collaboration through a posthuman lens means decentering the human without erasing human contribution. AI models, interfaces, datasets, templates, and physical/digital materials count as collaborators insofar as they make differences that matter as they constrain, invite, delay, amplify, and redirect creative action (Barad, 2007; Orlikowski, 2007). Collaboration is therefore a configuration: who participates (students, mentors), what participates (LLMs, music/video generators), and how they are li nked together define the activity, where any change in participants or linkages can explain shifts in creative direction (Suchman, 2007). Further, breakdowns can be considered as productive frictions where the participating entities and their relations ope n up unexpected creative possibilities  (Malafouris, 2013; Suchman, 2007). This stance supports a plural notion of authorship negotiated across humans and nonhumans, with ethical implications for acknowledgment, provenance, and accountability (Kafai & Burke, 2014; Orlikowski, 2007).

## Context

The study took place in Mumbai, India where middle school children from resource constrained families participated in hands-on workshops that focus on GenAI tools for creative explorations. The workshop was conducted by two lead instructors and two facilitators. The structure of the workshop was in the form-Day 1: setup and image generation with ChatGPT; Day 2: Hero profile building with ChatGPT, Suno, Sora; Day 3: Play Telephone game manually with hero characters; Day 4: Automate Telephone game with heroes characters using vibe coding, and Day 5: Presentations to parents and fellow school mates. Students were introduced to the AI tools: ChatGPT, Suno, Sora and Cursor. In the workshop, the telephone game  is a pass-the-work activity where each student first creates a superhero profile (backstory, image, short jingle), then writes a short merge instruction so someone else can blend their hero with a neighbor’s. The hero characters are then passed around the group in rounds, with each person applying the merge prompt to produce a new hybrid before passing it on again.

## Data analysis

We adopt a posthuman-sociomaterial stance that treats learning and creativity as outcomes of entanglements among humans, nonhumans, and materials (Barad, 2007; Keune, 2022; Orlikowski, 2007). As we think with these theoretical perspectives (Jackson & Mazzei, 2012), we encounter intriguing  “hot spots,’’ and ‘‘glowing’ moments” in the coming together of the learner and GenAI tools. We attend to how GenAI tools operate as actants and participate in the co‑construction of activity through intra‑action in the  creative engagement. We use diffractive reading (Taguchi, 2012) of the data corpus to analyze these entanglements so that the relational autonomy of GenAI tools are taken into account that enact agential cuts and shape creative trajectories. To capture a comprehensive understanding of the workshop experience, we followed onfield observations, collection of artifacts and  semi-structured interviews. The observation notes focused on capturing student interaction with the Gen-AI tools, the iterative nature o f their creative processes, and instances of "creative dissonance" where unexpected AI outputs sparked new directions.The observations also documented the level of human agency maintained by students, noting any instances of passive consumption or over-reliance on AI versus active interpretation. Whereas the semi-structured interviews helped in following subjective experiences, perceptions, and reflections of the students.We follow a case study methodology (Merriam, 2002) to get a deeper understanding of th e engagement between students (S1-male, S2-male) and GenAI tools, where these cases acted as representative accounts of similar student participations. The close reading of the data in diffractive ways helped us in understanding how the students engaged with GenAI tools to arrive at collaborative creative outcomes. The students and GenAI engaged in continuous  negotiation through resistance and accommodat ion where agency in actions was distributed among these actors. Such 1703 distributed ways of engagement and authorships surfaced in the students’ perceptions and reflections related to the experience working together with GenAI tools. These insights are presented as posthuman vignettes as illustrated below: Vignette 1: “The” and “He” In the semi-structured interviews with the two participants, we observed strikingly different relational framings of the GenAI tool that map onto distinct patterns of agency distribution. Student S3 consistently referenced the model impersonally as “the ChatGPT,” describing it as a source of options and opinions. S1 noted, “I asked the ChatGPT when I had problems or doubts....the ChatGPT gave some answers. When the design was not coming, I gave some prompts, it worked….the ChatGPT gave the images”. Meanwhile S2 repeatedly personified ChatGPT as “he,” narrating a dialogic relationship reported, “I used ChatGPT for many things.. He is like a friend, teacher, sometimes my worker.. I give commands and he works”. Read through a posthuman sociomaterial lens, the students’ pronoun framing shows divergent socio-epistemic stances and marks different participation structures that materially reconfigure the assemblage.  The first positioning (“The”) points to direct inpu t-output and selection-rejection based engagement  whereas the second positioning (“he”) underlines a generative and coconstructive stance. This vignette demonstrates that students’ relational framings - “the ChatGPT” versus “he”are not merely rhetorical but are consequential resources that redistribute agency across humans, GenAI, and shared artifacts.

Figure 1 Superhero Characters Created by Students and ChatGPT Vignette 2: Affective entanglements The data analysis further revealed affective entanglements as students worked with GenAI tools, where surprise acted as expansion and disappointment manifested as misalignment and deviation. We observe that such distribution of agency is anchored around students’ use of “listening” as a relational metaphor to evaluate fit between intention and output. S2 noted, “I asked him for small changes to the hands of my hero and the color… he added different things I never thought of…. It was different but it gave an orange like funny look to the hero.. I liked it.”, see Figure 1. Here, “listening” is used as a relational metaphor to mark near-constraint divergence where the output extends the stated intent by extending the idea and expanding the design boundaries. In a similar way S1 added to the idea of “listening”, “ It was difficult in the beginning…the ChatGPT was not following my commands. I asked to make a hero with mysterious powers.. like shadow…invisible…. not listening,  I was disappointed”. The “disappointment” pointed out is not noise around the work but it organizes the next steps of engagements where differences among inputs and outputs are negotiated agentially. These two narrative variants show how agency manifests when students set intentional outputs, the system proposes variations, and shared artifacts reflect corresponding changes.

Vignette 3: Telephone game-fragments and fadings As we read the data, the telephone game proved to be hotspots of understanding how agency and collaboration play out especially in the case where students and GenAI engaged in creating the hero characters. S1 pointed out,"When we were playing the telephone game, the ChatGPT was  very slow... took a lot of time to respond…I waited,... I restarted again and again. I want all the tools at one place,.. not changing from one to another… it 1704 breaks… at last it came out.. Everyone added new things to my hero”.  S2 on the other hand talked about how the superhero started to evolve beyond his ideas as passed through different students and their corresponding GenAI tools, “ My first hero didn’t stay… After two passes it felt like someone else’s. …I couldn’t see my original anymore, that was not mine”.  Here in the telephone game, the rule to pass on the characters to other students and involvement of  corresponding GenAI tools make original authorship less visible in a way that one student experiences as collective extension (S1), another experiences as personal loss (S2). The agentive center of the creative activity is not in any one person or the GenAI tools but along the temporal rhythms of waiting, rituals  of restarting, platforms of scattered  and  unified toolspaces, rules of  telephone game together with the students and GenAI tools  co-producing progress, halts, drift and deviations. This study reframes youth GenAI making from the familiar image of “students using a tool” to the coordination of human–AI–material assemblages in which agency and authorship are negotiated moment-to-moment. Reading our data diffractively, we find that creative outcomes are not the property of any single contributor but the effect of relations among learners, conversational partners, task rules, shared representations, and the rhythms of activity. This reframing helps address our central question: how middle-school students engage with GenAI to arrive at collaborative creative outcomes?, by following the engagements among the students and GenAI tools,  through which collaboration is reorganized. We show that students’ relational stances toward the agent (“the assistant” vs. “he”) function as socio-epistemic cues that shift participation and progress that adds to the hybrid-intelligence claims and aligns with ISLS’s calls to examine augmentation in situ (Järvelä et al., 2025) By repositioning creativity as an assemblage-level accomplishment, our analysis also connects to collective and distributed creativity traditions (Hutchins, 1995; Glăveanu, 2014; Sawyer & DeZutter, 2009).The data also speak directly to posthuman-sociomaterial arguments that agency is co-constituted across humans, artifacts, and activity structures (Barad, 2007; Bennett, 2010; Orlikowski, 2007; Suchman, 2007). In our vignettes, rules (e.g., pass-along merges), representations, and rhythms enact agential cuts that channel what becomes thinkable and doable, in alignment with notions of material-engagement that tools and inscriptions participate in rather than merely mediate action (Ingold, 2013; Malafouris, 2013). Students’ “surprise” and “not listening” judgments make these cuts visible where near-constraint divergence is experienced as creative extension, while hard-constraint violations are experienced as deviations. This distinction refines posthuman accounts with markers for scenarios where  nonhuman contributions are considered valuable collaboration. More broadly, posthumanizing creativity made non-human participation visible and inspectable without displacing human contribution. For learning scientists, this implies that the unit of design and assessment is neither the learner nor the technology alone, but the relations that bind them. Designing for those relations creates conditions under which distributed agency becomes a resource for learning rather than a source of confusion or loss.
