# University Instructors' Aesthetic Encounters in Maker-Centered Learning through Posthumanist and New Materialist Perspectives

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines university instructors’ aesthetic encounters in maker-centered learning, drawing on posthumanist and new materialist perspectives. Two key themes emerged—material-sensory entanglement and distributed agency for aesthetic engagement—suggesting how material and sensory engagement foster imagination and emotional resonance while aesthetic encounters are distributed through dynamic interactions with peers and materials. The findings suggest the potential of prioritizing aesthetic encounters in maker-centered learning and provide implications for future studies.

## Introduction

The maker movement has gained global recognition in  recent decades for its emphasis on hands-on, experiential learning within collaborative and interdisciplinary environments (Pepper et al., 2016). While maker activities are often framed through STEAM lear ning (science, technology, engineering, art, and mathematics) (Martin, 2015), the arts and aesthetic experiences (A of STEAM) are  less prioritized compared to STEM components (Clapp & Jimmenez, 2016; Clapp, 2017). Echoing Baumgarten’s philosophy, aesthetics extends beyond artistic or v isual appeal to the “science of perception,” which system atizes sensory experiences as integral to knowledge construction (Heybach, 2020; Guyer, 2014). Aesthetic encounters encompass perception, sensation, imagination, and their relationship to understanding and feeling  the world (Clapp & Jimmenez, 2016). In maker-centered learning—an approach rooted in the maker movement that empha sizes creative, hands-on problem-solving through the design and creation of tangible artifacts —these experiences emerge as students actively engag e with physical materials to produce visible, audible, or tactile objects. By guiding learners through sensory and material interactions, aesthetic encounters are believed to challenge traditions and monotony (Schonmann, 2020), encourage personal expression and emotional engagem ent (Greene, 2000; Kafai et al., 2014), and foster cultural and identity explorations (Halverson, 2013). By aes thetically re-distributing and re-examining predetermined standards or norms (Lewis, 2014), aesthetics in lea rning empowers learners to find value in sensory ex periences and uncover the richness and meaning in the often-overlooked aspects of mundane life. Posthumanism and new materialism are emerging persp ectives for understanding maker-centered experiences (Renlund et al., 2023; Sheridan et al.,  2020). Posthumanist frameworks reimagine human age ncy as interconnected within a more-than-human world, and new materialist perspectives highlight the entanglement  of actions, materials, and environments (Barad, 2007; Rousell et al., 2022). These perspectives encourage  a move beyond dualistic and human-centric thinking, and the ability  to embrace uncertainty and ambiguity through entangled sensory experiences and interconnections in the Anthropocene era (Leonard, 2020: Rousell et al., 2022). While these frameworks have gained popularity in th e learning sciences, the broader theoretical history of the distributed agency has deeper roots in earlier trad itions—including distributed cognition and Indigenous epistemologies that have long emphasized reciprocal  and ecological relations with the more-than-human world (Hutchins, 2017). This study  aligns with posthumanist and new materialist tradit ions while acknowledging this intellectual lineage.  Despite the increased relevance of posthumanist and  new materialist perspectives due to recent environmental and humanitarian crises that s urfaced the limitation of human-centric views, aesthetic encounters in maker-centered learning remain underexplored. To address this gap, this study explored: How do university instructors' experiences in and perceptions of maker-centered learning environments reveal aesthetic encounters in maker education through the lens of posthumanist and new materialist perspectives?

## Methods

We conducted a qualitative thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2012) of data collected from university instructors who participated in a series of three maker-based professional development workshops that introduced the makercentered learning approach. These workshops were fa cilitated by the authors in collaboration with a un iversity library makerspace during the Spring term of 2024. Each session included a shared challenge (e.g., bui lding a functional paper boat), followed by open-ended discussions reflect ing on the experience, tools, materials, and pedagogical implications. Participants included uni versity instructors from disciplines such as materi al science, English literature, and health sciences. Data for a nalysis included audio recordings from workshop gro up discussions and two follow-up interviews (approximately 50 minutes each) with two participants who attended all three sessions. Thematic analysis was c onducted focusing on identifying patterns and theme s regarding the instructors' perceptions of maker-centered learning and their reflections on the learning process.

## Findings

Two themes emerged, manifesting aesthetic encounters in maker-centered learning —material-sensory entanglement and distributed agency for aesthetic engagement. A prominent aesthetic experience expressed by participants was the affective and imaginative enga gement that emerged through tactile and sensory int eraction with materials. This interaction often blurred the boundaries between thinking, feeling, and knowing — demonstrating what Barad (2007) describes as intra-action, where agency arises not from the individual alone but through dynamic engagement with the material world. Participants consistently highlighted how interacting with "visible, tactile materials" spa rked imagination and facilitated access to non-linear and affective dimensions of knowledge. One instructor explained, "It is easier to imagine things when you can see and touch stuff. " Maker activities were also described as inherently "actio n-oriented," where creativity emerged through the dynamic interplay between tactile engagement and brainstorm ing. One instructor shared her experiences where students were asked to draw the digestive system and identif y potential problems: " some students [are] really, really connected with it. And it was like one of our most fun sessions that we had." The instructor pointed o ut that embodied experiences fostered imaginative engagement and emotional resonance that made the learning pr ocess more enjoyable and meaningful.  This sense of tactile knowing often translated into  confidence and openness as one instructor reflected, "when you touch something or you are holding somethin g, it feels like you know it in a different way. If [students] can connect with an as pect of the material in that way, it makes them fee l more confident in their own knowledge and understanding of the concepts." The second theme was the dynamic and collaborative process of knowledge construction from the interplay of perspectives, tools, and materials. Pa rticipants described their experiences as “co-constructed"—not just with peers but with materials that afford o r resist particular actions. One instructor describ ed a design challenge, “the exercises are structured as problem s, and students solve them collaboratively through whatever medium they choose…this helps students practice cre ative problem-solving and learn  from one another.” In a boat-making activity during the workshops, participating instructors highlighted how their various teaching fields, specialties, and familiarity with maker materials i nfluenced their collaborative process. An instructo r described, “There’s constant negotiation as you interact with the materials and the challenge … you quickly figure out what your place is on the team…what you can contribute.”   Here, aesthetic encounters arise not from pre-planned design but from the fluid and dynamic reconfigurati on of roles, affordances, and interpretations. Coll aboration extends beyond interpersonal interactions, as parti cipants must also engage with the unique affordance s and resistances of the materials themselves. Play and i teration were central to this collaborative process. By framing failure as a low-stakes opportunity for growth, participants embraced ambiguity and exploration. As one instructor said, “Failure wasn’t painful..the stakes were low,  so nothing would happen if our boat sank.” Such op enness created space for aesthetic encounters, and this distributed moment allows participants to see learning as entangled with feeling, form, and function.

## Discussion and conclusion

This study explored aesthetic encounters in a maker-centered learning environment through posthuman and  new material perspectives. The findings suggest that ae sthetic encounters arise through intra-actions among participants, materials, tasks, and spatial setups. Instructors’ reflections demonstrate how se nsory engagement, material agency, and collaboration shaped affective  experiences and meaning-making. Learners' confidence and imagination were not solely the results of instruct ional design or personal creativity, but were influ enced by physical contact with materials, playful risk-taking, and uncertainty of open-ended making. By putting aesthetic encounters as one of the central components of learning, this exploratory study supports that aesthetic encounters could influence how learners perceive, feel, and navigate uncertainties in learning. This study relies on workshop discussions and interviews as primary data sources,  which may limit its ability to analyze tacit and n on-verbal aspects of aesthetic encounters. Future research should include video analy sis of workshop interactions and data collection involving both teachers and students to gain a deeper understanding of designing actionable pedagogical strategies in maker-centered learning that prioritize aesthetic encounters.
