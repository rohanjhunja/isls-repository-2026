# Racial Equity Frames in Creative Learning Organizations

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Creative learning organizations (CLOs) play an increasingly important role in educational ecosystems—and the pursuit of racial equity. Despite recent efforts, many such organizations serve disproportionately White and affluent visitors, particularly among museums. Utilizing public-facing website text, we used CLO’s "about us,” mission, and DEIA statements, to capture the degree to which racial equity is or is not central to each organization’s overall organizational identity. We identified five overlapping organizational fields in which CLOs hold membership, and found that, despite modest differences across fields, most CLOs make use of exclusively race-and equity-evasive frames, apart from ethnic/cultural organizations. CLOs that do mention race or equity tend to de-center these concepts through location or lack of depth. Implications include insights for both practitioners and researchers, as to what field-level pressures exist for CLOs, and how these pressures may be contested for greater racial equity.

## Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to identify and theori ze ways that creative learning organizations  (CLOs) position themselves and their work in regards to racial equi ty. CLOs are educational and cultural organizations  that promote creative learning as a discipline and proce ss, often in the form of art or making, in informal  and out-ofschool environmen ts (Akiva et al., 2019). CLOs are a cornerstone of the learning ecosystem necessary for children’s development and academic achievement, and hold the potential of serving to counteract i nequities in the formal education system  (Smith et al, 2017). However, currently, families of color often do not have equal access to CLOs due to structural reasons, such as location, finances, and time (America After 3PM Report, 2022). Moreover, these organizations and their spaces themselves are often not welcoming or affirming (Chalmers, 1999) meaning that even if equal access is achieved, experiences may not be equitable (Price & Applebaum, 2022). As such, despite their promise, CLOs currently often serve to reproduce racial inequity and stratification in society. This is particularly true of museums. Museums frequently serve (and employ) a disproportionately White and affluent demographic base (Mellon Foundation, 2022). By contrast, community-based organizations (CBOs) that are youth focused, often serve higher proportions of youth and families of color through their out-ofschool-time programming (America After 3PM, 2022). This work examines how CLOs frame racial equity and their role in working towards it, utilizing publicly available statements made by the organizations on t heir websites. We posit that such organizations’ di stinctive locations within overlapping organizational fields  may provide access to a correspondingly distinctive  set of frames for articulating their organizational identity. Pri or research indicates that frames that explicitly a ttend to racial equity are important supports for effective racialized chan ge work (McCambly, 2023). We seek to investigate: How do creative learning organizations frame their work, particularly in regards to racial equity, and what is the relationship, if any, between that and the organizational fields to which they claim membership?

## Conceptual framework

This work draws together theorizing on organizations and racial equity frames. We understand organization-level identities, and field-level pressures, as key sites for reproducing—and contesting—patterns in CLOs’ orientations towards racial equity (Anderson & Colyvas, 2021).

Organizational fields refer to the constellation of  similar and interconnected organizations operating  in relationship with one another within a given sector (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). An early and enduring insight of organizational theorizing is that organizations in the same field both face and exert shared pressures on each other through norms, resource distribution, rules, and re gulations. While all fields experience a degree of cohesion, some are more developed than others, meaning that t hey face stronger pressures to adhere to certain no rms (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). For example, organizatio ns that focus on providing services to youth (such as the CBOs within the sample), represent a relatively young  and fragmented field (Scott et al, 2006), while mu seums represent a highly rigid field that has experienced  many cycles of construction (DiMaggio, 1991). Organizations may exist in overlapping yet distinct fields, and therefore experience multiple, contradictory field-level pressures simultaneously; these contradictions often serve as sources of transformation. Frames are a shared ways of thinking within an organization (Lamont & Small, 2008) and influence the way problems are defined and motivate potential solutions. How an organization frames racial equity, particularly in respect to how it relates to their work as an organization, heavily influences their actions (Villanosa, 2021). We draw on previous scholarship that distinguishes between frames that are conscious or evasive on issues of race and equity (Bonilla-Silva, 2017; McCambly, 2023; Robertson et. al 2023). The normative and takenfor-granted use of race-evasive frames is one mechanism by which racialized stratification is reproduced (BonillaSilva, 2017). On the other hand, frames that explicitly address race in their considerations of inequity and call for systemic change are more likely to disrupt existing  racial orders (Mc Cambly, 2023). Our work builds on these distinctions by further attending to the centrality of race and/or equity in organizational frames. Despite sharing an organizational purpose of supporting creative learning, CLOs address racial equity in different ways (if at all). We explore both the standardization and variation o f racial equity frames across overlapping fields in which CLOs hold membership, which may give insights into existing field-wide norms and pressures. We theorize that these field level stand ards currently serve as obstacles to racial equity within CLOs, yet may also serve as an area for change – by resisting, contesting, or recombining the current norms, organizations may push the field forward towards greater racial equity.

## Methods

To understand the field-level norms around engagement with racial equity, and various organizations’ use of these norms, we undertook a qualitative analysis of the website text of a purposive sample of CLOs. The data was collected from a sample of organizations on three levels: first, we identified the population of CLOs within a mid-sized US city, based on a previous report on the local “creative learning ecosystem” (Akiva et al, 2019). Second, theorizing that children’s museums might offer a n interesting point of intersection, as they operate both as primarily youth-serving and as museums, we select ed a sample of 14 nationally-recognized children’s museums by cross-referencing several “best of” lists. Lastly, a sample of 10 field-level professional organizations were selected, for a total of 49 organizations in the sample. Data consisted of website text collected between August to September of 2023. Beginning on each organization’s home page and “about” page, we collected statements that spoke to the organization’s identity, and racial equity. Usually, this came in the form of a mission, vision, and/o r values statement. In addition, many organizations used “DEIA statements” to address diversity, equity, inclusion, and/or accessibility.  We captured both content (i.e. text), and location and digital pathway to access the statements. Our analysis process included three phases of qualitative coding and memoing: identifying organizational fields within the sample; analyzing the frames employed in relation to racial equity; and examining the relationship between organizational fields, ide ntities, and racial equity frames. The first phase utilized deductive coding, using keywords drawn from our own  knowledge of the domain, to identify the set of overlapping fields to which these organizations each belong. In the second phase, analyzing racial equity frames, we engaged in iterative rounds of deductive and inductive coding. First, deductive coding was deployed along the dimensions of equity consciousness/evasiveness, and  race consciousness/evasiveness (Bonilla-Silva, 2017; McCambly, 2). An organization was counted as having a conscious frame if specific words referencing race and/or equity were used anywhere on the website, and an ev asive frame if none of these words appeared anywher e on their website. Next, among organizations that used conscious frames, we analyz ed the centrality of racial equity; which was operationalized in terms of location  and depth. We defined centrality of location as conscious frames present in an organization’s mission statement. For depth, we used critical discourse analysis  (Hypolite & Stewart,

2021) to identify moves towards shallowness and moves towards  depth in the substantive meaning and usage of

frames. Finally, we examined the relationship between field  identification, organizational identity, and racia l equity frames used through data clusters and analytical memos to identify patterns across the sample.

## Selected findings

Organizational fields Overall, we found five overlapping yet distinct fields within the sample of CLOs. Two fields reflect organizational form: museums or community-based organizations (CBOs). Organizations signaled membership to these fields often by using these words themselves, “museum” or “community” in their name or mission statement. Three fields reflect content focus: STEM, art, and ethnic /cultural, by using words such as “science or “arts”, or bot h through use of the term “STEAM,” in their mission s tatement, while ethnic/cultural organizations would  usually name a specific group, using phrases such as “celebrate African art” or “support Black youth.”  Most organizations in the sample signaled membership in one form-related field, and at least one content-related field. Children’s museums typically indicated membership in the Museum field and both STEM and arts-based content fields. Conscious or evasive frames Across all of the text on their websites, around half of the organizations in our sample (23/49) made exclusive use of frames that were both equity and race evasive—that is, making no explicit mention of either construct. Despite this overarching similarity in evading mention of race or equity, some patterns emerged across fields. For example, across content-based fields, ethnic-cultural organizations were the most likely to use race conscious framing (5/7), while STEM focused organizations were the least likely (6/24). Comparing across organizational forms, museums most often used frames that were evasive on both race and equity (16/29); yet, they were also more likely than CBOs to use frames that were conscious of both race and equity (9/29 vs. 2/10). That is, museums typically addressed both race and equity together or not at all, while CBOs tended to have a more varied spread across different types of frames, most frequently using frames that were race conscious but equity evasive (4/10). Beyond conscious to central Additional analysis of organizations’ race-and equity conscious frames revealed emerging patterns regarding the centrality of racial equity to organizational identity. First, we found that many organizations mention race and/or equity but de-center these frames by doing so only in relatively peripheral locations. While other components of organizational work are typically described on a home page, or mission statement, these more peripheral mentions were typically several clicks “deep,” most often taking the form of DEIA statements. We also identified several repeated moves towards s hallowness and moves towards depth that organizations employed in their usage of race and/o r equity conscious frames. Moves towards depth incl uded connecting race and equity, such as bringing explicit attention to the issue of ‘racial equity’; referencing material resources, such as economic revitalization in a Black community; naming concrete actions that the organizations has taken, as these demon strate that the statements are tied to what the org anization actually does; and lastly, intentionally countering deficit narratives of comm unities of color by centering joy, dreaming, and de sire. In comparison, moves towards shallowness included limi ted use of frames in both frequency and length, suc h as single mentions or short sentences surrounding the frames, as some organizations wrote statements such  as “We care about equity,” and stopped there. Several orga nizations exhibited shallow use o f frames by using the term equity, yet describing meanings or actions connected to other “DEIA” elements, such as diversity or access. Often this occurred in statements such as “We value diver se voices” and “All are welcomed and included here. ” These types of statements would sometimes include the word “race” listed as a dimension of identity among with many others, such as gender or religion, that are all “w elcome” or “included.” We considered this a move to wards shallowness as well, because when everything is a priority, nothing is, and commitment to racial equity fades into the background among multitudes of other concerns.

Centrality across fields Despite the relatively small proportion of organiza tions that mention race or equity at all, we identified  some preliminary patterns in the centrality of racial equity frames across fields. First, we found that the museum field tends to de-center their commitments to both race and equity by only mentioning them peripherally, if at all, with 21/29 museums using some sort of DEIA statement. These DEIA statements sometimes served as an opportunity for museums to provide depth to their statements on  race and or  equity, though at other times they remained shallow. We found similar patterns among professional organizations (8/10  used DEIA statements). In comparison, the only organizations that centralized race and/or equity by addressing it in their mission statements, were CBOs, who also rarely used DEIA statements. Across content focus, ethnic/cultural organizations typically centered race by nature of their content focus, while arts and STEM focused organizations varied. Sometimes, ethnic/cultural organization lacked explicit attent ion and depth towards equity, though one could argu e that implicit equity goals exist within a focus of racial uplift.

## Discussion and conclusion

Overall, we found that many CLOs utilize frames that are both race and equity evasive. When they do mention race or equity, CLOs frequently decentralize these frames through their location or  depth. This is particularly true in the museum field, which has a strong pattern of using separate DEIA statements, w ith wide varieties in degrees of depth or shallowness of the ir frames. CBOs tend to not have separate statements, and include race and/or equity in their mission stateme nt or not at all. Children’s museums follow the patterns of the museum field—either making no mention of race or equity, or using frames that reference both, situated within a DEIA statement.

These findings indicate that field level pressures may exist around how to (if at all) address racial equity in CLOs. Future research could continue to investigate this relationship by applying similar methods of evaluating racial equity frames to different or larger samples  to further develop relationship to fields,  or track change over time. The dimensions of centrality, conscious, and evasiv e could also be used with other data, such as grant proposals, policy documents, or interviews with staff, that more explicitly tie statements to action. In additional to academic implications, the se findings have practical importance as well, demonstrating that, despite the movements in 2020 that pressured organizations, particularly museums, to address issues of racial equity explicitly,  many CLOs in our sample are not doing so.  Nonetheless, our findings also suggest some variation in the pressures organizations face relating to their use of frames surrounding race and equity, based on their organizational field. For example, the tendency of museums to discuss race and/or equity in the form of a DEIA statement suggests that it is the inclusion of a DEIA statement, rathe r than the use (or non-use) of race-or equity-conscious frames that seems to be a strong norm wit hin the museum field. Moreover, the norm is to not position racial equity as central to organizational  identity by addressing race and equity in mission statement, as no museums in the sample did so.

In contrast, CBOs did sometimes address race and/or equity in their mission statement, and almost never used separate DEIA statements – suggesting that this field does not experience the same pressures as museums. In part, this could be due to the lower level of institutionalization and cohesion overall across CBOs. This lack of cohesion on a particular way of doing things may leave space for the occasional CBO to center race and equity in their mission statement, which by contrast may be considered outside current norms for the museum field. This study offers a moment of reflection for museums, as our findings suggest that field level norms exist to not address racial equity, yet some CBOs, which share t he goal of providing creative learning experiences for children, do center racial equity. By reflecting on what field level norms exist, and what they are currently drawing from, organizations may see how these norms constrain them in their work towards racial equity by limiting what types of frames are available to them. CBO s that do center rac ial equity may be especially important models and/or partners in expanding the frames available to museums, and potentially shifting the norms of the museum field towards racial equity as a central concern.
