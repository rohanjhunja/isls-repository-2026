# “A Beautiful Little Thing for You”: Emergent Data Visualizations as Contextually Responsive Invitations to Data Reasoning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Though familiar to experts, the use of exclusively canonical data visualizations may foreclose reasoning opportunities consistent with everyday experience. We share an episode of teachers creating a display with materials from their surroundings. In debating this display’s status as a data visualization, teachers reflected on their criteria for data visualizations and on mediators to their learners’ engagement with data and science. We highlight emergent dat a visualizations as potential resources for contextually responsive reasoning with data. ## Motivation and background

Consistent with sound pedagogical practice, data science education seeks to position data as relevant to learners’ lives (e.g., Weiland & Williams, 2024; Wilkerson & Polman, 2020). But many teacher learning opportunities focus on canonical data visualizati ons—such as bar charts and scatter plots —that may foreclose data reasoning processes that support learning by leveraging everyday experience (Bertling & Hodge, 2025). We share an episode in which teachers in a professional development workshop constructed an emergent data display (Figure 1, top) by gathering objects from their immediate surroundings. In evaluating this display as a potential data visualization, teachers surfaced their criteria for data visualizations and considered mediators of their students’ engagement with data and science. Similar to design perspectives that position environmental configurations as sources of information (Lidwell et al., 2010; Figure 1, bottom), we conjecture that emergent data displays could serve as contextually responsive invitations for learners to reason with and about data. Within a 3-day professional development (PD) retreat in a US national park, eight science and math teachers and two researchers participated in a roughly 5-minute exchange as the group conducted a biodiversity survey of the surrounding wooded area. To categorize plant species for subsequent counting, participants worked in groups of 2746 2-3 to gather one sample of each plant species in their sample area (defined by a hula hoop on the ground). They grouped their samples on six whiteboards, lettered A through F (Figure 1, top) and arrayed the whiteboards in a row on the ground. The second a uthor, who facilitated the PD retreat and this activity in particular, then asked, “Is this a data visualization here?” Based on participating teachers’ responses in the moment and the frequency with which they mentioned this episode during post-PD interviews, the second author identified the conversation that resulted from his prompt as rich for insight into teachers’ thinking about data. The episode had been video recorded and automatically transcribed, so the second author applied pseudonyms at this point. The first author, who was not present at the PD, thematically coded (Braun & Clarke, 2006) the transcript at the utterance level, attending particularly to teachers’ reasoning about this display’s status as a data visualization. The first author then constructed the narrative presented below to emphasize those themes, verifying with the second author as a form of member checking.

Participating teachers’ conversation about whether or not their whiteboard display was a data visualization surfaced some of the teachers’ criteria for characterizing data visualizations. The presence of visual representations was one such criterion (“you’ve got different visual representations ”), where another teacher attended to learners’ sense of the aesthetics of those representations (“I know visually that some kids would not really see this as something that would be eye-catching”). While some teachers embraced qualitative data (“Qualitative data; we’ve got to take qualitative data”), others sought ways to make the display more quantitative (“We didn’t have any measurements” “You can if you count it ”). A sticking point for one teacher, Barbara, was her perceived sense of the display’s inaccuracy (“it's not [an] accurate representation of what we’re finding… It's not the same amount. ”). In response, other teachers argued that the display was accurate but for different phenomena than Barbara had in mind. One teacher specified that the display conveyed definitions rather than amounts (“We’re presenting a case for the data that defines each of our groups”). Another clarified that, instead of showing each plant’s relative frequency in the sampled areas, this display accurately showed the share of sample areas in which each plant appeared (“the most common things that we as a group found”). We interpret this exchange as unpacking assumptions around the relationship between a data display’s accuracy and the context it is meant to describe. The facilitating researcher then emphasized how the whiteboard array illustrated the potential diversity of data visualizations (“ Data visualization can mean a lot of different things and can tell us information. … I thought it was a beautiful little thing for you. ”) This generative conversation also gave way to higher-level reflections on the ways depictions of data frame learners’ disciplinary engagement. One teacher proposed that displays such as this “ …makes science less scary … because a lot of kids don't think in numbers. A lot of kids just see what they see outside and they're like, ‘Well I'm not a scientist because I'm just grouping things together.’ … in my 10-year-old brain of my daughter, that's science to her.” Another teacher similarly shared that, “to be able to have that word ‘data’ without it being this like, ‘Oh my gosh, that's math.’ … That's a whole different kind of math. ” It seems that expanding notions of what counts as a data visualization similarly suggested broader depictions of what counts as science and math, especially toward qualitative experiences of those fields and in ways teachers anticipated would resonate wi th their learners’ existing ways of being and interacting with the world. We considered an episode of teachers constructing an emergent data display as a proof-of-concept for how arranging elements in learners’ surroundings can prompt conversations that transcend particular focal objects (here, plant leaves and stems) to consider broader criteria for what count as data visualizations and how learners engage with data. Here, teachers decided that their whiteboard display – originally constructed to define groups – also served as a data visualization of environmental information (i.e., relative frequency of plants across sampling areas). Their conversation also linked this type of emergent data visualization to the often qualitative ways their learners engage with their worlds, echoing design and arts perspectives (Bertling & Hodge, 2025; Lidwell et al., 2010). Our larger professional development efforts around data science within environmental and life sciences introduce other such emergent data displays to study the extent to which they serve as a productive activity type more broadly.
