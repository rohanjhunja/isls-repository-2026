This study explores the impact of science research experiences using CO2 meters. ## Introduction

In the United States, data shows that the STEM workforce includes higher proportions of men, whites, Asians, and foreign-born workers than the proportions of these groups i n the U.S. population  (National Science Board, 2022). W omen, Blacks, Hispanics, American Indian and Alaska Nativ es are underrepresented in the STEM workforce and among students earning post-secondary STEM degrees. A longitudinal study found that of the students who chose STEM majors or degree programs, 48 percent of bachelor’s degree students and 69 per cent of associate degree students left these fields befo re graduation (Chen, 2013), with Black and Latinx y outh more likely to leave STEM majors than their white peers (Riegle-Crumb, King, and Irizarry, 2019). Community colleges play an important role in higher  education. In 2021, 41 percent of undergraduate students were enrolled in community colleges in the 2020-2021 academic year (Community College Research Center, n.d.). Community colleges are more accessible and affordab le than 4-year institutions, serve a diverse population, and offer a variety of degree programs and pathways (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2016).  While 50 percent of community college students enrolled in a STEM fi eld, 69 percent of students left STEM fields before graduation (Chen, 2013). Eighty-one percent of white women and students of color of both sexes report that poor instructional quality is an issue when they leave post-secondary STEM programs (Hunter, et al., 2019). The Engage to Excel report argues that the first two years of post-secondary education are the most critical to the retention and recruitment of STEM majors (President’s Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, 2012). To increase retention and recruitment, the report recommends expanding the use of discovery-based scientific research and engineering design courses in the first two years of post-secondary courses, as opposed to the traditional introductory laboratory courses. This study begins to explore the questions: What are the attitudes and reactions of the student s who conduct science research? and How does their participation affect their identity and STEM pathways?

## Framework and methods

The project focuses on redesigning entry level STEM courses at a community college  to provide students with STEM research experiences.  Research has shown that undergraduate STEM research  experiences have the potential to engage and retain STEM students (Natio nal Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicin e, 2017). However, post-secondary students often do not experience discovery-based STEM research until advanced STEM courses. This study takes place at a community college located in the n orthwestern United States  and focuses on an introductory biology course, the gateway course for students interested in pursuing a biology major. The STEM research experience leverages culturally relevant pedago gy, place-based education, and identity. Culturally relevant teaching focuses on centering students’ experiences and expertise and takes an asset-based approach to learning, where students’ everyday expe riences are seen as essential to lea rning (Ladson-Billings, 1995). Ladson-Billings’ framework includes goals of academic succ ess in the discipline, promoting cultural competence, and helping students develop critical consciousness through which they can challenge the status quo. Place-based education argues that place matters, bridges connections between students’ lives and learning in school, and makes learning more relevant (Gruenwald, 2003). The National Research Council (2009) asserts that science identity is fundamental for engaging in lif elong science endeavors, and r esearch shows that identity impacts STEM students’ persistence and career choices (Chang, et al., 2011; Johnson, et al., 2011). The science research experience in the biology course centered around using CO 2 meters to collect data in their communities. Students investigated primary literature a bout CO2, formed groups based on interest, generated their own research questions, and developed mini-proposals that included their research methods. Students collected data in their communities using aranet4 CO2 meters, analyzed their data, created scientific posters, and shared their findings with the class. The preliminary findings reported are of matched pair t-tests of pre-and post-survey and themes from post-interview data from the Winter 2023 quarter. The surveys and interviews focused on students’ experiences and confidence doing research; attitudes towards STEM coursework, STEM research, STEM professionals and careers in STEM, and questions related to STEM identity.

## Findings and discussion

We found that there were statistically significant increases from  pre-to post-survey in students’ confidence in their ability to design research, conduct research, analyze and interpret data, communicate findings, and use spreadsheets for analyzing data (n = 15, p<0.05). There was also a statistically significant increase in the students who thought “other people think I am good at STEM.” There were not statistically significant changes in questions about if students saw themselves as part of the STEM community at the college, if they saw themselves pursuing a STEM career, or if they intended to pursue a STEM careers. However, it is important to note that students rated themselves highly for these questions on both the pre-and post-surveys, with an average range of 4.12 to 4.7 on a 5 point Likert scale. While only six students participated in interviews that took p lace at the end of the quarter, they provide  some insight into student survey data, particularly rela ted to question s around their identity and career goals. The students  interviewed took the course as part of their biology pathway and all  of the students interviewed expressed interest in STEM careers and were involved in various STEM initiatives at the community college, such as a program that provided support fo r underrepresented students’ participation in STEM through counseling, workshops, and financial support. It is also important that students’ interest and intention to pursue a STEM career did not decrease after this quarter, wh ich suggests that the students will continue in the ir STEM pathways for at least the next quarter. More research needs to be done with more courses to better understand the impact of students’ STEM research experiences on their retention in STEM programs.
