# Participatory Design of Early Math Learning: A Culturally Situated Playful Approach in Hong Kong

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this work, we aim to address Hong Kong caregivers’ needs in early childhood math learning by taking a participatory design approach. Through an online questionnaire, we found that parents supported math learning less frequently compared to language learning, with particularly low frequencies in promoting spatial reasoning through drawing maps and patterning through digital tools. Based on these findings, we conducted a workshop with caregivers to generate playful ideas that promote children’s math learning. The workshop resulted in two culturally situated activities: shopping and dessert making. This work has implications for future research in designing resources that meet stakeholders’ needs and collaborating with stakeholders to support early math learning grounded in the local context.

## Introduction and literature review

Young children’s mathematical skills are a strong p redictor of later academic achievement, even strong er than reading and attention skills (Duncan et al., 2007).  However, there is a lack of developmentally approp riate resources targeting early math skills (Cerezci, 2019; Eason et al., 2020). Adapting the traditional didactic instruction used with older children, therefore, is a prominent approach to provide math instruction in kindergarten classrooms. Unfortunately, such an approach has bee n found to have some negative impacts on children’s  later academic and socio-behavioral outcomes (Durkin et al., 2022). This iss ue is especially critical in Hong Kong because local kindergartens often operate as a prep aratory program for primary education, with an emph asis on academic achievement (Wong & R ao, 2015). To address this issue, we implemented a participatory design approach to (1) identify needs in early math learning, and (2) design playful early math activities. Participatory design is a research methodology that involves partnerships with stakeholders in the design process (Spinuzzi, 2005). This collaborative approa ch allows researchers to ground their work in stakeholders’ perspectives, meeting stakeholders’ needs in the re al world. It also empowers stakeholders’ agency in teaching and learning (Booker & Goldman, 2016). While this a pproach is rooted in the field of Human-Computer Interaction, it has been adapted in fields such as Educational Psychology, Developmental Sciences, and Cognitive Sciences to improve research ethics, design timely studies in real-world contexts, and advance practices that directly benefit community members (Bermudez et al., 2023; Chan et al., 2023). Different from didactic instruction or free play, e ducational games combine children’s autonomy and adults’ knowledge to facilitate learning among youn g children (Hirsh-Pasek & Golinkoff, 2008). Research on game-based learning suggests that when learning is embed ded in games, children are more engaged with the content and improve their math skills (Connolly et al., 2012). Similarly, the literature on guided pla y—fun, flexible, voluntary activities with an extrinsic goal of developing children’s skills and knowledge—illustrates that preschoolers are often more engaged and show more learning gains from guided play than from receiving direct instructions or exploring without guidance (Toub et al., 2016; Weisberg et al., 2013). We first identify parents’ needs by conducting an o nline questionnaire that asks about the frequency o f various learning activities, and examine the math c oncepts for which they may need more resources to s upport children’s development. Next, we work with parents and teachers to design playful math activities that are feasible for them, meet their needs, and promote developmentally appropriate learning among children. Online questionnaires with parents To better understand the landscape of local stakeho lders’ needs in math learning  within the context of early childhood education, we designed an online question naire based on the local kindergarten curriculum (H KEDB,

2017) and the research on home math activities (Zippert & Rittle-Johnson, 2020). In the questionnaire, we asked

the frequency of which caregivers engaged their chi ld in activities that support the six learning domains outlined in the local curriculum—ethics, intellect: language, intellect: math, physi que, social skills, and aesthetics—from never (1) to everyday (6). We also asked the freque ncy of which caregivers engaged their child in vari ous math learning act ivities that support numeracy, spatial reasoning, and patterning (skills in noticing sequences and regularities that support predictions and logical r easoning). A total of 149 parents  from local kindergartens completed a 30-minute online questionnaire via Qualtrics and were included in the following analyses. Across the six learning domains, we found that parents engaged their child in language learning activities most frequently (M = 4.76, SD = 1.22), with 35% of parents conducting these activ ities every day. Parents also engaged their child in social learning frequently (M = 4.69, SD = 1.20). In contract, parents engaged their child in math learning (M = 4.51, SD = 1.08) and aesthetic activities (M = 3.88, SD = 1.14) least frequently  (Figure 1). Furthermore, only 17% of parents conducted math learning activities every day and only 7% of the parents did so for aesthetic learning. We conducted a paired-samples t-test to examine the relative frequency of language vs. math learning activities, and found that parents engaged their child in language learning activities significantly more frequently compared to math learning activities, p = 0.003. Within numeracy activities, parents most frequently engaged their child in counting ite ms (M=4.76, SD=1.33) and least frequently engaged them in playing computer games, apps, or visiting interacti ve websites that include number  games (M=2.77, SD=1.50). Within spatial activities, parents most frequently engaged their child in using spatial words (M=4.70, SD=1.28) and least frequently engaged them in drawing maps o r plans (M=2.11, SD=1.36). Within patterning activities, parents most frequently engaged their child in disc ussing pattern in days of the week, months of the y ear, or seasons (M=2.99, SD=1.27) and least frequently engaged them in playing comp uter games, apps, or visiting interactive websites that include pattern games (M=2.13, SD=1.31). Idea generation workshops with caregivers Guided by the  questionnaire findings, we conducted a workshop with caregivers. The goal of the workshop is twofold: (1) generate ideas for playful activities that caregivers can engage with children to support their math learning, and (2) empower caregivers to identify and incorporate math concepts in their interaction with children. A total of 7 adults participated in one 2-hour idea generation workshop (4 parents with child ren aged between 5 and 8 years, 2 former kindergarten teachers, and 1 math tutor; 5 female, 2 male). During the workshop,  we presented the preliminary findings from the questio nnaire to highlight the needs in supporting childre n’s math learning. Next, through a series of ac tivities, caregivers generated ideas for math activities and tasks that are feasible in their everyday context. Specifically, in Activity 1, the caregivers designed an imaginary five-year-old through drawing and questions related to the child’ s interests and motivation. Caregivers jointly create d an imaginary child to ground their design decisions  in their integrated experiences with children  (Coenraad et al., 2022). In Activity 2, within a time limit (6-8 minutes), the caregivers brainstormed as many themes and tasks as possible that their imaginary child would enjoy. Sprint approaches like this have been used to help individuals go beyond the least innovative ideas and generate crea tive solutions (Ferreira & Canedo, 2020). In Activi ty 3, the caregivers discussed, selected, and combined ideas they generated to design an activity that their imaginary child would enjoy and can also support their math learnin g. In each activity, the caregivers engaged in coll aborative design and discussions in small groups of 3 or 4, then shared their perspectives in a large group (Figure 2). Group A consisted of 2 former kindergarten teachers  and 1 parent. Their imaginary child, Dave, likes food, sports, cars, insects, and dinosaurs. Even though he is a bit naughty and ofte n causes trouble, he loves his family and is kind to people. He likes to be praised, loves to help people and make friends, and is a natural leader. Based on this imaginary child, Group A generated themes and tasks that Dave would enjoy, such as eating snacks and mooncakes, sharing, playing balls, traveling, and e xperimenting and exploring science. The group then designed a shopping activity with a checklist and store map. In this activity, children can observe and compare the quantity, type, and price of goods. They can al so use the self-checkout to make payments, and compare the items with the checklist to check for accuracy. Fur thermore, elements such as competition and time lim it can be incorporated to make this activity more exciting and engaging for children. Group B consisted of 1 math tutor and 3 parents. Th eir imaginary child, also a boy, is very active. He is constantly moving, both physically and mentally. He is curious and observant, and always asks “why not”. He is full of imaginations and has his own opinions. He l ikes to cook, eat, explore new hobbies, and imitate  other people’s behaviors. He loves to play different game s and with different people. Based on this imaginar y child, Group B generated themes and tasks that this child would enjoy, such as transportation, shopping, cook ing, learning about flags and culture, and exploring far ms, beaches, and insect exhibitions. Combining thes e themes and tasks, the group designed an activity of making mango pudding, which involved traveling to a store to gather ingredients, comparing the size of mangos and their  price, measuring the amount of flour and sugar, se lecting containers for pudding, experimenting with the recipe, and observing the thickness and texture of the pudding. In the end-of-workshop feedback, the caregivers shared that, thro ugh the workshop, they learned that math is ubiquitous in everyday life and that they are empowered to design learning activities based on children’s interests. ## Discussion and conclusion

We found that parents in Hong Kong reported a highe r frequency of engaging children in language vs. math learning. This is consistent with the observation that teachers spend twice as much time supporting reading skills compared to math skills in US preschool classrooms (Clasessen et al., 2014). Furthermore, similar to parents in the US, Hong Kong parents support numeracy development more  frequently compared to spatial reasoning and patterning (Zippert & Rittle-Johnson, 2020). Parents rarely leverage computer games, apps, or interactive websites to support math learning. These findings illustrate the need to provide resources that help caregivers support young children’s math thinking beyond basic numeracy. They also reveal a potential gap—low frequency of using digital tools for early math learning—that can guide future research and practice in Learning Sciences and Technologies. Informed by the findings from the questionnaire, we  conducted an idea generation workshop with caregivers to gather activity ideas that can support children’s math learning. Th e caregivers designed vivid five-year-olds, and used the imaginary children and local contexts to inform their activity design. Specifically, Group A developed a shopping activity that provided opportunities for children to develop  spatial reasoning, map reading, and route planning. Group B designed an activity of making mango pudding, involving a local fruit that is abundant in Southeast Asia and a dessert that is popular among children and adults. Within the activity, children can adjust the recipe and observe the thickness of the pudding, integrating proportional reasoning and  scientific experiments. Both activities involved the elements of play with a learning goal of improving math skills. This research is significant in two ways. First, th e questionnaire findings reveal specific needs in e arly childhood education, guiding future research and pr actice to support math learning with a focus on spa tial reasoning, patterning, and uses of digital tools. S econd, the participatory design workshop is feasibl e, welcome, and can generate culturally situated  activities to promote playful math learning in Hong Kong. This playful learning approach aligns with the effort in strengthening developmentally appropr iate pedagogies for young children. Based on this research, we are conducting additional workshops to further develop and refine the activity ideas. To conclude, this work can serve as a guidepost for future resear ch to effectively collaborate with stakeholders in designing practical resources. It a lso contributes to the diversity of research findin gs on early childhood education in the global context.
