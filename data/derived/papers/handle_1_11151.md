# Pandemic-Related Perseverance During Math Problem-Solving in MathSpring, an Educational Technology Platform that Responds to Student Affect

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Perseverance is critical for students’ achievement and may be particularly important after COVID-19. We analyzed teacher and principal interviews and student educational technology usage data to examine students’ perseverance during math problem-solving across three cohorts of students during and after COVID-19. Data were collected as part of a randomized control trial examining the efficacy of an educational technology platform, MathSpring, in supporting students’ math achievement. The results indicated that teachers and principals were concerned with their students’ perseverance, and students’ usage data similarly demonstrated that students may have reduced perseverance when solving math problems after COVID-19. Teachers and students may benefit from using tools that help foster perseverance in math class. MathSpring and other educational technology tools that support perseverance could be useful for encouraging students to put forth effort when solving math problems, which could in turn support students’ math learning outcomes.

## Introduction

Mathematics learning continues to be an important f ocus for national improvement. In Massachusetts, a northeastern U.S. state, over half of 5th and 6th grade students (ages 10-12) did not meet expectations in math in 2022 (Massachusetts Department of Education, 2022).  Math achievement is also particularly concerning a fter COVID-19; math outcomes continued to decline in 2023 (NAE P, 2023), likely due in part to the transition to distance learning during COVID-19 (Jack et al., 2023). In particular, middle scho ol is a pressing time for math achievement (Siegler et al., 2012) when students demonstrate lower engagement and motivation (Barber & Olsen, 2004), especially in math (Wigfield et al., 2006). In light of the pandemic’s effects on math achievem ent, it is important to address students’ engagement with math in middle school. Grit, or “perseverance and passion for long-term goals” (Duckworth et al., 2007: 1087-1088), has been shown to improve academic achievement (Bashant, 201 4). The “perseverance of effort” component of grit has been shown to be central in promoting academic outc omes (Lam & Zhou, 2019; Tang et al., 2019). Since perseverance could influence students' academic out comes, it is important to understand students' math-related perseverance during and after COVID-19. Perseverance positively affected students’ lear ning outcomes during the pandemic (Chen et al., 2022; Sulla et al., 2022). However, studies have yet to examine whether stu dents experienced changes in perseverance in math class after the pandemic. This paper seeks to examine middle school students’ perseverance and teachers’ perceptions of students’ perseverance in middle school math during and after COVID-19. We further examine whether an educational techn ology platform designed to improve students’ approaches towards math could improve students’ perseverance during problem-solving. MathSpring An educational technology program, MathSpring, was designed to support middle school students’ a pproaches towards math at this critical time in students’ dev elopment. MathSpring was developed by University of Massachusetts, Amherst and Worcester Polytechnic In stitute. It is a supplemental intervention that provides students with personalized math problems to solve, supports problem-solving practice, and responds to and tracks student affect (Arroyo et al., 2012). MathSpring provides students with problem-solving support such as hints, worked examples, and videos (Figure 1.a). MathSprin g uses computational models that detect students’ engagement and emotions (e.g., frustrated, bored, i nterested, confident) and offers supports, includin g a digital learning companion that offers growth mindset messa ges and a progress “garden” with plants that grow w hen students solve problems (Arroyo et al., 2014). An effort-based tutoring algorithm analyzes students’ in-system actions to produce summary data for teachers about stud ents’ effort, including whether students skip probl ems, use hints, or solve on their first attempt (Arroyo et al., 2010). Teachers can view effort data for the class and for individual students (Figure 1.b), project the data for the class (Figure 1.c), and use the data to inform discussions, encourage students to exert effort, and make adjustments to their math instruction. Figure 1 MathSpring’s Learning Environment Student View (a) and Data Views for the Teacher (b) and Class (c) (a) (b) (c) Randomized control trial To test MathSpring’s efficacy in supporting students’ math achievement and attitudes towards math, WestEd (an external evaluator) conducted a cluster randomized con trol trial (RCT) with three cohorts (Cohort 1 (C1): 2020-2021, C2: 2021-2022, and C3: 2022-2023) of 5th and 6th grade teachers and their stude nts in Massachusetts. These cohorts participated in the study during and following the COVID-19 pandemic, with all students participating in distance learning for at least part of C1, then returning to more typical in-person instruction during C2 and C3. Teachers were randomly assigned to the t reatment or control group. Teachers in the treatmen t group used MathSpring with their students as a supplemental math curriculum for 30 minutes per week. Students solved problems in MathSpring and teachers reviewed studen ts’ effort and progress data. Teachers in the contr ol group continued with their regular math instruction and u sed existing supplemental programs. This study was overseen by the IRB at WestEd. The present paper is derived from data collected as part of the RCT.

## Research questions

This paper has three research questions.

• RQ1: Did teachers and principals discuss their students’ perseverance across the cohorts?

• RQ2: Did the ways that students solved math problem s in MathSpring indicate their levels of

perseverance across the cohorts?

• RQ3: Could MathSpring’s analytic data support teachers in identifying students whoneed perseverance-related support?

Sample The RCT study sample included 64 5th or 6th grade m athematics teachers (34 treatment, 30 control) in t hree cohorts from 47 public schools in 41 districts with  diverse backgrounds in Massachusetts, focusing on lowperforming schools and schools with student s of lower socioeconomic status. The qualitative an alyses (RQ1 and RQ3) include data from 36 teacher interviews (19 tr eatment, 17 control), 26 principal interviews, and 10 postobservation interviews (7 treatment, 3 control teachers). The analysis of students’ User Data (RQ2) included data from 803 students from the treatment group (C1: N = 200, C2: N = 155, C3: N = 448) on 132,047 problems solved in MathSpring.

## RQ1 and RQ3: Qualitative analysis

To address RQ1 and RQ3, we conducted qualitative analyses of teacher and principal interviews across the three cohorts. All teachers and a subset of principals we re invited to participate in interviews. A subset o f teachers in C3 were also selected to part icipate in classroom observations, which were follo wed by post-observation interviews. All interviews were audio-recorded and conducted over videoconference by a trained interviewer in a semi-structured manner, following a fixed set of questio ns with flexibility to change topics to accommodate the conversation flow. In teacher interviews (lasting o n average 30 minutes), teachers were asked about th eir math instruction, socio-emotional learning (SEL), concerns with students’ p erformance and engagement (RQ1),  and MathSpring’s implementation and their use of the Ma thSpring data (RQ3; treatment teachers). Principals  were asked about how COVID-19 was affecting their students and their concerns with students’ math learning (RQ1) in 30-minute interviews. Post-observation interviews (15 minutes on average) allowed teachers to expand on their reasoning behind the instructional choices observed in classroom observations (RQ1 and RQ3).

## RQ2: User data analysis

A descriptive quantitative analysis of students’ User Data was conducted across the three cohorts to address RQ2. MathSpring logged data on students’ in-system actions (User Data; Table 1). Students’ effort level was determined per problem using in-system actions and categorized into one of eight effort ca tegories (Table 1; Arroyo et al., 2010). We additionally examined the number of hints students accessed per problem. ## RQ1 and RQ3: Qualitative analysis

Audio recordings from interviews were transcribed using an automatic transcription service (Rev). Transcription errors were manually fixed by referencing the audio  recordings. Transcription files and classroom obse rvation notes were imported into NVivo (Lu mivero, 2020) for analysis. Data were analyzed usin g a hierarchical qualitative data analysis approach (Creswell, 2014). Initial data reduction was performed and data were assigned categories of meaning. Coding was initially done de ductively to capture the  effect of the pandemic on SEL, but as themes emerged, new codes were inductively added through an emergent coding method to create detailed subcodes within the categories (see Table 2; Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006). Coding was performed by two coders who discussed and resolved discrepancies in the coding. Coded and sub-categorized data were then compiled and analyzed to present a coherent story.

## Findings

RQ1: Perseverance concerns The perseverance analyses indicated that teachers a nd principals across cohorts observed students’ lac k of grit. Across the three cohorts, teachers and principals o ften noted these issues (23 interviewed teachers: 6 3.4%, 8 principals: 30.8%, 2 post-observation interviewed teachers: 18.2%) and a subset spec ifically linked these issues to COVID-19 (15 interviewed teachers: 41.7%, 3 principals: 1 1.1%). Perseverance was commonly discussed when teachers were asked whether COVID-19 was still having an effect on their teaching and  whether they had concerns with students’ math performance. Principal s typically discussed perseverance when asked about  their school’s most acute need for mathematics, learning,  and instruction. Teachers and principals were not explicitly asked about perseverance; the frequency of their concerns suggests that this was a pressing issue at the top of their minds. In general, issues with perseverance were re ported frequently in C2 and C3 despite schools retu rning to more typical in-person instruction during these school years (see Table 3). The pandemic may have exacerbated this trend, hindering student learning. One teacher expressed, "I've never experienced a group with such high needs and because of the attentional issues, some days it's literally like h erding cats” (Teacher 1831; C3; Control). Another e laborated, “We're also seeing a lot of attentional issues-kids not reading directions, not just in the typica l way that kids, they just don't read directions or that typically not like that. RQ1.2 Perseverance 25% of teachers and 14.8% of principals also noted a decline in perseverance post-COVID, sharing that students lacked the willpower to struggle through challenges. One teacher mused, “...it's almost like an instant gratification. They want the answer and they want it now. They don't wanna have to go through the work…their stamina, their rigor… they just don't have the ‘stick-to-itness’” (Teacher 1831; C3; Control). Students seem ed unprepared for the struggles that inherently come with learning, r esulting in quitting: "They don't know how to struggle, try to get through a challenging spot. And unfortunately, a lot of them are quitting very quickly” (Teacher 7 489; C2; Control). Similarly, another teacher noted that man y students have the mentality of “wanting things to  be easy and the answers to come easy and not to do work [so ] if they don't get it, they're just kind of like, ‘I don't get it'” (Teacher 7284&1222; C3; Treatment). Instead of endu ring productive struggle to reach a rewarding but d elayed success, students gave up easily, which could reduce learning. RQ1.3 Reliance on the teacher Related to students’ decreased perseverance was an increased reliance on the teacher. According to 19. 4% of teachers, students were reported to be increasingly  dependent on their teachers. One teacher described  this behavior as “...kind of like just automatically go to look for teacher help...You've got those kids that need a lot of handholding. I think we're seeing more kids that ca n't get started on their own than in the past” (Tea cher 3486&9545; C3; Control). Other teachers echoed thes e sentiments; in re cent years there has been “a lot more dependence on the teacher and less independence,” a nd that “it’s getting a lot harder to wean them off  of the dependence” (Teacher 4646; C3; Control). One teacher offered a potential explanation: “I think they're so used to everything being done for them or set up so that th ey could just do it quickly and not have to go thro ugh all of those intense steps of learning the process” (Teacher 7201; C3; Treatment). Attempts to ease the learning process may also be diminishing students’ perseverance to endure when they face challenges. RQ1.4 “Speedrunning” 25% of teachers and 7.4% of principals reported tha t students tended to speed through the learning pro cess with little regard for quality or accuracy. This suggest s that students had less patience and tolerance for  struggle. One teacher remarked that since COVID, many students “are more about getting something done as opposed to doing it well or following all the steps. So there's less  striving for accuracy” (Teacher 7201; C3; Treatmen t). This was also the case for other teachers; one described their students as “all coming in and just like wanting to know how to do it and just get it done,” and another focused  on “trying to slow them down and think about the w hy behind everything” (Teacher 3486&9545; C3; Control). The focus on speed and completion over accuracy could indicate low levels of perseverance, which could decrease learning outcomes. RQ1.5 Effort 25% of teachers and 11.1% of principals also report ed that students were less willing to put in effort  into their learning compared to before the pandemic, which may  set them up for increased learning gaps in the fut ure. During the pandemic, teachers graded “pass or fail, ” which may have also had the unintended effect of enabling a mentality of not needing to put in work for school: “[during distance learning] We were told to pass these kids. So they, from March 13th to June... we passed them.  Now they'd said, ‘oh, I'm going to go back into vi rtual so I don't have to do any work’” (Teacher 4487; C1; Trea tment). One teacher noted their students’ lack of e ffort in general, saying, “they don't practice... they're like, ‘hmm, you can just give it to me. I'm not going to do it o n my own.’ So they're not willing to put in the effort they need to practice” (Teacher 2106; C1, Control). A willingness to exert effort in learning is an important facet of perseverance. Similarly, students in C3 used fewer hints per problem than students in C1 or C2 (Figure 2.b). These trends are e vident across the school year as well (Figure 2.c). Over t ime, students in C1 and C2 appeared to reduce skipp ing, while students in C3 consistently skipped at higher rates. Likewise, students in C1 and C2 used more hints per problem at the beginning of the school year and gradually r educed their hint use over time, while students in C3 had consistently lower hint usage. This descriptive dat a suggests that students in C3 may have demonstrate d less perseverance than those in C1 or C2 particularly since they were more likely to skip problems without attempting to solve and were less likely to use hints.

While these formal programs and ap proaches are likely beneficial, overall, the contro l group teachers did not have specific SEL supports integrated into their math instruction. MathSpring could help fill this gap by explicitly connecting SEL concepts to math instruction.

## Discussion

Perseverance is an important driver of academic ach ievement (Bashant, 2014; Chen et al., 2022). It is therefore important to examine students’ perseverance in math following the COVID-19 pandemic. The findings presented here indicated that teachers and pri ncipals noted issues with their students’ persevera nce following COVID-19. Specifically, 5th and 6th grade teachers and principals interviewed as part of a larger RCT examining the efficacy of MathSpring indicated a deficit in their students’ grit. Compar ed to before the pandemic, students during and after COVID-19 had decreased focus and attention in class, lowe r resilience and tenacity for the challenges that come with learning, increased dependence on teachers to make learning easier with less effort, and a mentality of trying to finish work as quickly and with as little  effort as possible. These issues were prevalent in  the years following distance learning.

In addition, User Data from MathSpring similarly de monstrated that students may have reduced levels of perseverance when solving math problems after co mpared to during the pandemic. When students solved problems in MathSpring, they appeared to skip more problems and use fewer hints than students in the two prior years. These usage indicators suggest that despite retur ning to typical in-person instruction, students may still be challenged to exert effort and diligently solve math problems. They may be more likely to rush through problems without attempting or fail to use available resourc es rather than working in a self-directed way. These data align with the interview findings, suggesting that students may be experiencing issues around reduced effort even after returning to in-person instruction.

It should be noted that these analyses are somewhat limited given their exploratory nature. Teachers and principals were not explicitly asked about the analyzed SEL sub-categories; thus, these findings reflect the number of participants who brought up the categories unpro mpted and represent a conservative estimate. Future  studies could address these research questions more directl y through quantitative and qualitative means. This study is also limited to the U.S. Societal level traits influence the effectiveness of growth mindset practices: The U.S. has a high affinity for growth mindset social norms com pared with other countries (Lou & Li, 2022), which could make this intervention’s effects stronger in the U.S. than in other countries. These results suggest that teachers and students ma y need tools that help foster grit in math class. T he math platform studied in the RCT, MathSpring, could  aid teachers and students in understanding student s’ learning attitudes and prompt discussions about imp roving grit. MathSpring’s SEL features also encoura ge students to consistently apply growth mindset practices into math. These features could help students internalize perseverant attitudes, which requires consistent habit-building. Educational technology developers, policymakers, and practitioners may see a benefit to incorporating similar effort-related supports, such as data on students’ effort, into math educational technology platforms to encou rage students to persevere in math. This could impr ove students’ reduced perseverance post-pandemic, and students may see learning outcome ben efits in domains beyond math if they can transfer these growth mindset skills and attitudes to other learning areas.
