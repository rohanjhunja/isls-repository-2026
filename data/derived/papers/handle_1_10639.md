# STEM Curricular Adaptations as Ideological Stances

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As Edelson et al. (2021) explain, “well-designed instructional materials can support shifts in teacher and student practices” (p. 782). Yet, these resources are not one-size-fits-all—teachers adapt instructional materials for use in their classrooms (Taylor, 2013). The ways that teachers modify curricula likely depend on ideologies about students, interactions, and STEM. In this paper, I propose that teachers’ curricular adaptations are a form of ideological stance-taking (Louie, 2020; Philip, 2011) through which teachers express commonsense understandings about STEM education. I analyze 31 5th grade teachers’ adaptations of a 4-week STEM robotics curriculum to illustrate the ways that ideological assumptions influence their implementation of a district-designed curriculum. These findings contribute to our understanding of the role of ideologies in teaching, which can help researchers, administrators, and teacher educators contextualize and support the use of generic instructional materials.

## Objectives and significance

As Edelson et al. (2021) explain, “well-designed instructional materials can support shifts  in teacher and student practices” (p. 782). Following from this premise, a number of organizations have aimed to provide teachers with instructional materials that represent reform perspectives of Science, Technology, Engineering, and Math (STEM). Rather than focusing on rote memorization of fact s and procedures, these materials center disciplina ry practices for building knowledge. Some materials are free (e.g., OpenSciEd, TeachEngineering), while others are purchased by teachers, schools, or districts (e.g., Engineering is Elementary, Picture-Perfect STEM). Still others are designed by district leaders for their teachers. Regardless, these resources are not one-size-fits-all—teachers adapt instructional materials for use in their clas srooms (Taylor, 2013). The ways that teachers modif y curricula likely depend on ideologies about students, interactions, and STEM disciplines. Research demonstrates that asset and deficit narratives about students and their abilities shape  teachers’ beliefs about their students, and, in tu rn, their teaching practices (Daniel et al., 2023; Louie, 2020; Philip, 2011). Attending to the ways that such beliefs influence teachers’ implementation of curricula could help researchers, administrators, and teacher educators contextualize and support the use of instructional materials. In this paper, I analyze data from 31 teachers wh o implemented a 4-week STEM robotics curriculum designed to address science standards (force and motion), math standards (angle, proportion), and engineering standards (engineering design process). In this context, I ask, how is teachers’ ideological sensemaking expressed through their adaptation of STEM instructional materials?

## Theoretical framework and literature review

Ideologies are socially constructed “mental framewo rks... [through which we] make sense of, define, figure out and render intelligible how society works” (Hall, 1996, pp. 25–26). On their own, ideologies are “not necessarily good and not necessarily bad” (Louie, 2020, p. 3). Even so, dominant ideologies can be used to reproduce societal inequalities (Daniel et al., 2023). For instance, ideologies of whiteness unde rgird pervasive racism and inequity in the US (Delgado & Stefancic, 2017; Harris, 1993). Ideologies surface through discourse that exposes commonsense understandings about the world (Louie, 2020). These understandings are communicated within social groups through narratives that are used to make sense of events and experiences (Louie, 2020). At a microlevel, individuals rearticulate ideologies and narratives through stances, or verbalized sensemaking (Philip, 2011). In STEM education specifically, research demonstrat es that dominant ideologies influence teachers’ pra ctices in complex and sometimes contradictory ways (Louie, 20 20; Philip, 2011). For instance, Louie (2020) descr ibes how mathematics teachers simultaneously took up agency-focused discourses that advanced equity while also perpetuating deficit assumptions (e.g., some childr en are smarter than others). Similarly, Daniel et al.  (2023) describe how science teachers disrupted deficit narratives about young learners’ capabiliti es while also perpetuating deficit narratives of multilingual lea rners. While existing research focuses primarily on teache rs’ discourse, I propose that teachers’ curricular adaptations are also a form of ideological stance-taking through which teachers express commonsense understandings a bout “the world” of STEM education, including their understandings of students, interactions, and STEM disciplines. These stances are important because they influence the potential impacts of curricular materials, and, in tun, students’ experiences and l earning opportunities. Whereas research has historically focused on helping teacher s implement curricula with fidelity (designing “teacher-proof” curriculum), Taylor (2013) argues that teachers’ adaptations are inevitable and can be beneficial for students. Broadly designed instructional materials are by def inition generic, yet Calabrese Barton and Tan (2020) explain  that equitable STEM education relies on explicitly valuing students’ unique resources. Following from this perspective, teachers have effectively adapted curricula r materials to meet students’ needs and draw on students’ unique experiences, ideas, and practices (Bauml, 2016; Burkhauser & Lesaux, 2017; Rapp, 2014). For instance, Chiu et al., (2021) describe how two 5th grade teachers made adaptations to an NGSS-aligned curricular unit. The teachers’ adaptations contextualized the unit and drew upon students’ community-based ways of knowing. Such adaptations imply an  ideological assumption about STEM learning: that students’ unique resources are relevant to their disciplinary learning. These assumptions challenge the dominant ideology  that STEM learning involves the absorption of a universa l, objective, and fixed body of knowledge (for more  detail about this and other ideologies, see Louie et al., 2021). While Chiu et al. (2021) describe curricular  adaptions in service of equity, it is also possible for teachers  to modify curricula in ways that perpetuate social  inequity (for example, by offering certain learning opportunities only to “high students” that can “handle that;” Louie, 2020, p. 18). This paper analyzes teachers’ adaptations of i nstructional materials through the lens of  ideological sensemaking in order to demonstrate the influence of ideologies  on students’ learning opportunities, despite teachers’ use of a shared curriculum.

## Method

This study was conducted in a large metropolitan school district in the midwestern US. The study focused on a 4week STEM robotics curriculum designed to address  state standards for 5th grade science, mathematics, and engineering. The curriculum was developed because the school district purchased Vex GO kits (vexrobotics.com) for every elementary school in the district. The cu rriculum was co-designed by the district’s elementary science curriculum coordinator and the author, with support from the Vex Robotics education team. The curriculum aimed to offer students opportunities to ask and answer p ersonally meaningful questions, express creativity,  and reinforce standards-aligned learning. The unit began with a scavenger h unt to familiarize students with the k its. Then, lessons involved students building cars power ed in different ways (push, rubber band, motor) and  using their cars for investigations related to force and motion. Finally, the lessons introduced students to  a visual programming language that they could use to program their cars in a parade route. In addition to the robotics kits, teachers were provided with lesson plans that included scripted teacher language (directions, questions), studentfacing handouts, and slides. Teachers were introduced to the curriculum in two half-day professional development sessions, and they were supported to implement the curriculum with optional weekly “office hours” with  the curriculum designers. They also had access to onlin e “PD+” resources from Vex Robotics. Participation in the project was optional, and resources were not contingent on teachers’ implementation of the curriculum as written. Although the district encouraged participating teachers to imple ment the curriculum in May (after state assessments), some teachers chose to implement the curriculum earlier in the year. Across the district, 64 teachers participated in professional development and received instructional materials; 31 of these teachers participated in this study. This paper analyzes questionnaires completed by those 31 teachers. Questionnaires were an informal form of feedback for the district elementary science curriculum coordinator: teachers scored lessons on a 1-5 scale and shared comments about each of the lessons. My analysis focuses on the teachers’ comments about the lessons. These comments ranged from one sentence to three paragraphs per activity, and they reflected how teachers would change the lesson s (suggested or enacted adaptations to the instructional mate rials). I analyzed teachers’ comments with inductive coding (Charmaz, 2006) grounded in teachers’ language, identifying three categories of adaptations: (1) logistical, (2) scaffolding, and (3) opening/extending. Then, I considered the ideolo gical assumptions that that were expressed through teachers’ adaptations of the instructional materials.

## Findings and discussion

Using teachers’ questionnaires, this section descri bes how teachers’ ideological sensemaking influence d their adaptations (suggested or enacted) of the robotics curriculum (see Table 1 for codes, frequencies, and examples). Most often, teachers suggested or enacted logistical adaptations to the curriculum (18/31). These changes focused on how instructional materials were organiz ed or presented to students. For instance, many tea chers adjusted the amount of time allotted to individual activities. Some also modified the student facing handouts, for example, by adding reflection prompts from the teac her script to the worksheets. While these adaptation s may initially seem ideologically neutral, some conveyed  assumptions about students and their abilities. On one hand, some teachers adapted instruction by adding specifi c build instructions to the  slide decks—requiring students to build their cars step-by-step as a whole-class. On the other hand, some teachers adapted instruction by uploading the slide decks to the districts’ learning management system for students to access and reference at their own pace. These adaptations imply different images of equity in STEM learning. Following step-by-step instructions as a group could help all students achieve the same outc ome (building the same car), but this approach cons trains students’ agency and ability to ask and investigate  personally meaningful questions. Working at their own pace could instead offer students opportunities to branc h out from the builds suggested by the curriculum, making space for student agency. For example, in a classroom where students built ca rs in pairs, students modified their cars in novel ways that lead to generative insights. One student used magnets from the kit to pull his un-powered car, rather than pushing it, leading to a broader d iscussion of the types of forces that can move objects (Figure 1a). Frustrated by the need to continuously hold the switch for the motorized c ar in the “on” position, another group of students designed a solution: holding the switch “on” by using a rubber band (Figure 1b). These different outcomes could be attributable  in part to ideological frames that shape d teachers’ adaptations of the curriculum. Teachers who saw the purpose of STEM education as conveying fixed knowledge (perpetuating dominant ideologies) might adapt whole-class slides by adding step-by-step instructions to support students in building the cars described in the curriculum. Teachers who saw students’ deviations from the planned builds as  generative resources for classroom learning might instead give students the freedom to navigate slides intended for the whole group on their own in order to make space for students’ ideas, practices, and interactions. Several teachers (13/31) described adapting the lesson to add scaffolding th at would help students to complete activities—one described scaffolding in service of the goal of having “all students finish and finish correctly.” While some described specific forms of scaffolding (“show examples” or “explicit directions”), others used the term “scaffold” more broadly. For example, one teacher wrote “There were  a few groups that didn’t know what to do at all…I am going to think of ways to scaffold this activity a little bit for groups that have trouble in the future.” On its own, providing stude nts with scaffolding support  could be ideologically neutral (scaffolding is not incompatible with an asset-based perspective); however, in these data, scaffolding was more often associated with deficit-based statements about students. Of the 31 teachers, four expressed explicit deficit perspectives of students. For instance, about the coding lesson, one teacher wrote, “I noticed those [students] who were weak in math skills did not try or accept the challenge.” All four of the teachers who expressed deficit perspectives also described modifying the lesson s to include scaffolding. This connection suggests th at “scaffolding” may at times be used to code for assu mptions about students (e.g., students are not will ing or able to engage in learning activities with the degree of freedom or agency written into the instructional materials). In contrast, 10 of the 31 teachers described modifying instructional materials to make them more “open,” to offer students more “freedom,” or to “extend” learning activities. For instance, one teacher added a day of “open exploration” with the kits rather than comple ting the scavenger hunt as written, because “kids just wanted to get their hands on the pieces and try and build. ” With this modification, the teacher offered their  students additional agency yet still accomplished the learning goal of familiarizing students with their kits. Similarly, one teacher suggested giving students more “freedom to create…without instructions” noting that students w ere capable of designing their own solutions to enginee ring problems. Several teachers also described extending or modifying the “parade” activity at the end of the u nit to follow students’ interests: two teachers supported their students in creating “marching band” style choreogr aphy for their robots, and two  teachers allowed students to program their robots to navigate obstacle courses (Figure 1c) rather thandecorating their robots and programming them to perform a parade. All of these adaptations relied on an ideological assumption about the ability of students to draw on their unique strengths and resources to participate in instructional activities. The extens ions and modifications also relied on a flexible rather than fixed image of STEM learning that takes into account students’ goals and priorities. Like scaffolding above, opening/extending adaptatio ns on their own may be ideologically neutral; however, in these data opening/extending a daptations were more consistent with asset-based perspectives—all three teachers who described making adaptations based on students’ ideas (e.g., adding obstacle courses based on students’ suggestions) also described opening/extending adaptations. While these data present scaffolding and opening/ex tending adaptations in contrast to each other, it is important to remember that ideological sensemaking is complex and even contradictory (e.g., Louie, 2020). Four of the teachers described both scaffolding and open ing/extending adaptations to the curriculum, demons trating that these categories are not mutually exclusive. Moreover, asset-based perspectives can justify scaffolding (e.g., to support students’ ac cess to disciplinary knowledge and practices) and d eficit-based perspectives can justify opening/extending (freedom allows the “motivated” s tudents to benefit from curricula). Still, these fi ndings demonstrate that beyond instructional materials and  professional development, ideologies can shape tea chers’ enactment of curriculum. These findings show the challenge and the potential for researchers, administrators, and educators of attending to ideological sensemaking as a mediator of teachers’ enactments of curriculum.
