# STEPS: An AI Tutor for Scaffolded Problem Solving in Introductory STEM Courses

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Introductory STEM courses often reveal a disconnect between taught content and students’ ability to solve complex, multistep problems. We present STEPS—an LLM-based tutor drawing on principles of deliberate practice—that scaffolds planning, step-by-step reasoning, and verification without giving away answers. Through iterative prompt engineering aligned with a validated problem-solving framework, STEPS was embedded into weekly assignments, quizzes, reflections, and lectures in large-enrollment chemistry and physics courses. We examined plan quality, solution correctness, student engagement, perceived helpfulness and confidence, and performance on targeted concepts. Results show high confidence, strong homework performance on STEPS-assisted problems, and increasing perceived helpfulness, with students shifting from answer-seeking to process-oriented inquiry and demonstrating growing AI literacy. This session will provide a brief overview of the framework, hands-on use of STEPS, and a guided workshop to craft authentically complex problems and translate them into STEPS-style prompts across STEM disciplines. We conclude with actionable guidance for pedagogically sound, learning-aligned AI integration in STEM courses.

Chemistry and physics are among the most challenging introductory courses at the college level, and one of the frequently cited challenges in these courses is students’ perceived disconnect between content covered in the course versus their ability to solve complex problems on homework and exams (Schwartz Poehlmann et al., 2024). Traditional supports (worked examples, office hours, etc.) provide little opportunity for scaffolded, deliberate practice of problem-solving process. On the other hand, while generative AI tools are widely available, their unstructured use for coursework can lead to answer-seeking, over-reliance, or inaccuracies that undermine learning and trust (Wang et al., 2025). Such unstructured use is especially problematic in the context of extensive research showing that productive struggles deepen students’ learning (Kapur, 2016). In this work, we are addressing these two challenges by developing an AI tutor that explicitly scaffolds planning, step-by-step reasoning, and verification aligned with course goals, while embedded into coursework and assessments. Drawing on principles of deliberate practice, we developed and implemented an LLM-powered chatbot named STEPS, which stands for STEM Tutor for Effective Problem Solving, in order to scaffold students’ problem-solving process and help them develop problem-solving expertise in introductory chemistry courses. We developed STEPS by prompt-engineering an LLM using role definitions, behavior guidelines, and question sequences aligned with an established problem-solving framework in chemistry (Schwartz Poehlmann et al., 2024) and physics (Burkholder et al., 2020) to deliberately scaffold student problem-solving processes. We further piloted STEPS by embedding it into weekly assignments and complemented by quizzes with an individual planning phase followed by small-group discussion, homework reflections on exam performance where students identified a parallel problem and document problem-solving steps, and lectures that explicitly taught problemsolving processes. We measured how STEPS affects: (i) plan quality and adherence to a problem-solving framework, (ii) solution correctness, (iii) student question-asking behavior and engagement, (iv) students’ perceived helpfulness and confidence, and (v) course performance on targeted concepts. T heoretical framework The deliberate practice approach emphasizes structured, goal-oriented learning that focuses on developing expertise through targeted practice and immediate feedback (Ericsson et al., 1993). Unlike rote memorization or passive study, deliberate practice requires learners to engage with challenges that stretch their current understanding, receive timely feedback, and refine their skills through repeated cycles of reflection and adjustment. In the context of college-level STEM education, this approach encourages students to move beyond surface-level comprehension toward deeper conceptual mastery, fostering habits of metacognition, persistence, and strategic problem-solving that are essential for long-term success in scientific disciplines. STEPS draws on the principles of deliberate practice to teach students problem solving, an essential skill in chemistry courses (Tsaparlis, 2021), by: (i) dividing the skill into discrete subskills (problem solving  identifying relevant concepts, connecting relevant concepts to the given problem, identifying knowns and unknowns, etc.); (ii) designing learning activities suitable for learners (the instructor creates a problem based on recently covered content); (iii) providing specific, individualized, and timely feedback (students have access to STEPS when the problem set is released); and (iv) providing an opportunity to incorporate learned subskills (students are directed to solve the problem using the plan they wrote and to finish the rest of the problem set on their own). Learning activities with STEPS are embedded biweekly throughout the term, giving students regular opportunities to learn and practice problem solving.

Design In contrast to general-purpose AI tutors, STEPS is designed not to provide answers but rather to guide students to develop a step-by-step plan based on a validated problem-solving practices framework (Burkholder et al., 2020; Schwartz Poehlmann et al., 2024). This framework scaffolds the problem-solving process for physics (Burkholder et al., 2020) and chemistry (Schwartz Poehlmann et al., 2024) students using a template based on empirical studies of the problem-solving practices of expert scientists and engineers. In order to get STEPS to guide students along the problem-solving process, we used best practices in prompt engineering along with key elements of the first two phases—planning and execution—of the problemsolving template. The prompt defines STEPS as a problem-solving coach that guides students through a structured question sequence: identifying relevant concepts, explaining their relevance, specifying needed details and equations, and creating a Figure 1 step-by-step plan. Before deployment, our team conducted extensive testing by adopting different student personas, including answer-seeking students, students providing minimal responses, and engaged students offering detailed explanations, to evaluate the chatbot’s behavior across varied interaction styles. Based on these tests, we refined further the behavioral guidelines and response patterns to handle different student approaches effectively. I mplementation While we deployed STEPS in several introductory chemistry and physics courses, in this present work, we focus on our initial deployment in an introductory chemistry course that taught foundational skills and concepts. Table 1 provides relevant details for this course. Students in the course interacted with STEPS on four separate occasions to solve a problem in a weekly problem set. Students were told to work with STEPS to develop a problem-solving plan for the assigned problem as a required part of the problem set. After developing a detailed plan to solve the problem, students solved the problem on their own (without STEPS) and submitted their work for assessment by the teaching team. After each interaction with STEPS, students took a post-interaction survey in which they uploaded the step-by-step plan they developed with STEPS, answered questions on perceived helpfulness of STEPS (Likert), confidence in their problem-solving plan (Likert), and general likes and dislikes (open response). Although interacting with STEPS was a required component of this course, students could opt in to have their data used for research purposes, and the study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB#70070). For the four post-interaction surveys collected in this study, between 24 and 34 students consented per survey (out of 48 students total).

In the four post-interaction surveys collected during this study, students reported high confidence in their STEPSsupported problem-solving plans and achieved strong performance on three graded homework problems (see Table 2). Perceived helpfulness of STEPS increased across implementations, approaching “very helpful,” with students rating the tutor’s questions and feedback comparably. When asked what aspects of the interactions with STEPS were helpful in supporting their learning, students identified strength areas that aligned with STEPS’ pedagogical design: (i) providing guidance on how to solve the problem without directly giving solutions, (ii) asking questions to promote independent problem solving, (iii) providing helpful feedback and explanations, (iv) maintaining an encouraging and supportive tone, and (v) custom-designed for the course and chemistry problem solving. Open-ended responses were coded into thematic categories, and percentages reflect the proportion of consented students whose responses included each theme. Over the course of the term, students increasingly highlighted in their responses the value of ‘feedback/explanations’ (rising from 17% to 37% of consented students) and less often emphasized ‘guiding without solving’ (dropping from 42% to 15% of consented students), suggesting that the iterative prompt-engineering process produced improved prompts and that students were becoming more familiar with STEPS and its intended function. When asked what aspects of the interactions with STEPS could be improved to support their problem solving, students most frequently requested clearer instructions/explanations (30%–41%) and fewer repetitive questions (7%–18%). Although prompt refinements reduced complaints about repetitive questioning by STEPS, these refinements resulted in an increase in students noting answer leakage (from 4% to 15%). Other improvement needs included more resources and examples, help extracting and organizing given values, and concern that an overly encouraging tone could mask errors. Post-course reflections from consented students indicate growing AI literacy: students assumed more agency, asked more and better-targeted process-oriented questions, and shifted from seeking solutions to planning their approach (e.g., identifying relevant concepts first). Their advice to future u sers emphasized active engagement—brainstorming before asking, using STEPS to diagnose missteps, and asking follow-up ‘why’ questions—to deepen understanding rather than skip directly to answers. Through this project, we demonstrate that STEPS, a prompt-engineered AI chatbot, grounded in a validated problem-solving practices framework and thoughtfully integrated into coursework, can help scaffold student problem-solving in introductory STEM courses. The successful design and implementation of the tool required interdisciplinary collaboration and iterative refinement and highlighted the importance of pedagogical expertise in LLM applications in educational contexts. In future implementations of STEPS, we will introduce best practices for maximizing its learning value based on this year’s data. We will also have instructors demonstrate effective ways of using the tool in class through expert modeling before having students use it independently. We are currently analyzing conversation histories to understand the types of interaction patterns that emerge among students and identify which interaction patterns lead to optimal learning outcomes. This analysis will help refine both the tool design and instructional approach for future implementations. Additionally, longitudinal studies are needed to assess whether the problem-solving skills and AI literacy students developed in these introductory STEM courses transfer across courses and contexts.
