# The Relations Between Motivational Beliefs, Academic Delay, and Academic Achievement in Online Learning Environments

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The purpose of this study was to examine the relations between motivational beliefs, academic delay, and academic achievement in an online science course. The sample included one hundred and sixty-eight undergraduate students. We extracted data on timestamps for assignment submissions from the learning management system to capture students’ behavioral delay for each assignment over the course of the semester. Using multilevel modeling, the results showed that the relation between academic delay and academic achievement was moderated by motivational beliefs. The negative effect of momentary delay on academic achievement depended on academic self-efficacy, while the negative effect of habitual delay on academic achievement depended on emotional cost. These findings point out that academic delay is not always harmful. Lateness in online courses is particularly detrimental to demotivated students but less consequential for motivated counterparts. Simply focusing on academic delay without considering who is engaging in the dilatory behavior could be misleading for intervention.

Major issues Academic delay is a common phenomenon experienced b y college students. Delay can be understood as behavioral postponement of one’s own work and could  signify the act of task avoidance (Ferrari et al.,  1995). Academic delay is composed of two distinct components. Habitual delay refers to the extent to which an individual has a greater tendency than others to postpone coursework across various situations, while momentary delay refers to the extent to which an individual is engaged in postponement more than usual within a given situation during a certain period of time (Kljajic & Gaudreau, 2018). According to some reports, between 70 to 90 perce nt of college students engage in unnecessary delay in the ir coursework (Steel, 2007). This number is dauntin g given that meta-analyses have consistently shown that irrational tendency to postpone work is negatively correlated with all types of academic achievement, including course  grade, quiz, and GPA (Steel, 2007). This tendency can be even more prominent in online learning environments. Although online courses feature autonomy, flexibility, and convenience, students are expected to learn with fe wer regulatory cues from instructors and peers and need to override lower and distracting processes often occurring in self-paced learning environments that are competing with long-range goals (Levy & Ramim, 2012). In addition, students may be prone to delaying tasks until the last minute, owing to their frustration and emotional di stress from technical difficulties in online courses (Cheng & Xie, 2021). For these reasons, academic delay in online courses is an area worthy of investigation. Owing to the affordances of school and learning management systems (LMS) to capture various learning analytics, scholars are interested in determining valid measures of academic delay that are consistently associated with student achievement in online courses (Elvers et al., 2003; McElroy & Lubich, 2013). This inquiry is of great value in that it could offer stakeholders and instructors a clear idea in identifying problematic dilatory behaviors in real time and in a less intrusive manner. Althou gh several measures have been examined in the liter ature, findings are mixed and inconsistent at times. In some instances, a behavioral measure, such as date of first posting in class or days between assignment submissions, was related to student achievement (McElroy & Lubich, 2013). In other instances, it was not (Lim, 2016). As Balk is (2011) has found, the relation between irrationa l postponement and academic achievement is quite comp lex and can be moderated by students’ motivation. However, this complicated relation has been overlooked in the literature on academic delay. In addition, most of the previous studies have dominantly focused on the  relation between students’ habitual delay and acad emic achievement (Lim, 2016; You, 2015); therefore, whet her students' momentary delay is related to academi c achievement is under-examined and still unclear. For online instructors in particular, it is important and useful to know if and under what condition students’ habitual  and momentary delay are associated with academic achievement. The goal of the present study is to answer these major issues through a longitudinal data analysis.

## Theoretical perspectives

ICLS2022 Proceedings 945 © ISLS Academic delay and academic achievement Several studies have been conducted to explore if academic delay is negatively related to academic achievement. Elvers et al. (2003) found that the difference betw een the date of first access to course content and the date of exams was negatively correlated with exam scores in an online class. However, in some cases, academic delay is not consistently related to academic achievement. For instance, McElroy and Lubich (2013) showed that although date of registration for class and date of first posting on discussion boards predicted students’ grade earned-letter, they failed to predict grade earned-percent. Simila rly, a study by Romano et al. (2005) indicated that  although exam scores were related to the extent to which stu dents completed learning sessions in one learning u nit, this relation was not significant in the other learning units. These studies highlight that academic delay has been operationalized in different ways. In addition, these findings suggest that the association between academic delay and academic achievement is not consistent and, hence, is inconclusive. Motivational beliefs and academic achievement Motivation can be defined as “the process whereby goal-directed activities are instigated and sustained” (Schunk et al., 2014, p. 5). This process encompasses diffe rent subjective beliefs about engaging in a task, i ncluding academic self-efficacy (perceived competence), task  value (reasons for engaging in a task), and cost (negative evaluations of what is required to engage in a task) (Eccles & Wigfield, 1995). Research has consistently shown that these motivational beliefs have a significant association with students’ academic achievement. Ko marraju and Nadler (2013) found that college students’ academic self-efficacy and task value were significantly correlated with GPA. Jiang et al. (2018) also found that acade mic self-efficacy, task value, and cost were signif icantly correlated with midterm exam scores among 8th graders.

Methodological approach The design of this study is guided by one central q uestion: What is the relation between motivational beliefs, academic delay, and academic achievement in an online science class?

## Sample and procedure

A total of 168 undergraduate students enrolling in an entomology course at a mid-western university participated in this study. About 74% of the participants were female and 26% were male. Eight percent of the participants were African American, 62% were Wh ite, 19% were Asian American, 2% were Hispanic, and 9% were other races. On average, participants were 21.23 years old. In terms of academic standing, 8% of participants were freshmen, 17% were sophomores, 34% were juniors, and 41% were seniors. During the second section of the Spring 2019 semester, student s enrolled in this course were invited to complete an online survey on their demographic information and motivational beliefs and grant the authors permission to access their learning analytics and course grades.

## Measures

Motivational beliefs Five five-point Likert scale items (1 =not at all t rue; 5 =very true) adapted from the Patterns of the  Adaptive Learning Survey (PALS; Midgley et al., 2000) were u sed to assess students’ academic self-efficacy. A s ample item is “I’m certain I can master the skills taught in this course.” Cronbach’s alpha is 0.93. Six seven-point Likert scale items adapted from the Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire (MSLQ; Pintrich et al., 1993) were used to assess students’ task value. A sample item is “It is important for me to learn the course mate rial in this course.” Cronbach’s alpha is 0.95. Six nine-point Likert scale items (1 =completely disagree; 9 =completely agree) adapted from Flake et al. (2015) were used to evaluate students’ negative emotional reaction to putting forth effort to completing academic tasks in a course, such as stress, frustration, anxiety, and worry. A sample item is “This course is emotionally draining.” Cronbach’s alpha is 0.95. Academic delay Timestamps for assignment submissions extracted from the LMS were chosen as the indicator of academic delay. In the present study, timestamp was defined as the difference in days between the submission time and the deadline for a graded assignment. Each timestamp was compute d by subtracting the submission time from the deadl ine. For instance, if a student submitted an assignment on March 6, 2021 at 19:17 PM and the deadline was s et on March 6, 2021 at 23:59 PM, then the timestamp for t his assignment would be 0.20 days. Since there were  11 assignments in total, each student had a total of 11 timestamps if there was no missing assignment. Since there were 11 assignments in total, each student had a total of 11 grades.

Statistical analyses Multilevel tobit regression analysis was employed to assess the relation between motivational beliefs, academic delay, and academic achievement. The tobit model was used considering the ceiling effect of student achievement data in our sample (Twisk & Rijmen, 2009). The stra tegy for model building was developed in line with Hoffman’s (2015) recommendations. Person-mean-cente ring was implemented for the level-1 predictors to separate the habitual and momentary effects of acad emic delay (Hoffman, 2015). Fixed effects were eval uated using Wald tests.

## Major findings

The results of multilevel tobit regression showed t hat the relation between momentary delay and academic achievement depended on academic self-efficacy (b =.86, p =.01). Also, the relation between habitual delay and academic achievement depended on emotional cost (b = -.47, p =.004). We further probed the interaction using the pick-a-point approach. The result showed that when academic self-efficacy was at or below the mean  level, the relation between momentary delay and academic achievement was significantly negative (see Table 1). The lower academic self-efficacy was, the more negative the relation would be. On the contrary, when academic selfefficacy was one or two standard deviations above t he mean, the relation was not significant. Also, th e relation turned significantly positive when academic self-efficacy was three standard deviations above the mean. In terms of emotional cost, the result showed that when emotional cost was two standard deviations below the mean, the relation between habitual delay and academic achievement was not significant. On the contrary, when emotional cost was one deviation below the mean or higher, th e relation was significantly negative. The higher e motional cost was, the more negative the relation would be.

## Conclusions and implications

The present study examined the relations between mo tivational beliefs, academic delay, and academic achievement. The current findings have important im plications for online instructors and the design of  course analytics to support online learning. Most of the c ourse analytics currently provided by learning mana gement systems only present a student’s timestamp for each assignment submission, which possibly conflates the effects of momentary delay with habitual delay, and, hence,  may not be a reliable indicator for early interven tion. Our findings suggest that future course analytics should also provide information on 1) whether a student postpones more than usual at a given time and 2) whether a student postpones more, on average, than other students in the ICLS2022 Proceedings 947 © ISLS same course. These pieces of information need to be integrated with measures of motivational beliefs to effectively identify at-risk students in an online course. As the present study shows, academic delay is particularly detrimental to demotivated students but less consequential for motivated students. Simply focusing on academic delay without considering who is engaging in the dilatory behavior could be misleading for intervention.
