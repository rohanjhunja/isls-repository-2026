# Fostering the Collaborative Diagnosis of Cross-Domain Skills in Video-Based Simulations

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite the importance of teachers’ diagnostic competences, little is known about how teachers might benefit from interdisciplinary collaboration when diagnosing students’ (cross-domain) skills, like scientific reasoning. The purpose of this study was to investigate whether collaboration improves teachers’ diagnostic accuracy and whether such improvement or the subsequent acquisition of collaborative diagnostic competences is influenced by distribution of information or collaboration support. 194 pre-service teachers generated diagnoses of simulated students, first individually and later in dyads. Depending on the condition, information was either distributed or shared, and collaboration was supported with a collaboration script or not (intervention phase). Results showed that collaborative diagnoses are not per se better, but collaboration support, not distribution of information, can improve diagnostic accuracy. Neither distribution of information nor collaboration support had a significant effect on the acquisition of collaborative diagnostic competences. Further research has to specify how these results can be explained through process data.

## Introduction

Teachers’ diagnostic competences and, therefore, their ability to asses s students’ knowledge or skills are often seen as particularly important when looking at the different tasks and responsibilities teachers have to fulfill on a regular day-to-day basis (e.g., Helmke & Schrader, 1987). This is the case because corr ect diagnoses of students not only affect the way teachers choose individual ways of supporting their students but, as a result, they also (at least indirectly) affect student learning outcomes (e.g., Urhahne & Wijnia, 2021). The diagnosis of students is often regarded as an individual task, and the possibility of generating diagnoses in teams of at least two teachers is rarely considered. While there are discussions on how teachers (or sc hools) can benefit from collaboration among teachers, most of the time, these discussions are focused on broader or different contexts; for example, in terms of effects of collaboration in the context of school decision making or general collegial support concerning school effectiveness or school climate (see e.g., Reynolds & Teddlie, 2002). Yet, collaboration may also be helpful for teachers in other, more specific and student-centered contexts. Collaboratively diagnosing students’ knowledge and skills can be regarded as an example. In this case, collaboration mainly takes place outside of the classroom in additional face-to-face meetings in which teachers share observations or notes from their experiences with individual students during their own lessons with each other. This might, for e xample, be helpful when a diagnosis refers to knowledge or skills relating to multiple school subjects, i.e., w hen diagnosing cross-domain skills, such as scientific reasoning (see Wecker et al., 2016), and therefore also to multiple teachers (from different disciplines), who may be able to contribute to a diagnosis. Scientific reasoning can be characterized as an accumulation of a range of activities and types of reasoning that are frequently used in science (Dunbar & Fugelsang, 2007), and it ca n therefore be regarded as relevant for all science-related school subjects (such as biology or physics). As different teachers can make different experiences and observations during their lessons, it might be useful to  share individual information about specific students’ scientific reasoning skills to then arrive at a collaboratively generated diagnosis that might improve on an individually generated diagnosis by providing a more comprehensive picture of a student’s actual skill level. Frequently used inquiry tasks, such as experimenting with the growth  of plants (biology) or experimenting with optical lenses (physics), are cases in which scientific reasoning skills can be observed. Medical research has already pointed out that medical students’ diagnoses a re more accurate when generating a diagnosis in teams of two (Hautz et al., 2015). Even if the two conte xts of medicine and education seem difficult to compare, collaborative diagnosing in a medical context is also characterized by the collaboration of team members with different professional backgrounds. Past research has also shown that such a heterogeneous CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 140 constellation of team members can lead to more successful collaboration bec ause of more contradicting views (see de Wit & Greer, 2008).

Collaborative problem solving and collaborative diagnosis Generating a diagnosis collaboratively can be regarded as a special form of collaborative problem solving and is therefore comparable in terms of its demands. Collaborative problem solving itself can be characterized as a rather complex and coordinated interaction among individuals that requires cognitive as well  as social skills (Hesse et al., 2015). Whereas the involved cognitive skills depend on the specific contexts, social skills are more universal and include the ability to participate, the ability to take the perspective of others, and the ability of social regulation, which includes knowledge about oneself, knowledge about other group members, and th e ability tonegotiate with others (Hesse et al., 2015). Ideally, these skills support a goal-or iented application of different collaborative activities, such as sharing and negotiating ideas (see e.g., Liu et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2020), which can lead to the successful creation of shared knowledge (Rochelle & Teasley, 1995; Sun et al., 2020). Collaborative problem solving is, therefore, not trivial, and the success of a collaboration heavily depends on the social skills and the available knowledge of everyone involved in the collaboration (Cannon-Bowers et al., 1993; Wegner, 1987). Nevertheless, collaborative problem solving can be taught (Hesse et al., 2015), and the successful use of coordination and communication strategies (Fiore et al., 2010), as well as a  successful regulation (Järvelä & Hadwin, 2013), can be supported. These demands can be expected to hold for collabor atively generating a diagnosis as well, while the necessary cognitive skills refer to specific diagnostic activities, such as evidence generation, evidence evaluation, or drawing conclusions (Fischer et al., 2014). The distribution of information in collaborative diagnosis At this point, the question arises whether it is helpful for teachers to diagnose students collaboratively or whether collaboration is not necessary because it is sufficient to diagnose a student ba sed on observations and potential test results from only one school subject. Considering the complexity of collaborative diagnosis described above, certain problems may arise during such a collaboration, and even if collaboration proves to be helpful in general, instances of collaboration could still fail to improve on individual diagnoses. Naturally occurring hidden profiles are one possible source of emerging problems. In a diagnostic situation, a hidden profile occurs when an optimal individual diagnosis differs from an optimal collaborative diagnosis  because certain information (observed by other teachers) would alter conclusions drawn from a single teacher’s observations (see Mesmer-Magnus & Dechurch, 2009; Stasser & Titus, 1985). Regardless of possible problems, the potential of collaborative diagnosis can be expected to be especially high in such situations (because of comple mentary distributed information) and might even have a positive effect on the acquisition of optimal collaboration strategies. Supporting collaborative diagnosis To overcome such potential problems, several useful collaboration strategies ha ve been identified by research. Overall, it seems to be better to share lots of (relevant) information (Mesmer-Magnus & Dechurch, 2009), but this strategy is not the norm, as group members tend to share too little (relevant) information during collaboration (Tschan et al., 2009). Additionally, group members also tend to overvalue initially sha red information, and consequentially, initially unshared information has too little impact on group judgments (Gigone & Hastie, 1993). This seems rather plausible, as shared information is also discussed more often and earlier than unshared information (Larson et al., 1998). A better strategy would therefore focus more heavily on initially unshared information because this strategy also leads to more accurate diagnoses (Lars on et al., 1998). Thus, it appears to be necessary to support teachers during collaboration, at least when they do not ha ve much collaboration experience, to ensure that collaboration does not fail due to the application of insufficient collaboration strategies. Computer-supported collaborative learning has the potential to create the possibility for supporting collaborative diagnosing in an effective way, as it can, for example, be used to  foster the sharing of information during collaboration (Engelmann & Hesse, 2011). One way to implement such support are  simulations, such as video-based simulations, designed to foster teachers’ diagnostic competences. Simula tions may contain representations of specific segments of reality while simultaneously offering the opportunity to manipulate those segments (Heitzmann et al., 2019). Simulations may also be an appropriate setting f or the implementation of scaffolding for collaborative diagnosis. A recent meta-analysis has shown tha t scaffolding can be beneficial for learners’ diagnostic competences, especially for less advanced learners, such as stu dents or pre-service teachers (Chernikova et al., 2020). A particular type of scaffolding that seems to fit the purpose of fostering collaborative diagnosis are collaboration scripts. Collaboration scripts are representations th at are distinctively designed to support collaborative activities (Fischer et al., 2013). Each collaboration script is  constituted by one or more specific learning objectives, specific learning activities, their sequencing, a spe cific role distribution, and its CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 141 representation (Kollar et al., 2006). Collaboration scripts have been found to be e ffective for improving collaboration itself (Radkowitsch et al., 2020; Radkowitsch et al., 2021; Vogel et al., 2017) as we ll as resulting products of a collaboration; for example, collaboratively generated diagnoses (Radkowitsch et al., 2020).

## Research questions and hypotheses

In light of these considerations, the present study investigates the following research questions: 1: To what extent do collaboration (collaboratively vs. individually generated diagnoses), the distribution of information (distributed vs. fully shared information), and collaboration support (with vs. without a collaboration script) affect pre-service teachers’ diagnostic accuracy  when diagnosing students’ scientific reasoning skills?

We examined the hypotheses that (H1a) collaboratively generated diagnoses e xhibit higher diagnostic accuracy than individually generated diagnoses, that (H1b) the effect of collabor ation is stronger when information is distributed across collaborating pre-service teachers rather than fully shared among them, and (H1c) when a collaboration script is provided during the collaboration. Furthermore, we tested the hypothesis that (H1d) the effect of collaboration with collaboration script (compared to collaborat ion without collaboration script) is stronger when information is distributed among collaborating pre-service teachers rather than fully shared. 2: To what extent do collaboration (collaboratively vs. individually generated diagnoses), the distribution of information (distributed vs. fully shared information), and collaboration support (with vs. without a collaboration script) affect pre-service teachers’ acquisition of collaborative diagnostic competences? We tested the hypotheses that (H2a) the distribution of information and (H2b) collaboration support both have a positive effect on the acquisition of collaborative diagnostic competences  and that (H2c) the effect of collaboration support is stronger when information is distributed across collaborating pre-service teachers.

## Method

The simulation environment A video-based simulation environment was employed in this study as a setting to  support pre-service teachers’ development of diagnostic competences. The simulation cases all depict a spec ific classroom situation in which pairs of students have the task of conducting several experiments to determine  which out of four independent variables influence a given dependent variable. This kind of scenario frequently occurs in several science subjects. Therefore, we created two simulation scenarios from two different subjects: one in biology, dealing with the growths of plants, and one in physics, dealing with optical lenses. The simulations include staged videos showing the students while experimenting, an inquiry table (containing information about th e progress of t he students’ task), which is also featured in the video, a note pad and an interactive navigation area. The navigation area enables the participants to “interact” with the students in the video-based simulations by clicking on links in order to “ask” them questions to collect additional diagnostically relevant information. Whenever one  of the questions is selected, a video segment is inserted in the main video in which one of the students answers the selected question. (For a screenshot of the simulation and more information, see Pickal et al., 2022.) After completing the simulation cases, students were first asked to write an individual diagnosis and the n to write a collaborative diagnosis in dyads.

## Design and participants

The design (see Figure 1) was a 2x2x2 mixed design with collaboration (collaboratively vs. individually generated diagnosis) as a within-subjects factor and distribution of information (distributed vs. fully shared information) as well as collaboration support (with vs. without a collaboration script) as between-subjects factors. Par ticipants were assigned to dyads based on availability, which were then randomly assigned to one of the four conditions. The data set comprises 194 pre-service teachers (75 % female, 24 % male; age: M = 22.65, SD = 4.67) assigned to dyads for the collaborative diagnosis. The participants differed in terms of the focus (school type, school subjects) and progress of their studies (semester: M = 3.76, SD = 0.95). Several dyads had to be excluded from the dataset because of technical issues during one of two collaborations or be cause at least one of their members did not complete the study. Additionally, we excluded dyads who participated in the study but comprised at least one member who was not enrolled in a teacher preparation program but in other degree programs.

## Procedure

All participants first read a text about the diagnosis of scientific reas oning in order to equip them with relevant background knowledge about the topic. In addition, they completed several tests and questionnaire s before and after the simulation cases, which were not included in this study. After a short video-based introduction, they were CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 142 given the opportunity to become acquainted with the features of the video-based simulation environment using a shortened video case. In the subsequent intervention phase, they first complete d two video-based simulations. Immediately after the video-based simulations, they individually wrote a diagnos is of the scientific reasoning skills of one of the two students they had seen in the simulations. Upon completion of their individually generated diagnoses, the two members of each dyad were moved to the same videoconfere ncing room and were asked to collaborate in order to arrive at a joint diagnosis. ## Design and (simulation environment) procedure of the study

Manipulation of the independent variables Collaboration The within-subjects factor collaboration was implemented during the intervention phase by comparing the individually generated diagnoses and the collaboratively generated diagnoses. Distribution of information During the intervention phase, in the conditions with distributed information, one member of each dyad worked on the biology simulation while the other member worked on the physics simulation, and both of them saw their simulation twice. All participants in the conditions with fully shared information  worked successively on two different video-based simulations, one depicting a biology lesson, the other depicting a physics lesson. Collaboration support During the intervention phase, participants in the conditions with a collaboration script  were supported by a collaboration script when they were writing their collaborative diagnoses. The collaboration script structured the interaction in three phases (the discussion of information on (1) the formula tion of hypotheses, on (2) experimentation, and on (3) drawing conclusions). During each of these three phases, instructions were presented on the screen that encouraged the participants to (a) share relevant inf ormation, (b) ask for relevant information, and (c) discuss how their (potentially different) observations affect the c ollaborative diagnosis. Each participant was free to decide when to proceed to the subsequent phase of the scr ipt. Once a participant decided to proceed, it was no longer possible to return to a previous phase of the script. Par ticipants in the conditions without a collaboration script did not receive any such support while collaboratively writing their diagnoses. Dependent variables Diagnostic accuracy was measured based on the individually and collaboratively generated diagnoses during the intervention phase. The acquisition of collaborative diagnostic competences  was measured based on the collaboratively generated diagnoses during the test phase, where participants collaborated with one out of four standardized actors via videoconferencing. The participants did not know that the ir collaboration partner was an actor but expected him or her to be another participant. The standardized acto rs were student assistants who CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 143 received detailed instruction during multiple training sessions on how to interact with the participants. We ensured that the percentages of the assignments of each actor were similar across all conditions. Both measures were based on the same analytical approach: For each sub-skill of scientific reasoning (in the areas of the formulation of hypotheses, experimentation, and drawing conclusions), as well as for exhibited knowledge about differences between observable behavior and latent skills, it was  coded whether the diagnosis contained information on the respective sub-skill and whether the sub-skill was diagnosed correctly. Each diagnosis was coded by one of two independent raters (about 10% of the diagnoses were coded by both raters). Again, we ensured that for each rater, the percentages of  diagnoses coded by that rater were similar across all treatment groups. The agreement between both raters was ac ceptable to good (intervention phase, individual diagnosis: ICC absolute (19, 19) =.67; intervention phase, collaborative diagnosis: ICC absolute (19, 19) =.79; test phase, collaborative diagnosis: ICCabsolute (19, 19) =.75). The sum of the number of sub-skills mentioned and the number of sub-skills diagnosed correctly, plus an additional point for recogni zing differences between manifest behavior and latent skill, was divided by the score maximum, yielding values between 0 and 1.

## Results

Effects on diagnostic accuracy The effects on diagnostic accuracy (H1a-d) were analyzed on the dyadic level (utilizing the averaged diagnostic accuracy of the dyads when referring to individually generated diagnoses) by conducting a r epeated-measures analysis of variances with collaboration (collaboratively vs. individually generated diagnosis) as a within-subjects (i.e., within-dyads) factor and distribution of information  (distributed vs. fully shared information) as well as collaboration support (with vs. without a collaboration script) as between-subjects (i.e., between dya ds) factors. In the following, only the findings for effects mentioned in one of the hypotheses are reported. Concerning H1a, collaborative diagnoses (M =.35, SD =.16) were slightly more accurate on average than individual diagnoses (M =.34, SD =.14; for further means and standard deviations, see Table 1). Nevertheless, collaboration (collaboratively vs. individually generated diagnosis) did not have a statistically significant effect on pre-service teachers’ diagnostic accuracy, F(1, 93) = 1.34, p =.251, η2 =.014. With respect to H1b, the difference in accuracy between collaborative and individual diagnosis  was slightly smaller for participants in conditions with distributed information than for participants in conditions with shared information. However, there was no statistically significant interaction effect between collaboration and distribution of information, F(1, 93) =.07, p =.800, η2 =.001. Concerning H1c, the difference in accuracy between collaborative and individual diagnosis was larger for participants in conditions with a collaboration script than for participants in conditions without a collaboration script, whose collaborative diagnoses were less accurate than their individual diagnoses. This interaction effect between collaboration and collaboration support was statistically significant and had a medium effect size, F(1, 93) = 7.45, p =.008, η2 =.074. Finally, concerning H1d, it was found that for conditions with distributed, as well as for conditions with shared information, there was an improvement in accuracy between collaborative and individual diagnosis only with a collaboration script, but not without a collaboration script. Consequently, there was no statistically significant three-way interaction between collaboration, distribution of information, and collaboration support, F(1, 93) = 2.07, p =.153, η2 =.022. Effects on the acquisition of collaborative diagnostic competences The effects on the acquisition of collaborative diagnostic competences  (H2a-c) were analyzed on the individual level by conducting an analysis of variance with distribution of information  (distributed vs. fully shared information) and collaboration support  (with vs. without a collaboration script) as factors. Furthermore, dyad (from the intervention phase) was included as an additional random factor, nested within the conditions, in order to take the multilevel structure created by the interdependence between particip ants who collaborated during the intervention phase into account. The  main effect for dyad was not statistically significant, F(93, 97) =.88, p =.734, η2 =.457.

Concerning H2a, collaborative diagnoses during the test phase were slightly more accurate when information was distributed during the intervention phase and not fully shared (for means and standard deviations, see Table 1). However, the main effect of the distribution of information was not statistically significant, F(1, 93) =.11, p =.742, η2 =.001. With respect to H2b, collaborative diagnoses in the test phase were more accurate when collaboration in the intervention phase was supported with a collaboration script tha n when it was not supported with a collaboration script. Nevertheless, the main effect of collaboration support was not statistically significant, F(1, 93) =.86, p =.355, η2 =.009. Concerning H1c, in the conditions with a collaboration script, there was a slight difference in the accuracy of the collaborative diagnoses in the te st phase between the condition with distributed information  and the condition with fully shared information, whereas in the conditions without a CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 144 collaboration script  it appeared to be identical. The corresponding interaction effect between distribution of information and  collaboration support  on the acquisition of collaborative diagnostic competences  was not statistically significant, however, F(1, 93) =.39, p =.535, η2 =.004. ## Discussion

The goal of this study was to enhance our understanding of the potential of collaborative ly generating diagnoses of students’ skills in a school context. Contrary to the first hypothesis, the re sults indicated that pre-service teachers do not benefit from collaborative diagnosing (compared to individual diagnosing)  per se. A possible explanation for this can be found when looking into the results for the effectivity of the collaboration support, as our data also showed that pre-service teachers’ collaborative diagnostic accuracy was, in fact, significantly higher (compared to the individual diagnostic accuracy) when the collaboration was supporte d by means of a collaboration script. This suggests that collaborative diagnosis can be beneficial,  but presumably only when performed “the right way”. In accordance with prior research, it seems  plausible that successful collaboration is not easy to achieve and that there are many reasons for the application of suboptimal collaboration strategies (see e.g., Gigone & Hastie, 1993; Larson et al., 1998; Tschan et al., 2009). Potentially, these problems can (at least to some extent) be overcome by the right kind of support, such as collaboration scripts (see e.g., Radkowitsch et al., 2020; Radkowitsch et al., 2021). Therefore it is possible that pre-service teachers w ere not able to choose functional collaboration strategies on their own and that they, therefore, could not fully benefit from collaboration (at least when the collaboration was not supported with a collaboration script). Furthermore, diagnostic accuracy of the collaborative diagnoses (compared to the diagnostic accuracy of the indi vidual diagnoses) was not significantly enhanced when information was distributed across the collaborating pre-service teachers (instead of fully shared). This somewhat counterintuitive result might have several possible explanations. First, the preservice teachers may just have shared too little information, which is a likely explanation due fact that it is a common problem of collaboration (Tschan et al., 2009). In addition, (pre-service) teachers might not need all the potentially available information to properly diagnose a student’s scientific rea soning skills; their own observations might be sufficient for this purpose. This explanation is, however, les s likely, as our results also showed that collaboration can be beneficial (at least when there is support). Finally, we assessed whether possible positive effects of distribution of information and collaboration support can also be  found for the acquisition of CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 145 collaborative diagnostic competences. The results showed no significant effects  of distribution of information, collaboration support, or the two-way interaction between distribution of information and collaboration support on the acquisition of collaborative diagnostic competences. Comparing those results to the aforementioned results of our first analysis, it is not that surprising that no effect for the distribution of information or the interaction term could be found. Somewhat more surprising is that the effect for collaboration support  was also not statistically significant. However, the descriptive findings indicate slight differences in  the expected direction (see Table 1). In sum, it seems possible that collaboration scripts may have an effect on th e acquisition of collaborative diagnostic competences when employed over more extended periods of collaborative practice. This study is limited by its research design, as the factor collaboration (individual vs. collaborative diagnosis) was confounded with the time of measurement; i.e., the generation of in dividual diagnoses always came prior to the generation of the collabor ative diagnoses. An additional limitation is the study’s sole focus on the products of (collaborative) diagnostic activities, i.e., the generated diagnoses. We will address this issue by also analyzing indicators of the quality of the collaboration (in terms of sharing information, eliciting information, and negotiating the meaning of information). These additional measures should lead to further insights concerning important but still unanswered questions, such as: How exactly do collaboration scrip ts support collaborative diagnosing between teachers, and what can be done to increase the effectiveness of collaboration support?
