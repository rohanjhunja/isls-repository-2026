# Fostering Diagnostic Skills with Simulations: A Research Synthesis of 237 Studies in Higher Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This research synthesis aggregates the evidence on effects of simulation-based learning on fostering diagnostic skills in higher education. It includes evidence from 237 primary studies aggregated using meta-analytic procedures as well as qualitative analysis. Findings show that (1) improvements in learning processes and outcomes occurring during simulation-based learning do not happen automatically just as a learner is confronted with a diagnostic task, but because learners engage in diagnostic activities during the diagnostic process; (2) facilitating this engagement can be facilitated through providing authentic and engaging tasks in different contexts, as well as personalized scaffolding.

## Theoretical background and problem statement

Making professional decisions in different fields (e.g., medicine or teaching) requires identifying, understanding, and predicting relevant situations and events. This in turn sets a challenge for higher education to equip future professionals with effective diagnosti c skills to enable such decision-making. To address this challenge, both conceptual and methodological advances need to be considered. On the one hand, the scientific understanding of di agnostic skills improved significantly in the past years, and a range of measurement tools emerged (e. g., Loibl et al., 2020). On the other hand, recent technological developments allow creating complex l earning environments (e.g., simulations), which al low approximation of practice through combining differe nt representations of that practice with increasing complexity (Grossman et al., 2009), essential for d eveloping complex skills; and learning opportunitie s which augment real-world settings. For example, simulations allow prac ticing professional tasks (e.g., diagnosing) and sub-tasks (e.g., history taking) repeatedly in differen t authentic scenarios, in a safe environment (i.e., errors would not lead to critical consequences). Furthermo re, simulations also allow providing various forms of scaffolding (Fischer et al., 2022): for example, pe rformance-based personalized feedback or prompts; allowing more time for collecting information or less waiting time to access it; provide less typical scenarios. Existing empirical evidence supports the claim that  problem-solving facilitates complex skills in different domains (e.g., Belland et al., 2017). Problem-solving and reasoning in many domains rely on epistemic activities, for example, problem identification, ge nerating hypotheses, or collecting evidence (Fische r et al.,

2014) to make a professional decision (e.g., in educational context or in medicine) and these activiti es can be

facilitated through simulation-based learning (e.g., Sailer et al., 2023). Perform ing such activities and advancing in them during simulation-based learning can be considered as  learning processes, which in turn contribute to the learning outcomes (e.g., diagnostic quality, reasoning skills, etc.). Although empirical research provides supportive evi dence on the effectiveness of learning with simulations in different domains of higher educatio n (e.g. Cook, 2014; Theelen et al., 2019), the evid ence on what exactly makes simulations effective in differe nt contexts and for students with different learnin g prerequisites remains scarce. It is yet unclear to what extent and which factors contribute to the effectiveness of simulations.

This research synthesis relies on theories of skill  development (Anderson, 1983; Jonassen, 1997; Renkl & Atkinson, 2003; Van Lehn, 1996), and empirical fi ndings which suggest that learners need sufficient prior knowledge and instructional support (e.g., Lazonder  & Harmsten, 2016) to engage in and benefit from co mplex practice opportunities to improve their professiona l skills. We also utilize the conceptual framework suggeste d by Chernikova et al. (2022) to systematize the exis ting evidence on learners’ prerequisites, context a nd design features of simulations to facilitate diagnostic skills. The current paper represents a research synthesis s ystematizing empirical findings on fostering diagnostic skills in simulation-based learning environments. One of the goals of th is synthesis is to collect evidence to support a conceptual framework (Chernik ova et al., 2022) on effective facilitation of diag nostic skills within different domains and contexts of hig her education. We further aim at informing theory b y systematizing evidence on hypothesized relationships, exploring and explaining observed phenomena.

## Research questions

1. To what extent do learners’ prerequisites moderate the effects of simulation-based learning on learning

processes and outcomes in developing diagnostic skills?

2. To what extent do learning processes moderate the e ffects of simulation-based learning on developing

diagnostic skills?

3. To what extent do contexts moderate the effects of simulation-based learning on learning processes and

outcomes in developing diagnostic skills?

4. To what extent does scaffolding moderate the effects of simulation-based learning on learning

processes and outcomes in developing diagnostic skills?

5. To what extent do learners’ prerequisites and the c ontext moderate effects of scaffolding on developing

diagnostic skills via simulation-based learning?

## Methods

To address the aims and research questions mentione d, this study aggregates and systematizes evidence from 237 primary studies. To estimate average effects of simulation-based learning in higher education domains on learning processes and outcomes and consider the ro le of different factors (e.g., prerequisites of the  learners, design features of simulations, context variables, etc.), a state of the art meta-analysis (e.g., Schmidt & Hunter, 2014; Carter et al., 2017) was performed. It includ ed data from primary studies published until Decemb er 2020 and indexed in ERIC, MEDLINE, PsycINFO, and PsycARTICLES databases. The initial search with keywords simulat*, competenc*, skill*, teach*, medic* and higher education resulted in 5560 hits. Eligible studies (N=217) focused on complex skills (e.g., diagnosis, decision-making), used simulations to facilitate knowledge, skills, or competencies, reported objective measures of learning outcomes and reported control conditions (e. g., pre-test or control group) and relevant statistical values. Key components of the conceptual framework (Chernik ova et al., 2022) were coded as moderators in the meta-analysis: learning prerequisites (e.g., prior knowl edge, coded as high vs. low), context (e.g., categorized into different domains, and types of si mulated diagnostic situation; for example, individu al vs. collaborative diagnosing process), learning process es (e.g., categorized into epistemic-diagnostic activities performed to collect and interpret relevant informa tion), instructional support (e.g., scaffolding,  adaptive instructional support, coded as present vs. absent), and learning outcomes (e.g., categorized into pro fessional conceptual and strategic knowledge, diagnostic skills, and quality of diagnosis). Additionally, we included published studies and stu dies being in preparation for publication (N=20), from COSIMA research unit, which used the simulations designed in accordance w ith the mentioned conceptual framework (Chernikova et al., 2022) and provided  information to the simulation details, which were d ifficult to find in the primary studies from outside the resear ch unit (e.g., data on learning processes) to complement th e findings from the meta-analysis. We applied the same coding scheme and asked authors of primary studies to provide additional information about measures, proc esses and outcomes of their simulations. We also as ked authors to categorize their work according to the coding schem e. With the smaller set of studies we were also able to run invariance testing to see if measures work similarly across domains and types of simulations. The methods used in this research synthesis allow ed to estimate average effects of simulation-based learning in higher education domains on learning pr ocesses and outcomes; role of different factors (e. g., prerequisites of the learners, design features of s imulations, context variables, etc.). Qualitative s ummary of primary studies further allows exploring complex de pendencies between these factors and their contribu tion to the learning processes and outcomes.

## Results

Regarding learning prerequisites (RQ1), the systema tized evidence suggests that learners with low and high levels of prior knowledge and motivation show diffe rent patterns of behaviors in the learning process (e.g., asking seemingly random questions vs. asking questi ons to reduce uncertainty). Although, average effec ts of simulations are comparable for learners with high and low levels of  prior knowledge, individual studies find significant effects of cognitive and motivational p rerequisites on learning out comes (e.g., improved quality of diagnosis). Learning processes (RQ2) are positively  and significantly associated with learning outcome s. I n many cases, learning process measures have explanatory power be yond learning prerequisites, suggesting that individualized (i.e., matched with prerequisites) learning opportunities have the potential to signif icantly improve the learning outcomes.

Regarding contexts (RQ3), generalized evidence sugg ests comparable effect s of simulations on learning outcomes  in different domains of higher education (e.g., med ical education, teacher education). Interaction-based simulations are associated with higher effect s thandocument-based simulations;  simulations with real agents are associated with higher effects than simulations with virtual agents. Scaffolding (e.g., prompts) has positive effects (RQ4) on devel oping diagnostic skills, however different types of scaffolding should be matched with learners’ prerequisites and learning process  (e.g., performing appropriate diagnostic activities in spe cific order) for larger effects. For example, reflection phases as type of scaffolding are more beneficial for advanced learners; prompts seem to work better for beginners. Effect of simulation-based learning  vs. no simulation on learning outcomes  is comparable across domains and different contexts (RQ5). In line with our hypotheses, l earners’ prerequisites play a significant role, however this role decreases if the scaffolding is tailored to the needs of learners. ## Discussion

This research synthesis aimed at (1) collect eviden ce to evaluate the validity of a conceptual framewo rk (Chernikova et al., 2022) on effective facilitation  of diagnostic skills within different domains of h igher education; (2) informing theory by systematizing ev idence on hypothesized relationships, exploring and explaining observed phenomena; and (3) informing pr actice and facilitating transfer of effective practices in designing simulation-based learning environments for higher education. The collected evidence supports the structure sugge sted in conceptual framework  (Chernikova et al.,

2022) with learning prerequisites, instructional support,  context, learning processes and outcomes being

essential factors in explaining effectiveness of si mulation-based learning in facilitating diagnostic competenc es in higher education. This synthesis also allowed sp ecifying and quantifying the relationships between these constructs and characterizing their relative weights in defining effective simula tions. There is evidence suggesting that the conceptual framework including its specifications and quantified relationships bet ween constructs can be generalized across different doma ins of higher education. The evidence also suggests that improvements occurring during simulation-based learning do not happen automatically just bec ause a learner is confronted with a diagnostic task, but because lear ners engage in certain activities  (see also Loibl et al., 2020) during the simulated scenarios (indicated by a stro ng relation between diagnostic activities during th e learning process and learning outcomes).

Furthermore, scaffolding matched to the learners’ p rerequisites (e.g., prior knowledge) is a promising form of assistance (e.g., Belland et al., 2017; Lazonder & Harmsten, 2016). Although (fixed) adaptation of scaffolding based on level of prior knowledge  can improve learning outcomes, especially for begin ners; more beneficial is adaptation of scaffolding based on ac tual performance. For example,  when the learners behavior in simulation shows they are unable to recognize cruci al information or are lost not being sure about the  next step, prompt might be needed even if learner was assessed as having a high level of prior knowledge. Limitations of this study are of two kinds: 1. scar ce descriptions and lack of common language in describing the design of the simulat ion-based learning environments, hindering aggregation of fin dings; 2. vast variety of different factors and high heterogeneity of the results impacting the power of statistical methods. The lessons learned from this synthesis might also be transferable to other relevant higher education skills (e.g.., problem-solving) and learning environments, facilitating approximation of practice.
