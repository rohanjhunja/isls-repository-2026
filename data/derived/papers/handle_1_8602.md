# Imagining Inquisitive Futures for Learners With Developmental Disabilities

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Two activists with developmental disabilities designed a learning activity for learners with developmental disabilities. Their talk during design meetings was analyzed using discourse analysis (Gee & Green, 1998) to learn what imagined futures (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016) they assumed for the learners. These futures tended towards the inquisitive, and suggest that including activists in the development of learning activities for youth from the communities those activists represent could lead to fruitful new designs.

## Introduction

Disabled learners, especially learners with develop mental disabilities (DD), are an understudied commu nity of learners within the learning sciences. This communi ty is nonetheless an important part of humandivers ity, and one whose members deserve justice and equity in the study and design of learning environments, pedagogies, and beyond. One of the central rallying mottos of disab ility activist movements is “nothing about us, with out us” (Charlton, 2000). This motto demands that in order to work justly and equitably, research about disabled people must be led by disabled people (Autistic Self Advocacy Network, n.d.). This study turned to adults with DD who self-identify as activists and have experience working with youth with DD in mentorship and/or teachin g roles. Activists with DD are an ideal community of practice with whom to design learning environments because they frequently envision and work towards more just futures for people with DD. How one imagines potential futures is informed by what has occurred in the past, resulting in imagined futures (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016). Therefore, it is  important to trace the imagined futures that co-de signers of learning activities, curricula, and environments as sume learners could take up. Activists with DDs’ im agined futures may differ from non-disabled researchers’ and educators.’ This poster describes initial findings from a research study that asks: What imagined futures do activists with DD assume while designing a learning activity intended for non-speaking youth with DD? In order to answer this question, an adult activist with a DD engaged in a co-design process with the researcher, themself an activist with DD. This poster describes findings from the meetings between the researcher-activist and participant-activist, while they co-designed an adaptation of an inductive logic game. The researcher-activist and participant-activist are both Augmentative and Alternative Communication  (AAC) users. AAC are systems of communication for people who cannot speak, such as text-to-speech devices.

## Methods & Findings

In order to trace the imagined futures that activis ts with DD assume for learners with DD, this resear ch took up discourse analysis, an analytic approach that claims knowledge is constructed via discourse between members of a social practice, rather than existing solely in the minds of individuals (Gee & Green, 1998). Discourse analysis was used to unearth the imagined futures contained in talk during the co-design process. This talk too k place across three 90-minute design meetings, which took place on Zoom. The meetings were recorded and transcribed. By engaging in discourse analysis of design meeting  transcripts, the researcher-activist learned what imagined futures appear in the co-constructed space between the two designers. Analysis of designers’ talk prov ided a setting within the situated practice of designing f or learners (as opposed to methods that occur outsi de of that process), which afforded unique insight into the activists’ assumptions. The pair designed an activity in which learners ca n use inductive logic to explore their interests. Design decisions sought to center the learners’ ways of being in the world, and position assessment as an opportunity to validate what the learner learned, regardless of how that learning might compare to an external standard. Data was coded using a grounded theory (Charmaz, 2006) approach. Initial coding delineated the kinds of talk that were present, and which were analytica lly relevant to the research question. Codes were a pplied to turns and sometimes to a set of sequential turns, based on emergent themes. While developing these codes, their definitions were recorded in a dictionary of codes.

Next, during focused coding (Charmaz, 2006), the re searcher-activist sorted the coded turns into pasts and futures. Turns that did not hold any indication  to past or present were left out of this coding st ep (and this poster). The researcher-activist wrote a memo descr ibing the nature of the past or future contained wi thin each turn and used these to develop a second set of codes describing the types of pasts and futures apparent in the talk. This led to the development of several categories of imagined futures assumed in the design meeting talk. Finally, a member check (Hammel, et al, 2008) was done on this report prior to submission to ensure that the participant-activist was not misrepresented.

Four explicit imagined futures (Validating Futures, Accessible Futures, Inquisitive Futures, and Ableismliterate Futures) and three implied imagined future s (Invalidating Futures, Compliance-centered Future s, Learning-restricted Futures) emerged.

Explicit imagined futures were directly apparent in  the talk. For example, in Meeting 3, Turn 32, the participant-activist says, “The one thing I would add to what you wrote is that preferences can change over again, especially depending on how long you call the game to be.” Here, they are positioning an imagined learner in an Inquisitive Future, in which the learner might change their gameplay over time to follow multiple lines of inquiry according to their interests and preferences.

Implied imagined futures were not directly described, but were hinted at through descriptions of pasts. For example, in Meeting 3, Turn 7, the researcher-a ctivist says, “Yes. It made me think of algebra cla ss from middle and high school and how much easier it would have been if they explained that stuff [inductive logic] first.” This turn was initially coded as “Statements about ourselves that are also about imagined learners,” a nd secondarily coded as “Learning-restricted Pasts.” T he turn refers to drill-based learning environments  the researcher-activist struggled in. A specific future is not named, but the nature of imagined futures as informed by past experiences suggests a Learning-restricted Future.

## Discussion

On a whole, the activists in this study imagined va lidating, inquisitive futures for learners who were  given the opportunity to direct their own learning. These fut ures were explicit in their talk while designing. A  number of less-preferred futures were implied, but talk about those futures ultimately fizzled out before it began. Interestingly, the futures that were explicitly talked about had m ore directly positive overtones, whereas those only  implied, through discussion of pasts, tended towards the neg ative. This is surprising due to the tendency for a ctivists to engage in critical work, pointing out the flaws in the various systems, including educational, that th ey find or found themselves within. Here, instead, they focused on the imagined futures they preferred, rather than critiques. These explicit imagined futures were embedded in the process of design. The inquisitive, learner-centered imagined futures explicit in activists’ design talk may differ from those non-disabled educators and researchers take up. Wor king with activists from the non-dominant communiti es learners are members of may lead to valuable new in sights that push educational design towards more ho peful imagined futures.
