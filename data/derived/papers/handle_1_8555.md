# Mapping the Landscape of Assessment in Out-of-School Time Arts Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study represents the first USA-based qualitative investigation on assessment in out-of-school time (OST) arts education. While analysis for this project is ongoing, preliminary findings show how arts leaders conceive of assessment and how those conceptions influenced their practices for documenting and assessing arts learning. By mapping the current landscape of assessment in OST arts learning, we can more clearly identify existing practices and opportunities to adapt and reimagine assessment.

Major issue(s) addressed Discussions around assessment in arts education env ironments can be fraught “if not overtly hostile... like two opponents in a boxing ring” (Soep, 2004, p. 579). I n the United States (USA), concern endures that the  current era of accountability testing precludes the arts from being featured regularly in classroom curriculum (Chappell & Cahnmann-Taylor, 2013). Debates persist about the  extent to which arts education should conform to standardized testing culture or pave its own way (Willerson, 2019; Winner & Hetland, 2008). Additionally, arts educators continue to express sentiments of frustra tion at scholars’ narrow assessment conventions tha t fail to capture many of the valued arts learning outcomes (e.g., Omasta et al., 2020). As evidenced by present ations at the 2021 World Alliance for Arts Education Virtual World Summit focused on arts assessment, such challenges transcend international borders.

However, the majority of scholarship and discourse on assessment of arts learning is derived from schools, leaving out-of-school time (OST) environme nts (e.g., children’s museums, public libraries, yo uth arts programs) to either integrate formal assessment strategies that may not suit their learning goals, adapt or develop their own strategies, or forgo assessment altogethe r. To our knowledge, no investigation has been done  to understand how assessment occurs in situ in OST art s education in the USA, nor how its inclusion is pe rceived by stakeholders. Without a rich foundation of scholarship, OST arts leaders and educators are left to make sense of fragmented research that was never conducted wit h their learning settings in mind. This study begin s to fill those gaps in scholarship. Drawing from data collected for a nation-wide, qualitative research project on OST arts organizations, we begin to map the landscape of ass essment in these learning environments. Analysis fo r this project is ongoing, but preliminary findings provide a foundation for understanding how arts leaders conceive of assessment, and how those conceptions influence their assessment practices. By mapping the current landscape of assessment, we can more clearly identify existing practices and potential opportunities to adapt and reimagine assessment in OST arts education.

Theoretical and methodological approaches pursued

## Theoretical framework

The theory of educational assessment that guided our analysis is primarily based on the work of Pellegrino et al. (2001), which describes assessment as the relations hip between “a theory of what students know and how  they develop competence in a subject domain (cognition);  tasks or situations used to collect evidence about  student performance (observation); and a method for drawing inferences from those observations (interpretation)” (p. 36). We chose this theory of assessment as the foundatio n for our study for two reasons. First, it highligh ts the connection between theories of learning and assessm ent practice, which has been historically ignored i n educational assessment and led to the proliferation of outdated assessment approaches (Shepard, 2000). Second, the framework does not employ complex terminology from education research, nor does it presuppose any specific theory of learning, which allowed us to be flexible  in how we analyzed participants’ varied understand ings of assessment. Because this study focused on OST arts education, we adapted the model by Pellegrino and colleagues. We expanded the “cognition” element by replacing it with the phrase “models of success,” which better described the broad array of outcomes possible in OST arts organizations, some of which have little to do with direct learning (e.g., feeling a sense of belonging among peers and mentors).

## Data collection

ICLS2022 Proceedings 1621 © ISLS Data for this study was collected between September 2020 and June 2021, and was originally used for a report on OST arts organizations in the USA that predominantly serve BIPOC youth and youth from low-income families. In the summer of 2021, sub-groups of the research t eam conducted five mini projects using the same dat a to explore in depth several themes that emerged in the  report. This study is one such mini project. The m ain data sources for this study include: (a) 12 individual i nterviews with nationally recognized experts in OST  arts education; (b) 6 regional (1) focus groups with a total of 26 organization leaders; and (c) 7 program-centered focus groups with a total of 36 stakeholders that include d organization leaders, teaching artists, and young  people. In all, 72 stakeholders participated in the study, wit h 9 policy makers, 8 researchers, 28 organization l eaders, 17 teaching artists, and 10 young people.

We identified potential experts for the individual interviews through nominations by our advisory board (consisting of researcher-practitioners in the fields of OST youth education and participatory design research for learning) and the literature review we developed fo r the project. We then asked each interviewee to no minate other experts and leaders who might participate in a focus group. This approach to purposive sampling is often referred to as “snowball sampling” (Merriam & Tisdell, 2016). This process led us to include stakehold ers who predominantly identify as Black, Indigenous, and people of color (BIPOC), and who mostly serve BIPOC youth.

## Data analysis

We approached data analysis by treating assessment as more than a pedagogical tool; we conceived of it  as a complex phenomenon in which ontological constraints are interwoven with axiological commitments and implicit assumptions about epistemology that influence how s takeholders experience and facilitate learning in O ST arts organizations. Our study was designed as a phenomenological exploration of assessment, a framework we adopted from Omasta and colleagues’ (2020) study of assessm ent in theatre arts classrooms. To capture the full phenomenon of assessment, we sought to answer the following research questions: ¥ How do arts leaders conceive of assessment in OST arts learning? ¥ What and how do arts leaders assess?

¥ How are arts leaders’ values for learning and conce ptions of success reflected in discussions around assessment?

Analysis occurred in three phases. Phase one and two coincided with analysis for the larger study. Phase one consisted of a literature review on OST arts programs serving communities with a history of oppression. The literature review provided insight into promising practices, persisting challenges, and remaining gaps and tensions within the field.

In phase two, the interviews were transcribed, divided among the research team, and analyzed inductively using emic, open coding strategies: descriptive coding, in which the data was labeled by the response topic (e.g., assessment, art discipline, or success); concept coding, which entailed labeling the data by the respo nse content (e.g., learning by saturation, place making); and i n vivo coding, to highlight specific ideas verbatim  (e.g., “community-based process”) (Saldaña, 2016). Analyzing the interviews yielded a list of codes that were then used to analyze focus groups transcripts. Throughout thi s phase, scholars developed analytic memos (Merriam  & Tisdell, 2016) that were used in weekly team meetin gs to discuss emergent themes and to triangulate and differentiate patterns of meaning-making. In phase two, broad themes surfaced ranging from description s of assessment tools to arguments about the purpose of assessment and were discussed. For phase three, which focused specifically on ass essment, three scholars from the full research team conducted the analysis. The first author had participated in both preceding phases of analysis, and so was familiar with participants’ responses regarding assessment. No further data was collected for this phase. Phase three began with an iteration of structural coding, in which we applied a conceptual phrase representing a topic of inquiry to a segment of data as a method of coding and categorizing the data (Saldaña, 2016, p. 97). We used our research questions and related sub-questions as the conceptual phrases. The second iteration of analysis consisted of writing analytic memos about how each expert or focus group answered the conceptual phrases. To triangulate analysis (Merriam & Tisdell, 2016), transcripts were divided  into three sets of data, and one researcher was as signed to each set. Moving into memo writing, researchers rotated data sets, so each data set was analyzed by at least two scholars who were then able to make connections and  discuss discrepancies in the interpretations that emerged. The final iteration of analysis included compiling all the memos by theme. For instance, all memos abo ut conceptions of success from all three data sets wer e compiled. Once compiled, the data was then coded by all three researchers simultaneously using concept and in vivo coding. Those codes were then “lumped” together or “split” apart through code mapping, in which codes are organized under a list of categories and then c ondensed to reveal the central themes (Saldaña, 2016, p. 218). Analysis for this project, including code mapping, continued ICLS2022 Proceedings 1622 © ISLS until the central themes had reached saturation, the point at which the same concepts or themes emerge over and over again, indicating that the data has been thoroughly analyzed (Saldaña, 2016).

## Major findings

Data revealed assessment in OST arts education to b e a source of tension and triumph. In this short pa per, we focus our major findings on arts leaders’ conceptio ns of assessment, and how those conceptions influen ce their practices for documenting and assessing arts learning.

Stakeholders in OST arts education define assessment in dichotomous ways. On one hand, they describe assessment as a formal practice of capturing and me asuring the knowledge or skills young people acquir e as a result of engaging in arts programs. On the other hand, participants allude to assessment as an informal, intuitive process of observation and conversation with young people to gauge their progress. Participants descri be assessment results as “tangible” only if they are documented using quantitative data. However, participants also assert that certain arts learning outcomes can only  be understood through qualitative evidence like st ories. This qualitative evidence is usually considered more imp actful than quantitative data, but is less often fo rmally collected or considered credible assessment. It is clear that participants believe quantitative measurement is what “counts” as assessment in the field, even though th ey personally see the value of, and often prefer, attending to qualitative evidence of young people’s progress.

All participants define assessment in OST arts organizations in relation to assessments used in schools. They refer to such school-based assessments as “tra ditional” or “typical,” and associate them with qua ntitative measures (e.g., increased attendance, high school graduation rates, standardized test scores, and classroom grades) and assessments that seek to capture evidence of discrete skill and knowledge acquisition (Sfard, 1998).Despite the value and preference arts leaders indicate for more qualitative measures, participants rarely arti culated an alternative to school-based assessment. One teaching artist suggested that checking off students’ abil ity to play scales in music was characteristic of public school music education, and he set that in contrast to as sessing how well a young person is meeting their own goals for learning music (e.g., to develop a joyful hobby, to gain mastery as an artist, or to pursue a specific job). Another participant had no alternative to offer, but asserted that one of the greatest things we could do, or try to train this next generation to do, is to change the evaluation process of what people think is supposed  to happen or what we're supposed to measure. because if we don't measure differently, people will think we're not doing all the work that we're doing. And we're doing a lot.

Participants suggest that aligning with school-based assessment was either a necessary compromise or an undesirable association they tried to avoid. Many participants consider schools sites of marginalization where BIPOC youth have fewer opportunities to succeed, in  part due to assessments that value narrow definiti ons of achievement. And yet, their conceptions of assessme nt are deeply tied to school-based assessment. Thre e categories emerged in participants’ approaches to assessment: the comply/ cooperate with assessment approach, the creatively compromise on assessment approach, and the do it yourself (DIY) approach. The comply/ cooperate approach represents organizations that conducted s chool-based assessment practices regardless of whether they produced examples of success valued by organization leaders and teaching artists. We use both verbs to describe this response, signifying both the negative and positive valence participants associated with it. In the creative compromise approach, stakeholders engage in assessment in cre ative ways that both reflect and resist school-based expectations about assessment and achievement. The challenge of the creative compromise approach is to either find a middle ground between different  assessment practices or to execute several assessm ents in parallel. Finally, the DIY approach to assessment essentially rejects school-based assessment practices and expectations outright. All three responses reveal t hat assessment in OST arts education largely revolv es around notions of school-based assessment, even if arts leaders choose not to engage in school-based assessment at all. Across all three approaches, arts leaders have formal tools and practices for school-based assessment and rely on informal processes for gauging qualitative evidence of learning and engagement. Formal assessments are characterized by assessment practices that are employed in systematic ways, and which collect concrete evidence for assessment. Informal assessments refer to insta nces where arts leaders casually check in on or tak e note of learners’ progress toward goals and outcomes without documenting what they notice or hear (Ruiz-Primo, 2011). Michalchik and Gallagher (2010) refer to the latter  as naturalistic assessments and argue that this ap proach to assessment is not an argument for anti-intellectualism, but an appeal to the idea that assessment is a natural part of everyday activity, a practice that can be honed even when it not accompanied by systematic, and for mal documentation. Examples of formal assessments include logs for quantitative metrics (e.g., attendance, programs offered, college acceptance rates, etc.), observation tools that document student behavior, pre-and post-surveys, ICLS2022 Proceedings 1623 © ISLS and checklists. These were predominantly used to as sess discrete outcomes that could be represented th rough quantitative data. Examples of informal assessment include talking with youth and their caregivers, ca sual observation, and collecting narrative-based evidenc e like quotes, impact stories, or testimonials. The se were usually employed to qualitatively gauge a young person’s level of engagement, joy, or sense of belonging, or the broader impact a program may have had on their life.

Surprisingly, despite how many participants valued qualitative evidence of their impact, most did not have systematic or formal ways of collecting this i nformation. As an explanation for this gap in practice, most participants indicated that they did not know how t o “measure” the outcomes they informally assess. Ot hers admitted to feeling immense pressure from outside stakeholders (e.g., caregivers, policy makers, and funders) to use practices that align with school-based understandings of learning and assessment, or risk losing funding. There is a disconnect between the outcomes arts leaders t hink they should assess and what they perceive “cou nts” as assessment, indicating that dominant models of assessment do not sufficiently capture all that arts programs have to offer.

## Conclusion

Perceptions about what “counts” as assessment great ly influence how assessment is practiced in OST art s organizations. Stakeholders in OST arts programs in  the USA largely rely on formal assessment of discr ete knowledge and skills or quantitative metrics, which is often associated with school-based assessment, despite their assertion that informal assessment of qualitative d ata on learning yields more meaningful evidence of their program’s impact. There is an apparent lack of reso urces available (i.e., assessment tools, practices,  or professional support) for stakeholders to formally assess the qualitative outcomes they value, or else  a lack of access to readily existing resources. Ultimately, m ore research dedicated to OST arts education is nee ded to understand why current assessment practices are insufficient, and to identify what stakeholders need for improved (or reimagined) assessment practices that illustrate the full impact of their programs. We are pleased  to present this study, which we hope is the first of many that  begin mapping the landscape of assessment in OST a rts education.
