# Toward Expansive Teacher Imagination: Design Principles from a Review of Speculative Education Literature

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Speculative imagining—the act of envisioning a future beyond the constraints of the present—is at the core of a growing body of research in the learning sciences. The aim of this literature review is to analyze the limited subset of this research that engages teachers and teacher educators in speculative imagination in order to identify the qualities of learning environments that support the complex imaginative work of speculation for teachers. Out of the analysis of these qualities, I propose four design principles for designing learning environments for teacher speculation. These principles have utility for guiding future speculative learning experiences for teachers, as speculative activities are growing in their use in teacher education and professional development. The personal, historical, creative, and collective resources that come together across the four proposed principles coalesce to outline a path for designing for teacher speculation that challenges the systemic constraints of the education system.

Speculative fiction is a broad genre of writing and other creative expressions that directly engage with an imagined future. Sometimes called social science fiction, speculative fiction is typically an artistic depiction of a future that is created to put forth a political critique. Speculative engagement  is intimately tied to activism through radical imagination, as the imagining of new relationalities for the future is a precursor for activist movements (Imarisha & brown, 2015). This act of speculation requires imagining the details of a desired future and conveying that future to others in some form, often through creative writing, visual art, or digital media. The purpose of speculative fiction is to depart from the status quo of reality in order to open up new possibilities for the future while critiquing the constraints of the present. This imaginative work, however, is complex. It involves constructing visions of something that has not yet existed and is  often in opposition to the institutional structures that self-perpetuate. Thus, it is worth considering what qualities of a learning environment could support this imaginative work.

Some research within the learning sciences has begun to explore this question. A special issue of the Journal of Learning Sciences, “Other suns: Designing for racial equity through speculative education” (Garcia & Mirra, 2023b), brought together five speculative research projects in education that explored activity systems that promoted speculative learning and radical futures. Garcia and Mirra (2023c)  recently edited a book focused on the design of learning environments to promote speculation in informal and formal educational spheres, under the umbrella of Speculative Pedagogies. These chapters detail learning experiences aiming to disrupt social inequities and transform future educational possibilities. Much of this research has explored designing for speculative engagement by youth to promote justice-driven learning and futuring practices (e.g. Arada et al., 2023; Beghetto, 2023; Peralta & Marciano, 2023).

Less is known, however, about designing for justice-driven speculation by teachers and teacher educators. Teachers are in positions where their decision-making and collective action have the potential to impact the inequitable structures in their communities and classrooms; thus, Suoranta and colleagues (2022)  argue that educators must engage in speculative work to help shape the future of education or it will be shaped by others and the constraints of the past. However, teachers are not often positioned to or supported in reaching outside the constraints of institutional and systemic structures, and education systems often constrain what educators perceive as possible  (Beghetto, 2023). As bell hooks (2009) reminds us, “in dominator culture the killing off of the imagination serves as a way to repress and contain everyone within the limits of the status quo” (p. 60). Facilitating the type of imagination that challenges the status quo with teachers has the potential to disrupt and transform the systems of power that are naturalized through the training and evaluation of teachers in many settings. The goal of this literature review is to explore the limited but growing body of research that has engaged teachers in speculative imagination  about the future of education in order to identify qualities of learning environments that could support this type of imaginative work. I do so by examining the existing literature focused on the speculative visions of teachers to identify characteristics that support teacher speculation beyond the status quo. Further, from these characteristics I propose four design principles for designing learning environments for teacher speculation. These principles could be useful for the field of teacher education, as speculative activities are growing in their use in teacher education programs and professional development. They also could contribute to the learning sciences research on designing for learning through speculation more broadly. Thus, in this review 1028 I investigate the question: What are the features of learning environments that researchers included when aiming to support teacher speculation for educational transformation? To explore this question, I focused on research with teacher and teacher educator participants in formal or informal learning environment s that includes some speculative imagination activity. In the following section, I outline the purposes of speculative fiction and provide some framing context for how and why speculative fiction has been taken up in education.

Throughout speculative research, there are two primary aims of speculative engagement. First, speculative imagination is intended to generate new possible futures. These are often focused on breaking from the status quo, such as disrupting systems such as racism, capitalism, and colonialism, which limit collective imagination (Khasnabish & Haiven, 2014). Without such creative work, imagining new futures becomes impossible (Garcia & Mirra, 2023a), and the possible futures are limited by the inequitable structures of the present. As educational scholars Conrad and Wiebe (2022) contend, “imagination is the beginning of becoming otherwise —we need to envision a future before we can hope to shape it” (p. 8). A second aim of speculative fiction is to make change in the present. Imagined possibilities that aim to depart from the status quo must begin with resistance to that hegemony (Kruger & Le Roux, 2017). Thus, the speculative futures implicitly (or often explicitly) critique the present and drive change in the here and now (Ross, 2022). In this way, speculative fiction can highlight avenues for action and support new theorizations for educational change (Conrad & Wiebe, 2022). Speculative methods are worldbuilding, activist, and pedagogical, and candeepen our understanding of both the present and the future (Houlden & Veletsianos, 2023). Erik Olin Wright, a sociologist and utopian scholar, illustrates that “the actual limits of what is achievable depend in part on the beliefs people hold about what sorts of alternatives are viable” (Wright, 2011, p. 23). Through speculation, these limits of what is achievable and viable can be expanded. Speculation, however, is not limited to viable futures. In conceptualizing a framework for speculative pedagogies, Holbert et al. (2020) identify that the aims for speculation could range between describing  probable or plausible futures, to those that are only possible or are complete fantasy. While these distinctions have utility when designing learning experiences within speculative pedagogies, Dune and Raby (2013) contend that crafting even completely unviable futures is important. For them, speculation not only creates new alternatives for the future, but also creates new orientations towards reality. The expected reality underscores the status quo, hegemonic systems, and habits (Bazzani, 2023), whereas alternative futures can be a form of protest against those constraints (Dunne & Raby, 2013).

Much previous speculative research in education has focused on youth. In such  projects, the aim of speculation is to support transformative learning for and with youth.  For example, Arada and colleagues (2023) propose five elements of Critical Speculative Design Pedagogy, aiming to help teachers design their science classrooms to support youth learning beyond normative science. In another example, Beghetto (2023) offers eight “emergent possibilities” for how educators could shape learning environments to prepare youth for uncertain futures. This review, however, synthesizes the qualities of learning environments that promote speculation for teachers themselves, not how they candesign learning for their own students. Due to the different constraints on teachers and their embeddedness in inequitable systems of education, the speculative pedagogies that might support teachers as creators of disruptive futures could be distinct from those studied with youth. Further, the speculative futures that educators produce could be altogether different from those imagined by youth. Therefore, in this review, I characterize what we know so far about the design principles for learning environments that might lead teachers to imagine more radical speculative futures. This review focuses on opportunities have been studied for educators to engage in speculative activity in informal and formal learning settings. This sociopolitical knowledge, which has been advoca ted for widely in teacher learning (e.g. Cochran-Smith et al., 2016), also plays a role in facilitating teacher speculation. Suoranta and colleagues (2022) point out that speculations are not inherently liberatory. Therefore, building understandings of emancipatory and critical pedagogies, and the political conditions they are responding to, is essential when engaging with teachers for transformative speculation. 1031 Principle 2: Engage with teachers’ personal desires and values Related to the first principle, Principle two describes learning environments that encourage and see as knowledge the desires, values, and identities of the teachers. Several of the activities studied to promote teacher speculation began with teachers examining and sharing their own experiences and viewpoints. Young and Malone (2023) asked teachers to rank their readiness to engage with climate activism on a qualitative scale of “scared” to “ecowarrior,” and then asked teachers to write “ecobiographies” to examine how their personal histories shape their viewpoints about climate and sustainability. These activities were precursors to teachers’ speculative activities. Similarly, Kruger & Le Roux, (2020) included an initial activity where teachers shared “individual narrative tellings,” (p. 247) which included how their life experiences shape who they are and how that informs what their future will be. Both of these studies encouraged teachers to reflect, in the traditional sense, on their own experiences and values, but also to engage in anticipatory reflection (Conway, 2001)— to uncover how these inform their future actions and visions. These pedagogical activities also underscore what Veletsianos et al. (2024) highlight as important in all speculative research: a deep consideration of the implications of the population that is speculating, their histories and contexts, and how their speculations are locally specific. Principle two emphasizes not only how designing learning activities for speculation should begin with a consideration of the teachers’ values, but also how effective speculative environments let these personal desires drive the speculative activities. After asking the teachers to write their ecobiographies, Young and Malone (2023) invited them to think of their desires, the things they care about, and their values, and to begin imagining a future from there. They asked groups of teachers to design a school set 25 years in the future, focusing on their shared values for what a sustainable community is/should be. This process of driving the speculative work with the desires of the teachers further supports identifying and articulating shared values, which is a precursor to Principle four: collective imagining.

To center theoretically on the process of letting one’s values and dreams be the beginning of the speculation, Kruger and Le Roux (2017) draw on philosophers Deleuze and Guattari (2009) by considering desire as a productive force. The productive utility of desire, through teacher speculation, is that experiments of new social arrangements are inherently driven by those desires. Principle two illustrates that teachers’ experiences, multiple knowledges, and values must be explicitly welcomed into speculative learning environments and used as inspiration for teachers’ imagined futures. As Gallagher and Stobbs (2023) highlight, “by putting personal and professional values into reflexive action, students might tentatively theorize about their own practice and professionalism” (p. 9). This Principle, however, must also be balanced with Principles one and four, as individual speculations driven by personal experiences and beliefs need to be grounded in broader systemic structures and challenged to account for multiple viewpoints and hopes for shared futures.

## A brief aside about Utopia as Method

These first two principles for learning environments for speculative futures can be thought about in conversation with Levitas’ (2013) Utopia as Method. Levitas outlines three modes of engaging with utopia: the archeological mode, the ontological mode, and the architectural mode. In discussing how these modes can be applied to the design of learning experiences for teachers, Gallagher and Stobbs (2023) outline that these modes can also be thought of as the head, the heart, and the hands. In the archeological mode, the histories, policies, and pasts that have constructed our present moment must be uncovered and analyzed, as in an archeological site. This mode embodies the first design principle for teachers to focus on grounding in history and political contexts. In the ontological mode, Levitas argues that starting from feelings and desires  allows for questioning current arrangements of the social in comparison to one’s values and the human flourishing that is enabled or foreclosed upon by these social arrangements. This is the heart in the process and aligns with Principle two  for teachers. Lastly, the architectural mode is the construction of those futures: the hands. This mode is the act of speculating, the imagining of new configurations that resolve some of the issues identified in the ontological mode and historicized in the architectural mode. To consider the factors that enable or constrain the architectural mode, or the concrete speculations, that teachers are able to create, I introduce the next design principle: creative resources and artifacts.

Principle 3: Provide creative resources and tangible artifacts Principle three focuses on the tangible aspects of speculative learning environments. When considering how to facilitate teacher speculation, the collection of identified studies illustrated that the specificity of the speculative prompts, the physical objects and materials on hand, and the time period of the speculative vision all have impacts on how the teachers imagine speculative  futures. Cerratto Pargman and colleagues (2023)  specifically highlight the importance of considering materiality when designing for educator imagination. Through analyzing the results of a futures workshop for Swedish teachers, they concluded that the tools available in the workshops—the prompt 1032 specificity, the epistemic orientations to teaching and learning brought by the participants, and the semiotic resources for narratives of the future —shape  and are shaped by the future-oriented learning taking place. They propose that materiality be deeply considered when designing for speculation. Veletsianos et al. (2024) also considered speculative resources  when designing their own speculative prompt. In their study, they asked Canadian faculty to imagine what a “typical” day working in higher education might look like exactly five years later. Through analysis of the resulting futures, they note that this specific prompt led to futures that largely depicted modes of work and pedagogy that already exist. They highlight that to engage in more transformati ve imagination, prompts should be open-ended enough to allow for intersecting crises of the world to be addressed and for teachers to imagine outside of their “typical” days. Two of the reviewed studies discussed the use of physical objects and materials to spur speculation with teachers. Young and Malone (2023) discussed how they presented to teachers a collection of natural found objects, such as shells or sea glass, and aske d teachers to imagine pasts and futures for these objects. They noted that through providing physical objects for the teachers to hold and play with, the teachers were more able to engage with embodied, creative, play-based speculative writing about the im agined stories of those objects. Kruger and Le Roux (2020) gave a group of teachers collage materials such as magazines, cameras, and craft paper and asked them to create a visualization of a future community that they all could be a part of. Each group member also was tasked with  identifying their own role in both creating and sustaining that imagined community. Through the negotiation of selecting images  from the magazines, Kruger and Le Roux note that the teachers negotiate d the boundaries between expectation, or the status quo, and new socialities that were  less defined by borders. In particular, the use of common images in magazines elicited an investigation of class and race within the South African context, where teachers investigated tensions  in the social imaginaries of the urban vs. rural resident. Sparked by the materials at hand, new worlds that contested these boundaries emerged through the teachers’ Several studies attended to creative resources and speculative scaffolds to support  teachers who were reticent to use their imaginations in what they perceived as academic settings. Gallagher and Stobbs (2023) worked to combat the social imaginary of the higher education classroom as a non-creative space by bringing in resources familiar to teachers that involve speculation. For example, they demonstrated that modes of imagining are already used in academia through the discussion of historical reconstructions and 3-D renderings of destroyed historical sites like Pompeii. Gallagher and Stobbs noted that this introduction helped the teachers combat expected forms of engagement in higher education and engage with creativity and imagination. Young and Malone (20 23) emphasized that their speculative activities in their workshops were somewhat timebound and were explicitly non-graded. For them, giving time parameters supported the teachers to focus less on having the “right” answers or perfect solutions, while engaging their creativity and lowering their concerns for performing the role of a good student. This supported speculative learning as spaces of experimentation and creativity over a solution-based orientation or limiting to a “right” future (Rahm, 2024). Across these examples, Principle three illustrates that there are many dimensions to the tangible prompts and resources that impact the speculative futures that teachers create. A written prompt has a time period, a place, an orientation (utopian/dystopian), a perspective (first-person/third person), and a level of specificity, among other qualities. Objects, artifacts, and media can also act as prompts or materials for speculative engagement. The parameters around the speculation, such as the mode of output, the aims of the speculation, and the time provided all play a factor in what is created. Principle three highlights that all of these choices afford certain types of futures while foreclosing on others, and when designing for teacher speculation, each area should be considered in relation to the learning being designed for. Principle 4: Facilitate collective imagining To facilitate learning and expansive futuring through speculation, engagement with others’ futures, imagining multiple possibilities, and collective imagination is essential. Many of the speculative opportunities in the identified studies included at least a portion of the speculation done in groups. Rahm (2024) discusses the design of speculative workshops for teachers where small groups designed future technological products that “solved” a pressing issue that teachers identified in their schools. In the final phase of these workshops, after teachers had speculated about and developed a poster representing their products, groups were tasked with “selling” their product to their peers. This involved each group sharing to the others what they had developed a nd why, and fielding questions from the other teachers. This phase of the workshop, Rahm notes, was generative both as a satirical critique of EdTech consumerism and to push the speculative ideas forward. Through this discussion with peers, the groups who created each product came to reflect on its inherent biases and assumptions and to link their creation to larger societal issues such as privacy and educational hierarchies. Without engaging in each other’s speculations, Rahm contends, “participants in speculative workshops often neutralize, naturalize, and legitimize 1033 certain technologies and values” rather than open up expansive possibilities for the future (2024, p. 11). This where reflection and discursive opening can occur.

Beyond engaging with others’ speculations, facilitating collectivity involves grappling with multiple perspectives to navigate towards a collective imagination around a shared purpose. The majority of the reviewed studies (Cerratto Pargman et al., 2023; Helliwell & Ng, 2022; Kruger & Le Roux, 2020; Lizárraga, 2023; Rahm, 2024; Tomin, 2021; Young & Malone, 2023)  involved some of these collective futuring practices with teachers. This is likely because the larger field of radical imagination and speculative pedagogies emphasizes how collective imagination opens up further opportunities for disruption and resistance (Benjamin, 2024). Learning environments that expand opportunities for collective speculation, however, take time to develop. In their study that tasked pairs of teachers to speculate on the future of mathematics education, Helliwell and Ng (2022) note that while collaborative pairs bring different resources which contribute to a nuanced and complex speculation, pairs alsoneed time to forge relationships and come to a shared understanding before collaborative speculation work begins. To do this, they begandiscussions with familiar, local topics to initiate a process of genuine contextualization and connection. When designing learning environments for teachers to speculate, relationships must be given time to develop and a shared sense of purpose and potential abstract vision for the future can be formed from connection. This is also served by principles one and two, that calls for speculative environments to critically engage with the political and social histories, as well as the personal experiences and values, that shape  the local educational landscape. Further, Principle four presents opportunities to contest biases and assumptions  that can arise if only focusing on individual desires and values, as called for in Principle two. Collective imagining pushes beyond seeing speculation as an individual capacity to consider collective activity that might move towards collective action (Claiborne, 2024). Taken together, these four principles outline characteristics that emerged from the limited but growing research on supporting teacher speculation. Through a review of speculative research with educators, an agenda for supporting teachers to speculate about future educational possibilities becomes clear. Productive conceptual frameworks, such as Utopia as Method (Levitas, 2013), provide theoretical basis for speculative studies with teachers. Learning sciences approaches emphasizing contextual embeddedness and collective activity demonstrate characteristics that can support radical speculation with educators and the potential for  learning to take place in future-oriented learning environments. The personal, historical, critical, and creative resources that come together across these four principles coalesce to outline a path forward for teacher speculation design. Taken together, the affordances for educators, education, and society of engaging educators with speculative imagination are wide-reaching. These four principles supporting speculative imagination for teachers could be applied to the design of future learning environments promoting the goal of educational transformation and systemic disruption. T he work of imagining beyond the status quo to envision new futures is challenging and important thinking these principles have the potential to support. Further, collective futuring by and for teachers is needed to move towards broader social transformation, which Gutiérrez and Jurow (2016) argue is necessary to further educational justice. Design researchers, learning scientists, and teacher educators could take up these principles when designing for teacher learning for /of the future, and the potential transformative visions of teachers could shape educational policy and practices more broadly.

Beyond the design and practical implications of the findings from this review, each of the four principles highlight theoretical considerations for the broader learning sciences community. In the reviewed studies, drawing from personal experiences serves as an epistemological anchor that allows teachers to critically examine their own autobiographies and identify moments where systemic inequities shaped their learning, while simultaneously positioning those experienc es as objects of critical reflection rather than fixed narratives. Engaging in sociopolitical context transforms imagination from individual optimism into historically-informed praxis (Freire, 1970; Muñoz, 2009), positioning imagination not as escape from constraints but as critical engagement with them. Providing creative materials functioned to expand imaginative resources beyond personal experience, serving as cultural tools that mediated imagination and provided symbolic resources that enable new forms of thinking (Vygotsky, 2004). P rinciple four operationalizes sociocultural learning theory ’s emphasis on the social construction of knowledge while extending it to include the collective construction of possible futures (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998). Together, these four principles constitute an epistemological infrastructure that positions imagination as neither purely individual creativity nor passive replication  of alternatives, but rather as the disciplined, socially-mediated work of constructing and critically examining possible educational futures. Further, this research provides a theoretical foundation to see teacher learning as fundamentally involving imagi native work—the cognitive, collective, and creative labor of envisioning alternatives t o existing practice, positioning 1034 speculative practice as a legitimate form of teacher learning. These principles extend sociocultural theories of learning (Vygotsky, 1978) by foregrounding power relations and structural inequities as central to the learning process while also emphasizing imagination as a collective resource distributed across people and artifacts. This framing advances learning theory by demonstrating how imagination can be systematically supported as a form of professional learning that bridges critical analysis of existing systems with generative construction of alternatives. Further research that takes up the view of imagination as a socially-mediated, ep istemologicallygrounded practice  could illuminate how the four design principles function as pedagogical infrastructure for expansive imagination for teacher learning.

To these ends, f urther speculative research must incorporate the perspectives of practicing  teachers. Speculative scholars argue that speculative imagination  is essential for activist, liberatory, and transformative world building (Khasnabish & Haiven, 2014). However, compared to the broader speculative research field, there is little research on the speculations of in-service K-12 teachers who are central to the educational system that needs reimagining (see Table 2). The four principles proposed in this review could offer guidance to speculative researchers in how to engage this specific population in speculative experiences. Further, given the transformative goals of many teacher educators and professional development programs, speculation should be included within teacher education or professional development settings to ease inequities in access to this worldbuilding practice. This would serve both practice and theory-building agendas, where, guided by these principles,  teacher learning for collective imagination could be facilitated through speculation in formal teacher education settings, and the research on educational speculation could include the visions of those most closely engaged with the work of teaching and learning with today’s youth.

Lastly, many of the speculative workshops and prompt designs centered around a particular technology (e.g. AI (Rahm, 2024) or automated decision-making technologies (Cerratto Pargman et al., 2023)) or a single world issue, such as the environmental crisis (Gandolfi, 2023). Veletsianos and colleagues contend, however, that multiple crises co-occur, and speculative research needs to “account for the range of factors that influence education futures. … R ather than asking about the impact of a singular issue (e.g., an extreme weather event, a pandemic, a particular technology), it may be more fruitful to ask what might education futures look like at the intersection of a variety of factors” (p. 1866). While attending to a particular focus does support speculative workshop discussion and future-making, further research asking open questions to educators would highlight the complexity that teachers contend with in their daily work, as well as their priorities for structural change (Claiborne & Cordero-Siy, 2024).

As I alone reviewed the articles for inclusion and conducted the analysis, the findings are likely influenced by my own commitments, scholarly background, and speculative learning experiences. While the principles described arose from my analysis, they were not always explicitly analyzed within the research article and may not have been intentionally included in the learning environment. In fact, much of the reviewed research likely included further design considerations and pedagogical decision-making than could be included in the final published papers that I reviewed. Future research that could interview the authors and teacher educators involved in these projects could glean further insight into the intentionality behind the learning environment characteristics. Lastly, additional research that uses these four principles to guide the design of speculative learning environments is needed to empirically validate their potential to support speculati ve imagination with teachers. Future work should design learning experiences for teachers around these four principles to examine the possibilities afforded by these design features. Additionally, although these principles were derived for teacher speculative learning and from research focused on teachers, it is possible they could be applicable to broader populations as well. Further research could explore how these four principles support different groups of speculators, including teachers, youth, activists, and others. The limitations of this review notwithstanding, these principles have the potential to serve teachers, teacher education, speculative research, and the learning sciences. As a mathematics teacher educator, I am interested in creating a more just mathematics education in the future. As Helliwell and Ng (2022) note, they found zero studies specifically on mathematics teacher speculative visions for the future. In my review, I found none since theirs was published in 2022. The visions for educational futures crafted by those closest to the realities of the educational landscape offer grounded, urgent nuance to shape the educational future, and this is much needed in a field suc h as mathematics, which has sweeping implications for the future of students and the world in times of technological crisis. More can be learned about what subject-specific visions of the future can illuminate about teacher learning, values, and the discipline. Bringing together the critical, activist work across speculative research sets forth a theoretical and practical basis to design and study speculative learning in mathematics teacher education. If speculative methods are essential for broadening transformative futures, and if supporting students to imagine otherwise is essential for their collective flourishing, then teachers need imaginative spaces where they can creat e new possible futures for the education landscape they live every day.
