# Gamified Self-regulation Assessments in Early Childhood Education: Aiming for Equity and Feasibility

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Self-regulation skill development in early childhood lays the foundation for academic success by enabling children to take advantage of learning opportunities. Early childhood educators must be prepared to target self-regulation skills using developmentally appropriate assessments, which limit the potential for bias which is common in observational approaches. This study reports on n=14 teachers’ perceptions and experiences with a new teacher-facilitated, largely child-led tablet-based assessment of self-regulation called HTKS-Kids. Findings have implications for increasing equitable assessment opportunities for students with disabilities and English language learners.

## Background

Early childhood educators struggle to include observational assessments in their busy days (Cameron et al., 2023). This type of assessment holds the potential for teacher bias  (Tripathy et al., 2020)  and are based on standards upholding a deficit lens and hegemonic norms that disadvantages students of color, students with disabilities and ELLs (Garcia et al., 2019; Miller‐Cotto et al., 2022). Self-regulation (SR) and executive function  (EF) are two developmental skills that are assessed in early childhood. The Head-Toes-Knees-Shoulders-Revised (HTKS-R) research assessment (McClelland et al., 2021)  requires children to apply working memory, self-control, and attentional EF skills to  self-regulated behavior by responding to prompts such as  “touch your toes” and “touch your head”, and then responding with the “opposite”  action (touch your head when told to touch your to es). The larger research study gamified this task into a tablet-based assessment game called HTKS-Kids. A previous study showed good preliminary validity and reliability for this measure (Cameron et al., 2024). In this study we garnered early childhood educators’ feedback on its feasibil ity and its opportunity to present new information concerning their students’ school readiness skills.

The study The goals of this study were to 1)  view the feasibility of the  psychometrically valid and reliable tablet-based HTKS-Kids task to assess self-regulation and EF in the classroom, 2) examine how teachers explain individual children’s performance on HTKS-Kids and 3)  if there is evidence that the HTKS-Kids task provides new information about individual children’s s chool readiness skills. Recruitment spanned two distinct areas of New York State, including several urban regions in upstate New York and the New York City Department of Education. Of 15 teachers who consented, 14 completed the study. 10 were from upstate, while 4 were from the NYC DOE. All teachers were trained in how to use HTKS-Kids. 98 children completed the task with parental consent, with 55% being female, 17% being ELLs, and 16% being students with disabilities.  Data sources were a feasibility feedback survey, along with recorded interview sessions over Zoom in groups of 4 or fewer, or 1:1 interaction.

## Findings

Of 14 teachers surveyed, 7 (50%) selected “agree” w hen asked if they liked HTKS-Kids, while 4 (29%) selected “strongly agree.” The remaining 3 (21%) selected “neutral.” The survey and interviews showed that some teachers reported using HTKS-Kids during center time, others during nap time, and for  some, whenever there was extra time available. Staffing constraints were noted, as some teachers reported wanting to give HTKS-Kids outside of the classroom, but couldn’t due to adult: child ratios.  The game was reported as generally well-received by both teachers and children. Teacher T9, felt the tool was informative, stating it could help teachers "get them to where they need to be," and would use HTKS-Kids if implemented.  Teachers T1, T2, and T3  found the automatic calculating, grouping, and reporting less burdensome than traditional assessme nts, with Teacher T2 saying that she would prefer using it in the classroom.

Teachers offered v aried interpretations of children’s performance. Teacher T4 believed that one child had gotten confused and that was why he performed p oorly. Teacher T6 said that her highest scoring child in the class had gotten lucky by pressing the right buttons during the game. A handful of teachers reported that the game itself was distracting the children. T10 said that one child, who received Special Education Itinerant  Instruction services, did so well beca use of exposure to technology at home. Teachers T2,  T3, and T4, were surprised that their ELL students  scored as high as they did. Teacher T14 said her ELL students did so well because of extra support. Teacher T8 stated that it just, “depended on the child” and  explained that a child’s emotions may affect scoring.

Some children's performance on HTKS-Kids contradicted teachers' prior conceptions of their abilities. ELL students received higher scores than their teac hers thought they would. Teacher T14 was surprised by her bilingual children doing so well “because they’re learning English. ”, especially with one who performed at a “lower level”. Teacher T4 said that two of her high er performing children were bilingual. Two teachers reported on their two students  receiving special education services, who did better than anticipated. Meanwhile, three teachers reported that students who were seen as academically bright in the classroom scored lower than expected.

## Conclusion

Overall, this study revealed insights into the pers pectives of preschool teachers on using the new HTK S-Kids tablet-based tool for assessing self-regulation in young children. Early childhood educators from different regions in New York favored HTKS-Kids for its interactive nature and automated repor ting. Logistical challenges were noted, such as preference to screen time and managing the classroom. Teachers equated children’s academic scores to thei r expectations on how they would perform on HTKS-Kids. Higher academic students were expected to do better, and lower were expected to require support, particularly students of color, students with disabilities and English language learners. Teachers attributed a lower academic child’s higher scoring to having access to devices at home, receiving explicit modeling before the task, or pressing buttons.  Only one teacher brought up that it may depend on t he child’s emotional regulation at the time of the task. This creates an inference of deficit-based conclusion in both EF tasks and intellectual/ academic abilities in standardized assessments (Miller‐Cotto et al., 2022). Teachers who had bilingual students complete HTKS-Kids were surprised that they did so well compared to their monolingual peers, upholding racial discrepancies (Garcia et al., 2019). These reactions support the notion of a deficit mindset concerning minoritized childre n and their EF development across different culture s (Miller‐ Cotto et al., 2022). Teachers who had students with developmental delays and disabilities that completed the task performed better than anticipated when compared wit h typically developing peers.  These statements from these teachers, perpetuates the idea of ableism, a misunderstanding that having a disability lends one to having poor cognitive skills, which is not always accurate.

This study shows the importance of using direct assessment for  SR and EF skills, as other types may lead to bias. HTKS-Kids may be able to provide new information about c hildren’s school readiness skills, as results showed that ELLs and students with disabilities performed better than anticipated, challenging a deficit mindset.
