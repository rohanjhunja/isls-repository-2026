# Unveiling Critique: Exploring AI’s Future and Societal Impact through Interdisciplinary Epic Theatre and STEM Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores the role of student critique within a high school integrated theatre-STEM curriculum focused on Artificial Intelligence (AI). By conceptualizing student critique as a nested activity within the broader learning activity through the lens of Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT), this design-based research examines how students perceive and engage in critique-including peer critique, self-critique, critique of AI concepts, and critique of the curriculum. Thematic analysis of six selected datasets, including student survey responses and livediscussions, revealed three key themes: (1) the significant social and emotional dimensions of student critique; (2) the expansion of critique from theatrical elements to interdisciplinary aspects such as technological accuracy and ethical and societal considerations of AI; and (3) contradictions and tensions within the critique activity acted as catalysts for development and transformation. The findings highlight that a theatre-STEM curriculum encouraging and incorporating student critique can promote student agency and enhance interdisciplinary learning.

## Introduction

Integrating Arts with Science, Technology, Engineer ing, and Mathematics (STEM) education may enhance students’ critical thinking, creativity, and interdisciplinary understanding (Bequette & Bequette, 2012; Henriksen, 2014; Rinne et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2023). This study focuses on an integrated theatre-STEM curriculum where high school students produced an original play about how they imagined the future of AI systems and robots. The process of student critique was central to the learning environment, encompassing p eer critique  (Lowe et al., 2023), self-critique (Wanner & Palmer, 2018), critique of their theatric presentations as well as AI technologies and their ethical and societal implications, and critique of the curriculum itself. While research exists on the role of peer critique in learning, especially in arts education but increasingly in STEM education (Nicol et al., 2014; Van Gennip et al., 2009), there is a gap in understanding how student critique is perceived, provided, and received by students in interdisciplinary settings. This stu dy aims to fill th e gap by exploring the criteria students use when critiquing, and how their perceptions and practices change over time. The following questions guided the study:

1. How do students perceive and experience student critique in an inte grated theatre-STEM environment,

and how do these perceptions change over time?

2. How do students engage in student critique, and wha t criteria do they use when critiquing theatrical

elements compared to STEM aspects? How do their criteria and engagement change over time?

3. How does the curriculum design support and influence the student critique process among students?

## Theoretical Framework

Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) CHAT provides a framework for analyzing learning as a socially mediated activity (Engeström, 2005; Vygotsky, 1978). Within CHAT, an activity system consists of the fol lowing components that interact with each other: subjects, objects, instruments (tools and signs), community, rules, division of labor, and outcomes. Contradictions and tensions between components drive development a nd transformation within the system  (Engeström, 2005; Roth & Lee, 2007). CHAT offers insights into how learning processes are socially and culturally mediated, which is essential for understanding the complexities of this integrated theatre-STEM environment. For this study, student critique is conceptualized as a nested activity within the broader learning activity in the integrated theatre-STEM environment  (see Table 1). This nested activity involves studen ts analyzing, evaluating, and providing critique on their peers’ work, their own work, the subject matter, and even the curriculum. Student critique  operates with its own objectives and mediating comp onents while interacting dynamically with the broader learning activity. Thi s perspective allows for a more nuanced analysis of  how critique contributes to learning and how contradictions and tensions emerging within the critique activity influence students’ educational experience.

Epic theatre and dialectics Epic Theatre, based on Bertolt Brecht’s principles, aims to provoke rational thinking and c ritical self-reflection, encouraging performers and audiences to engage critically with the play’s themes and question societal norms through theatrical performance (Brecht, 1964). Epic Theatre employs techniques such as the alienat ion effect to prevent the audience from becoming emotionally abso rbed, and breaking the fourth wall, directly addres sing the audience, challenging passive reception, promoting analytical detachment and critical thinking, stimulating active engagement.

Dialectics, as integrated by Vygotsky, involves exploring contradictions and opposing forces, leading to the synthesis of new ideas (Vygotsky, 1978). In thi s study, dialectics provides a framework for integr ating Epic Theatre’s methods with STEM concepts, particularly AI’s ethical and societal implications. Epic Theatre and dialectics serve as instruments within the nested critique activity, facilitating exploration and und erstanding by encouraging students to challenge assumptions and consider multiple perspectives.

## Methodology

This study employed a  Design-Based Research (DBR) methodology (Barab & Squire, 2016; Cobb et al., 2003). DBR emphasizes theory building through continuous c ycles of design, implementation, analysis, and rede sign, allowing flexibility in response to emerging findin gs. This iterative approach aligns with CHAT’s focu s on collaboration and student agency, and it guided the  design and refinement of the integrated theatre-STEM curriculum by the first author, who is one of the researchers and also  the teacher in this study. DBR also complemented the Epic Theatre and dialectical methods by encouraging critical reflection and transformation.

## Participants and data collection

The study was implemented in a small, high-performing charter school in the southeastern United States, focusing on mathematics. Participants (n=38; grades 9-12) included those who chose theatre or creative writing as an elective course and consented to participate in the study. Multiple data sources were collected throughout the school year, including field notes and observations, an open-ended questionnaire on student critique, written critiques after improvisations and presentations of scenes on stage, and v ideo recordings of class discussions and critique s essions after on-stage presentations of scenes. Six datasets were selected for in-depth analysis  of this study. These data sets were selected because of their relevance to answering the research questions, their diversity (questionnaire, written survey critique session, whole-class discussions, and live critique sessions), and span throughout the entire school year:

• Dataset 1: Open-ended questionnaire on student critique (September 2022).

• Dataset 2: Written critiques in survey format after student presentations (November 2022).

• Dataset 3: Transcript of whole-class discussion on critique methods (January 2023).

• Dataset 4: Transcripts of live critique sessions after student presentations (February-March 2023).

• Dataset 5: Transcript of discussion on “Pause Giant AI Experiments: An Open Letter” (March 2023).

• Dataset 6: Transcript of discussion connecting the selected scenes for the performances (April 2023).

## Findings to date

Dataset 1: Perceptions of critique At the beginning of the course, students completed an open-ended questionnaire about their perceptions of student critique (Dataset 1). The analysis revealed several critical categories connected to the components of the nested critique activity system.

Perceptions of Critique (Subjects and Objects): Students valued multiple perspectives  (Reddy et al., 2021; Serrano-Aguilera et al., 2021) and  viewed critique as essential for refining their work and enhancing learning (Sheridan et al., 2022). One student noted,  “[C ritique] gives them multiple interpretations of the ir presentations, and the more feedback, the better you can understand what you did good and what you did bad.” Process and Iteration (Instruments and Rules): Students emphasized iterative improvement, for example, “Critiquing is never-ending. There will always be possible improvements. Especial ly in this world where […] everyone is full of bias. ” Also, they viewed critique as governed by specific rules. A student noted, “Critique should be accompanied by a clear and direct suggestion for improvement, should be insightful, not generic.” Criteria in Providing Critique (Instruments and Div ision of Labor): Students mentioned criteria related to basic (volume, audibility, visibility, staging, staying in character, portraying emotion) and advanced (character development and dynamics, plot clarity and developm ent, emotional range, and realism) theatrical skill s when providing critique. These criteria serve as instrum ents that mediate the nested critique activity. The  division of labor is evident as students alternate between the roles of performers and critics. Social and Emotional Aspects (Community and Rules): Students preferred peer over teacher critique, as seen in the quote,  “[Critique] helps a lot, especially when you hear it f rom peers rather than a person of power like a teacher. It’s more inviting and less anxiety-inducing.” Moreover, students felt responsible for delivering critique with empathy and sensitivity, reflecting  the community component, where the classroom environment supports collaborative learning, and rules emphasize respect and support: “I think it is important that the team know that when others critique, they are only trying to help you improve. One cannot take critiques to heart.” Modeling Critique (Instruments and Community): Students modeled their critique practices based on the teacher’s approach. A student wrote, “Since the teacher normally targets a specific range  of topics/ideas when critiquing, everyone else including myself, start to think like the teacher and provide critiqu es similar to his. ” Furthermore, students internalized the methods and language used for critique (instruments) and applied them as self-critique. As an example, a student stated, “I use what others have critiqued about presentations and use them for myself. ” This modeling expands the instruments used in the nested critique activit y, developing shared language and methodology within the community.

Dataset 2: Expansion to STEM criteria After two months of in-class peer critiques, the teacher introduced online critique surveys (Dataset 2) to encourage participation by shy students overshadowed by more outspoken classmates. This change in the curriculum shows that DBR was the appropriate methodology, flexible and responsive to emerging findings. Integrating AI Concepts (Objects and Instruments): Students included in their critiques the assessment of technical accuracy and the ethical and societal implications of AI representations on stage. This expanded the objects in the critique activity to include understanding of AI concepts and their ethical considerations. The instruments used include their knowledge of AI and ethical considerations (Runco, 2023). A student commented, “It was pretty realistic by capturing that robots wi ll be used for house labor in the future. ” Also, “I think that the concept was super interesting and honestly on track with how peoples’ interpretation of AI is.” Expanded Interdisciplinary Criteria (Instruments and Objects): The students’ interdisciplinary critiques expanded the objects  to include  more advanced acting, writing, and directing skills  and understanding of AI concepts (Hardiman et al., 2014; McGregor & Precious, 2014). For example, (1) “Change the time you guys dated to four years because it now shows that it isn’t possible to differentiate a robot and a human.” (2) “I feel as if later in the future there would be some kind of distincti on between humans and androids (AIs) that would sep arate them from each other, like a branding or something.” Dataset 3: Addressing contradictions Dataset 3 consisted of a transcript of a whole-class discussion on critique methods, prompted by a  student. The analysis revealed tensions within the critique acti vity system that led to adjustments in the curricul um. This exemplified the DBR process: a student-initiated discussion prompted a mid-course change, and later the critique format was significantly altered by returning to live sessions. Tensions Arising from Instrument Changes (Instruments, Outcomes, and Contradictions): Students were dissatisfied using surveys as a  tool for critique, stating that it obstructed meaningful  engagement and weakened the community. A student remarked, “The surveys kind of take away what the theater clas s was about.” Another student stated, “I feel we don’t have the community, the team that we used to have, and I feel this is because we’re going online to critique, and we’re not talking to each other. So, we don’t feel so close. So, we’re not able to be a real team.” This reflects a contradiction between the instruments for the critique activity and the desired outcomes. Collaborative Resolution (Rules and Outcomes): Students advocated returning to live critique sessions, and the teacher decided to re-introduce them:  “I take full responsibility, and I know why that was a mistake. Because yes, one of the reasons that I thought of th e surveys […] I thought, okay, maybe there are stud ents that are too shy to participate, but on the other hand, they have valuable things to offer.”

## Discussion

In response to the research questions, we highlight  the following points. First, students initially valued critique for improving presentations. However, the shift to online surveys introduced contradictions within the  activity system, such as reduced interaction and engagement,  leading to a weakened sense of community. Recognizing these contradictions as expansion opportunities (Engeström, 2005) led to  a return to live critique sessions, addressing emotional tensions and promoting a supportive community of learners (Osborne, 2010). Second, students expanded their criteria from only theatrical elements to include AI aspects, resulting in more nuanced and advanced critiques. This interdisciplinary engagement supports the notion that integrating arts and STEM promotes holistic learning (Henriksen, 2014). Similarities i n their criteria included a focus on being constructive to improve understanding and learning. However, there were striking tensions, such as emphasizing self-expression, originality, and emotions in theatre ve rsus technological accuracy, plausibility, and logi c in AI. These tensions, aligning with Engeström’s (2005) notion that contradictions within an activity system can lead to expansion, led students to include ethical issues raised by AI applications in their critiques. Third, the curriculum greatly influences student critique p ractices and community dynamics, as seen in the change of critique instruments  f rom livedialectics to online written surveys, and back, accompanied by corresponding shifts in participant roles and inter actions. Adapting the curriculum in real-time to be responsive to student critique exemplified Engeström’s (2005) third-generation CHAT, where interacting activity systems and emergent contradictions lead to change, development, and  expansive learning, i.e., participants collaboratively resolve contradictions, transforming the activity. Lastly, the theory-building aspect of DBR enabled us to conceptualize student critique as a nested activity within the broader learning activity through the lens of CHAT. This CHAT-informed perspective was essential for collaboratively resolving tensions from the cur riculum changes, which in turn enhanced the sense o f community, encouraged student agency  (Cook-Sather, 2006), and expanded the critique activity (Engeström, 2005), ultimately promoting deeper interdisciplinary learning.
