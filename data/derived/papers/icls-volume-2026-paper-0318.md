# Assessing Interdisciplinary Learning in STEAM Education: A Critical Narrative Review

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Interdisciplinary STEAM education increasingly emphasizes integration across disciplines, yet learning assessment has been focused on measuring discrete knowledge outcomes rather than integration processes. This theoretical paper examines assessment approaches in interdisciplinary STEAM and higher education, revealing gaps between educational goals and assessment methods. Through a critical narrative review, we identify three major approaches (objective tests, questionnaires, and performance-based assessments) and analyze their limitations for capturing integration dynamics. Drawing on Actor-Network Theory (ANT), we provide a theoretical perspective for understanding interdisciplinary learning as emergent phenomena arising from human and non-human actor interactions. ANT's translation concept offers a lens for making visible the mechanisms through which learners negotiate meanings and construct new understandings across disciplinary boundaries, contributing toward more equitable and theoretically grounded assessment practices.

In secondary and higher education, interdisciplinary education, such as STEAM, has emerged as a policy priority worldwide (Markauskaite et al., 2024). This paper focuses specifically on interdisciplinary STEAM education that bridges disciplinary boundaries, rather than treating STEAM as an aggregate of separate disciplines. Various theoretical frameworks have been developed to guide  learner-centered, inquiry-oriented activities in interdisciplinary STEAM (e.g., Kolodner et al., 2003; Thibaut et al., 2018), with primary attention toward learning processes. For example, Learning by Design (Kolodner et al., 2003) provides a lens for making student learning processes visible, articulating trajectories of inquiry activities, and iterative movements between them. However, assessment of learning processes has emerged as a critical challenge (Perignat & Katz-Buonincontro, 2019; Gao et al., 2020). Despite the centrality of processes in interdisciplinary STEAM, assessment has relied on discipline-specific tests measuring knowledge acquisition. Many programs over the past two decades articulated goals related to interdisciplinary understanding and integrated skills, assessment methods remain limited to single-discipline knowledge or affective dimensions (Gao et al., 2020). This misalignment between educational program goals and assessment methods has been identified as a critical challenge; consequently, the development of methods to evaluate learning processes (e.g., Yang et al., 2025)  is emerging as a key theme for future interdisciplinary STEAM education.

When examining learning processes, attending to degrees of integration becomes essential. This study focuses on inter-and trans-disciplinary approaches exhibiting high integration (Gao et al., 2020), aimed at creating new understandings impossible within single disciplines, rather than multidisciplinary approaches that primarily treat disciplinary content separately. To clarify assessment challenges, we also draw on interdisciplinary higher education, where substantial scholarship has accumulated (Spelt et al., 2009; Boix Mansilla et al., 2009), providing broader perspectives on validated assessment methods and analytical tools applicable  (Shipley et al., 2017). Moreover, this study examines learning processes by drawing on theoretical frameworks that highlight sociomateriality. Recent developments in learning sciences emphasize the interdependence of social and material elements in learning (Fenwick, 2015; Rook et al., 2024). Posthumanist perspectives (Keune, 2022) and Actor-Network Theory (ANT) (Latour, 2005; Papendi eck & Clarke 2024) offer productive lenses for making these interdependencies visible. Keune (2022) demonstrates how posthumanist perspectives reveal learning as emerging through human-material entanglements. While focusing specifically on the learning processes within interdisciplinary learning, this study adopts ANT's "translation" concept (Callon, 1984) as its specific framework. This concept, which describes how actors transform meanings and establish relationships through four stages (problematization, interessement, enrolment, mobilization), provides a foundation for understanding complex inter-and trans-disciplinary learning trajectories.

## Method

This paper undertakes a critical narrative review (Takeuchi et al., 2020) of the literature on assessment in interdisciplinary STEAM and higher education. The review proceeded in two phases: a systematic search within selected journals, followed by snowball sampling to complement the corpus. In Phase 1, we selected eight journals spanning science education and interdisciplinary higher education: five science education journals (International Journal of STEM Education, Research in Science Education, Studies in Science Education, International J ournal of Science Education, Science & Education) and three interdisciplinary higher education journals (The Journal of Higher Education, European Journal of Higher Education, Studies in Higher Education). Within these journals, we identified articles published between 2000 and 2025, written entirely in English, whose titles or abstracts using the following Boolean operators and search terms: (interdisciplin* OR transdisciplin *) AND (assess* OR rubric OR measure). From this initial pool, we screened articles through close reading of titles, abstracts, and full texts to identify those focusing on assessment creating new understandings through synthesizing multiple disciplinary perspectives, rather than multidisciplinary approaches treating disciplines separately. This phase yielded 14 articles. In Phase 2, to complement the corpus with relevant research beyond the selected journals, we conducted snowball sampling by tracking both the reference lists and citing articles of the 14 articles identified in Phase 1. Through this procedure, we incorporated additional studies from journals addressing interdisciplinary education (e.g., Sustainability). This process yielded 4 additional articles, bringing the final review corpus to 18 articles assessing students' interdisciplinary learning processes or outc omes. The first author categorized each article's primary assessment approach through iterative coding, with categorizations reviewed and confirmed by the second author. We additionally conducted a narrative review of foundational theoretical frameworks (e.g., ANT, sociomateriality) informed by seminal works (Rook et al., 2024) and recent reviews in learning sciences. RQ1. What are the characteristics of assessment methods for interdisciplinary learning within STEAM and higher education?

Current approaches in interdisciplinary STEAM education fall into three categories (objective tests, questionnaires, and performance-based assessments), while in interdisciplinary higher  education fall into three categories (Self-report, Performance-based assessment, and Interview) (Table 1). Current assessment approaches in STEAM show a trend from measuring outcomes to, more recently, processes, yet all struggle to capture integration mechanisms. Objective tests, for example, focus on employing knowledge from multiple disciplines to explain specific phenomena (e.g., osmosis or carbon cycling), often using Rasch analysis for validation (Shen et al., 2014; You et al., 2018). While advancing direct knowledge integration assessment, they primarily capture outcomes, leaving multifaceted competency development and process unexamined. Questionnaires indirectly measure learning outcomes by developing scales for interdisciplinary competencies (e.g., Wang & Song, 2021) or e pistemological dimensions (Martínez -Martínez et al., 2025). However, validated tools specifically measuring learning processes remain limited (Gao et al., 2020). Performance-based rubrics represent the most recent trend toward directly evaluating processes. These include frameworks grounded in Learning by Design (Fang et al., 2025), Bayesian-validated rubrics (Yang et al., 2025), and tools for collaborative problem-solving (Herro et al., 2017). Interdisciplinary higher education research, which often defines its goal as fostering "interdisciplinary thinking" (Boix Mansilla et al., 2007), faces a parallel challenge. Recent reviews conclude that evidence for interdisciplinary learning outcomes rema ins early-stage, with process assessment rigor as a future priority (Oudenampsen, 2024). Assessment methods in this field, attempt to capture both process and outcomes; for example, Interviews (Haynes & Leonard, 2010) capture students' post-hoc narratives and perceptions of their integration process, while Questionnaires  (Schijf et al., 2023) measure self-reported understanding. The most developed tools are Performance-based rubrics, which define quality dimensions for process-informed outcomes like "disciplinary grounding" and "advancement through integration" (Boix Mansilla et al., 2009). Formative rubrics for "boundary crossing" have also been developed (Gulikers & Oonk, 2019). A common limitation remains, however: while these methods assess the evidence of a process (through narrative or final products), they still face challenges in directly capturing the temporal, dynamic, and often messy mechanisms of integration as they occur.

RQ2. How can ANT's framework provide prospects for analyzing and assessing learning processes in interdisciplinary STEAM education? The assessment approaches reviewed in RQ1 reveal a fundamental limitation: while measuring integration outcomes, they inadequately capture the dynamic processes through which integration emerges. Actor-Network Theory (Latour, 2005) addresses this gap by conceptualizing learning as relational achievements constructed through ongoing negotiations among heterogeneous actors. Rather than treating integration as cognitive phenomena within individual minds, ANT directs attention to how understanding emerges through assembling human and non-human actors intonetworks. The following analysis is conceptual and prospective: we apply ANT's translation framework to existing assessment approaches to identify what current methods structurally cannot capture. ANT's application to learning has been empirically explored by Papendieck and Clarke (2024), who traced productive engagement in interdisciplinary courses. Our analysis extends this direction into the assessment context.

Central to ANT is translation  (Callon, 1984), describing how actors transform meanings through four stages. For each stage, we identify what current assessment captures and what it structurally misses. In problematization, learners encounter phenomena requiring multiple disciplinary perspectives, recognizing singlediscipline insufficiency. Consider Shen et al.'s (2014) osmosis assessment: students must recognize that explaining osmotic pressure requires coordinating physi cal, chemical, and biological perspectives. Current assessments measure whether students achieve this recognition but not the negotiations through which problem definitions emerge. ANT-informed assessment would track the moment of recognition—observable th rough shifts in discourse and tool use—as a processual indicator. In i nteressement, students engage disciplinary concepts and materials, negotiating how these resources address identified problems. Concepts resist, tools malfunction, materials behave unexpectedly; ANT suggests assessing how learners respond to these resistances rather t han only whether enrolment succeeds. This stage can be captured through interaction analysis of how shared representations are reorganized when new disciplinary resources enter the network. In e nrolment, multilateral negotiations define and coordinate actors' roles as learners synthesize multiple perspectives. Current rubrics (Boix Mansilla et al., 2009) assess synthesis quality but treat coordination work as invisible; ANT foregrounds how specific collaborative configurations enable or prevent integration. In mobilization, learners articulate integrated understanding through performances. ANT adds critical attention to representation politics: which disciplinary voices dominate in final products, and whose integration pathways become legitimized (Chandra et al., 2025). To illustrate how these stages might be recognized empirically, consider a hypothetical data science inquiry unit in which secondary students formulate research questions grounded in their own disciplinary interests, such as an educational question about study habits, and then collect and analyze data to test their hypotheses. During problematization, a student's survey data contradicts the initial hypothesis derived from educational research; this moment of discrepancy between hypothesis and data is observable as a recognition of single discipline insufficiency. In interessement, the student searches for concepts from other disciplines that might explain the unexpected data pattern, encountering a behavioral economics paper on cognitive biases. This engagement with a new disciplinary actor (the academic paper as a nonhuman actor) is observable through the student's written rationale for selecting this cross disciplinary reference. During enrolment, the critical question becomes whether the new disciplinary resource merely serves as an interpretive supplement, explaining the data within the original educational framing, or whether it fundamentally reconstitutes what the data means. ANT informed assessment would distinguish these trajectories: in the former case, enrolment remains partial (the new discipline is borrowed but the research question is unchanged), while in the latter, enrolment succeeds and the 2083 data becomes evidence for a qualitatively different question. In mobilization, only when enrolment fully reconstitutes the meaning of data does the student's research question itself transform, shifting, for instance, from "How do study habits affect academic achievement?" to "How do cognitive biases shape students' self reported study behaviors?" Current rubrics would evaluate the quality of the final research question but would miss the mechanism through which disciplinary boundary crossing occurred or failed to occur at enrolment. This translation framework reveals power dynamics that current approaches overlook. Translation highlights that integration involves negotiations where certain perspectives may dominate while others become marginalized. Chandra et al. (2025) found that assessment styles advantaged students from particular disciplines, for example law students excelled in essays while arts students performed better in worksheets. ANT moves beyond noting such inequity to analyzing the sociomaterial mechanisms producing it: when assessment formats require written reports, the material affordances of t ext (e.g., argumentation conventions) may systematically privilege students socialized into humanities-oriented discourse. ANT-informed assessment makes this visible as a network effect rather than individual deficit, opening pathways for redesigning assessment arrangements. This provides a method for challenging "disciplinary hegemonies" (Takeuchi et al., 2020) by tracing how they are enacted through specific sociomaterial arrangements in classroom practice. This paper reveals misalignment between interdisciplinary STEAM education's integration-focused goals and outcome-oriented assessment practices. Through a critical narrative review, we identify three major approaches (objective tests, questionnaires, and performance-based assessments) and analyze their limitations for capturing integration dynamics, compared with interdisciplinary Higher education. ANT's translation framework addresses this by making visible the specific moments, negotiations, and reconfigurations through which integration emerges or fails, while revealing power dynamics shaping whose knowledge counts (Rook et al., 2024). These findings suggest equity demands central attention. ANT-informed assessment examines whose actors get enrolled, which voices mobilize networks, and what material arrangements enable or constrain integration, which means moving beyond measuring disparities to understanding mechanisms producing them. Future work should develop and validate ANT-informed analytical frameworks by operationalizing translation stages into observable indicators.  As the hypothetical scenario illustrates, the shift ANT offers is in what assessment makes observable. Rather than evaluating whether integration was achieved, each translation stage directs attention to how integration unfolds or breaks down: from the mom ent learners recognize that a single discipline is insufficient, through their negotiation with new disciplinary resources, to the point where data or problems are reconstituted across disciplinary boundaries, and finally to whose disciplinary voice prevai ls in the integrated product. These are the mechanisms that existing tools structurally miss, and operationalizing them into observable indicators is the central task for future assessment development. Moving forward requires partnership among assessment developers, learning scientists, educators, and students to construct assessment approaches honoring interdisciplinary learning's complexity, creativity, and equity imperatives.
