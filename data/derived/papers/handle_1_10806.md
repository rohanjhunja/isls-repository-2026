# Exploring the Role of Prior Knowledge During Embodied Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this study, we investigate whether effects of performing versus observing actions are moderated by learners’ prior experience in the domain. We investigate this possibility in the context of an embodied learning intervention designed to teach the concept of randomness and the shuffle function in R. Findings reveal a significant main effect of the intervention, with performing hands-on activities producing better learning outcomes than observing hands-on activities or live coding sessions. Notably, self-efficacy emerges as a critical moderator, indicating that learners with low self-efficacy benefited more from performing hands-on activities thandid learners with high self-efficacy, who in fact benefited more from observing hands-on activities. The study underscores the importance of considering prior experience in studies of embodied learning and advocates for further exploration of different measures of prior knowledge to guide future research in the embodied learning field.

## Introduction

Embodied pedagogy has proven to be helpful in a variety of STEM fields (for a review, see Nathan, 2021) but not all bodily experience is the same. For example, pas t research has demonstrated an advantage of perform ing gestures over observing gestures (Goldin-Meadow et al., 2012). However, less is known about why performing actions can be more effective than observing action s. One explanation is that  when a person first performs an action, this experience activates the mirror neuron, which then can be reactivated during simulations of the earlier experience (Barsalou, 2008; Rizzolatti & Craighero,  2004). Following this logic, without the initial activation of the mirror neuron (by performing actions), observing actions would not have the neural foundation to support its comprehension. This absence of the firsthand  experience might make it challenging for people to fully comprehend the underlying intent or significance of  the action as executed by someone else. Given that  humans rely on their knowledge of their own body to unders tand other people’s movements (Grafton, 2009), it makes sense to consider learners’ prior knowledge in the domain when we examine the effect of performing  versus observing actions in learning. Although researchers  sometimes attempt to control for prior knowledge, either by random assignment or by treating it as a covariate, rarely is it a central focus of the research. Below, we briefly review existing evidence supporting an interaction between prior knowledge and types of embodied pedagogies. Evidence for moderating effects of prior knowledge have been most clearly shown in motor domains. For example, what infants perceive when they observe an action (e.g., a hand reaching for an object) depends on whether they themselves have experience performing such an action in the world  (Sommerville et al., 2005). In adults, Calvo-Merino et al. (2005) found that ballet and capoeira dancers showed greater activity in mirror neurons when they saw movements of their own genre in a video.

However, learning abstract concepts is different from the development of motor skills. Yet, studies in STEM domains  that do take prior knowledge into account  suggest that learners with different levels of prio r knowledge might benefit differently from performing versus observing an action. For example, Fyfe et al. (2015) found that children with high prior knowledge did n ot benefit from physically manipulating the balance  scale. A similar finding is observed in Zacharia et al. (2012).

The existing evidence in STEM domains predominantly came from studies involving younger populations, focusing on relatively simple concepts, albeit more abstract than motor skills. T his raises the question: does the significance of prior experience  persist into adulthood, especially in more abstract domains? Some evidence suggests that the role of prior physical experience indeed extends beyond childhood. For instance, Dixon and Bangert (2002) found that 8 -, 12-, and 19-year-olds who correctly used their hands to trace the gears were much more likely to later discover the rule of alteration than were students who did not trace manually, suggesting the importance of physical experience that precedes the development of abstract strategies. The current study In this study, we designed an instructional intervention to teach students the concept of randomness and the shuffle function in R. For the control condition, we presented students with two v ideos designed to mimic a pedagogy known as live coding, in which students observe as the instructor types a nd runs code on a computer. In two experimental conditions, we replaced the first live-coding video with a concrete and embodied version. To investigate the relationship between prior knowledg e and different levels of embodiment,  we designed one experimental group who would only observe the movements (low embodiment) and another group that would, in addition, use manipulatives as they viewed the embo died video to physically perform the actions in the  video (high embodiment). We hypothesize d a main effect of the intervention such that the perform hands-on group would learn better than the observe hands-on group, and that the observe hands-on group would learn better than the control group. Importantly, we hypothesized an interaction between prior knowledge and the levels of embodiment such that participants with low prior knowledge will benefit more from a high level of embodiment.

## Participants

Participants were 72 undergraduate students taking an introductory psychological statistics course at a large public research institution. The gender and ethnic composi tion of the study matched that of the course, which was 80% female and ethnically diverse  (72% non-white). Students who participated in the study received e xtra credit for the course. The study was reviewed and approved by the university’s institutional review board.

## Design and procedure

The study was conducted in a laboratory using Qualtrics, administered through laptops. After signing a conse nt, participants were randomly assigned by Qualtrics into one of the three conditions: control (n = 24), observe handson (n = 24), and perform hands-on (n = 24). Participants first answered two open response questions that assessed what they had learned and retained about the shuffl e function in R thus far in the course: “What is the shuffle function?” and “When would you use the shuffle function?” Participants then watched the two intervention videos. The first video varied by condition, with participants watching someone code using the shuffle function (control) or watching someone shuffle data on pieces of paper (o bserve hands-on). For the perform hands-on group, however, the observe hands-on video was broken into nine clips. After each cli p, perform hands-on participants were asked to reenact the physical movements of the instructor in the video using the pieces of paper and a pair of scissors provided on their table.

The content of the first video was carefully matche d between the live-coding condition and the two embodied conditions. Both versions of the video explained the use of the shuffle function to simulate randomness by showing what a small dataset would look like aft er shuffling based on rows versus columns. The only difference was the language used to describe the process of shuffling. Participants then watched the second video, which was identical across the three conditions. In this video, participants watched a screen recording of an instructor writing and running R code with a realistic, larger dataset. After watching the second video, participants took a posttest.

## Measures

We used two measures to approximate participants’ prior experience: pret est performance and self-efficacy for learning. Participants’ pretest performance was measured using two open response questions: “In your own words, explain what the shuffle () function does.” And “In your own words, explain when you would use the shuffle () function.” Past research has revealed a strong positive correlation between self-efficacy and prior experience (e.g., the number of courses taken)  (Jan, 2015; Shen et al., 2013). Thus, we also measured s elf-efficacy using the Perceived Competence for Learning Scale (Williams & Deci, 1996). Participants answered four items on a sevenpoint Likert scale. We modified the items slightly to fit the context o f learning statistics and programming. The four questions are: “I feel confident in my ability to learn programm ing”, “I am capable of learning the material in this course”, “I am able to achieve my goals in this course”, and “I feel able to meet the challenges of performing well in this course.” Internal consistency for the scale was excellent (Cronbach’s alpha =.91). Participants’ posttest performance was measured using a combination of seven multiple choice questions and 15 open response questions, designed to evaluat e participant’s understanding of the shuffle functi on, the concept of randomness, and how to use the concept o f randomness to make statistical inferences. All questions were graded blind to conditions by two trained coders based on a predetermined rubric, given a maximum of one point. A partial credit of 0.5 was given to responses that had missing pieces or showed minor misunderstandings. Possible scores ranged from 0 to 2 for the pretest and 0 to 22 for the posttest.

## Discussion

The findings revealed that participants in the perform h ands-on group performed better on a posttest than participants in the observe hands-on group and the control group. Pretest performance  did not significantly moderate the effect of condition  but self-efficacy significantly did. The observed significant main effect of condition is in alignment with find ings that engaging in or even just observing bodily actions h elps learning (for a review see Nathan et al., 2021). It is possible, however, that students who were not asked to perform the handson activities themselves simply paid less attention. However, all students went through the study with the presence of an experimenter in the room, which may have exerted some pressure on the students to pay attention. One novel design of this study was to measure prior experience and consider it as a moderating factor of embodiment effects. Simultaneously, one challenge of the current stud y was to determine what is the relevant prior experience. We used two ways to measure it: what students know about the specific concept covered in the intervention and self-efficacy, which has been found to correlate with br oader measures of prior experience. Interestingly, only self-efficacy significantly moderated the effect of condition. Although exploratory, this finding demonstrates a potential interaction between prior knowledge and the type of embodied intervention. Future studies of embodied learning  should not only consider learners’ prior experience in the domain but also investigate different ways of measuring pr ior experience. Different forms of prior knowledge may be relevant for embodied learning, which poses an open challenge for the field of embodied learning research.
