# Evaluating Student Agency in AI-Generated Lesson Plans

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Generative AI tools are increasingly deployed in education to support practices such as lesson planning, while little attention has been given to the alignment between these tools and core values in education. This poster reports on the extent of student agency reflected in AI-generated lesson plans across four conditions. The findings reveal AI’s underlying assumptions and bias against student agency and demonstrate the possibility of strengthening student agency in lesson plan generation.

## Introduction

A lesson plan is a foundational teaching tool outlini ng learning objectives, activities, materials, and assessments for specific topics. Recently, commercial generative AI (GenAI) tools like MagicSchool and School AI, powered by large language models (LLMs), have demonstrated significant potential to assist educators by generating lesson plans in seconds (Cameron & Mesiti, 2024; Karpouzis  et al., 2024). However, research has cautioned aga inst reliance on these AI-powered generation tools due to current limitations including insufficient detail they provide on teaching, cultural misalignment, and lack of appetite for addressing diverse student needs (Cameron & Mesiti, 2024; Karpouzis et al., 2024; Tao et al., 2024).

The interest in fostering student agency is rooted in social constructivist perspectives and further sharpened in a more recent “socio-political turn” in the learning sciences. Indeed, s tudent agency is a vital component in developing lifelong learners (OECD, 20 19) and an important facet of AI competence for stu dents (UNESCO, 2024). However, limited research has explo red how student agency is supported or hindered in the emerging practice of AI-powered lesson planning. This poster bridges this g ap by reporting on the extent of student agency reflected in AI-generated lesson plans across four conditions, whic h included two commercially available off-the-shelf AI tools and two workflows we created. Three-dimensional student agency framework Student agency is defined as learners’ capacity to set goals, reflect on their learning, and take purposef ul action toward their learning and growth (Bandura, 1989; OE CD, 2019). Departing from Vaughn’s (2020) work, we conducted a literature review (Aldi et al., 2018; Bandura, 1989; Klem enčič, 2014; Moses et al., 2020; Vaughn, 2018; Vygotsky, 1978) and created a three-dimensional framework. The dispositional dimension concentrates on self-perception, intentionality, and a sense of purpose.  It reflects students’ understanding of themselves as active participants in learning, capable of setting personal goals and taking purposeful action. Key constructs under this dimension include engaging with authentic problems and encouraging goal-directed behavior. The motivational dimension deals with beliefs and values that drive students to engage and pursue their goals. It inclu des internal factors that inspire persistence, intentional decision-making, and consistent action toward learning objectives. Key constructs include the capacity to make choices aligned with personal values, take responsible actions when facing challenges, demonstrate resilience in overcoming ob stacles, and self-efficacy. The positional dimension highlights how students experience agency within social and situational contexts. This dimension emphasizes the role of peer relationships, classroom culture, and social interaction in fostering collaboration, nego tiation, and expression of agency. It can be nurtured by creating op portunities for social learning and cultivating an environment where students fe el their voices matter. By articulating these three  dimensions and relevant eleven constructs, in total, this framework offers practic al strategies for supporting student agency and gui des the evaluation of learning tools and environments in the learning sciences.

## Method

To examine the extent to which student agency is re flected in AI-generated lesson plans, we selected two widely recognized educational platforms, MagicSchool and S chool AI, and compared them against two lesson plan generators we developed: one with “Vanill a” out-of-the-box AI and the other with custom AI. The Vanilla AI generator employed OpenAI’s GPT-4 language model and followed a standard workflow w ith basic prompts (max_tokens=500, temperature=0.7). The prompt directs the model as follows: “You are a lesson plan generation assistant. A teacher has provided the following det ails for a lesson: Grade, Subject, Topic, Learning Objectives. Please generate a detailed lesson plan.” In contrast, the Custom AI prompt expanded upon the Vanilla version by embedding an 11-element scaffo ld grounded in our three-dimensional student agency framework. To ensure consistency across AI conditions, we selected Grade  8 math, science, and history as focus subjects, us ing topics and objectives from the Common Core State Standards (CCSS), Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS), and New York State K-8 Social Studies Framework to generate standards-aligned lesson plans. For each subject, two topics were randomly selected from these standards.  To address the non-deterministic nature of LLMs, which may produce varied responses to the same prompt, we gen erated five lesson plans per topic per AI condition  to ensure consistency and mitigate significant discrepancies. In total, 120 lesson plans were generated and collected for further content analysis.

Based on the student agency framework, we developed  a coding scheme corresponding to these three dimensions and eleven constructs. Each lesson plan was evaluated against the definitions of these cons tructs to assess the extent of student agency. A score of “1”  was assigned if a construct was present in a lesso n plan, and “0” if it was absent. This allowed us to create an aggregate score of each construct to measure the extent to which the construct is reflected in lesson plans generated by a specific AI tool. Given that we generated 30 lesson plans using each AI tool, a tool can score a maximum of 3 0 on each construct of student agency. Two authors independently coded the lesson plans and compared r esults to ensure consistency. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, enhancing the reliability of the coding process.

## Findings

The analysis revealed two primary findings: student agency in AI-generated lesson plans and enhancing student agency through prompt design. Within the first finding, three key themes emerged. (a) A teacher-centric tendency was found across most lesson plans generated by MagicSchool, School AI, and Vanilla AI. These plans frequently positioned teachers as the central authority, direc ting activi ties and limiting students’ opportunities to make choices or take initiative. (b) An unbalanced presence of student agency constructs was observed. While constructs like interaction, opportunities to share ideas, and  problem-solving were frequently represented in AI-generated lesson plans, constructs such as goal-directed behavior, initiative, and shared authority  are absent. These lesson plans consistently score low and include only half or fewer of the eleven constructs, indicating limit ed opportunities to foster student agency. Additionall y, no significant differences were found between ST EM and social studies subjects. (c) Constructs of student agency appear in patterns. Authentic problems are o ften introduced at the start of the course. Interaction and collaboration are primarily facilitated through  group discussions. Self-efficacy is addressed through journal reflection. In contrast, our custom AI generator scores significantly higher in different dimensions of student agency. The lesson plans generated using custom AI promote self and peer assessment, enabling students to take on diverse roles. They also offer more resources and choices, allowing students to select presentation formats and research topics, fostering exploration and initiati ve. Additionally, the plans prioritize setting personal  learning goals and self-reflection, moving beyond traditional exit tickets focused solely on domain knowledge. Instead of always starting with real-world scenarios, the plans connect to students’ com munities. These enhancements provide greater opportunities for students to direct their learning and exercise their own agency.

## Conclusions

While AI has the potential to streamline lesson pla nning and reduce educators’ workload (Fan et al., 2 024; Karpouzis et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024), this study reveals significant limitations of AI-generated lesson plans in fostering student agency. We argue that educator s need to engage with AI tools mindfully, and AI to ol developers need to work with educators and learning scientists to bake core education values in these tools. Future research needs to investigate ways to diversify per spectives in AI tools and expand this investigation to other areas such as cultural relevance.
