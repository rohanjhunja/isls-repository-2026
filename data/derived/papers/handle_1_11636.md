# Designing Measurement for Analytical Thinking in Discerning Fake News in Social Media Context

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The study describes the design and evaluation of a measurement for analytical thinking to judge news credibility in simulated social media contexts. With responses from 94 university students, we generated Kernel density estimation plots and regression models to evaluate the construct validity of the instrument. We conclude by discussing further instrument revisions and the complexity of analytical thinking in social media contexts.

## Introduction

In the information-driven society, students’ ability to c ritically judge news credibility becomes increasingly important. However, measurements for this ability s hould go beyond merely measuring judgment accuracy,  as seen in previous studies (e.g., Maertens et al., 2024), but also assess the thinking process behind the judgmen ts. A typical classification of thinking processes distinguishes between analytical thinking and intuitive thinking. The former, characterized as a slow, deliberative, and conscious thinking style, is regarded as essential for successfully identifying fake news (Bronstein et al., 2019), in contrast to the latter, which is fast and relies on intuition and gut feelings (Pennycook et al., 2015).  Informed by the evidence-centered design (ECD)  (Mislevy et al., 2003), we developed a measurement and tried to validate our inference from observable  variables (i.e., response accuracy and time) to the latent construct (i.e., the use of analytical thinking) with this measurement. Measurement design and evaluation In our measurement, students evaluate news credibility when mixed information exists. The measurement includes eight four-point Likert-scale items. As shown in Figure 1, each item includes three components: (a) one simulated news post to be judged by its credibility, (b) a scale ranging from 'Not Credible at All' to 'Very  Credible,' along with information on how popular each option is, and (c) an explanation for the most popular answer. The popularity of options and explanations supports students to make the correct judgments in some items while they distract them from the correct answer in other items, as indicated in the four conditions in Figure 1. We tried to add “time spent” to complement inferences from observables to the latent construct (i.e., the use of analytical thinking) per Pennycook et al. (2016). To explore whether the inference is reasonable, we analyzed data with the following questions: (1) Can time spent on items be the observable variable that distinguishes students using analytical thinking versus intuitive thinking  in the four conditions?  (2) In what ways  does time spent on items correlate with the accuracy of students’ judgments on news credibility in the four conditions?

## Methods

The instrument was delivered via Qualtrics to 94 university students. Qualtrics automatically recorded the time each student spent on completing each item. Student s’ responses were scored dichotomously (i.e., correct versus incorrect). A student’s final accuracy score on the measurement  is the proportion of the correct answers over a total of eight items. Kernel density estimation (KDE) plots were generated to explore the distribution of students’ time spent on items, and Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression w as conducted to investigate the relationship between time spent and judgment accuracy.

## Results

Overall, students spent an average of 38.89 seconds (SD = 38.83; N = 94) on each item. The results of the KruskalWallis H test indicate that the distributions of students’ time spent significa ntly differed across the four types of questions, with H (3) = 38.07, p <.001. The KDE plots of log-transformed time spent for each type of question are shown in Figure 2.

Figure 2 Distribution of Log-Transformed Time Spent by Question Type The OLS regression analysis was c onducted to model the relationship  between log-transformed time spent (predictor variable) and students’ judgment accurac y (outcome variable). T here is a significantly positive relationship (t = 2.614, p =.010) between the two variables. Separate OLS regression analyses were conducted to explore this relationship in different conditions. It was found that, among the four types of questi ons, significant relationships between the log-transformed time spent and judgment accuracy can only be found in responses to Type 2 (t = 2.089, p = 0.039) and Type 4 questions (t = -2.023, p = 0.046). Interestingly, in Type 2 responses, increased time spent correlates with higher accurac y, while in Type 4 questions, more time spent is as sociated with lower accuracy.

## Discussion

Regarding the first question, the KDE plots suggest that time spent candifferentiate populations, although its ability to distinguish populations is dependent on the conditions in which students make judgments. The bimodal distributions in Type 2 and Type 4 plot s indicate that some students may spend some time on the discrepancy between the news credibility level recommended by the popular answer and their own perceptions, while other students may overlook it. If this is the case, using the popular answer as a distractor could be an effective design feature for measuring different thinking strategies. Regarding the second question, the results of OLS r egression analyses suggest that the two populations identified in Type 2 and Type 4 plots may employ different thi nking strategies as log-transformed time spent is found to be significantly related to judgment accuracy in these two question types. This implies that the reasoning inference from time spent to jud gment accuracy and to the use of analytical thinking may be reasonable in the two conditions. This highlights the importance of the design of conditions where students make judgments. The negative correlation between the two variables r evealed in the regression model for Type 4 questions may suggest t hat spending too much time might lead to overanalyz ing or second-guessing themselves, causing confusion and leading to errors in judgment. This challenges the traditional view that a longer time implies analytical thinking which leads to improved judgements. However, it remains unclear whether the populations differing in time s pent also differ in thinking strategies, raising do ubts about using time spent as an observable for measuring analytical thinking. Further steps To address the alternative explanations regarding t ime spent and analytical thinking in judging news c redibility, qualitative data through interviews or think-aloud may provide some insights into students’ thinking proce sses (e.g., how and why they spent time on the items). These further attempts will help us explore cognitive strategies used to judge credibility, guiding revisions to measurements and informing the role of analytical thinking in social media contexts.
