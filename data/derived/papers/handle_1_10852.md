# Navigating Cognitive Engagement in AI-Enhanced Education: Lexical Diversity and Open-Ended Inquiry in Journalism Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates the interplay between lexical diversity and cognitive engagement in an AI-enhanced journalism course for high school students. Through an explanatory sequential mixed methods design, we categorized students into three clusters based on their Measure of Textual Lexical Diversity (MTLD), cognitive engagement scores, and completion rates. Our findings reveal a nuanced relationship between lexical diversity and cognitive engagement. Notably, students in Cluster 0, characterized by lower lexical diversity, displayed predominantly passive engagement, suggesting a potential area for pedagogical intervention. In contrast, Cluster 2 students demonstrated both high lexical diversity and active cognitive engagement, highlighting a more desirable educational outcome. Further qualitative analysis of student responses illuminated the qualitative differences in engagement across clusters, challenging the assumption that lexical diversity alone can predict the depth of cognitive engagement. This research emphasizes the complexity of fostering meaningful engagement in technology-rich educational settings and points towards the need for strategies that not only enhance lexical diversity but also actively promote deeper cognitive engagement.

## Introduction

Recent advancements in big data and Artificial Inte lligence (AI), particularly machine learning (ML), are reshaping various professional fields, including jo urnalism (Hendler & Mulvehill, 2016; Broussard et a l., 2019). AI is redefining journalism by fostering a hybrid e nvironment where human journalists and machine lear ning algorithms collaborate, emphasizing the need for in terdisciplinary teams and ethical considerations to  ensure accountability and enhance the integrity of news pr oduction. This integratio n challenges traditional practices, requiring a balanced co-evolution of journalistic values and technological innovation (Gutierrez Lopez et al., 2023). The integration of AI in educational settings, espe cially in subjects like journalism, is becoming increasingly vital for both technical skill development and fostering cognitive engagement among learners (Zhang & Aslan, 2021). Cognitive Engagement (CE) is  a psychological process involving the attention, in terest, investment, and effort students expend in learning. At the highest CE mode, students operate at a heightened level of engagement compared to passive information absorption (Deslauriers et al., 2019). One effective way to increase CE is through open-ended questions (Anderson et al., 2001). These questions are vital in educational discourse as they encourage active participation and foster deeper understanding among learners (Smith & Higgins, 2006). Addi tionally, open-ended questions not only measure observable aspects of student CE but also produce diverse answ ers and require abstract language skills. Among lin guistic tools, lexical diversity (LD) has gained prominence  as a measure of vocabulary richness  and breadth in text, indicating deeper engagement with subject matter (M cCarthy & Jarvis, 2007; McNamara et al., 2010). Des pite the value of open-ended questions, their effective utilization and th e understanding of CE within these questions by teachers is limited (Lee & Kinzie, 2012).

This study proposes a novel method to assess cognitive engagement through lexical diversity in students' open-ended responses, grounded in language acquisition a nd cognitive development theories. It aims to uncov er how varied linguistic expressions correla te with levels of engagement in a technology-rich educational environment. Specifically, this research seeks to u nderstand the cognitive mechanisms driving learning  in such environments and contribute to learning sciences by  providing empirical insights that could reshape pe dagogical strategies. To this end, the study is guided by the  following questions: RQ1) What are the predominant characteristics or behaviors observed in clusters o f students with high lexical diversity but varying levels of cognitive engagement? RQ2) What are the key charact eristics and patterns observed in clusters of stude nts with varying levels of lexical diversity and cognitive e ngagement? and RQ3) How do lexical diversity, total  word count, and demographic factors predict students' levels of cognitive engagement?

## Theoretical background

Revised Bloom's taxonomy Our research heavily leans on the revised Bloom's Taxonomy by Anderson et al. (2001), a fundamental model for understanding cognitive processes in learning. This taxonomy delineates six levels of cognitive learning, ranging from basic information recall (' Remembering') to the application of higher-order thinking skills like 'Evaluation' and 'Creation'. Such a structured framework enables educators to design their teaching strategies and questions to target specific cognitive domains, thus guiding lea rners towards comprehensive understanding and sophi sticated critical thinking. Krathwohl (2002) emphasized its utility in educational settings for fostering deepe r cognitive engagement. Interestingly, studies in various subje cts, including English and Science, ha ve utilized Bloom's taxonomy to categorize and improve the cognitive levels of questions posed in classrooms. However, as Ng et al.

(2021) identified, there is an existing research ga p in applying this taxonomy to foster higher-order cognitive

skills, part icularly in AI literacy learning, where emphasis is  often on lower levels like 'Understanding' and 'Applying'.

ICAP model Next, we incorporate the ICAP model, conceptualized  by Chi and Wylie (2014), which classifies learner engagement into four distinct modes: Passive, Activ e, Constructive, and Interactive. Passive engagemen t, typically involving simple information recall, de notes minimal cognitive involvement, whereas Active engagement takes this a step further by integrating  new information within familiar contexts. Construc tive engagement goes beyond this, encouraging learners t o generate new ideas. The most involved, Inter active engagement, necessitates a dynamic interaction amon g learners, fostering an environment of collaborati ve learning. This model is pivotal for understanding t he various depths of cognitive involvement in learn ing processes. For example, research by Mo rris and Chi (2020) demonstrated that after profess ional development interventions, teachers shifted their questioning m ethods from passive to more constructive and intera ctive formats, significantly enhancing the quality of cla ssroom interactions. Further studies, like those by Wang et al.

(2016) and Yogev et al. (2018), have utilized the ICAP model to assess higher-order thinking in online educational

settings, such as MOOC discussions and virtual course student comments. In the specific context of AI education, the ICAP framework has been instrumental in guiding learners from passive information absorption to interactive and engaged learning modes, as seen in pharmacology  education, where the application of ICAP led to mo re interactive and constructive pedagogies, significantly enhancing both student engagement and learning outcomes (Quesnelle et al., 2021).

Lexical Diversity (LD) Lastly, our study explores the concept of LD, as de fined by Ransdell and Wengelin (2003). LD, which me asures the variety and richness of vocabulary used in text, is a vital indicator of language proficiency and the degree of engagement with the subject mat ter. This quantitative assessment allows for a deta iled examination of students' language skills and depth of content engagement. Ha mpson & McKinley (2023) suggest that LD can provide insights into how effectively students are interact ing with and comprehe nding the material. Studies like that of Wood et al. (2019) have shown that LD, as reflected by metrics such as the Number of Different Words (NDW), increases with academic progression, indicating an evolution in linguistic richness and sophistication. However, the relationship between LD and cognitive engagemen t is multifaceted. For instance, Farrow et al. (202 2) found in online course discussions that while longer messages with a greater word count correlated with higher cognitive quality, a lower level o f lexical diversity, as indicated by measures like the Type-Token Ratio (TTR), was surprisingly linked to increased cognitive presence. This suggests that the role of LD in educational settings is complex and merits further exploration, particularly in understanding its impact on cognitive engagement.

## Participants and context

The study was conducted in a US High School Journal ism Course whose teacher attended an AI professiona l development workshop prior to implementing the Stor yQ AI Curriculum for ELA. StoryQ is an online tool leveraging AI and machine learning designed for stu dents in grades 6-12. It enables them to explore machine learning concepts and interact with unstructured text data without the need for coding (Chao et al., 2022). Twentyeight learners with varying backgrounds per gender,  grade level and race/ethnicity (i.e., 17 female, seven male and four unknown; thirteen 12th-grade students, eight 11th-grade students, and two 10th-grade students with 43% Black/African, 17% Hispanic/Latinx, 18% White/Cauca sian, 4% other and 4% didn’t answer). The curriculu m did not require prior coding knowledge and included  eight distinct modules. The main study included a pre-post survey and each module included hands-on activities, with open ended and closed ended questions to assess their progression.

Our study focused on three modules that encapsulate d ML practices: Sentiment Analysis, Features and Models, and All Words. Within the “Sentiment Analys is” module learners were asked to evaluate negative  or positive reviews with questions like “What feature s do you think are indicators of negative reviews?”  In the "Features and Models" module, learners explored feature engineering and answered questions like how the feature "great" influenced a model. In “All Words as Features” questions probe learners to analyze why the model learned a particular “feature” and its corresponding “weight.”

## Data collection and analysis

To address the research questions, we reviewed stud ents' open ended questions ((N=30 per student, N= 840 per 28 students) from each of the three modules to iden tify cognitive engagement levels within their writi ng. These segments of data were then extract ed to a spreadsheet and analyzed for instances that  demonstrated the highest level of cognitive engagement using an internally d eveloped coding manual. The coding manual, which was derived from two theoretical frameworks, ICAP (Chi & Wylie, 2014) and the revised Bloom’s taxonomy (Anderson et al., 2001) were adapted to include thr ee cognitive modes: Passive (0), Active (1), and Co nstructive

(2) (Chi & Wylie, 2014) as seen in Table 1. The rev ised Bloom’s indicator Transfer informs the Constructive

mode, revised Bloom’s Understand and Apply indicator informs the Active mode, and finally, the revised Bloom's indicators Recall and Store informs the Passive mode within our adopted CAP model. To label the CE to the selfreported answers, two doctoral students revie wed the adapted cognitive engagement coding scheme and after reviewing disagreements and independently coded 10 % of the self-written responses the raters yielded a alpha score of.84 which is an acceptable level of agreement (Cohen, 1968).  The most experienced rater coded the rest of the self-reported answers.

The study drew on an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design (Creswell & Clark, 2017) using quantitative and qualitative analysis. Initiating a  qualitative relationship between the variety of vo cabulary used by students (TNW) and their CE and then examines the LD indices and CE scores of students in different clusters. Since our text answers are short, we chose to use measure of textual lexical diversity (MTLD). MTLD quantifies the lexical richness of a text through the analysis  of consecutive word sequ ences, aiming for a specific TTR threshold, typically.72 for English texts (McCarth y & Jarvis, 2010). This metric averages the lengths  of contiguous segments of text where the TTR remains above the predetermined threshold. In practice, the algorithm assesses each word's contribution to the TTR from the be ginning of the text, recalculating as each new word  is added. Following this qualitative groundwork, the r esearch transitioned into a quantitative phase, emp loying statistical tools such as MTLD, TNW. Addi tionally, since the assumption of equal variances w as not met we performed a Welch’s ANOVA, and multinomial logistic  regression. These measures were chosen to systematically examine and corroborate the qualitative findings, providing a robust framework for understanding the dynamics between students' language use and engagement levels. Data processing We chose to perform our analysis with Python using Google Colab. While following the learning analytic s workflow, the first thing we did was wrangle our da ta which includes a cleaning phase (Krumm et al., 2 018). To do this we used several packages like Pandas for data analysis, Textblob for processing text ual data and re for regular expression. For missing data values we impu ted unknown for categorical (race and gender) and-999 for numerical (grade) since we were unable to know.  Then we paid particular attention to refining the textual content within the students self-reported answers column. Initially, we tackled the prevalent issue of concatenated words. This was accomplished through the application of pa ttern matching techniques, designed to identify and  rectify instances where words were incorrectly merged into lengthy strings. The core of this process involved the detection and subsequent separation of these strings, primarily focusing on splitting patterns such as "rn" and "n" which were frequently fo und in our dataset. Following, we implemented a spe ll-checking procedure. For this purpose, we employed TextBlob, a versatile library,  which allowed us to systematically identify and co rrect spelling mistakes present in the students' responses. This two-step approach significantly improved the readability and accuracy of the data, setting a robust foundation for Lexical diversity analyses. The MTLD varied from 1.0 to 286.72, indicating a breadth of vocabulary usage. Additio nally, the TNW in responses spanned from 1 to 83 words (see Table 2). In this analysis, we applied a cluster analysis technique, categorizing students into groups based on their MTLD, CE scores and completion rates. The results from the Elbow Method and Silhouette Scores suggested an optimal number of clusters of three. We c hose a K-means analysis. a wide range of completion rates (ranging from 78.3 % to 90.3%, with one outlier at 31.3%), with the ma jority displaying high engagement. This suggests that whil e lexical diversity may be limited, the overall eng agement with the material is generally high.

Cluster 1 consists of a unique group of nine studen ts with moderate levels of passive (average = 13), active engagement (average = 8) with a small amount  of constructive engagement (average = 1) and moder ate MTLD score of 39.88 (SD =8.16). This cluster dem onstrates moderate lexical diversity and a broader spectrum of completion rates (ranging from 35.9% to 94.5%). The variability in completion rates within this clu ster may reflect diverse approaches to learning and interaction with the course content. Cluster 2 consists of 11 unique students with the l owest passive engagement (average = 7) but highest active (average = 16) and highest constructive engagement (average = 3), indicating a highly engaged group. The MTLD score of 47.99 (SD = 9.49) is evidence of high er LD in this group. Completion rates in this group  are predominantly high (often exceeding 80%), highlight ing a strong correlation between active engagement,  high lexical diversity, and course completion.

In examining the demographic characteristics of the  clusters we observed a diverse mix of students, closely mirroring the class's demographic diversity. This similarity across clusters suggests that the clusters, while distinct in their CE and LD, do not exhibit signifi cant demographic segregation. Therefore, the cluste ring primarily provides insights into variations in cogn itive engagement and lexical diversity rather than highlighting demographic disparities.

RQ3: How do lexical diversity, total word count, and de mographic factors predict students' levels of cognitive engagement?

A Welch ANOVA was conducted to assess the impact of  CE on LD (MTLD). Levene's test confirmed the assumption of equal variances across groups was met, F(2, 27) = 511.20, p <.001. The ANOVA analysis highlighted significant differences in MTLD scores by CE levels, F(2, 108.28) = 75.65, p <.001, η² = 0.18, indicating a substantial part of MTLD variability is explained by engagement. Post hoc comparisons using Games-Howell tests showed that students with the lowest e ngagement (Mean = 21.34) had significantly lower MTLD scores compared to those with Active (Mean = 53.67, p <.0 01, Hedges' g = -0.93) and high engagement (Mean = 54.23, p <.001, Hedges' g = -1.12). No significant difference was found between Active and Constructive engagement levels (p =.995) (see Figure 1). The model significantly improved the fit over the intercept-only model, χ²(10, N = 684) = 312.66, p <.001, indicating that the pre dictors collectively contributed to predicting cognitive levels.

For TNW, the odds of being in the Active level vers us Passive increased by a factor of 1.20 (SE = 0.018, z = 10.16, p <.001) for each additional wor d, and the odds of being in the Constructive level versus Passive increased by a factor of 1.30 (SE = 0.022, z = 11.73, p <.001) for each additional word, indi cating a strong and significant association between the total number of words and higher cognitive levels. This means that each additional word is associated with a 20% increase in the odds of being categorized as "Active" rather than "Passive" and a 30% increase in the odds of be ing categorized as "Constructive" rather than "Pass ive". This is consistent with Farrow et al.  (2022) findings. Regarding LD, the coefficient for MTLD when predict ing Active versus Passive levels was 0.0063 (SE = 0.0039, z = 1.64, p =.101), and for Constructive versus Passive levels, it was 0.0043 (SE = 0.0064, z = 0.67, p =.505). These results suggest that while t here is a trend towards higher LD being associated with higher cognitive levels, this was not statistically significant at the conventional alpha level of.05. The residual deviance of the model was 755.04, with  an AIC of 775.04, suggesting a good fit of the model to the data.

## Discussion and implications

The study's findings dovetail with the frameworks of the Revised Bloom's Taxonomy and the ICAP model, which delineate cognitive processes and engagement levels (Anderson et al., 2001; Chi & Wylie, 2014). Jon from Cluster 0, characterized by high passive en gagement and low LD, and Adama from Cluster 1, exhi biting moderate LD and cognitive engagement, demonstrate the nuanced i nterplay between lexical usage and cognitive engage ment. Jon's straightforward listing of negative descripto rs, although indicative of recognizing words linked  tonegative sentiments, suggests a more passive form of engagement, aligning with the 'Passive' mode of the ICAP model. In contrast, Adama's analytical approach in understand ing classification models through keyword analysis suggests a 'Active' mode of engagement, where information is  manipulated but not necessarily integrated with pr ior knowledge.

Jordan, representing Cluster 2, showcases high LD and higher active and constructive engagement levels. Her ability to not only identify key phrases but al so infer their implications on sentiment analysis m irrors the 'Constructive' mode of the ICAP model, where learners integrate new information to generate novel insights. This capacity for deeper analysis and application of knowledge in new contexts also resonates with the 'Transfer' level of the Revised Bloom's Taxonomy (Anderson et al., 2 001), underlining a sophisticated level of CE that extends beyond mere lexical variety.

The study challenges the conventional emphasis on L D as a primary indicator of language proficiency and engagement (McCarthy & Jarvis, 2007; McNamara et al., 2017), suggesting instead that the depth of CE plays a pivotal role. While a trend towards higher LD being associated with higher cognitive levels was observed, it did not reach statistical significance, highlighting LD's limited predictive power within this study's scope. This insight is in harmony with Farrow et al. (2022), who posited that the cognitive quality of messages is not merely a function of their length or lexical diversity but also of the depth and complexity of thought they reflect. This research underscores the importance of fostering not only lexical breadth in educational settings but also deep, critical thinking and comprehensive unde rstanding, as advocated by the ICAP model. It sugge sts a reevaluation of engagement strategies in technology-rich learning environments, emphasizing the cultiva tion of both expansive vocabulary and profound cognitive en gagement. This approach will ensure that students a re not only linguistically proficient but also cognitively  engaged, capable of applying their knowledge in di verse and complex situations, crucial for navigating today's data-driven world. Future research should focus on expanding the scope  of this study to different educational contexts and age groups, to better understand the relationship b etween LD and CE across a broader spectrum of learners. Investigating how different instructional strategies impact these variables could also provide valuable insights for educators. Longitudinal studies would be particular ly useful in assessing the long-term effects of integrating AI and technology in education, while expanding the re search to include diverse subject areas would help in identifying subject-specific patterns in cognitive engagement. Finally, the findings of this study, while insightf ul, are limited by factors such as the sample size and demographics, which may affect their generalizability to other educational settings or age groups. The sensitivity to text length is a methodological constraint that needs consideration in future resea rch. Additionally, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to  infer causality or long-term effects. Further studies in diverse technological and educational contexts would be beneficial to validate and expand upon these findings.
