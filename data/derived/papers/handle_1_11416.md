# Scaffolding Third Graders’ Computational Thinking in an Artificial Intelligence Course: Concepts, Practice, and Perspectives

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The effect of scaffolding type and timing on computational thinking (CT) for elementary school students in artificial intelligence (AI) learning is unknown. To address this research gap, a 2×2 factorial experiment was conducted in this study to examine the effect of scaffolding type (conceptual versus problem-solving) and timing (immediate versus delayed) on CT concepts, practices, and perspectives. A total of 136 third graders from four classes were assigned to four experimental conditions. The results, as measured by knowledge tests, showed that problem-solving scaffolding and immediate scaffolding were more effective in developing students’ CT concepts. Lag sequential analysis indicated that students guided by problem-solving scaffolding and delayed scaffolding developed more complete problem-solving paths and achieved higher levels of CT practice. Additionally, an analysis of students’ learning reflections revealed that most students could explain the functions of machine learning techniques. By highlighting effective scaffolding methods, this study advances CT education.

## Introduction

Computational thinking (CT) is recognized as an essential competency for children in the 21st century, serving as a general problem-solving method in various domains (Sun et al., 2024). Educators and scholars increasingly recommend integrating artificial intelligence (AI) knowledge and CT into K –12 education. However, this integration presents considerable challenges, espec ially for elementary-aged children (Tedre et al., 2021). Scaffolding as a teaching strategy has been shown to be effective in developing CT in elementary school students (Wang et al., 202 4). Scaffolding type and timing are key factors that  influence its effectiveness, but a notable research gap is the combined effects of scaffolding  types and timing on different CT dimensions.  This study developed this line of research and developed an AI course featuring conceptual scaffolding and problem-solving scaffolding and conducted a 6-week quasi-experiment using the machine learning (ML) unit of the course. Students from four intact classes partici pated in this study. Data resources included studen ts’ pre-and post-knowledge tests, final worksheets, and learning reflections. Statistical analysis and lag sequential analysis (LSA) were used to compare the effects of scaffolding typ e and timing on various dimensions of CT. Three research questions (RQs) were addressed:

• RQ1: What are the effects of scaffolding type (conc eptual scaffolding VS problem-solving scaffolding)

and timing (delayed scaffolding VS immediate scaffolding) on third graders’ CT concepts?

• RQ2: What are the effects of scaffolding type and timing on third graders’ CT practice?

• RQ3: What are the effects of scaffolding type and timing on third graders’ CT perspective?

## Research contexts and participants

This study was conducted on 136 third graders at an  elementary school in central China. The 6-week AI course (with weekly 45-minute sessions) focused on ML. A 2×2 factorial design was used: the first and seco nd factors were scaffolding type  and scaffolding timing, respectively. Four parallel  classes were randomly assigned to the following conditions: delayed conceptual scaffolding (C1, n=34), immediate conceptual scaffolding (C2, n=32), immediate problem-solving scaffolding (C3,  n=33) and delayed problem-solving scaffolding (C4, n=37). The same teacher taught under all four conditions.

Instructional design for scaffolding students’ CT development The participants worked on two tasks: creating solu tions to help the machine distinguish between (1) f ish and trash and (2) marine life and trash. The solution to Task 2 is an iteration of that for Task 1. The participants were guided to use iteration as an element of CT practice by redesigning the machine. Each learning task lasted 3 weeks and consisted of an online AI teaching tool (AI for  Oceans) experience and five learning activities: i dentify the problem; gather, represent, and analyze the data; g enerate, select, and plan solutions; and implement, assess, and improve solutions. These learning activities were d esigned using the IGGIA (I: identify the problem; G: gather, represent and analyze the data; G: generate, select and plan solutions; I: implement solutions; A: assess solutions and continue for improvement) framework proposed by Kalelioglu et al. (2016), which combines CT and problemsolving. To aid their learning activities, C1 and C 2 were given conceptual scaffolding, whereas C3 and C4 were given problem-solving scaffolding. Both scaffolds were presented in the form of mind maps and incorporated elements of CT. Figures 1 present ed the conceptual scaffolding  and problem-solving scaffolding provided to students by Activity 3 (Generate, select, and plan solutions). In addition to different scaffolding types, different scaffolding timings were used. The participants in C1 and C4 experienced the online AI teaching tool befo re engaging in scaffold-supported learning activities. The participants in C2 and C3 first participated in sca ffold-supported learning activities and then experienced the online AI teaching tool.

## Results

Effects of scaffolding type and timing on CT concepts The ANOVA results  showed no significant difference in the pre-knowledge test scores for the four conditions. Therefore, prior knowledge before the start of the experiment did not differ significantly for the four experimental conditions. Post-knowledge test scores for the four groups were, in descending orde r, C3 (M = 86.42), C4 (M = 83.46), C1 (M = 79.65), and C2 (M = 77.81).  We applied ANOVA to determine the main effect of sc affolding type and scaffolding timing, and their interaction effects on the post-knowledge test. The ANOVA results revealed no significant main effect of scaffolding type (F (1, 132) =.463, p =.497 >.05, η2 =.003) and scaffolding timing (F (1, 132) =.674, p =.413 >.05, η2 =.005) on the post-knowledge test scores. The results also showed no interaction effects between the two factors (scaffolding type and timing) on the post-knowledge test (F (1, 132) =.070, p =.792 >.05, η2 =.001).

## Discussion

Effects of scaffolding type and timing on CT concepts Scaffolding type did not significantly affect the s tudents’ knowledge test scores, but it affected how  they understood and applied different concepts. This ali gns with Wang et al. (202 4), who found that problem-solving scaffolding in elementary school students may better activate p rior cognitive structures, aiding in the application and transfer of new knowledge. Scaffolding timing a lso did not significantly affect the students’ know ledge test scores. However, those who received immediate scored higher and showed a better understanding of more complex concepts, similar to findings from other st udies (Barzilai & Blau, 2014; Sharma & Hannafin, 20 07). Providing scaffolding before students engage with the AI teaching tool may help them form connections between concepts and interactive resources.

Effects of scaffolding type and timing on CT practice The students using PS engaged more in CT practices, developing a complete problem-solving path. This aligns with Zhou et al. (2023), confirming that scaffolding focused on problem-solving processes enhances CT skills not only in college students but also in  elementary students. Kim et al. (2018) emphasized t he need to provide scaffolding that informs students about critical issues and effective strategies during problem-solving, rather than providing just conceptual scaffolding. With delayed scaffolding, the students solved problems in a logical order, developing a complete CT practice transition path. This is consistent with previous findings (e.g., Chevalier et al., 2022), which have suggested that delayed scaffolding  benefits complex problem-solving. This effect may be explained by the trial-and-error strategy, where learners form solutions throu gh multiple attempts (Weintrop & Wilensky, 2015).

Effects of scaffolding type and timing on CT perspective Most students were able to articulate the functional ity and usefulness of ML techniques. These findings  support the idea that scaffolding helps learners clarify th e learning topic (Wang et al., 2024) and cultivate CT (Chevalier et al., 2022). As expected, the students guided by problem-solving scaffolding were more likely to reflect on the elements of problem-solving in their learning reflections. Zhu et al. (2023) reported that providing structured paths for problem-solving can focus learners’ attention on  the process and steps involved in problem-solving. Additionally, scaffolding timing did not significantly affect the distribution of perspective codes.

## Implications for educational practices developing students’ CT

This study’s findings have several implications for  advancing empirical research on developing CT in elementary school students. First, implementing PS can foster the development of CT con cepts, practices, and perspectives. Teachers should choose between immediate scaffolding  and delayed scaffolding  based on their specific teaching goals. Finally, combining AI teaching tools with scaffolding may further enhance the integration of AI knowledge and CT skills.

## Limitations and future research

Several limitations of this study should be conside red when interpreting the findings. First, the part icipants were from four parallel classes in the same school, may limit the generalizability of the study’s conclusio ns. We will expand the participant pool to include a broader range of students in future. Second, more process data are needed to analyze changes in students’ CT during problem-solving. Finally, this study focused on a single ML unit in an AI course. In the future, we will extend the study duration and include the entire AI course to explor e the effects of different scaffolding types and timings on students’ CT in various topics and over a longer period.
