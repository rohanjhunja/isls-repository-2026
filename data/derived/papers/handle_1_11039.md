# Art as a Cornerstone for Learning and Community Engagement: A Case Study on Supporting Children’s Affect-driven Identities as Change Makers

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster investigates how the arts can support children’s identities as learners and valuable community members who engage in environmental issues. We build on literature that positions art as a political medium, and places affect at the forefront of learning, identity, and engaging with communities. Findings come from an art installation children (6-8 years) created as a PSA for the school community on the importance of pollinators in our ecosystem. We use artifact analysis to examine wire sculptures, signs, and videography to illustrate how art supported children’s identities as change makers who cared for the environment.

Over the last decade, the field of education has grown to recognize the importance of the arts, leading to initiatives like STEAM education. Within the Learning Sciences scholars have investigated how children learn in and through the arts, how to design art-based learning environments, and how the arts are an integral part of children’s identities as learners and community members (Halverson  & Sheridan, 2014). The arts have been commonly positioned as an important part of developing children’s social emotional learning, problem solving skills, and creativity (Efland, 1990). More recently, scholars have found and argued for the importance of “designing socially supported learning environments in art classrooms” (Dahn, 2022, p. 622), where children are engaged in rich dialogue and reflection that centers affect. This aligns with literature that argue s emotion and affect are part of disciplinary practices and are central to children’s sensemaking (Jaber & Hammer, 2016). As the conference theme focuses on moving towards healing and community, our poster brings together theories on affect and art to understand how we can support children’s identities as change makers who care for the environment. We argue affect is a central part of doing art, especially when we consider how art has historically been a political medium for critical, social, and political issues. History is full of artists who created protest art, including Banksy’s anti-war artwork like the CND Soldiers  and Robert Birch’s Bush’s Aids Flag  to call attention to the HIV/Aids epidemic (Fortes & Smith, 2023). The arts are central to building community, and are an important part of our identities to communicate our affect and emotions in ways that bring care to our world. We build on this body of work and ask: How can a rt support children’s affect-driven identities as change makers within their communities?

The context of this case study is a larger design-based research project, Science through Technology-Enhanced Play (STEP), that examined play-based science learning. The STEP project utilized play-based learning by using motion tracking technology for embodied sensemaking as well as a diversity of art mediums including drawings, theater/skits, paper mache, sculpting with wire, weaving, painting, sketching, costume making, and videography. This poster presents findings from children’s art (6-8 years) after a unit focused on interdependent relationships between plants and pollinators. We analyzed children’s art as an integral part of learning and becoming environmental activists. Specifically, w e use d artifact analysis (Pahl & Rowsell, 2019) of an art installation children created as a Public Service Announcement (PSA) to the school community at the end of the school year. The PSA art installation consisted of the following art mediums: videography, posters, and wire sculptures of flowers and pollinators with woven fabric. We focused our artifact analysis on the transcripts of children’s talk in the PSA videography and the slogans written on children’s posters and signs that were placed next to wire sculptures throughout t he art installation  (see Figure 1). Thus, this poster  examines how children utilized  art to display their stance  on the importance of pollinators (Dahn, 2022), and illustrates  the importance of  affective spaces that support and develop children’s voices and identities as environmental stewards. 2405 the i mportance of pollinators and plants in our ecosystems. Artifact analysis revealed that (1) children used art to communicate the importance of pollinators, and (2) the art in the PSA installation was a medium for children to affectively share and encourage community members to  take action. Throughout the PSA video, children expressed the importance of pollinators by sharing how humans depend on bees. For example, one child shared, “I firmly believe that without bees we are going to be endangered species.” Another child state d, “The bees are very small but they work hard and they are very very important”. In the background of the PSA art installation, a sign was propped against a large wire sculpture of a flower and read, “Our lives depend on bees”. Several children in the PSA video also reminded the school community how much of what we eat depends on pollinators like bees, e.g., stating, “I firmly believe that we need to help the bees…we won’t have a lot of fruit.”

PSA Videography (left) and Sculptures of Plants and Pollinators (right) The art installation also provided a platform, or a medium for children to share how humans can support pollinators in our ecosystem. Several children created signs with slogans like, “Save the Bees” and “Plant Pollinator Friendly Seeds!”. Throughout the PSA video, children also urged audience members to reflect and consider changes that we can make to support and help pollinators like  bees survive, e.g. “I firmly believe that people should stop using pesticides because if people use pesticides they’re going to affect the bees”. In this study, we examined how children’s PSA art installation became more than  a showcase of knowledge on scientific phenomena (interdependent relationships of pollinators and plants). Art was also a political medium for illustrating children’s affective stance on the importance of pollinators in our ecosystem. Through art, the PSA installation became a n opportunity to engage with school community members by encouraging others to take action (e.g. planting pollinator friendly seeds or reducing pesticide use). Therefore, the art in the PSA installation became a way for children to engage in affect-driven action that brought information, awareness, and a call for change to the school community members.

Our findings build on ongoing conversations within the Learning Sciences on how to design and support dignity affirming learning spaces that position and honor children as valued members of the community (Espinoza, et al., 2020).
