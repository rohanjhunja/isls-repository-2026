# A Case Study on the Impact of Game Design Variations on Students’ Reasoning About Fraction Concepts

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Previous studies have demonstrated the potential of digital video games to support students’ fraction learning, but few have explored how students’ understanding about fractions evolves during gameplay. Grounded in Maturana and Varela’s conceptualization of cognition as effective action and the goal-directed nature of human behavior, this study conceptualizes cognition as emerging through three fundamental actions: identification, association, and retention, and posits that goals direct the three actions to specific elements within a given context. Through an analysis of the elements identified, associated, and retained by a single student across two distinct game designs, this study reveals how different game designs elicit varied mathematical reasoning around targeted concepts. The findings underscore the influence of game design on shaping students’ engagement with targeted mathematical concepts.

## Introduction

Studies in elementary mathematics education have re ported a positive impact of digital games on students’ math achievement (Lister & Moyer-Packenham, 2020; Shirkarami et al., 2020). Specifically, with regard to students’ fraction learning, diverse digital games have been used in previous studies (Cyr et al., 2019; Ninaus et al., 2017; Tsai & Yen, 2016; Zhang et al., 2020). These studie s report improved post-test scores among students  after gameplay. However, as Cyr et al. (2019) stated, “the reasons that led to this finding must be investigated further” (p. 25), how the interaction with games contributes to positive results is still in the black box. This study seeks to address this gap by analyzing one student’s interaction with an app-based mobile game around fraction concepts. Drawing on the framework of cognition as effective action (Maturana & Varela, 1987) and the goal-directed nature of learning, this study conceptualizes cogni tion as emerging through three actions —identification, association, and retention —guided by the goals pursued in a given context.  It examines how a student reasons about fraction concepts by analyzing what they identify, associate, and retain while interacting with a mobile math game. Finally, the study discusses the implications of these qualitative findings for desi gning digital games that effectively support students’ fraction learning.

Framework Cognition as an effective action in an autopoietic organization Autopoiesis is the term that Maturana  and Varela (1972) coined to refer to the organization that defines li ving beings as autonomous systems —a class  capable of self-production. According to them, an autopoietic organization continuously regenerates and maintains itself through its own operations (Maturana & Varela, 1987). Extending this autopoietic organization to human co gnition, they approach that the being and the doing of cognition of human beings are inseparable, leading to their pivotal definition of “cognition as an effective action” (p.29, italics in origin). Building on this view, this study posits that human  cognition starts as the emergence of foundational actions, whose self-production gives rise to more complex actions. Spec ifically, it proposes that identification, association, and retention constitute these fundamental actions. Identification, Association, and Retention Maturana and Varela (1987) explain that, in the bio logical dimension, a cell regularly interacts with certain ions (e.g., sodium or calcium) in ways that preserve its  autopoiesis. This implies that a cell can identify specific ions and associate them with its systemic processes and retain this information through regulatory mechanisms. These three capacities—identification, association, and retention—seem to be thus already embedded in the most basic biological interactions.

In the cognitive domain, developmental studies show  that even young infants exhibit similar actions (e.g., Baillargeon et al., 1985; Craighero et al., 2020; Simion et al., 2008; Rochat & Hespos, 1996). For instance, Rochat and Hespos (1996) found that 4 –8-month-old infants gaze longer at impossible orientation outcome of objects, indicating the infants may identify the outcome a s unnatural by associating it with a prior state of  the objects. Piaget’s classical observations (1952; Piaget & Inh elder, 1969) of  sensorimotor intelligence similarly suggest the presence of such foundational actions.  In one example (Piaget, 1952, p. 26, Observation 6), his son Laurent sucks on an index finger but, finding it un satisfying, rejects it and cries. While the infant likely does not consciously recognize the object as “an index finge r,” he experientially identifies that it is not wha t meets his biological need, by asso ciating the experience with the outcome.  As infants grow, they begin to retain the outcomes of certain behavior patterns, even without explicit instruction. These findings suggest that, even before language, human cognition can (1) identify elements in a context, (2) associate them with others in context or memory, and (3) retain memories and information, including —but not limited to— associations between elements and other contextual features. Therefore, this study proposes these as fundamental actions that constitute cognition—as cognition is understood as their ongoing enactme nt. Since cognition is autopoietic —continually self-producing—these actions recursively enact feedback loops, enabling increasingly complex and adaptive actions.

However, this raises a key question: among the coun tless elements available in any context or memory, how do we determine what to prioritize? The next se ction explores this question by examining the role of goaldirectedness in shaping the enactment of these foundational actions. Goals and the enactment of identification, association, and retention Learning has long been recognized as a goal-oriented activity (Bruner, 1961; Piaget, 1954; Sweller, 1988). Combining this goal-directed nature of learning with the framework of cognition as effective action, this study approaches cognition as a dynamic system of actions shaped by goals and the challenges —perturbations—that arise in their pursuit. Drawing on Maturana and Var ela’s (1987) notion of perturbations, the enactment of cognition can be understood as shaping further interactions w ith the environment, especially in how it identifies and responds to elements that interfere with goal achievement. Extending this approach to an analytic lens, this s tudy proposes that students’ reasoning about mathematical concepts  can be analyzed through the recursive enactment  of identification, association, and retention on elements perceived as related to goals and perturbations in specific contexts. This study uses this lens to examine how students’ reasoning about fraction concepts emerges through the enactment of these foundational actions on specific elements in the game context, in response to game-based goals and challenges. Fractions as part-whole and as measurement Among several fraction constructs (Behr et al., 199 2; Cramer et al., 2019; Kieren, 1980; Lopez-Martin et al., 2022), the part-whole construct is typically the first introduced in mathematics standards (e.g., TEA, 2012; CCSS, 2010), followed by the fraction as measurement  construct. Understanding fractions as part-whole requires students to coordinate key elements such as the who le, its parts, and the unit within specific context s (Moyo & Machaba, 2021). Similarly, interpreting fractions a s measurement involves considering elements like unit length 1 (Kieren, 1980) and unit fractions (Steffe & Olive, 2010), which are also highly context-dependent. These elements manifest in diverse ways across different learning contexts, including digital games (Cyr et al., 2019; Kärki et al., 2022). Games, in particular, provide a rich environment for exploring how students reaso n about fractions, as they need to interpret game elements while pursuing game goals. To examine this, I designed a mobile game for this study with game components intentionally structured around the two constructs of fractions. Further details about the design will be presented in the method section.

## Research questions

The central question is whether and how game design components shape players’ perception of  goals, which, in turn, directs the enactment of identification, asso ciation, and retention  toward specific elements in the game context. To examine the question, the study addressed the three specific research questions: RQ1) What elements of the game context do students identify, associate, and retain while pursuing game goals? RQ2) What reasoning related to fraction concepts does emerge through the enactment of the three actions on perceived game elements? RQ3) Do different game components lead students to identify, associate, and retain different elements, thereby activating distinct reasoning about fractions? If so, how?’

## Method

Game design I designed and developed a mobile game, Bridge the Cloud, which is a mobile digital game designed for touchscreen devices. The goal of the initial design is to block falling stones by creating clouds at specific positions on the screen, using different numbers for the denominator and numerator (Figure 1). If players use blue numbers, which represent denominator, to divide one stick in to equal pieces, and green numbers, which represent numerator, to count the number of partitioned pieces, their reasoning aligns with fraction as part-whole construct. ## Participant and data source

One 4 th-grade student, Jason  (pseudonym) participated in the study at a public charter school in the southern United States. The data set includes video and audio recordings collected for ten 1-hour sessions with Jason over two months. During each 1-hour game session, Jason played the game on an Android tablet for the first 30 minutes while the researcher observed. The gameplay was rec orded using the tablet's screen recorder function. In the following 30 minutes, the researcher conducted an interview, asking Jason questions about his gameplay. The ten interview sessions with Jason were transcribed to c apture his verbal responses. Additionally, the transcripts documented his written expressions and gestures that accompanied his verbal communication. Analysis Using transcripts as the primary data source, I focused on how Jason enacted the three foundational actions in his explanations of the game context. When he interpret ed specific elements as distinct from the broader c ontext— such as referring to three half-sticks as “it”—this was analyzed as an enactment of identification, suggesting he perceived them as a single object. When he related elements, like saying “blue numbers and green numbe rs are working together,” this was interpreted as association. Instances  where he referenced past gameplay —for example, recalling sticks formed by green 3 and blu e 1 while explaining those formed by green 3 and bl ue 2— were analyzed as retention.

While explaining the specific position of the cloud in Figure 3-(a), Jason identified and retained the stick formed in previous game scenes by green 3 and blue 1 (Figure 3 -(e)) as a single entity, noting it was larger than the stick formed by green 1 and blue 1 (Figure 3 -(d)). When discussing the role of green 3, he associated the length of the stick made with green 3 and blue 2 with that made with green 3 and blue 1, interpreting that blue 2 shortened the stick formed by green 3 and blue 1. At the end of the interview, Jason associated the green numbers with addition and the blue numbers with subtraction. Throughout the gameplay and interview, Jason consistently focused on the changes in stick length, identifying the stick as one whole stick  as getting larger or shorter based on the green and blue numbers. However, when asked how "subtracting 2 from 3" resulted in a cloud at the specific position (e.g., Figure 3-(a)), Jason became stuck. Despite this, he continue d framing the problem in terms of addition and subtraction, without activating any reasoning close to fraction subconstructs. To catch the gem shown in Figure 4 -(a), Jason expe rimented with various combinations of green and blue numbers and ultimately chose blue 9 and green 3, as depicted in Figure 4 -(b). When asked why he selected green 3 instead of other green numbers, he explaine d, “Well, I counted it a little bit and I figured out it’d be 3 so I just chose 3.” In this instance, Jason identified  one small piece of the given whole stick as a disc rete object he could count. His counting action and focus on the number of small pieces within the unit stick emerged within the context of the bonus stage.  Although this does not necessarily indicate that Ja son grasped that each small piece represents 1 9 of the whole stick, his actions and the elements he  identified and associated demonstrate reasoning more aligned with the part whole subconstruct. This  shift suggests that the updated design directed hi s attention toward counting fractional units, moving closer to the intended interpretation of fractions.

## Conclusion and discussion

The findings indicate that different game designs p rompt player s to enact the three actions—identification, association, and retention —on distinct elements, thereby leading varied interaction with targeted mathematical concepts through gameplay. This does not imply that players will automatically construct these concepts through game-based actions only. What this study highlights is that intentionally de signed games can encourage specific cognitive engagements, laying a foundation for conceptual development. The results provide insights into a possible explan ation for how digital games can improve post-test outcomes reported in previous studies.  T he design of games can guide students to apply the  three actions to specific elements, resulting in game actions that a lign with targeted mathematical concepts.  Through recursive enactment, this process may strengthen students’ conceptual understanding over time. However, to confirm this explanation, further resea rch is needed. Studies involving a wider variety of game designs and more diverse student populations a re essential to explore how different design elemen ts influence cognitive engagement and learning outc omes. Future research should continue investigating how fraction-focused games can serve as autonomous learning envi ronments that scaffold students’ cognitive processes through purposeful design.
