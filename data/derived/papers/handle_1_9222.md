# Co-designing Technology Scaffolds and Scripts for Co-regulated, Adaptable Learning Community Curricula

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Knowledge Community and Inquiry (KCI) is a learning community model that offers a set of principles to guide the design of collective inquiry scripts, however, the model has yet to include any direct role for a student model, nor any explicit mechanism for adaptable scripts. This study presents a technology innovation, co-designed with a middle school mathematics teacher, that will serve to support student modeling and adaptable scripts. Part of a larger study presently in progress, this paper presents a work-in-progress of the design-based research method, focused on problem identification, design, and testing of the scripts and technology innovation. The conjecture of the broader design experiment is that by adding new forms of scaffolds and student-sensitive adaptable scripts to KCI, we can facilitate adaptations and co-regulation of learning that promote student outcomes (e.g., content acquisition, engagement, social-emotional learning, and the development of learners’ identities in mathematics).

## Introduction

Knowledge Community and Inquiry (KCI) is a learning community model that offers a set of principles to guide the design of collective inquiry scripts. An important dimension of KCI resea rch is the design and development of technology environments that help collect group knowledge, distribute it back to individua ls, and give the community a general sense of awareness of its own progress (Slotta et a l., 2018). Since 2011, the  Common Knowledge environment has provided the important functionality of collective idea boards and workspaces (Fong & Slotta, 2018) – but it has never included any level of sensitivity to individual student metadata (i.e., a learner model), no specific group process supports, nor any level of adaptability. As opposed to pur ely auto-adaptive approaches that reduce learner and teacher autonomy, adaptable scripts guide learners’ interactions (i.e., through choices of prompts or roles) to provide learners and teachers with the control to adjust the scripts in ways that can consider each individual’s awareness of themselves and others (Vogel et al., 2021). To address the challenge of students taking on these new responsibilities in the classroom, external scrip ts (i.e., often prompts) can guide students through the intended behaviors until their internal scripts are appropriated. Specifically, co-regulation of learning (CoRL) involves processes of strategic monitoring, evaluation, and adapt ation of cognitive, behavioral, or affective outcomes (Hadwin et al., 2017) whereby co-regulation scripts m ay guide students through the enactment of these processes. In turn, such scripting can be combined with (self-and) group-awareness tools to aid in reflection, socio-cognitive scaffolding, and activation of proper components of these scripts (Schnaubert et al., 2020). Although scripting, awareness tools, and CoRL are each well establis hed in computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) research, they are rarely studied together, in relation to one another. The present study is part of a teacher-researcher co-design projec t that investigates new patterns of learning community pedagogy that aims to (1) develop an adaptive technology environment to scaffold and script the development of internal regulation scripts of individuals and groups within a lea rning community and (2) allow shared-control between teacher and student-led adaptations, promoting deeper, more responsive, meaningful learning. This study focuses on the initial phases of context analysis,  problem exploration, and the design of a new technology environment for scaffolding adaptable, student-responsive KCI scripts. Our research questions include: (1) When designing technology scaffolds and co-regulation scripts for adaptation and co-regulation as a learning community, what are the (a) str engths to be leveraged and (b) constraints to be mitigated in this context? (2) What forms of (a) adaptiv e technology scaffolds and (b) coregulation scripts can be designed to support teacher-and student-controlled adaptations, informed by self-and group-awareness tools (i.e., for content knowledge, engagement, and social-emotional learning (SEL))? (3) What are the design conjectures guiding this research, regarding technology scaffolds, co-regulation scripts, and student outcomes (e.g., content knowledge, engagement, SEL, and development of a math identity)?

## Methods

This study presents our work with a middle school mathematics teacher tha t began with our collective aim to develop and enact learning community curricula in her classroom. Participants w ere recruited from a private CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 310 school in a Canadian city that included one teacher and 78 grade-seven mathematics students across four cohorts during the first year and focuses on 15 grade-eight mathematics students during the second year. This project employed design-based research (DBR) methods to iteratively develop our technology and curriculum innovations in authentic classroom contexts. We frame this study in terms of McKenney and Reeve’s

(2018) three DBR phases: (1) analysis and exploration (i.e., problem identification and understanding), (2) design

and construction (i.e., articulation the problem for critique and implementation of problem sol utions), and (3) evaluation and reflection  (i.e., investigation of the feasibility, effectiveness, and impact as well as  a review of principles of designs and interventions).

## Procedure

Year 1 & 2: (a) Analysis & exploration-we performed observations and interviews to identify appropriate interventions and are collaboratively exploring script  and technological problems; Year 1 & 2: (b) Design & construction-we have refined measurements (i.e., content, engagement, and SEL) and continue to iteratively design and develop scripts and technology; and Year 2: (c) Evaluation & reflection-we piloted the measurements and are now performing data collection and analysis of content knowledge, engagement, and SEL baselines and re-assessments while performing emerging scripts and applying the designed technology. Research analysis plan RE RQ1: We investigated the strengths, challenges, and opportunities of our proposed s olutions using (i) a field portrait, drawing from classroom observations, document analysis (i.e., lesson plans), and teacher interviews; and (ii) a Strength, Weakness, Opportunity, Threat (SWOT) analysis (McKenney & Reeves, 2018) was conducted after pilot testing the technology. RE RQ2: The forms of technology scaffolds are presented in terms of mappings to KCI principles and a morphological chart used to generate the ideas for teacher-and student-led adaptations, summarized below. RE RQ3: To outline our plan for evaluating the scaffolds and scripts, we have explicated our conjectures regarding interactions between designed technology scaffolds and co-regulation scripts with mediating processes and outcomes by employing conjecture mapping (Sandoval, 2014). Future work will report on the final co-regulation scripts and investigate indicators of impact on the targeted outcomes.

## Context analysis and exploration: Field portrait & SWOT analysis

This field portrait summary is presented in terms of: (a) members & relationships (WHO); (b) context & success criteria (WHAT); (c) key design elements & trajectories (WHEN/HOW); and (d) connections to research problems (WHY). WHO: The classroom learning environment involves 15 participating students and one teacher interacting in-class often using a shared whiteboard as well as Google Slides and Docs to support in-class activities. WHAT: As this class ascribes to the International Baccalaureate Middle Years Programme (MYP), each unit is composed of: (a) the MYP Criteria System (content) and (b) Approaches to Learning (ATLs; behaviors/skills), with SEL as an emerging priority. WHEN/HOW: Each academic year (and occasionally each unit) begins with a content diagnostic. Diagnostics help identify “highfliers” and students who may benefit from “booster groups” (to receive extra support or resources). Throughout the year, students use personal calendars to  set goals. Occasionally process journals are used for metacognitive reflections, involving portfolios of student work, diagnosti cs, and summative assessments. At the end of a unit, students may review the uni t’s success criteria and update their process journals. Units scaffold skills and knowledge toward summative inquiry proj ects. WHY: Research connections: inquiry projects (with provocations, authentic problems, and authentic audienc es) map to KCI principles; planning, goal setting, monitoring, and reflection map to metacognitive reflection as well as self-and co-regulated learning; and differentiation of tasks and projects (for highfliers and booster groups) map to adaptive/personalized learning.

For the SWOT analysis, our determined objective was to integrate KCI learning community pedagogy and an adaptive scripting and orchestration environment to scaffold and script (i) co-regulated learning and (ii) adaptable curricula that promote student outcomes. The target outcomes were co-negotiated as (a) the content of the MYP Criteria System, (b) engagement (as a requisite for learning), and (c) SEL aligned with existing ATLs. The teacher and researcher provided independent responses to SWOT prompts that were negotiated, consolidated, and summarized as follows: (1) Strengths: Students have prior experience collaborating with peers, planning using calendars to set tasks and add links to resources, completing diagnostic assess ments, reviewing diagnostics to support gap-closing processes, and reviewing unit checklists to measure learning. Action: We can build upon existing practices and create new tools tailored to fit and extend those pra ctices. (2) Weaknesses: The class can tend toward off-task behaviors during group work, the time to complete group tasks  can be much longer than intended, some students are easily distracted by technical bugs or classroom di stractions leading to difficulty completing assigned classroom tasks. Action: We can adaptively provide more structure and scaffolding during CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 311 group work, assign accountability partners, subdivide and spread tasks across time, reserve particular open-ended tasks or new tech features to fewer/select students. (3) Opportunities: Students are more likely to remain on-task during shorter periods of group work, individual work, or when the teacher or one student is presenting. Students are accustomed to receiving differentiated work, such as extension work to “highfliers”, adapted inquiry projects, and “booster groups” for students seeking additional support. Students’ behaviors are also impacted by their performance and confidence. Action: We candesign scripts that dynamically differentiate tasks, technology tools, and roles for students to provide opportunities for confidence building, reduced distractions, and task scaffolding.

(4) Threats: The curriculum is quite full, so there is limited capacity for additional tasks  or pre-unit diagnostics.

Action: Classes from the end-of-year review period may be spread throughout units as co-regulatory preparation. Adaptations and technology innovations A morphological chart was utilized during co-design to guide ideation and negotiation of adaptation opportunities, organized in terms of (a) design requirements, (b) solution propositions, and (c) specific interventions. The established requirements were to design (i) teacher-and (ii) student-cont rolled adaptations. For each design requirement, content, engagement, and SEL adaptation propositions were conceive d. Proposed interventions included the following. Teacher-content: adapt focus based on student diagnostics  and goals. Teacherengagement: assign roles (e.g., content generator, content reviewer, and researcher) informed by engagement gap analysis; Teacher-SEL: perform check-ins and provide support for SEL goals; Student-content: Help generate lesson slides or resources for peers. Student-engagement: Review diagnostics  and negotiate engagement roles. Student-SEL: Co-create support strategies and form accountability pairs/groups. In practice, to facilitate these emerging scripts, we designed an adaptive technology environment aligned with KCI’s pedagogical principles. In support of KCI Principles 3 & 1 (design collective inquiry curricula targeted to learning objectives that use a student-generated knowledge base as a resource), we designed community boards for collectively contributing student-generated content. For KCI Principle 2  (students revisit and improve the knowledge base) we designed collections & workflows for organizing and distributing student-generated posts, and a task workspace for assigning tasks (e.g., tag, review, upvote). For KCI Principle 4  (the teacher plays an essential role during scripted activities) we designed CK Monitor for monitoring task progress and accessing awareness tools (also available to students). This architecture was iteratively extended (i.e., with personal boards and goal setting tools) guided by emerging research questions to additionally support co-regulated learning. The tools are intended to be applied across three regulation phases (Zimm erman, 2002) that cycle during each unit: forethought (assess, strategize, and goal setting), performance (enact, strategize, monitor), and reflection (evaluate and adjust for the future). Altogether, our design conjecture is that  KCI learning community pedagogy with designed technology scaffolds and co-regulation scripts can facilitate (a)  adaptable scripts and (b) coregulated learning processes that are supportive of student outcomes.

## Conclusion and next steps

This co-design project began with the goal of integrating learning community pedagogy in to a middle school mathematics classroom. The analysis and exploration phase identified the opportunity to build adaptable scripts into KCI using co-regulation of learning, by reinforcing and building onto existing regul ation practices of the class, while mitigating implementation challenges using teacher-driven adaptations  and classroom scripts. The focus, therefore, was directed to adaptable scripts that targeted both teacher-and student-controlled adaptations and co-regulation cycles to guide students through the enactment of intended metac ognition, regulation, and adaptation activities. Self-and group-awareness tools (i.e., of content knowledge, classroom engagement, and social-emotional state) were identified as essential tools to support these co-regulation processes. The design and construction  phase began with the development of a scripting and orchestration environment for learning communities. This technology environment was iteratively e xtended with tools to support adaptable scripts (e.g., differentiated workspace tasks) and co-regul ation (e.g., goal setting/monitoring tools and personal boards) based on emerging research questions. The construction of these tools aims to provide greater transparency between teacher and student, progress tracking, and monitoring for adaptation. During our co-design, our focus transitioned from auto-adaptive (system-controlled) scripts toward teacher-and studentcontrolled adaptations that were far more flexible and responsive to the needs of the students. Findings from the evaluation and reflection phase will be presented in future publications, however, the conjecture map above illustrates the intended role of each technology scaff old and script toward supporting the co-regulation process and the anticipated student outcomes. Although students already had some familiarity with self-regulation practices, albeit focused primarily on content learning, we have  aimed to make these practices more explicit and intentional, while spiraling the regulation of SEL and engagement into their practice and seeking to contribute further means of community supports for co-regulation. This study contributes to the CSCL research of scripting, (self-and) group-awareness tools, and regulation. We also contribute a student model and adaptivity to both the KCI technology and its scripts that widen the door to further research of adaptable KCI scripts, group process support, and co-regulation of a KCI learning community.
