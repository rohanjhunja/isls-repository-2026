# The Development of Creative Freedom: Youth Conceptions of Agency Through Participation in Arts Programming

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how playwriting supports agency as an outcome of participation among a group of elementary school students. We engaged in a multimodal linguistic analysis of student agency, analyzing youth interview transcripts and creative artifacts to better understand students’ sense of intentionality, autonomy, and creative freedom within arts practices. This paper offers possibilities for a more comprehensive study of student agency in the arts by introducing a multimodal linguistic approach.

## Introduction

Recent research on the value of arts practices in education has moved away from an instrumentalized perspective, where the arts are only valuable inasmuch as they h elp students perform better on traditional academic s, and towards an intrinsic perspective, where arts practices are valuable for their positive impact on students’ intellectual and social and emotional lives (Hetland & Winner, 2 004). Studies on what the arts-as-subjects give to students demonstrate that young people learn to create and share external representations, a core practice in most academic subjects (Halverson, 2021). In this study, we inves tigate how playwriting supports agency as an outcom e of participation among a group of elementary school student-participants of Whoopensocker, a pla ywriting and performing artist-in-residence program. We examine agency, defined by Konopasky and Sheridan (2016) as “the capacity to produce effects…[by] acting intentional ly and autonomously” (p. 108) in a variety of forms. Focal participants talked about and enacted agency in three ways: 1) through their engagement in meaningful choice making, 2) by centering themselves as active participants when speaking about their experiences, and 3) by emphasizing both collective and personal agency during collabor ative activities. In this paper, we focus on this third finding, highlighting conceptions of self-as-agent through collaborative playwriting. The arts are a key site for the agency and creative choice making of students in schools, especially in our current era of educational accountability that emph asizes test scores in evaluating students and limit s student choice within curricula (Bowen & Kisida, 2017). Wit hin arts educational settings, youth experience fre edom to construct storied worlds, to represent themselves, and to frame relationships to the events, ideas, and people around them. Griffin et al. (2017) describe agency in arts education as  providing opportunities for “exploration, ownership, and control of learning through the co-construction of knowledge” (p. 2). Through these ac tions, students may engage in creativity and critical thin king. When young writers have access to a variety of crea tive tools, their agency as composers is especially visi ble as they use metarepresentational competencies t o select various modes of meaning making for specific communicative purposes (Halverson, 2021).Through this inquiry, we aim to demonstrate how agency is expressed linguistically both in student talk and in the creative writing they produce and offer possibilities for a more comprehensive study of student agency in the arts.

## Methods

The 4th grade participant data (n=13) used in this paper was extracted from the initial round data set of fifty-three 3rd through 5th graders at one participating school. This data included audio recorded semi-structured interviews that focused on (a) experiences with artmaking, (b)  experiences within the art-in-residence program, (c) feelings about the programming, and (d) the program’s potential future impact on their lives. The data set also included a collection of student artifacts (n=8) including written stories and drawings, allowing us to triangulate our analysis between student interviews and work samples. Langua ge use can communicate students’ sense of intention ality and autonomy towards arts practices. As such, we engaged in a linguistic analysis of student agency (Konopasky & Sheridan, 2016) across interviews and artifacts.  This included consideration of both what students say about the creative agency they experience through artmaki ng and how students formulate their ideas through speech and on paper. To better understand the ways in which students’ talk signaled varying degrees of perceived agency, we developed a linguistic and thematic code book to  catalog student language (Konopasky & Sheridan, 20 16). Sample codes that suggested higher agency included first person subject pronoun (i.e., I, we) and high verbal transitivity, defined as “purposeful, completed actions by the se lf on animate others” (Konopasky & Sheridan, 2016, p. 5). Sample codes that suggested lower agen cy included adverbial mitigator (e.g., just) and speaker as object (e.g., He gave me instructions.). We also coded interview transcripts thematically using an in vivo method (Saldaña, 2013) and organized codes into thematic clusters including creative freedom and collaboration. As we engaged in these distinct coding processes, we looked for relationships between our thematic and linguistic codes. Alongside interview transcripts, we analyzed studen ts’ individually and group written stories (artifacts) through a multimodal perspective. In this paper,  we highlight one key finding: conceptions  of self-as-agent during collaborative writing activities.

## Findings: Agency through collaboration

Throughout the interviews, students repeatedly used agentic language and emphasized creative choice making in the content of their talk when speaking about collaborations with peers. We found that students felt free to choose whether and how to engage in group work and that th ese relational choices were a highlight of Whoopens ocker for many. Students generally used linguistic constr uctions that suggested high agency and emphasized t he imaginative and creative outcomes that emerged when  working with a team. For example, Abe explained that when writing a story with a classmate “we can like,  think of ideas together.” Abe centered himself and  his collaborators with the subject we. He also used the helping verb can, emphasizing the abilities of himself and his classmates. Similarly, when asked why she preferred not to write by herself, Addie responded, “well, I sometimes didn’t have enough ideas to put together a whole st ory.” Rather than focusing on the compromises assoc iated with collaboration, Addie focused on the potential to put together a whole story when multiple classmates contribute. In addition, Jack highlighted his preference for gr oup work by describing the challenges of writing alone. He explained, “it was just hard for me to think of a story, but lik e when I’m working with someone, um, it’s easier. Mm-hmm cause then I can just like express their ideas, my ideas, and then we can kind of mash them together.” Jack articulated that he could more clearly communicate his own ideas when he was able to create with a collaborator. Rather than feeling constrained by group work, the option to work with peers promoted creative agency in the story writing process.

Student work samples provide some insight into the ways that multiple students navigated roles during collaborative writing. This includes evidence of multiple handwriting on the same story, or the same story written in each student’s individual notebook. Sometimes, c ollaborative writing resulted in versioning or edit ing of a story, as co-writers negotiat ed their collective work. For example, in a story titled The Grumpy Star (Shape) written by three students who chose to work together, their original notebook page suggests that at some point in the writing process, a title change occurred. The w ord grumpy in the title appears over top of the word joyful which has been scratched out. While we don’t know w hat prompted this particular change, evidence of re visions such as this one suggests a collaborative negotiati on of meaning making among group members as their s tory takes shape in real time.

## Discussion

Our findings suggest that students express and enact agency in what they say about their playwriting experiences, how they say it, and in the artistic products th ey create.  In this work, agency is an outcome of collaborative playwriting. This aligns with Griffin et al.’s (2017) study of arts practices as opportunities for artistic expression: “Through agency, choice, and opportunity, students have the hopeful prospect of working in and ac ross various modes to cultivate meaningful learning experiences”  (p. 2).  This pilot study paves a path forward for larger inquiries which triangulate artifacts of student work in arts programs with student reflections on their experiences.
