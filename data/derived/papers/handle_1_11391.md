# Growth Mindset Interventions to Make Failure Desired in Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We tested the impact of developing and validating a growth mindset intervention targeting domain-general and domain-specific skillsets that affect learning from failure for sixty six ninth-grade students. Repeated prediction followed by explanation cycles were used to trap myths associated with growth mindset and induce surprise in the learning process. Following mixed methods analyses, results showed a significant pre-post improvement in beliefs about the malleability of mental abilities and expectations for success in future math tasks. Internalizing the growth mindset message also positively impacted persistence in idea generation on a challenging follow-up math task. Qualitative student reflections suggested transformed perceptions about the desirability of failure, with students articulating a contrast between their revised conceptions about the value of failure and past cognitive/affective approaches towards dealing with those failures. Compared to past work, our intervention produced stronger effects.

## Introduction

How do we make failure desir ed for students? Given contemporary learning sciences work that speaks to the benefits of deliberately designing for failure  (e.g., Kapur & Bielaczyc, 2012; Sinha & Kapur, 2021; Fields et al., 2021; Wong et al., 2022; Sinha, 2022)  a long with  growing empirical evidence on students ’ unwillingness to engage with such failure-prone learning activities (Pan et al., 2020), this becomes a challenging undertaking. Growth mindset interventions (Yeager et al., 2019), which emphasize the importan ce of failure as a learning opportunity, offer one solution to prime students towards goal-oriented behaviors such as attending to task-relevant information and embracing the discomfort that comes with working on challenging learning tasks. Prior student-focused growth mindset interventions in mathematics (Bui et al., 2023) have, however, (i) implemented learning materials that are exclusively domain-general (e.g., how the brain works and strengthens neural connections through failure) or domain-specific (e.g., how beliefs about math can affect perception  of failure), (ii) used direct teaching and/or reading exercises to educate students about these materials, which can be potentially damaging to students with low expectanc y for success, (iii) prioritized quantitative data to assess the intervention impact, leaving a gap in qualitatively understanding why and how these interventions affect students’ mindsets towards failure, and, finally (iv) been carried out primarily in American or European contexts. In the current work, we design and validate a growth mindset learning intervention for N = 66 secondary school students in Singapore that tackles the afore mentioned gaps by combining the complementary stren gths of domain-general and domain-specific characteristics of how students can transform their beliefs about failure. We follow a prediction followed by explanation cycle rather thandirect teaching to allow students to develop intuition about the key myths associated with growth mindset and introduce surprise in the learning process (Brod et al., 2018). To complement self-reports on general beliefs about ability and domain-specific expectancies, we also provoke verbalization about these myths and collect  qualitative reflections to understand how students would approach failure in mathematics differently post-intervention. W e further assess  whether participation in the intervention results in persisting with suboptimal idea generation in a follow-up problem-solving task. This work contributes to our growing efforts to make failure more desired during learning.

## Theoretical background

Growth mindset is a well-established psychological phenomenon (Dweck, 2006) subject to substantial public interest over the past decade. Students’ self-beliefs about the malleability of their abilities are an important predictor of academic achievement (Yeager et al., 2019). Critically, these beliefs impact whether students attribute failure to controllable factors like effort or strategy use rather than uncontrollable, stable and internal factors like ability. Such attributional styles, in turn, affect how students approach challenging learning situations and whether they practice, seek to learn from failures and improve (Dweck, 2006). The effects of endorsing a growth mindset extend beyond performance – prior observational and intervention work, carried out across upper secondary and tertiary education, has shown that it can also reduce socio-economic gaps and resist stereotype threats that plague deep engagement with learning materials (e.g., Claro et al., 2016, B roda et al., 2018). Recent critiques have, however, questioned the universal effectiveness of growth mindset interventions, with the majority of the criticism centered on huge variation in true effects across studies (e.g., Burnette et al., 2023 reported Cohen’s d for academic achievement ranging from-0.08 to 0.35). These mixed results highlight the importance of contextual and implementation factors, including pedagogical designs used to administer growth mindset interventions. At the intersection between mindset theory and achievement motivation theory (Wigfield et al., 2021), a specific component of motivation – expectancies (confidence that one is able to comple te a task) – has been deemed critical for goal-directed behavior. Expectancies are “domain-specific, future-oriented beliefs (e.g., believing that one’s abilities in a specific domain will impr ove” (Zeeb & Voss, 2024, p. 2). In the context of mathematics, it is plausible to expect that growth mindset interventions may foster stronger beliefs in the potential to learn and succeed in the future. Empirically, prior meta-analytic research has shown that nudging a strong belief in skill malleability directly impacted learners’ c onfidence in their own skill development and succes s expectations (Burnette et al., 2023). The present work therefore not only assesses intervention impact on students internalizing the growth mindset message but also in fostering positive expectations about the potential for success in math. Additionally, we attempt to understand whether growth mindset fac ilitation results in a willingness to exert effort despite obstacles on a challenging math task targeting a new concept. What sits at the heart of developing and validating  growth mindset interventions is our goal to make failure more desired during learning. Drawing on a longstanding history of theoretical and empirical w ork in the desirable difficulties (Bjork & Bjork, 2020) and productive failure (Sinha & Kapur, 2021) literature, we conjecture that challenging situations causing a temporary dip  in performance but serving as strong preparation f or future learning may not be desired by students (Zepeda et al., 2020). In fact, a large-scale survey conducted across three large public universities in North America (Pan et al., 2020) showed that even within tertiary education, students hold maladaptive beliefs towards actively engaging with failure-prone learning situati ons, despite often recognizing the need and value of learning from fai lures. More generally, recent meta-analytic work has empirically shown that mental effort feels aversive, that is, it positively correlates with negative affect independent of educational levels and the task feedback structu re (David et al., 2024). When students prioritize i mmediate performance over long-term learning, they may equate increased mental eff ort with poorer learning and failure (Deslauriers et al., 2019) – this may, in turn, impede their motivation to activ ely tackle challenging problems where exploring the problem-space and gaining negative knowledge (what does not work and why) is as important as gaining conceptual knowledge (what works and why). Without supportive pedagogical designs that foste r resilience and emphasize self-generation of the value of failure in the learning process, learners cannot be expected to naturally take up opportunities to engage with d iscomfort beyond the intervention period. Our prese nt work takes a small step in this direction.

## Participants

After obtaining informed consent from students and their parents, N = 66 upper secondary school students (grade 9, age 14-15, 72.7% male) from a specialized independent school in Singapor e participated in the study. Their mathematics prior knowledge (max 100), as assessed by the most recent standardized annual national examination was M = 74.4 (SD = 11.3). The study was approved by the university ethics commission (IRB-2023-1040).

## Study design

All participants went through a 25-minute online intervention (implemented via Qualtri cs). A brief battery of relevant validated questionnaires following a five-point Likert scale was administered at the start – (i) learning goal orientation (8 items, 𝛼 = 0.85, e.g., “the opportunity to do challenging work is important to me”, “I try hard to improve on my past performance ”), (ii) attitude towards mistakes (8 items, 𝛼 = 0.82, e.g., “when I make mistakes, I am afraid that others look down upon me ”, “I believe it is smart to avoid making mistakes duri ng learning”), (iii) domain-general beliefs about growth mindset (6 items, 𝛼 = 0.81, e.g., “mental ability is a part of a person that cannot really be changed ”, “ you can learn  new things, but you can’t change basic mental performance”), and (iv) domain-specific expectancies (3 items, 𝛼 = 0.92, e.g., “how much do you think your knowledge of mathematics will develop in the future ?”, “how good do you think you can be at understanding mathematical findings in the future?”). These questionnaires were adapted from recent measurement advances in capturing incoming cognitive and attitudinal determ inants of failure-driven learning (Sinha & Kapur, 2021) as well as applied empirical work based on growth mindset and expectancy value theories (e.g., Zeeb & Voss, 2024). Subsequently, everyone went through a three-part introduction to the brain (part I), growth mindset and its associated myths (part II), and the relevance of growth mindset situated within a mathematical learning context (part III). In the end, everyone completed an identical post-questionnaire on domain-general beliefs about growth mindset (6 items, 𝛼 = 0.80) and domain-specific expectancies (3 items, 𝛼 = 0.95). After the intervention was complete, everyone attempted to generate solutions to a novel open-ended problem on a yet-to-be-learned math concept (standard deviation) for another 25 minutes, drawing on productive failure materials from Kapur (2014). Development of growth mindset learning materials An interactive storyline, drawing on and extending validated materials from Yeager et al. (2019), was used to introduce participants to brain plasticity, domain-general growth mindset and domain-specific growth mindset in the context of math. In part I, the shortest sub-phase of the intervention, everyone started off with foundational knowledge on neurons and synaptic connections that make up the brain, before moving on  to making two predictions on myths about the brain – (i) do you think your brain can get smarter?, and (ii) do you think your brain grows bigger when you experience failure? Customized verbal and video-based explanatory responses were provided depending on the predictions made, with the goal of raising awareness of how our brain strengthens its synaptic connections with failure-driven practice, making it more efficient for future problem-solving tasks. In part II, the relatively longer sub-phase of the intervention, the knowledge of neuroplasticity served as a bridge to introduce participants to the definitions of growth mindset and contrast it with fixed mindset. However, rather than presenting elaborate information on dif ferent aspects of growth mindset upfront, the inter vention continued by asking participants to make three new predictions – (i) do you think people with a growth mindset view failures as opportunities for growth rather th an shortcomings?, (ii) a good growth mindset is about effor t – if you try hard enough, you’re bound to improve  – true/false?, (iii) do you think you can only have one type of mindset – either a fixed or growth mindset at a time? Similar to part I, customized responses were provided each time after participants had made their predictions. These responses focused on how growth mindset relates to persistence in the face of failure and comprises effort along with using the right strategies for improvement (e.g., asking peers/teachers for help, adjusting problem-solving approach). The idea of mindset as a continuum was also introduced when explaining how learning situations may differentially bring out growth and fixed mindsets. The intervention sub-phase concluded by showing students evidence from PISA’s 2018  assessment for the effectiveness of growth mindset in a local context. A bar graph was used to illustrate how secondary school students in Singapore, who believed that their inte lligence could be developed through effort and pers istence achieved, on average, 41 points higher in reading scores compared to peers who viewed their abilities as fixed. Finally, part III, the longest sub-phase of the intervention, shifted the focus from domain-general aspects of growth mindset to developing intuition about its  relevance in mathematics. Similar to the earlier sub-phases, participants sequentially made predictions on six domain-specific myths – (i) do you think you are a math person?, (ii) the better I am at math, the less I will tend to fai l at math questions – yes/no?, (iii) the faster you solve math problems, the better you are at mathematics  – yes/no?, (iv) math is a man’s world – yes/no?, (v) m ath is only useful for people pursuing math-related careers – yes/no?, (vi) only people with advanced degrees (e. g. masters or doctorate degrees in maths) can be termed mathem aticians – yes/no? Customized responses for the se myths used several real-life examples of scientists (e.g., Albert Einstein, Katherine Johnson, Ada Lovelace) to show how everyone, regardless of gender, candevelop their math skills with effort and practice despite initial failures. Analogies drawing on video gameplay were used to il lustrate how slowing down with failures is not a re flection of ability but an opportunity to learn from them and grasp concepts (~learn game mechanics) better. Finally, a discussion of the usefulness of math, both career-wise as well as in everyday decision-making, set up the stage for showcasing how it isn’t an advanced degree but a problem-solving passion and willingness to embrace failures during the learning journey that shape the one’s identity as a mathematician. After parts I, II and III were administered, everyone answered two open-ended reflection questions on which myth they found  most interesting/surprising and why, along with a f inal consolidation question on how participants would approach failure in mathematics differently. Specifically, we nudged t hinking about a time when participants experienced failure (e.g., a topic or assignment that really challenged them) – what did they do back then, what were their emotions? what would they do differently now?

Analysis plan For RQ1 a and RQ 1b, because we expect ed post-intervention self-reported scores to be higher than pre-intervention scores, a one-tailed paired t-test was used for comparison of meandifferences. The non-normal nature of this self-reported data (inferred via a significant Shapiro Wilk’s test) led us to use the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test for this analysis. Rank-biserial correlation (rrb) was used as a frequentist effect size measure and Bayes factor (BF10) was used to quantify strength of evidence disfavoring the null hypothesis. For RQ1c, because the follow-up problem-solving task required developing multiple measures of consistency to rank two soccer players based on their goal-scoring records, we looked at number of solutions participants created and the diversity of those solutions (e.g., use of frequency-based methods) to evaluate growth mindset behaviors like persistence. For RQ2a and RQ 3, AI-assisted thematic analysis (De Paoli, 2024) was used to  explore participants’ open-ended responses regarding their perceptions of grow th mindset myths and approaches to wards failure in mathematics. Initially, generative AI was utilized to inductively identify themes from the qualitative data sources. To ensure the validity and reliability of the gener ated themes, the author, as a content expert, manually verified these themes against the original responses  and reorganized them as necessary. Subsequently, a codebook based on the verified themes was developed to engage a second human rater to assess the se themes and compute interrater reliability (Krippendorff’s 𝛼 = 1 for RQ 2a and Krippendorff’s 𝛼 = 0.68 for RQ3). Taken together, t his approach combined the complementary strengths of generative AI’s efficiency and the rigor of human judgment. For RQ 2b, we first extracted n-grams without stopwords (up to three sequentially appearing word patterns) from participants’ open-ended response on their rationale for the most interesting/surprising myth along with the response length. We then used these features as inputs to a  classic support vector regression (Flake & Lawrence, 2002) to evaluate the explained variance in pre-post changes to participants’ growth mindset beliefs and math expectancies. Because we had data from three different classrooms that were exposed to the intervention, a three-fold cross validation split by the classroom-id was performed to make sure participant data from the same classroom does not end up in the training and test sets, thus prev enting overfitting. Regression performance was assessed using Pearson’s correlation (ߩand mean square error (MSE). JASP (JASP Team, 2024), GPT 4o-mini (OpenAI, 2024), and LightSIDE (Mayfield et al., 2015) were used. Because GPT 4o-mini was accessed via Azure OpenAI service, all prompts were unavailable outside the analysis context, thus safeguarding participant privacy.

## Results

Manipulation checks To ensure implementation fidelity, we looked at two metrics. First, by analyzing the time taken (in seconds) across three key sections of the growth mindset interventi on, in alignment with our expectations, we found th at participants spent sufficient time during part I – introduction to the brain (M = 74.4, SD = 26.88, max = 160.06), part II – growth mindset and its associated myths (M = 106.90, SD = 44.95, max = 274.07), and part III – relevance of growth mindset situated within a mathematical learning context (M = 206.62, SD = 90.23, max = 375.32). Second, we looked at the distribution of word counts  for the two open-ended responses in the intervention. The reflection question on which myth participants found most interesting/surprising had a word count of M = 26.27 (SD = 13.37, max = 64) while the consolidation question on how would they approach failure differently had a word count of M = 33.41 (SD = 21.35, max = 95), showing appropriate engagement. All, but one participants’ response were valid and written in good faith (non-gibberish). Quantitative evidence for growth mindset (RQ1a, RQ1b, RQ1c) Our results for RQ1a showed that post-intervention domain-general beliefs about growth mindset (M = 3.65, SE = 0.09) were reported as significantly higher than pre-intervention beliefs (M = 3.42, SE = 0.08, z = 3.32, p < 0.001, rrb = 0.52, BF10 = 96.05), with very strong evidence disfavoring the null. Results for RQ1b suggested postintervention domain-specific (math) expectancies (M = 3.68, SE = 0.11) to also be significantly higher than pre-intervention expectancies (M = 3.47, SE = 0.10, z = 3.67, p < 0.001, r rb = 0.91, BF10 = 61.56). Results for RQ1c showed that the number of ideas generated during th e follow-up problem-solving task was high (M = 2.82, SD = 1.32, max = 6), with several suboptimal student solutions comprising a mix of methods based on (i) central tendencies (n = 53), (ii) frequency of goals scored (n = 22), (iii) graphing with qualitative insights (n = 22), and (iv) deviation-based methods (n = 87). Despite only n = 3 participants generating the canonical solution,  overall persistence seemed high. Taken together, our intervention demonstrated promising effects relative to previously reported domain-general (e.g., Blackwell et al., 2007; Dommett et al., 2013; Star et al., 2014) and domain-specific (e.g., Boaler et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2021; Gaspa rd et al., 2021) growth mindset interventions, achieving notable outcomes with a concise 25-minute implementation  (1). Furthermore, w hen considering the average effects reported in meta-analyses beyond mathematics for proximal intervention target s like mindsets (Cohen’s d 0.46), expectancies (Cohen’s d 0.18), and goal-directed behaviors (Cohen’s d 0.18) (e.g., Brunette et al., 2023), our findings also indicate a substantial impact. While direct statistical comparisons with these prior studies is beyond the scope of our work, the effect sizes we observed suggest a potentially strong contribution to the field. Qualitative evidence for growth mindset (RQ2a, RQ2b, RQ3) Our results for RQ2a highlighted five prominent themes in participant responses about why they found particular growth mindset myths interesting/surprising – (i) misunderstanding of the literal and metaphorical interpretations of growth in intelligence, where participants conflated cognitive growth with physical changes in the brain, (ii) misunderstanding of the broader implications of a g rowth mindset beyond mere persistence, where participants often equated effort with guaranteed improvement without accounting for the role of effective learning strategies, (iii) misunderstanding of failure as a learning opp ortunity, where participant responses conveyed a be lief that failure is inherently negative and leads to decreas ed motivation, (iv) misunderstanding about the application and accessibility of mathematics, where participants we re surprised to learn about its relevance and utili ty in real-world contexts and counteract their prevalent belie fs of mathematical ability being restricted to individuals with formal qualifications or specific career paths, and (v) misunderstanding of gender and math stereotypes, where participants’ beliefs about mathematics inclusivity was challenged. ## Discussion and conclusion

We developed a growth mindset intervention for secondary school students in the service of making failure desired during learning. In contrast to prior work that has  focused exclusively on domain-general or domain-specific components of such motivational interventions  (Bui et al., 2023), we uniquely combined their complementary strengths to contextualize mindsets within mathemat ics and prioritize connections to failure. Pedagogi cally, we relied on repeated cycles of students making predic tions on myths associated with the brain and growth  mindset before provision of expository multimedia-based instructions. We further performed a mixed-methods analysis to assess the extent to which the intervention impacte d student beliefs about their mental abilities, exp ectancies for future math performance, and growth  mindset behaviors in a novel follow-up math problem-solving task. There were significant improvements in beliefs and math expectancies after students went through our intervention, with post-intervention behaviors signaling persistence with s uboptimal idea generation. By fostering a positive relationship with failure, our intervention seemed to shift participants’ views regarding valuing effort  and prioritizing the iterative learning process over immediate success, a key aspect of growth mindset (Dweck, 2006). In light of recent critiques concerning independent replication and practical implementation of growth mindset findings (Yeager et al., 2019), our results show that brief, well-designed interventions can be impactful even for high-performing students and not just academically at-risk students (Paunesku et al., 2015). Making predictions when engaging with our learning materials also induced interest and surprise, especially during sensemaking with (i) domain-general myths that conflate growth mindset with the physica l growth of the brain and tireless effort devoid of the right learning strategies, as well as (ii) domain-specific myths that conflate an advanced degree or societal gender-based roles with who may typically possess a good l earning mindset within mathematics. Linguistic features drawn from students reflecting on these surprising myths were a better predictor of the  change in growth mindset beliefs and expectancies  than incoming motivational profile of students as well as solely time-on-task factors. Encouraging redundant critical reflection on plausible mindsetrelated misconceptions held by students may facilitate viewing failure as an opportunity for intelligence to grow, while also assisting with disassociating failure from the idea of a continually incapable student (Bui et al., 2023). Qualitative post-intervention data further showed how students shifted their perceptions of failure. Failure was perceived as desired because of (i) being a salient indicator of improvement, (ii) the controllability of actions that could be undertaken to learn from failure (e.g., help-seeking), (iii) the heightened awareness that quick responses may not indicate understanding and that one could  take the necessary time to engage deeply with mathematical concepts, and (iv) how unpleasurable e motions triggered by engaging with failure may fuel future problem-solving efforts. How can we induce and help our students internalize such non-normative perceptions? Designing authentic learning experiences where students deliberately practice failing in a safe space may offer them the opportunity to  gauge the relevance of critical factors like learning strategy usage, speed and emo tional reactions in driving learning through failure.  Designs such as productive failure (Kapur & Bielaczyc, 2012) or guided failure (Sinha, 2022) could offer a pedagogically sound starting point for educators. Limitations of our work stem primarily from assessing only the short-term impact of growth mindset interventions. Given the time constraints that come  with in-vivo studies, we also could not engage students in a class-level discussion as they made sequential prediction s within the learning materials. Future research could consider adding a dialogic component to the interve ntion and assess the impact on math performance and motivation to engage with failure-prone learning situations more directly. Replicatio n in different socio-cultural contexts with other age groups and gender distributions will strengthen generalizability of our findings. Sustaining growth mindset behaviors over longer time scales al so presents an opportunity to design distributed scaffolding that can strengthen resilience in our students and positively impact their academic work beyond mathematics.
