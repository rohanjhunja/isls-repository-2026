# Frame Alignment and Feedback in CT-ified Science Classrooms

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While computational thinking (CT) is an integral part of modern scientific inquiry, STEM teachers struggle to incorporate these ideas in classrooms. We report on the efforts of a design-based implementation research project in which teachers participate in a constructionist co-design process to design and then implement CT-ified STEM units in their classrooms. Using feedback teachers left on student responses from these unit implementations, we utilize a basic bag-of-words model to show that teachers did specifically attend to student learning at the intersection of CT and STEM. We then further explicate this evidence through a qualitative think-aloud interview with one of the teachers who designed and implemented a curriculum based around computational models in biology. What we find is evidence that STEM teachers in CT-ified classrooms can assess both CT and S TEM skills at the same time though this intersection still needs additional attention in terms of tools and PD for teachers.

What we find is evidence that STEM teachers in CT-ified classrooms can assess both CT and S TEM skills at the same time  though this intersection still needs additional attention in terms of tools and PD for teachers. “There was a time when I believed, as many people do, that teachers would be the most difficult obstacle in the way of transforming School…this simplistic belief is, in reality, a far greater obstacle to education change than the fact some teachers are act ually conservative.” (Papert, 1993) Computational Thinking (CT) practices have historically been featured mostly in standalone computer science (CS) courses, resulting in unequal access for students from historically underrepresented groups in CS, such as women and racial minorities (Margoli s & Fisher, 2003). CT has become a necessary and integral part of nearly every discipline, particularly STEM disciplines (Weintrop et al., 2015) given the near ubiquity of computation. The Next Generation Science Standards have made clear that using computational thinking (CT) is a cornerstone of modern science education (Quinn et al., 2012; Wilensky, Brady & Horn, 2014). By embedding CT practices into high school STEM classrooms like biology, chemistry and physics, we can simultaneously improve access to CT for all students, particularly those underrepresented in CS, while also providing a more authentic STEM experience for students in these classes.

Here we describe work done in a research practice partnership between a Midwestern U.S. research university and several urban high schools in a large Midwestern city. Previous professional development programs focused on “CT” have taken various forms, each choosing to treat CT as either a pedagogical framework or as content. For example, Brennan & Resnick (2012)  treat computational thinking concepts as new content (e.g., sequences, loops, parallelism, etc.) to be presented to students through the creation of digital hypermedia projects in the Scratch programming language. On the other hand, Mouza et al. (2017) treat CT as a pedagogical frame by introducing pre-service teachers to the basic concepts of CT and helping them design lessons that incorporated CT concepts. While both works aim to help teachers integrate CT into existing classrooms, they each go about it in a very different manner. The approach we follow here aims to treat CT as both a set of new concepts as well as a pedagogical frame that allows teachers to integrate CT throughout their curriculum (Weintrop et al., 2015). Though other work has focused on evaluating student learning and PD design (Kelter et al., 2021; Peel et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2020), here we are more focused on the teacher’s perspective of the alignment of CT practices with their classroom’s underlying STEM learning objectives –specifically when it comes their own evaluations of student outcomes (Bain et al., 2020). Specifically, we ask the question, how do science teachers attend to student learning in both computational thinking and STEM-content knowledge in a “CT-ified” science classroom? By analyzing data quantitative and qualitative methods from the 2nd iteration of a design-based implementation research (DBIR) project, we show that when tasked with integrating CT ideas into existing STEM coursework, teachers attend to both CT learning and STEM-content learning in their classrooms. The implications of this finding show that, given structured professional development opportunities, teachers see assessing student learning in both STEM and CT practices as part of their pedagogy. 185 Computational thinking professional development This work is part of a larger DBIR project that has already undergone two full year cycles. Within each cycle, first the research team integrated the three overarching goals: 1. Engage students in authentic science practice; 2. Deepen scientific content understanding; 3. Broaden participation in computing, into a 4-week professional development program. These goals are framed as student-level outcomes, yet the project’s  approach to intervention at the teacher-level means that teachers are framed as the main changemakers in each classroom. This is a purposeful choice which shows a clear philosophical  stance when it comes to the role of the teacher in education: that content/curricula alone are not enough to drive deep scientific content learning. We see the teacher as Papert came to see them later in his research–as a world builder, learning guide, and changemaker. As such, it is difficult to imagine any student outcomes without considering how teachers might enable those outcomes in their classroom.

The project found that very little work has been done to study helping teachers in CT-integration (Sands et al., 2018)  and gradually pivoted to designing PD that focused on constructing CT-ified STEM units in a co-design setting (Kelter et al., 2021; Peel et al., 2020; Roschelle et al., 2006), where teachers, framed as pedagogical and content experts, and researchers, framed as computational and educational design experts, worked together to build CT-ified units and explore what CT could look like in each teacher’s unique classroom. The role o f researcher varied from co-design team to co-design team, with some researchers taking on a role more akin to a computational expert.  However, it is important to note that this co-design format is not the same as previous software focused co-design projects, e.g., (Roschelle et al., 2006). The researchers were not software engineers and teachers were always encouraged to take part in the development of any computational tools used in their curricula. In this sense, all team members were working together to construct not simply new curricula, but alsonew understandings of what the modern science classroom could look like. Teacher practice To structure our conversation of practice, we borrow from Windschitl et al.’s (2012, p. 882) definition of teacher practice, specifically its mechanism for dividing practice into a three-step cycle: planning, enacting, and reflecting. While Windschitl et al. did not mean this to be an exhaustive taxonomy of practice, we leverage it here as a framing device to breakdown how we might study emergent teacher practice in a new type of classroom. The final part of teacher practice, reflecting, we see as grounded in not only self-reflection of the teacher on their own practice (e.g., “how do I think today went?”) but also as the process of tracking student learning across an implementation (i.e., looking at and assessing student work for progress toward learning goals).  It is this portion of the cycle we focus on here to answer our question: do teachers in such a CT-ified science classroom attend to learning in both CT and STEM content?

The main data at play in this work is the feedback teachers provide students within each CT-ified STEM unit. Teachers had the ability to leave individualized feedback for each student on each response to each question in their units. It is important to note that while all teachers were shown how this function could be used both live in their classroom and after  students had submitted their work, they were not required to use this function at any point.

Fifteen teachers participated across the two cycles of the project representing Biology, Chemistry, Environmental Science, Physics, and Statistics. They represented six different high schools in the greater area surrounding a large Midwestern city in the U.S. From these fifteen participants came twelve newly designed CT-ified STEM curricular units, each roughly two-weeks long.

Students participated in each unit developed by our participating teachers via a web-based learning management system (LMS). Teachers have access to each student response and can provide both point-based and text-based feedback to students’ responses. Thus, our main corpus of data is the feedback teacher’s chose to leave students. Across the two years of the study, 1219 students participated in CT-ified units contributing 106050 responses to questions. Of these responses, teachers only provided written feedback (i.e. a comment) on only 5654 responses. While the proportion of responses receiving feedback may seem low, the interviews we performed with teachers after their implementation indicate that this is part of their typical practice. This analysis does n ot capture in-the-moment feedback teachers provided in the classroom. At no point were any participating teachers were required to give feedback via LMS so this dataset must be considered using a sort of “opted-in” mindset. It is important to note that this does not  indicate that teachers were not probing students’ understandings –just that 186 they chose not to do so via LMS. For example, one of our instructors, Joshua, did not use the feedback mechanism on the website, yet in his post-interview, he specifically mentioned assessing students’ understanding: …any little bit is going to get in the way of them experiencing the experience they're supposed to have but, like, for the most part of me I’m telling you dude I can't believe how smoothly things went and how frequently when I would check in with students about something they like had the answer I thought they should have, and you know the answers that I looked at, you know as we looked through their work.

Targeted feedback Given that roughly 5% of responses received comment-style feedback, a reasonable question to ask is which questions receive feedback? Recall our research question: how do science teachers attend to student learning in both computational thinking and STEM-content knowledge in a “CT-ified” science classroom ? In this section, we analyze the content of questions where teachers decided to leave comments on student responses. By using some techniques from natural language processing and correlating them with qual itative post-implementation interview responses, we begin to build a picture of how and why teachers target their feedback in these CT-ified classrooms.

The distribution is quite skewed to the left with a secondary mode centered around 50 students. What this shows is that teachers seem to be engaging in two separate strategies: 1. Spot checks where either particular students are targeted for feedback or responses to some particular question are scanned but not everyone receives feedback; 2. Questions that are most targeted With this in mind, we now turn to the content of questions that did receive feedback. By using a bag-of-words model (Bain et al., 2021; Wallach, 2006), we attempt to identify similarities in questions targeted by teachers for 187 feedback. In Figure 3, we look at the most common words (removing common English stop words like articles) among questions that did receive feedback. Here we see that a number of CT relevant terms are particularly well represented in these targeted questions including “model ” (rank 1), “simulation” (rank 4), “data” (rank 14), and “code” (rank 15). We also see several scientific practice terms: “think”  (rank 2), “explain” (rank 10), and “relationship” (rank 20). This would seem to indicate that teachers are indeed targeting questions for feedback that address CT skills as well as science content. In fact, if we compare these most common words to the most common words across all questions in all of the units (regardless of whether or not they were targeted for feedback), we see the CT-related terms are overrepresented in the targeted set  with “model” remaining at rank 1 and “data” at rank 2. An anlysis at the bigrams of those same questions show “netlogo code”, “run simulation”, “collect data”, “run model,” and “computational tools” as the most frequently ocurring bigrams in these questions. This is another indicator that our teachers are indeed attending to both computational thinking and STEM content in the classroom through their feedback practice.

However, the trends in questions targeted for feedback as well as the quantity of feedback itself varies greatly across individual teachers. This could just correspond to existing pedagogical practice. In other words, by studying feedback across a single teacher, we might gain additional insight into teachers’ process of reflection. In the next section, we focus in on the teacher who gave the most feedback across the classr ooms: Edna. Focal teacher – Edna Edna was in her 3rd year teaching biology at a suburban high school in the Midwestern U.S. During the PD, Edna worked on a two-week curriculum centered on the central dogma of molecular biology:  that the flow of information is from DNA (information storage) to RNA (information transfer) to protein (functional product). Edna was particularly targeting two NGSS standards, HS-LS1.1 (construct an explanation based on evidence for how the structure of DNA determines the structure of proteins which carry out the essential functions of life through systems of specialized cells) and HS-LS3.1 (ask questions to clarify relationships about the role of DNA and chromosomes in coding the instructions for characteristic traits passed from parents to offspring) (NGSS Lead States, 2013).

Throughout the curriculum which spanned eleven, 42-minute classes, students used microscopes to look at human hair, discussed how genes could express in different ways (and how they are passed on), and also explored computational models of melanin producti on (Finzer, 2016), DNA/RNA synthesis (using the agent-based modeling language NetLogo), and protein synthesis via a Scratch program (Maloney et al., 2010; Wilensky, 1999).

Assessment interview protocol Rather than just look at Edna’s feedback in the same quantitative fashion as our earlier analysis, here we also rely on a think-aloud assessment interview to get a better understanding of how Edna, as a teacher, provides feedback on student work. The interviewer asks the teacher to first walk them through the process of giving feedback on the student work from the CT-ified science unit, occasionally prompting along three lines of inquiry: 1. Question selection; 2. Science content focus; 3. CT content focus. Then the teacher would do the same for a “traditional assignment” (as defined by them) in their course. Finally, the interview ends with a discussion of how this post-implementation feedback differs (if at all) from the just-in-time feedback the teachers provided in their classroom. Edna’s attitude toward assessment is generally directed more at mechanism than memorization when it comes to STEM content learning: “I don't particularly care about recall of things. I want to know how things are being used, so I don't do a lot of tests. And when I do have tests, you get to bring a “sheet of paper” with anything you want on it.” Her general strategy was to go through student submissions question by question, giving students feedback: “What I have been doing is going mostly by question. Okay, and that, so I can be consistent between students. and honestly, sometimes I will copy and paste feedback if I find myself getting the same kind of thing. ” However, we see (Figure 2b) the same trend in emulated here that we saw earlier in the overall data set: a few questions with most students getting feedback and a separate group of questions with just a few students getting feedback.

We see the secondary mode at around 50 students (close to Edna’s total student count) with a good number of questions targeted for feedback across the entire class. While not an example of a “rigorous” assessment of students’ engagement in CT, this question shows a willingness to ask students to reflect on the process of learning with computational models in her biology classroom. In her responses to students, she was careful to both attend to the models’ ability to demonstrate/represent the underlying science but also what sort of agency it gave students. For example, when responding to students who commented on the NetLogo model being the most “controllable and organized,” Edna left feedback that linked this controllability back to the underlying code of the model: “ I like that about the NetLogo, too (that and I kind of understand how the coding works for them).” Again, we see, even in feedback, Edna linking the students’ scientific experiences back to the underlying computational experiences If we look at the content of Edna’s feedback to students, we also see evidence of feedback pertaining to computational engagement as well as biological understanding. When asked to reflect specifically on her assessment of CT in these units, Edna started off by describing her computational identity: I am not a computer person so I think that it's more identity based. It was like before I went to this program in the summer, if you told me that I was going to code anything I would have laughed at you so hard, it was like later in the year we're going to use the model that I wrote…s o I like I shared that with them. Though not necessarily directly related to the question, she decided to bring computational identity into the discussion after being prompted to reflect on CT. While not necessarily present in her feedback entries, Edna did work in the classroom to overcome identity issues that were averse to engaging in computation in her students. However, this prefacing could also indicate that she still is hesitant in pushing students to engage in CT while also asking questions about CT Practice. In fact, she pivoted to talk about one of her co-design partners, Parth, and his role in helping her design the questions: And there, there are times that [Parth] was like you should put it in a question about just specifically about the model, and I was like yeah that's a good idea, but I wouldn't have thought of it necessarily without that prodding, even though we had gone through those  [demo] units where a lot of those types of questions were normal.

This shows that even though Edna was adept at addressing students’ computational practice in the classroom (as we saw in the previous chapter), she still saw herself as struggling to learn how to more formally assess CT practice. Edna then walked through her process of grading a summative assessment project from the same class, she followed basically the same set of practices: focusing in on elements of the project and looking 191 for understanding of the underlying biological mechanisms at play. She often uses quizzes which provide her “a way of scanning through and saying okay, like…these people are getting these particular aspects of the science.” When asked about her opinion on summative assessment for the course, Edna emphasized that she “preferred projects” and noted that “it was hard to make space for projects in the [CT] format.” Notice that this implies that while the previously discussed SAQs are “summative” in style, Edna’s idea of a summative assessment was much larger than a single question. In the assignment example Edna overviewed, students were given a number of options including creating a comic book about a topic in evolution. It is worth pointing out that this scal e of creative outlet was not featured in her curriculum, indicating a possible disconnect in Edna’s idea of assessment in her CT-ified classroom versus her traditional classroom. In this work we see evidence that teachers engage can engage in deep reflection on how students engage with the computational activities in their units. However, through our analysis of the  feedback teachers left for students, we see many opportunities to engage teachers in how they might adapt their existing feedback practices tonew computational activities. By focusing in on a single teacher’s feedback practice, we saw that while our teachers may be attempting to assess engagement in CT in their classrooms, there are still numerous difficulties to overcome to integrate their usual practices. While here we focused existence, in future work we plan to dive into the content of feedback to more specifically identify what dimensions of CT and STEM our teachers attend to in their practice. Linking this feedback to the in-classroom talk would give us a better understanding of the larger picture of frame alignment between CT and STEM in these class rooms. Finally, by analyzing assessment interviews across all our teacher subjects, we plan to tease out differences in feedback practices across these CT-ified units compared to these teachers’ “traditional practice.” These differences could then be used to construct additional tools to aid teachers in centering this practice in these new classrooms.
