# Qualitative Research as the Co-Construction of Knowledge: Designed and Emergent Opportunities for Equity in Data Collection

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Power imbalances are often operationalized in traditional qualitative research when participants are seen as having “settled” thoughts or truths that a researcher can extract by objectively limiting bias. Like scholars who have advanced humanizing and dignity-sustaining approaches to research (Paris & Winn, 2020), we instead see research as an opportunity to coconstruct knowledge and scholarship through building relationships and conversing as coauthorities. In this paper, we critically reflect on the methodology used in a recent study on community-based, out-of-school time (OST) youth arts programs across the United States, highlighting the ways we designed the study to subvert traditional roles and assumptions about meaning making in qualitative research.

Major issue addressed Conducting interviews and focus groups in qualitative research through an equity lens is about much mo re than including participants from diverse backgrounds. It requires researchers to adapt data collection methods to meet the needs of the communities they engage with, and to approach interviewing as a process of co-constru cting knowledge (Vossoughi & Zavala, 2020). Equitable met hods require researchers to intentionally reflect o n and mitigate power dynamics that may be present (e.g., between researcher and participant, or between mult iple participants in a focus group), and interrogate the  assumed roles played by all interlocutors involved. Power imbalances are often operationalized in traditional interview protocols when the participants are seen  as having “settled” thoughts or understanding that a researcher can extract by objectively limiting bias (Vossoughi & Zavala, 2020). Like scholars who have advanced humanizing a nd dignity-sustaining approaches to research (Denzin, 2015; Paris & Winn, 2020), we instead see research as an opportunity to co-construct knowledge and scholarship through building relationships and conversing as co-authorities. With that approach, interviews and focus groups become bi-directional pedagogical encounters between agents with porous roles whose meaning making is everdeveloping and mutually influenced (Vossoughi & Zavala, 2020).

In this paper, we critically reflect on a methodol ogy used in a recent study on community-based, out-ofschool time (OST) youth arts programs across the Un ited States. Taking a Critical Qualitative Inquiry (CQI) perspective on research methodology (Cannella et al., 2015), we highlight the ways in which traditiona l power dynamics in qualitative research were subverted through intentional design that guided the project. Our reflection is guided by the following questions: (1) How did d esign features allow for participants and researche rs to coconstruct the research experience? (2) What meaning  making opportunities emerged as a result of that c oconstruction? Theoretical and methodological approaches Critical Qualitative Inquiry The history of racism and exclusion in the scientif ic community, in both the natural and social scienc es, is undeniable (Gould, 1996; Bonilla-Silva & Zuberi, 20 08). For instance, writers and philosophers like Zo ra Neal Hurston, W.E.B. Dubois, James Baldwin, and many others who put forward Black scholarship anyway they could, are rarely credited for shaping social thought. However, in an attempt to counteract centuries of disc rimination, scholars have been making an effort to operationalize these philosophers’ work as methodological precedents that inform decolonial perspectives in research (Grant, 2021). In other words, they are returning to writing fro m the past to inform scholarship today, and filling the gaps in literature left by racism and settler-colonialism. Additionally, scholars are exploring new ways to conduct research that move away from discriminatory and paternalistic traditions. The national project we reflect on in this paper is one such example, wh ich was developed in the paradigm of Critical Qualitative Inquiry. Denzin describes CQI as a paradigm within qualitative inquiry that attempts to interrupt dominant methodo logy by taking both an interpretivist and activist role in research, and by “[making] a difference in the lives of socially oppressed persons” (Denzin, 2015, p. 32). That is to say, CQI interrupts the Eurocentric lineage of a cademic research when scholars interrogate and expo se oppressive practices and intentionally break from norms that continue to yoke the field to intellectual elitism and ICLS2022 Proceedings 1113 © ISLS white supremacy (Cannella et al., 2015). Because repressive structures of colonialism often operate invisibly in education and educational research (Kao-mea, 2004, p. 22, as cited in Cannella et al., 2015), the proj ect was designed to (a) subvert power imbalances that result from traditional researcher-participant relationships, and (b) create opportunities for methodological flexibility that can resist oppressive practices as they emerge and embrace a malleable meaning-making process.

## Critical Indigenous Research Methodology

The specific methodology that grounded the national  project in CQI comes from Critical Indigenous Rese arch Methodology (CIRM) (Brayboy et al., 2012). CIRM is described as a reclamation of the Indigenous thought world (Brayboy et al., 2012); it moves away from the assu mptions that limiting researcher voice makes it pos sible to objectively extract meaning from participants’ resp onses, and that it should be the central goal of qu alitative research. A CIRM framework for research is founded on relationship building, respect, and reciprocity (Brayboy et al., 2012). Intentionally fostering relationship s with the goal of taking responsibility, showing r espect, and enacting reciprocity was central to the design of the national project. To build relationships, the foremost priority during the research conversations was to nurture trust among all participants present. This approach was in direct contrast to positivist traditions of science that p rimarily seek to uncover the objective truth from p articipants’ observed behavior and elicited responses. Here, espousing a constructionist epistemology (Denzin, 2015; Brayboy et al., 2012), researchers and interview participants are understood as generating knowledge in conversation with one another.

## Project methodology

The study we reflect on in this paper consisted of a series of research conversations akin to interviews and focus groups with a variety of stakeholders in out-of-sch ool time (OST) arts organizations. The research goa l for the national project was to understand the needs, struggles, and successes of community-based youth arts programs across the United States. Arts organizations known for their work with Black youth, Indigenous youth, and youth of color (BIPOC youth), and who themselves have dee p roots in the community they serve, were purposely selected to participate. Rather than follow a tradi tional snowball sampling method (Merriam & Tisdell,  2016), “which implies “rolling up” relevant participants into our work in a linear fashion, as one person nom inates the next, we describe our approach as more like a “boat down the river”’ (Halverson et al., in press, p. 1 3). Starting with suggestions from our research advisory board, we followed leads to stakeholders, “guiding our boa t down particular river branches based on the types of communities and art forms we thought it important to collaborate with based on what we were learning along the way” (Halverson et al., in press, p. 13). In all, 72 stakeholders participated in the study, including 9 policy makers, 8 researchers, 28 organization leaders/ directors, 17 teaching artists, and 10 you th artists. Our data set included 25 research conve rsations: 12 one-on-one conversations, 6 small group conversatio ns with cultural-geographic regions, and 7 small gr oup conversations with specific OST arts organizations.  The conversations were semi-structured and mirrore d a loosely organized dialogue where the interview prot ocol was more of a guideline than a script. Almost all conversations took place over Zoom, with the exception of one participant who preferred to speak over the phone. Analysis for the national project was an iterative process of (a) emic coding (Saldaña, 2016) that described both the topic and content of stakeholders’ responses, (b) analytic memo writing (Merriam & Tisdell, 2016) to draw connections across responses and explicitly documen t meaning making as it occurred, and (c) regular an alytic meetings to build consensus on the meaning that was emerging from responses.

## Critical reflection methodology

After conducting the original study, five mini-projects were developed in which sub-groups of the research team re-analyzed the data following new inquiries. In this mini-project, we reviewed the data collection methods, the research conversation transcripts, the analytic memos, and the raw video footage in an attempt to understand how design features allowed researchers’ and participants’ to build relationships and co-construct knowledge and the research process together. We re-analyzed the data by coding for moments when (1) either the researcher or the participant took agency in the conversation, (2) where a researcher offered their own perspective or understanding based on what was said, or (3) where participants pushed back against a researcher’s offering, against each other, or against an underlying assumption inherent in the questions being asked. Additionally, we looked for moments where interlocutors shared personal reflections or memories, erupted into laughter, and seemed hesitan t or uncertain in their responses; we investigated these moments as possible examples where trust was being built or had been built.

ICLS2022 Proceedings 1114 © ISLS Critical reflection Flexible engagement Data collection for this project took place the summer after the pandemic hit the United States in March 2020. During a time when home and work boundaries were being upended, we made it a priority to invite participants to engage in the study however they were most comfortable. Before each conversation took place, a research team member would connect with an individual expert or point person for the small groups and discuss their preferences. For instance, one participant did not feel comfortable having their video recorded, and another chose to talk over the phone instead of over Zoom. If one holds that research is meant to extract “objective” truths by controlling for discrete variables that might affect a participant’s response, then this anomaly might have been thought to detract from the consistency of the research or from the reliability of the project’s findings. However, centering CQI in this work meant embracing the anomaly as a feature of the collective expertise we were gathering, not a flaw. Additionally, though we did not expect that being flexible meant offering a phone interview instead of a recorded Zoom call, we quickly adjusted to suit the desires of this participant, pointing to adaptability as a necessary condition for this design feature. We also designed flexibility around what was discussed during research conversations. As is common with semi-structured interview and focus group protocol, all conversations were not conducted identically. We wanted to remain flexible with how conversations unfolded and to prioritize whatever themes OST organization leaders thought was most important for the field to know. For example, it became clear in conversations with Indigenous experts and organization leaders who work with Indigenous youth that the separation between OST and school-based programs does not always exist, particularly in rural locations where some youth access art supplies through their schools. Instead, they chose to talk about the ways in which artmaking is both deeply embedded in some tribal culture and difficult to access through formal educational environments. Designing for flexibility allowed participants to share information that was most relevant to the work they do and take ownership over their participation in the study. Moreover, the flexibility in design also highlighted the importance of adaptability on the part of researchers, which emerged as a critical feature alongside flexible engagement.

Honoring expertise Participants’ expertise is not always valued in research, but we perceived all stakeholders as experts in the field of OST youth arts and experts of their own lived ex periences as artists, youth workers, and members of  the community they served. In other words, the role of “authority,” which is usually reserved for scholars, became porous as everyone who participated in a conversati on was considered an authority. We honored particip ants’ expertise when we invited organization leaders to d irect the program-centered, small group conversatio ns after sharing with them the general themes the research team was interested in. Additionally, organization leaders were encouraged to tailor the conversation questions for  the group depending on what would be most salient to the group based on their insider knowledge.

This design feature was included as a way to subvert traditional roles between researcher and participant, and empower participants to guide the study. Ultimately, we realized this feature also allowed us to highlight and tap into the trust, rapport, and shared knowledge t hat was already established between organization le aders, teaching artists, and youth artists. Some of the mo st powerful moments occurred when the OST organizat ion leaders guided the conversations themselves. Moreover, because everyone was an authority in their own right, the conversations were not one-sided in favor of either  the researcher or the participant. Approaching mea ningmaking in research as a collective process transformed the research conversations into bi-directional pedagogical encounters whereby all interlocutors could learn fr om another. Framing research as an opportunity for both researchers and participants to learn and evolve su bverts the expectation that interlocutors’ thoughts  and understanding is settled and static.

Nurturing relationships Building trust was a key component of fostering meaningful relationships with participants over the span of 4090 minute conversations. As we mentioned above, one  way we built trust was to tap into relationships w here it already existed and allow those bonds to guide the research. Another way we built trust, particularly in the small group conversations, was through the inclusion of a theatre improvisation game. At the beginning of each small group conversation, the researchers led everyone th rough an activity known as Gift Giving (though it p robably has many names), in which one person pantomimes handing another person a gift (in our case, through the Zoom screen). Then the person receiving the gift replies  something to the effect of, “Thank you for the gif t! How did you know that I needed this [fill in the blank] in order to get through the rest of my day?” The perso n receiving ICLS2022 Proceedings 1115 © ISLS the gift then pantomimes using whatever they improvised. This type of warm up game or icebreaker is a common ritual among theatre ensembles. By dedicating 10 minutes to this activity, it was our intention to begin building a connection that would be the foundation of the group conversation. We hoped the activity would provide everyone a point of joyful, silly, and meaningful connection  through a common experience (if they did not alrea dy have one), and a chance to learn a little about each oth er prior to opening up in conversation. Ultimately, the activity invited participants and researchers to acknowledge and reflect on their current state of mind in a fun, whimsical manner that seemed to create space for vulnerability and more candid conversation.

## Conclusion

Although more research is necessary to understand h ow the design features impacted participants’ experiences, we believe these features allowed participants to co-construct the research experience and play an integral role in directing the outcomes of the study. Participants w ere encouraged to take agency over the study and th eir participation in it. Their expertise was honored, allowing the roles of “researcher” and “researched” to be porous and in flux (Vossoughi & Zavala, 2020) and encourag ing everyone in conversation to learn from one anot her. They were met with flexibility, respect, and a genu ine opportunity to foster relationships through tru st. Opportunities for joy and silliness were specifically designed into this study in a way that invited participants to speak candidly during conversations. In watching th e video recordings, we noticed an air of familiarit y and uncertainty among the interlocutors, an atmosphere where they are simultaneously commingling, building relationships, and co-constructing knowledge. Analyzing and understanding the relationship between researcher and participant is not usually a core component of qualitative research even today. Although taking su ch an approach to research is complex and somewhat nebulo us, it is an important step to developing new visio ns of qualitative research as we endeavor to honor and gain insight from communities in equitable ways.
