# Supporting Learning Pathways of Justice-Oriented Data Science in Secondary Science and History Classes: Teachers’ Perspectives

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Data science has garnered interest in K-12 education and has catalyzed designs conducive to learning experiences that integrate data science, disciplinary content, and justice issues. This poster reports on recent progress made by a project that integrates data science in secondary science and history classes following a justice-oriented data science education framework. Teacher debrief meetings after two classroom pilots revealed both productive seeds of justice-oriented data science and opportunities to refine the designed support.

## Introduction

Data science is increasingly relevant to everyday l ife and has garnered attention across all levels of  education including K-12. One productive approach to introducing data science in K-12, as shown by prior efforts, is to treat data science as an opportun ity to engage learners in authentic problems, practices, and actions relevant to data investigations (Wilkerson & Polman, 2020). A salient theme under this approach is to mobilize data science tools and practices to create new opportunities for learners to examine justice issues related to society, attending to the personal, cultural, and sociopolitical dimensions o f data science (Lee et al., 2021). However, more gu idance is still needed to support the integration of data science, justice issues, and disciplinary content in K-12 classrooms. This poster bridges this gap by reporting on teachers’ perspectives on  an effort to integrate data science in secondary science and history classes following a justice-oriented data science education framework. Justice-oriented data science education in K-12 Over the past two years, our team has been working on an NSF-funded project to develop a justice-oriented data science program named DataX for secondary schools. Building on an extensive bod y of literature, we developed a framework to represent the rich intersection of justice issues, data science, and i nterdisciplinary inquiry. This framework calls out five intersecting areas that co llectively form a dynamic space for exploring justi ce-oriented data science (JODS). These areas include:

1. Data science practices: Learners work with data in authentic ways, includ ing wrangling data, making

data moves, generating data representations, and interpreting findings.

2. Disciplinary inquiry with data science: Learners engage in meaningful disciplinary inquir ies in which

they pose questions and tackle them by analyzing data while communicating with others.

3. Examining justice through data science: Learners leverage data science to understand justice issues and

develop tools for tackling these justice issues.

4. Critical reflection on data science: Learners consider the nature of data science as a  field, the ways in

which fairness and biases are reflected, and the connections between data science and societal discourse.

5. Identity and cultural practices: Learners see themselves as people who can use data for purposes that

interest them, recognize connections between data s cience and themselves and their communities, and identify ways to engage in data science in culturally congruent manners. These areas intersect each other and do not have cl ear boundaries, in alignment with visions of justic e and equity as multiperspective and heterogeneous (G utiérrez & Jurow, 2016). The JODS framework highlights multiple entry points to data science and  envisions dynamic and emergent  learning processes that legitimize student experiences with data and position them as active change agents in society (Wilkerson & Polman, 2020). In the DataX project, this framework has been used to guide part icipatory design workshops with teachers, lesson designs, tool development, classroom pilots, and ongoing data analysis of pilot data. The project’s overarching goal is to identify ways the DataX design facilitates learning pathways that illuminate different areas of the JODS framework. Teachers were introduced to the framework and invit ed to critique it and imagine ways to support these areas in their own teaching.  This poster, interested in teacher perspectives, is  focused on the following research question: From the teachers’ points of view, in which ways did the DataX design facilitate productive and improvable learning experiences in secondary science and history classrooms?

## Methods

The project was situated in a university-school partnership in a large midwestern metropolitan area of the United States. People involved in the partnership included  two teachers —one teaching 7th-grade life science and the other teaching 11th-grade history—and four university researchers. The teachers worked in two different schools within the same district, were keen on integrating data from students’ disciplinary engagement, and expressed varied comfort levels with data science. After a series of participatory design workshops th at spanned a year, the project team designed two  units—one for life science and one for U.S. history —that were then enacted in the teachers’ respective classes. The life science unit  was situated in ecosystems and engaged students in  examining a Child Opportunity Index dataset; the history unit  was situated in the voting rights movement in the U.S. and involved students investigating a dataset of U.S. elections dating back to the 1950s. Students in these classes came from diverse racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic backgrounds and had a wide range of prior experiences with data. To answer the research question, the poster primarily draws on two separate debrief meetings with each teacher (4 conversations in total). Before each debrief meeting, researchers spent ti me building conjecture maps representing the design of the piloted units (Sandoval, 2014). The documented design conjectures served as points of departure for researchers’ reflections on what w orked well and what was still emerging in these uni ts. From these reflections, researchers then distilled interview questions fo r each teacher and engaged in one-hour Zoom meetings to discuss their perspectives. Our analysis focused on the teachers’ reflections and started with separate thematic analyses of each teacher’s talk. We then w orked together to propose and reify common and uniq ue themes across teachers/domains, created a prelimina ry set of themes, and then collectively and itera tively reexamined the data and modified the themes through a process of negotiated agreement (Campbell et al., 2013).

## Findings

This analysis identified a number of themes from th e teacher debrief meetings. Themes around productiv e parts of the lesson designs included: (a) Students were engaged when working with data about their neighborhoods and society; (b) The DataX tool was successful in reducing barriers to data visualization and analysis; and (c) Eleventh graders took responsibility and collaborated well with each other in small groups. Of particular interest to this project are themes a round emerging parts of the designs that offer insi ghts for iterative refinement of the designed units. The se themes included: (a) Disciplinary engagement aro und data was present, but synthesizing disciplinary knowledg e was still emerging and required more time; (b) Students were engaged in data practices but did not have opportunities to unpack data concepts (e.g., “what is data”) or go through multiple data cycles; (c) Students worked w ith “messier” and “more real” datasets, which offered opportunities for critical reflection on data, sometimes at the cost of easier data manipulation if they were working with “tidier” data; (d) Students brought personal and community topics into discussions about data, but their ideas might not have been sufficiently taken up; (e) Students saw the potential of data work for social change towards certain justice visions, but also felt unsure wheth er change would actually happen; (f) More support was needed for students to work in small groups, especially with controversial topics.

## Conclusions

Based on our analysis of teacher perspectives, we found productive seeds of all of the components of the justiceoriented data science framework, and yet equally documented opportunities to go further in terms of centering in the classroom a deep understanding of history/scien ce, the political implications of working with data, the shortcomings of data science, and why the work matt ers to students and their communities. Ongoing analysis of student data would provide additional insights for us to continually refine the DataX design.
