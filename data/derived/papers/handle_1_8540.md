# Beyond Belief: Disinformation Strategies and Collective Knowledge Building Led by Jair Bolsonaro in Brazil

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The creation and distribution of disputable information is currently widespread and significant in the political debate. Explanations of this phenomenon usually imply that leaders exploit fragilities of their followers, such as lack of education or plain lack of understanding of the facts. We explore a competing interpretation: using a constructivist lens and building on scholarly work in the learning sciences, we investigate cognitive processes at play in political engagement that goes beyond “blind” belief in leaders. We conjecture that those leaders might be employing efficient learning strategies that legitimize their actions and inspire loyalty.

## Introduction

The crafting and distribution of alternative versio ns of facts is particularly relevant to present-day  political discourse. In this paper, we focus on this trend as it manifests in strategies employed by Brazilian President Jair Bolsonaro, who–in one of many similarities to Donal d Trump–had allegedly made 2,700 false or distorted statements in 1,000 days in office (Freitas et al., 2021). A widely held belief about Bolsonaro and Trump is that their constituencies follow them “blindly” without understanding of the real contents of their messages, and that a “better educational system” would eventually enli ghten these followers. Here, we investigate a compe ting explanation: audiences’ belief in these leaders is not necessarily blind; instead, these leaders and their followers work in tandem to build their own robust theories on the backs of flimsy assumptions and facts–but the resulting theories are strong and internally coherent. Research in information science has found evidence of similar mechanisms at play among alt-right groups (Zuckerman, 2019; Lewis & Marwick, 2017; boyd, 2018). We hypothesize that there are sophisticated cognitive mechanisms at play that go beyond blind belief in a leader, and that these mechanisms are powerful en ough to justify the creation of strong theories tha t guide political behavior. Our investigation focuses on wa ys in which Bolsonaro employs sophisticated activat ors to engage his audience in an active and collective knowledge construction process, which results in the audience’s feeling that they are creating their own knowledge.  Thus, rather than telling followers what to believ e, Mr. Bolsonaro might be (consciously or not) applying efficient teaching strategies. This paper builds on our previous work (e.g., Russo  & Blikstein, 2022; Russo et al., 2021)  to explore potential contributions of the learning sciences to  our understandings of how political leaders influe nce the construction of his or her followers’ political kno wledge. Despite bordering on conspiracism, that pol itical knowledge might sound like robust, solid theories t o those who own them. We draw on a classic construc tivist perspective and on work from the information sciences to analyze video data containing informal conversations carried out by Mr. Bolsonaro and citizens (similar to Trump’s “chopper talks”). With that, we hope to offer a contribution to the learning sciences in the form of new hypotheses to explain modern-day knowledge construction in contexts that affect people’s everyday lives.

## Theoretical framework

Constructivists propose that learners build their own knowledge in interaction with the world, and that individuals assimilate new information according to preexisting  mental schemes (Ackermann, 2001). diSessa (1993) advanced constructivist theories with the Knowledge in Pieces” framework, an explanation to how “exper ience feeds into knowledge.” He claims that children’s intuitions about the workings of the world can be used to advance their understandings of science and, more specifica lly, physics. Those intuitions, diSessa argues, for m phenomenological primitives (“p-prims”), mental str uctures that offer possible explanations to observe d phenomena. As p-prims develop, they come to form more complex mental structures named “coordination classes” (diSessa and Sherin, 1998). These structures suppor t individuals in their process of making inferences  from observable phenomena, sometimes even getting in the way of knowledge supported by evidence. We transfer that concept from physics to political science to formul ate our hypothesis that political knowledge built u pon a learner’s prior “pieces” would be much more solid than that formed by the unidirectional ready-made messages on which political leaders traditionally rely.

This transfer to the social sciences is not novel. Philip (2011) proposed the “ideology in pieces” (Ii P) framework, employed originally to study notions of race and racial justice in schools and teaching. Part of the IiP ICLS2022 Proceedings 1561 © ISLS framework is the notion of “naturalized axioms,” the parallel of p-prims in the context of the social sciences. The researcher posited that individuals’ observations about phenomena in society and politics are heavily contingent on historical and social contexts. In other words, naturalized axioms are described as elements of common sense that people employ as they make sense of the social world. For example, Philip (2011) identified that, to explain why some students are unsuccessful, teachers might use naturalized axioms like “kids would rather play  than work.” Like p-prims, the IiP framework implies, naturalized axioms also form coordination classes. One crucial difference from coordination classes formed by p-pr ims, however, is that there is no universally agree d-upon meaning of commonsense elements like “blame, respon sibility, or fault,” but they acquire socially shar ed denotations for specific groups, depending on the sociohistorical contexts. That is, although coordination classes are originally highly dependent on observable phenomena, the ideology in pieces framework ascertains a slightly different nature to it, now compatible with phenome na pertaining to the social world. These concepts b ecome important when we investigate constructivist theory-building as a cognitive device that citizens emplo y to understand politics and society.

## Data collection and analysis

Mr. Bolsonaro regularly confers with citizens and j ournalists from highly partisan media outlets. We i nitially watched 50 video recordings of those conversations (total length: 12 hours) that were published on You Tube between April 30th and July 26th, 2021. We then red uced the sample to pieces whose content included pr oper dialogues and that contained references to emergent, controversial topics (much of the initial sample resembled fan-idol encounters that are not relevant to the present analysis.) This filter left us with six recordings (total length: 1:18:50). Some of the selected clips had been edited after the original publication to exclude explicit disputable claims about authorities in the federal government,  but we accessed the original versions using the In ternet Archive Wayback Machine. Our analysis is based on those original recordings. After transcribing the videos, we employed grounded theory (Charmaz, 2006), developed initial codes over multiple cycles of discussion between the authors, and elaborated an analysis framework t hat draws on the concepts of coordination classes a nd naturalized axioms. Next, we assigned quotes and dialogue excerpts (N=78) to 3 naturalized axioms that form a larger coordination class and to groups of assumptions that compose those axioms. We then classified the quotes according to the kinds of activators employed in each one. Finally, we ranked the recurrence of activators across naturalized axioms.

## Conclusion and discussion

ICLS2022 Proceedings 1563 © ISLS Our data show that Mr. Bolsonaro employs powerful cognitive strategies similar to those studied in the learning sciences, guiding his followers through an apparent ly efficient knowledge building process. Instead of  simply stating that “elections will be stolen,” Bolsonaro makes available a complex set of facts and assumptions that most likely will lead to his desired outcome. Thus rather than statements, he offers “templates” that support people in their understanding of current political affairs, and engage in Socratic dialogue with followers, techniques that are employed to “activate” a coordination class (that b orders on conspiracy theories) and ensures loyalty among a consistently significant number of voters in Brazil.

Ironically, these strategies are frequently recomme nded by scholars in the learning sciences as mechanisms to enhance learning in classrooms. The f act a populist leader is engaging in collective kno wledge building with followers using these advanced educational strategies offers a counterintuitive explanation of how they can convince followers about flawed theories to legitimize their controversial (and disastrous) programs. One implication of this preliminary research is that ou r development as citizens possibly requires a deepe r understanding of mechanisms employed by contemporar y political discourse that borders, feeds, and taps  into conspiracism. Additionally, this complexifies the apparently simple idea that if people have access to more years of schooling, we will be successful in fighting disinformation. Rather, our educational systems might have to do more than the “instructionist” business as usual, and help learners strengthen their metacognitive skills, engage in deep cycles of inquiry throughout their school life, and become more aware of situations in which processes of critical thinking and theorizing about political af fairs are being manipulated to advance harmful theo ries. As educators and researchers, it is our role to investigate how to avoid irresponsible exploitation of human’s powerful capacity to build robust theories and make sense of complex phenomena even with faulty building blocks.
