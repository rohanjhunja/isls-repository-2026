# From Sound to Code: Creative Workflows and Problem-Solving in Music and Coding Tasks

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores the creative workflows and problem-solving strategies individuals use when composing music with code. By analyzing the experiences of seven participants with varying levels of musical and coding expertise, we identified two primary creative workflows for developing and encoding musical ideas: direct translation, where participants developed musical ideas using non-code representations and iteratively decomposed them into code; and tinkering, where participants developed musical ideas through experimentation with code. Additionally, we describe two methods used to resolve discrepancies between their mental conception of music and the coded output, which we call trial-and-error and analytical sound debugging. Our findings highlight the complementary roles of musical intuition and music theory in shaping participants’ reasoning and problem-solving processes for developing and encoding their musical ideas. We conclude by discussing design implications for these findings, both regarding music+coding platforms and curricula, and extending to broader hybrid STEAM contexts.

## Introduction

Musical creativity is often viewed as a mysterious or almost magical process, but upon closer examinat ion, it reveals itself to be highly patterned and even algo rithmic in nature. The structures that guide musica l creativity are not random; rather, they tend to follow consistent and recognizable patterns, even though these vary between individuals and can evolve over time (Burnard & Younker, 2002). These patterns are deeply rooted in fundamental human cognitive abilities, such as pitch discrimination and rhythmic sensitivity, as well as in subjective aesthetic judgments and preferences shaped by each individual ’s unique experiences. This is also affected by an individual’s past musical experiences and training (Bamberger, 1996; Folkestad et al., 1998), and stud ies have demonstrated that formal musical training augments human abilities relating to sound like tonal patter n recognition (Barrett et al., 2013). Moreover, at a more granular level, the specific tools available to a creator and the affordances of those tools influence the shape of these patterns in ways that can both enable and limit certain paths.

This paper examines how these creative pathways man ifest in the context of music composition with computer programming. While in recent years, educat ors and technologists have sought to explore the cr eative intersection that exists between music and compu ting, much of this prior work has focused on comput ing outcomes. This leaves a gap concerning the character of individuals’ creative processes in this hybrid domain. As cultivating creativity is often one of the stated goals of hybrid arts integrated learning environments (Weisberg et al., 2024), it is critical to build a more comprehe nsive understanding of the nature of this creativit y. To address this gap, we took an interactional approach to stud ying the creative process, analyzing how seven part icipants with varying levels of musical and coding experience constructed musical compositions with code during an openended, think-aloud task. We detail the different creative workflows  and problem-solving strategies that participants used, which in turn reveals  how they reasoned about music and code as well as h ow they deployed their prior knowledge of music and programming. By creative workflows, we refer to how participants progressed through a different series of small, sequential ste ps to bring their musical  ideas from initial conception to coded composition.

## Prior work

In response to the pervasiveness of computing techn ology, many music educators have investigated how technological mediation affects musical creativity. Scripp et al. (1988) found that adults without musical training used music notation software through a gradual proc ess of experimentation and revision, while trained adults employed a more linear, internalized process. Trained musicians often bypassed playback, composing directly on the musical staff and using their internal audiation skills to “hear” the music as they worked. However, this study focused on relatively narrow creative tasks, which focused more on producing “correct” solutions (conv ergent thinking) rather than producing and evaluating mult iple possibilities. Folkestad (1991) identified two  different usages of sequencer software, which allows users to  layer sounds, create patterns, and adjust timing, pitch, and musical effects: supplementary usage, in which the computer was used as a tool for  arranging musical ideas that were developed independently; and integral usage, in which the computer was used as part of th e compositional process to generate and develop musical ideas. Folkestad found that supplementary usage was favored by students with musical training, while integral usage was fav ored by those without. A follow-up study (Folkestad et al.,

1998) identified two compositional strategies: hori zontal (composing separate lines in full) and verti cal

(developing musical layers in parallel). Seddon and O’Neill (2003) found similar patterns, with trained musicians engaging in less exploratory behavior.

Another kind of music technology involves using com puter code as a compositional medium. Utilizing code as a form of representation goes beyond mere mediation and provides learners access to an entirely new set of tools and abstractions (Bamberger & diSess a, 2003). However, while cultivating creativity is often a stated goal in music+coding educational settings, as well as in STEAM education more broadly (Perignat & Katz-Buonincontro, 2019), educational work with music+co ding platforms has primarily focused  on music as a motivational context for learning computing concepts (e.g. Lusa Krug et al., 2021; McKlin et al., 2018). Some of this prior work has sought to remove obstacles to CS learning by simplifying or removing music theory concepts that might otherw ise pose challenges. In this way, music is often po sitioned as a tool that supports computer science education outcomes rather than as an equal,  mutually reinforcing domain. Although much of the prior work has treated creativity as an abstract and vagu ely defined concept, most definitions have converged on three main constructs: the product, the person, and the process. In the field of music+coding, several studies have adopted a product-focused conception of creativity that seeks to evaluate creativity based on the tech nical sophistication or novelty of the completed pr ojects. In a recent paper, Rahimi et al. (2024) presented an aut omated formative assessment of a music+coding proje ct’s creativity using an operationalized model of creativity in the EarSketch platform. This model considers a “musical product creative if it contains structural and comp ositional complexity, is expressive within a given context, and may contain elements of unpredictability” (p. 2). M andanici and Spagnol (2023) examined the use of the  Pure Data platform in a music programming course for und ergraduate students to enhance computational thinki ng. Their qualitative assessment revealed that while so me students created algorithmic music, others used Pure Data as a MIDI sequencer to capture more conventional musical ideas (2023, p. 394). In a follow-up study, participants reported increased computational thinking, creativity, communication, and collaboration (Mandanici & Spagnol, 2024). In addition, projects were categorized as “c onvergent thinking” (CT) if based on a single idea and “divergent thinking” (DT) if exploring multiple ide as. DT projects were assessed for fluency, flexibil ity, and originality, while CT projects focused on technical robustness. While product-based evaluations of creativity are common, other frameworks have emphasized personand place-centered conceptions. These approaches shift the fo cus from the final outcome to an individual’s characteristics, traits, and abilities as well as t he influence of the environment on creative express ion. One such study found that learners using EarSketch exhibited significant improvements in attitudes towards computing and creativity, which the authors link to learners’ per ception of the learning environment (Engelman et al., 2017). A related study with EarSketch found improvement in s tudents’ attitudes toward computing and their inten tions to persist in the field. Path analysis revealed that t he perception of an authentic learning environment,  particularly the act of sharing creative work, played a key role in predicting persistence (McKlin et al., 2018). However, these attitudes towards creativity may or may not correlate with actual creative skills. Finally, the process definition of creativity emphasizes the sequential stages through which creative ideas emerge and are developed; this definition highlight s creativity as a structured and recursive process,  moving between divergent and convergent thinking, and dire cted towards creating a product. Divergent thinking  is a thought process that generates multiple possibiliti es without focusing on a single outcome, while conv ergent thinking involves evaluating options and narrowing them down to arrive at a specific solution. Freeman  and Magerko (2016) presented a theoretical model of the compositional process that is highly iterative and interactive; they posited that the instantaneous feedback that computers afford facilitates a higher degree of experimentation, but this is not yet supported by empirical evidence. Similar to Folkestad (1991), Horn et al. (2022) identified two strategies that TunePad users employed in recreating a song they had previously created in TunePad. A “bottomup” approach decomposed the song into manageable chunks and coded them in chronological order. Alternatively, a “top-down” approach started with structural units and gr adually filled in detail. However, this recreative task was likely not representative of the participants’ creative process, as they had previously developed their musical ideas. In a recent paper, Roberts and Horn (2024) examined how music and coding intersect as a hybrid practice, which they termed “computational musicking.” They o utlined this as a set of interlocking processes, wh ich transformed practices from both domains. They found  that participants engaged in creative processes characterized by translation between musical and computational representations. We argue that the way that much of this work has co nceptualized creative thinking is largely disconnected from the rich traditions of arts schol arship, which views creativity as a fluid, social, and iterative process shaped by context, collaboration, and exper imentation (Halverson, 2013; Sawyer, 2018). Rather,  these models of creativity have tended to prioritize the end result, often sidelining the process-oriented and collaborative aspects of creativity that are central to artistic practice. Edwards (2010) suggested that this focus on a final product may hinder learners’ creative expression by imposin g preconceived notions of what counts as correct or  valid. Additionally, in their interpretive review of STEAM  literature, Perignat & Katz-Buonincontro called for a shift from artistic products to artistic processes saying, “creativity development is fostered, not through the art materials or products, but through a process of exploration, play, risk-taking, making mistakes, self-evaluation and feedback” (2019, p. 41). In this paper, we aim to e xpand on previous research on the creative process in music+coding to develop a more nuanced understandin g of how individuals navigated this process, ultima tely supporting more open-ended and process-focused learning environments. Music theory This study used TunePad, an online music+code envir onment (Horn, West, et al., 2022). TunePad projects  are organized as computational notebooks called playboo ks, containing cells with Python code, multimedia c ontent, and text. Code in TunePad represents musical elements like notes, rests, chords, and percussion sounds. The core feature is the playNote function, specifying numerical MIDI note and durati on parameters. Additional modules provide variables for note names, rhythmic values, and diatonic scales and chords. In much of Western music, diatonic scales serve as the fundamental organization of pitch, establishing the tonal foundation for melodies and harmonies. A scale is formed by a distinct pattern of intervals between its notes, usually consisting of both half and whole steps. Each scale is built around a centr al note called the tonic, which serves as the “home” pitch. This structured a rrangement of intervals around the tonic allows mus icians to create compositions with a sense of key, or tonal c enter, which is central to much of Western music. MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface) is a system that assigns numerical values to each musical note,  ranging from 0 to 127. These numbers correspond to specific pitches across a standard musical range, with 60 representing Middle C. Finally, beats are a measure of duration and timing, which can be combined to create rhythmic patterns. Beats are often expressed in powers of two, with ea ch symbol representing a specific duration, such as whole notes, half notes, and quarter notes.

## Methods

Our participants were seven undergraduate students participating in training programs to prepare them for teaching summer programs with TunePad, with data dr awn from a larger corpus of interviews conducted fr om 2022 to 2024. Each participant had varying levels of experience with both coding and music, ranging from being a complete novice in both domains to having advance d proficiency in one or both areas. Participants we re asked to compose a short, original melody using TunePad. Participants were asked to think aloud (Ericsson & Simon,

2002) about their musical process as well as to vocalize musical ideas by humming or singing. They had creative

freedom over musical parameters like tempo, key, and structure, with the goal of creating approximatel y 30 seconds of music. This composition session lasted 25 –45 minutes, followed by a brief interview about the ir process and background in music and computing, with the total session lasting about an hour. To make sense of this data, the first author induct ively coded video data of the creative portion of t he interviews to capture descriptions of participants’ actions and their compositional process using NVivo software. This process segmented data into short, discrete ev ents representing the actions they took, such as “w riting code for a rest,” “changing note parameter,” or “playing  melody on virtual keyboard.” These short events we re most often delineated by participants switching between different representations, such as moving from look ing at the track keyboard to writing code. For the next phase of analysis, these short events were aggregated int o larger sequences that represented what we interpreted as t he intention of the participants, using their utt erances to triangulate. The boundaries between larger sequences typically involved either a moment of listening and “moving on,” or a shift in how participants interacted with  a given representation, such as transitioning from  writing to editing code. For instance, within a sequence of events that are oriented towards encoding a musical idea, a typical sequence might include singing part of a musical id ea, finding the starting note on the keyboard, writ ing code to play a note, checking the piano roll notation, and then listening to playback of the note. In addition to identifying and classifying participants’ actions, the first au thor took extensive analytical memos that captured how the participants reasoned about their musical ideas.

## Results

This analysis revealed qualitatively different ways for generating and evaluating musical material, which we detail in subsequent sections.

Creative workflows We found that participants used two main strategies  for generating and encoding their musical ideas, w hich we are referring to as creative workflows. By creative workflows, we seek to characterize th e overall patterns of behavior through which participants brought their m usical ideas to life, from an initial idea to its f ormal coded representation. In other words, these workflows des cribe how participants progressed through different  series of small, sequential steps to complete the same task. Usage of these strategies was not mutually exclusive, although all participants favored one strategy over the othe r. These two creative strategies will be summarized  in the following sections.

Direct translation We call the first strategy “direct translation.” Th is method begins by developing a musical idea fully  using noncode representations and then iteratively encoding it by breaking it down into notes and rhythms. Participants first used more familiar forms o f musical representation —for instance, a virtual keyboard or vocalizing —to fully develop their musical idea. With their chosen representation(s), they experimented and selected between different musical ideas. They then iteratively translated thi s to code,  evaluating the “correctness” of the sound output of their code against their memory of the idea, and en gaged in further sound debugging until they found c oherence between the two representations  (see figure 1). In this strategy, experimentation and coding were  separate processes, and code in TunePad was used as a record ing device to capture musical ideas that were devel oped using more conventional forms of musical representa tions. That is to say, code was not i ntegral to the creative process—how they developed and chose between different musical ideas—but was rather used to document these musical ideas as a recording medium.

One approach to determining pitch was to u se the TunePad track keyboard, which displays both the note name and MIDI value, allowing the participant to look up each pitch. Participants would then sing or play th rough their musical idea, stopping to look up each individual pitch and writing code using these values. Alternat ively, some participants  applied their knowledge of music theory to mentally calculate the note value based on its position in a musical scale. For this, they either calculated the MIDI value based on the previous note and their  knowledge of the pattern of intervals of a scale o r took advantage of the builtin constants library that define d variables for note names. In the first instance, me ntally calculating a note value involved converting their mental understanding of pitch as a note within a sc ale into a chromatic, numerical representation. In the latter case—using the constants library, as Morgandid—the platform scaffolded this work by allowing them to leverage t heir knowledge of the notes in the scale in a modal ity more familiar to them. For Morgan, this meant he had to “look up” note values less often on the keyboard; when he did have to check a note, it was either to find his starting point or for a larger leap between values. Four of our participants (Sal, Ian, Desmond, and Jan) exclusively used direct translation, while two other participants (Morgan and Lewis) partially employed direct translation. We found that participants who primarily used this approach often spent less time coming up with musical ideas, but more time encoding and debu gging these ideas. Thus, there was a heightened focus on the coded product rather than the actual music content, as a majority of participants’ time was spent encoding a nd debugging their musical ideas. The process of developing an initial idea used more familiar forms of musical  representation, whether digital instruments like a  keyboard, other embodied forms (e.g. singing, humming, drummi ng), or entirely mental processes (i.e. audiation).  T his process was generally more internalized for partici pants with some amount of musical training, utilizi ng fewer external forms of representation.

Tinkering The second strategy, which we call “tinkering,” was  demonstrated by three participants. This process, in which participants took advantage of code to generate cre ative ideas, is analogous to the tinkering behavior  that is a fixture of many constructionist learning environmen ts. Borrowing from Lévi -Strauss, Turkle and Papert (1990) described bricolage as an exploratory, hands-on style of programming, treating code elements as mat erials to be experimented with and rearranged. Building on this and work from other constructionist thinkers, Resni ck and Rosenbaum described tinkering as a “playful, experi mental, iterative style of engagement, in which mak ers are continually reassessing their goals, exploring new paths, and imagining new possibilities” (2013, p. 164). In this strategy, participants experimented with code as they might improvise with a musical instrument. The compositional process did not follow a strictly  linear path, but rather shifted between divergent and convergent thinking and coding. Participants cycled between trying out ideas by making adjustments in code and evaluating the result of those changes  (see figure 2). That is, the musical ideas were largely formed th rough working with code, rather than being generated separately and translated into code. For participants who utilized tinkering, experimenting and encoding were coinciding processes that fed into one another. Figure 2 The Compositional Process in Tinkering This method sometimes began by translating an idea or by using a familiar “stock” pattern (such as a “four on the floor” beat) that participants had pre viously encountered. This initial musical idea migh t have been seeded through more conventional representations, but this idea only served as the germ. The participants listened and evaluated this idea based on personal taste and aesthetic judgment, asking themselves, “do I like this?” They then refined the idea by making changes in the code  by changing parameters for notes and rhythms. Graduall y, the participants honed in on the correct or cohe rent solution by iteratively adjusting parameters, listening to the result, and re-evaluating until the desired outcome was achieved. We observed that when participants were able to use  the translational method —playing a musical idea using representations more familiar to them and dec omposing it into code —they tended to use that approach. However, as they approached the edges of their mu sical capabilities, they shifted to tinkering. For instance, tinkering was used by Lewis to create a processed s ynth sound in the background of his composition, wh ich he had a rough concept of what he wanted but did not k now exactly what it might sound lik e. He started off by looking at the available synths, saying, “there is a Wawa sound somewhere, I think… but I have no idea what i t sounds like.” He tried out this synth, playing a lo w bass note and remarked, “now that ’s kind of what I want, except I wanted [it] to be like, sort of… I don’t know.” He continued by adding different musical effects until he reached an idea he liked. In this way, the tinkering approach worked as a way for learners to explore music without regard to ability, hear how different ideas sounded, and begin to find correspondences between sound a nd its different representations.

Whereas participants who engaged in direct translation alternated between various intermediate solutions in search of one that matched their mental model, participants who engaged in tinkering alternated between ideas without any one specific destination in mind. From the perspective of the participant using tinkering,  they alternated between different musically viable or “c orrect” ideas. Note that in saying “correct,” “inco rrect,” or “coherent” we mean from the perspective of the participant rather than by applying an external music theory lens. Experimentation in code was driven largely by intuition based on approximation, or a rough idea of what needed to be changed: for instance, “this is too slow/fast ” or “this is too low/high.” They then made a chang e in the desired direction, evaluated the new output, and made further changes if necessary. In determining which changes to make, some participants also leveraged their kno wledge of music theory. This music theory knowledge narrowed the space of possible options. In other instances, participants had a rough idea of what they wanted, but did not possess an exact means of accomplishing it. When Morgan and Lewis used this tinkering approach, it was when they could not otherwise produce the idea through other means. Morgan, who e xpressed that his music theory knowledge was a weakness in writing a chord progression, utilized the builti n chords module as a starting point. This tool, whi ch provided chord options within a given key, allowed Morgan to  bypass this perceived lack of knowledge and adopt a tinkering strategy, moving between multiple musical ly viable options until he found one he liked. He t ested out different possible chord options much in the same w ay that Susan tried different notes, however he did  not have to consider the “correctness” in the same way that Sus andid. Sound debugging In the process of encoding a musical idea, the part icipants listened and evaluated both the aesthetics  and the perceived “correctness” of their coded musical idea. At any point, this process might diverge into wha t we call sound debugging. This process seeks to reconcile differences betwe en the participants’ mental conception of the musical idea and the code representing it, and it e nds when they reach coherence—that is, when they believe the result is correct or good enough, even if it do esn't fully match the original idea, whether intentionally or unintentionally. We found that participants most fr equently attempted to debug rhythmic errors. We hav e identified two different approaches to sound debugging, which we will describe below. For both strategies, the participant had to translate between code, sound, and their mental conception of the musical idea. Trial-and-error debugging As with tinkering, trial-and-error debugging began with an intuition about what was wrong, such as a note being too high, or a rhythm too fast. The participant the n made a change to parameters in code in a given di rection and appraised these changes. This repeated until they reached coherence: they believe d that the code matched their initial musical idea or was “good enough.” Unlike t inkering, which primarily involved aesthetic judgme nt as the evaluation heuristic, these changes were in service  of reaching  a “correct” solution and required participants to leverage their aural skills to determine whether the code matched their mental conception of the musical idea. As with tinkering, having knowledge of music theory dr astically reduced the space of possible solutions a nd thus translated to less time spent debugging.

For Desmond, much of his time was spent debugging i n this way. One note in particular —a large downward interval within the scale—became a sticking point. At one point, he paused to  evaluate: “If you listen to the entire bit until here, it sounds good. But I 'm trying to find what should be the last note beca use I have it in my mind.” Desmond began with MIDI note 70, which he had deemed “incorrect.” He alternated between playing notes on the keyboard and singing, trying to find a  matching note value in TunePad. After changing his coded note value to 68, he played around with other notes  on the keyboard, both above and below his target. He began to express frustration, saying, “68 is down, and I would like something like up... so maybe 71. Let’s try 71, or maybe something higher.” He tried 71 and listened i ntently, but it again didn’t match. “So, 71 is a no, let's look at the keyboard again.” Changing his direction, he lowered it to 67, which was within the scale he was  working in, but still didn’t match the note he had been singing. “67… No, it’s too low,” he said, shifting it back up to 68. Deciding to shift focus, Desmond inspected the rhyt hm in a different section, noting that it was “too slow.” Desmond seemingly blurred the line between tinkering and debugging as he worked to align his code with the musical idea he had in mind. He had a rough sense of how it should sound, but refining it proved challenging. In his utterances, he repeatedly used language that suggested he was evaluating correctness: “No, no, no, no,” he muttered, or “Now this is wrong.” Throughout his pr ocess, he used variou s musical representations: listening to the output, finding correspondences between sound a nd code with the piano roll, and testing out differ ent notes in code and with the track keyboard. He developed theories based on his intuition, speaking in terms of magnitude and direction of intervals between notes (“too low” or “too high”), and then tested these theories in code, building up correspondences between sound, code, and other forms of representation. Analytical debugging In this approach to debugging, participants first i dentified an error through playback and then employ ed more structured strategies to determine which change to make. In diagnosing the error, they drew on their k nowledge of music theory and formal reasoning about sound. T his process typically involved a greater proportion  of time spent listening but fewer changes in the code. For example, Jan heard a mistake in the rhythm of her melody. She repeatedly listened to the section of her coded mel ody and then sang her intended idea to compare the two representations. This allowed her to locate the exa ct position of the error in her code. Then, to dete rmine the correct value, she tapped out the beats on the desk  while singing to calculate the correct beat value.  In a separate instance, Jan was trying to determine the duration of a note for her bassline. She had already determi ned what MIDI value to play for the final note but was not sure what the rhythmic value should be. She  knew the total duration of her idea was eight beats, or two measures. To determine her final beats value, she counted the number of beats for the code she had already written and subtracted this from the total beats. As mentioned previously, most errors that participa nts identified were related to rhythms rather than pitches. This is likely due to the affordances of t he platform, as participants could more easily “loo k up” pitches using other forms of musical representations, such as the track keyboard displaying note names and MIDI values. A common approach to identifying pitch involved singing or humming the note, then using the track keyboard to match the pitch. This strategy was present both in participants who had prior experience with keyboards and those without. Thus, reasoning about pitch was largely unnecessary in favor of this more direct “algorithm” for mapping sound to its numerical value.

## Discussion

In this paper, we have identified two qualitatively different ways of generating and encoding musical ideas, which we call creative workflows, as well as ways of evaluating and debugging the code representing these ideas through sound debugging. We identif ied two strategies that students utilized in their creative process: direct translation, in which a musical idea is created separately from coding and iteratively decomposed into code; and tinkering, in which the development of a musical idea and the cod ing of that idea are concurrent processes. As demonst rated in Morgan’s example, these strategies were not mutually exclusive, although participants did tend to favor one or the other. We also identified two methods for how p articipants resolved discrepancies  between their mental conception of a musical idea and the coded version.  For trial-and-error debugging, participants began with an intuition about the problem, followed by iterative code adjustments and assessments until coherence wi th the intended musical idea was reached; while for analytical debugging, they employed a set of more formal strategies to identify and resolve the error.

Additionally, we found that the pattern of interact ion at the event level for the tinkering creative workflow and the trial-and-error sound debugging was identical: listening to the out put, making changes to parameters in existing code, and reevaluating. The principal difference between these processes was the intention of the participant. With tinkering, a participant sought to generate musical material, while sound debugging aimed to reach a previously developed “correct” solution.  This similarity between  tinkering and sound debugging has implications for how we might study learners’ creat ive process, particularly given computational metho ds that analyze log data. Solely looking at behavior was not enough to distinguish between the patterns. In analyzing the intention, we had to take into account participant utterances as well as the match between the ideas that they sang or played on the keyboard and the output of their c ode. Given automated analyses that seek to characte rize behavior through looking solely at disconnected act ions, how might we account for or triangulate parti cipants’ intentions?

Given that we have observed several participants directly transcribing their musical ideas to encode them note-to-note as they might with other formal notations, suc h as standard music notation, the question should b e asked of how code is functionally different as a mu sical representation. To this end, we must consider  the affordances of note-based encoding—in which the user specifies musical compositions by defining each note and rhythm—as a musical structuration. By musical structuratio n, we refer to the encoding of musical information (such as pitch and rhythm) according to the represe ntational infrastructure used to express that infor mation (Wilensky & Papert, 2010). The note-based paradigm is widely utilized by many music+cod ing environments, including TunePad, Jython, Sonic Pi, and Scratch. Bamberger (1996) criticized the usability of this structuratio n for students in MusicLOGO for having a high barrier  of entry while also not illuminating the larger st ructural units (e.g. phrases and motifs) of music.  Specifically looking at Scratch, Payne and Ruthmann  (2019) further critiqued the usage of MIDI—which specifies numerical values ranging from 0 to 127—to specify pitch as it may obscure tonal relationships between notes. We obser ved that the use of MIDI to  define pitches asked users to create a conceptual mapping of notes from a diatoni c system (i.e. major and minor scales) to that of a  chromatic system, in effect superimposing one over the other.  Whereas traditional notation maps note and rhythmi c values to a discrete set of symbols, in TunePad, participants had to directly manipulate these numerical values. Therefore, we see the numerical, note-based paradigm of encoding music as perhaps more esoteric than traditional forms of musical notation, in that it moves from a small, finite set of symbols to a larger and more abstract set of numerical pitch values and potentially infinite set of beats values. Future research should investigate alternat ive structurations for representing music in code and i nvestigate the mechanism of musical development as well as explore differences between individuals with musical training and those without. This paper builds on prior work in music education (e.g. Folkestad, 1991; Scripp et al., 1988) by both replicating earlier findings on compositional proce ss in a computational environment, but also buildin g a deeper understanding of how individuals are reasoning diff erently, pulling from prior knowledge in music. One  of the focal points of our analysis concerned how particip ants leveraged their prior music theory knowledge t o reason about and refine their musical ideas. While music theory has sometimes been framed as a potential barrier to entry that should be removed (e.g. Magerko et al., 2016),  we observed that music theory—both intuitive and formal— served as a powerful tool for participants in organizing their perceptions and reasoning about sound and its formal representation. In particular, participants pulled from their knowledge of musical scales and beats va lues, which helped dramatically narrow the size of the space of  possible solutions while tinkering with and debugg ing their coded musical ideas. T he platform also scaffolded some of this knowledge for participants, as in the case of Morgan’s use of the chords library. Therefore, we see it as essential that tec hniques for reasoning about pitch, rhythm, and harmony be either taught in music+coding learning environments or scaffolded by learning platforms. We believe this has broader implications for what i s taught in other hybrid STEAM learning environments, extending beyond just the combination  of music and coding. In such hybrid settings, domain-specific tools play a crucial role in how learners reason and engage with different subjects. For exam ple, in music+coding, learners not only needed to understand programming concepts but also how these concepts interacted with musical structures. We might imagine similar connections in other hybrid learning environments. For instance, learners creating computational poetr y would likely benefit from learning about poetic f orms in addition to techniques for text generation; or, lea rners creating algorithmic images would benefit fro m learning about color theory and image composition as well as  how color is encoded in images. However, this migh t look very different between artistic disciplines, partic ularly as some do not have widely used formal repre sentational systems (such as the visual arts or dance). We believe that it’s important for educators and designers to think more expansively about hybrid learning environments, mov ing beyond traditional siloed disciplinary boundari es. In this way, STEAM is not just about using the arts to boost engagement in STEM, but about creating opportunities for learners to engage deeply with and make connect ions between artistic and technical domains  while empowering them to express ideas that are meaningful to them.
