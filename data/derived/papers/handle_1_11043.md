# Leveraging Student Choice and Interest to Design an Engaging Lesson about Artificial Intelligence

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) continue, we can expect that students will live and work with AI. It is important that students know how this technology works and its impact on their futures. This paper looks at a lesson that was designed to foster secondary students’ interest in AI technologies, and to help students consider how AI can be used to improve jobs and solve problems. This lesson was designed using the principles of self-determination theory (Reis et al., 2000) to support student engagement and interest.

## Introduction

With the continued advancements of artificial intelligence (AI), young people will likely work with AI in their future jobs, even in fields that have not been previously associated wit h technology. It is therefore critical to prepare all students for this new future (Jiang et al, 2023). N evertheless, AI is typically taught in elective courses, which have had persistent diversity issues (Code.org et al., 2021). We therefore  designed a module about AI that requires no computer science knowledg e and fits within a  high school  English Language Arts course to ensure access for all students. Our module was designed according to the principles of selfdetermination theory (Reis et al., 2000)  and a s students gain ed autonomy, competence, and relatedness, we found that the students gained an understanding and interest about AI and AI-powered careers. Our module and its final lesson Drawing on the AI4K12 Big Ideas in AI (Touretzky et al., 2023), we have created and tested a 10-hour module to support secondary students to learn about text classification and mac hine learning, and to begin to envision themselves in a future with AI-powered careers. Most module exercises are completed online, although there are opportunities for groupwork and full class discussi on. Throughout, students explore, create, and test text classification models, as well as learn how these models are used in everyday life. In the module’s final lesson, once students are familiar with how machine learning and text classification works, students explore  existing and emerging AI applications. Throughout, students are given several “meaningful choices” to gain ownership of t heir learning and tailor the experience to their ow n individual interests (Parker, Novak, and Bartell, 2017). First, students choose an application of a  text classification model  from a list to explore. Students then select a professional (whose short summary of job tasks are provided) and suggest how a text classification model could support their job performance. After this, students consider  a problem they see in the world (at any scale) and brainstorm how AI could contribute to finding a solution. Finally, students choose a short video to watch about AI applications, and are asked to imagine themselves taking on one of the jobs descri bed within. Overall, students are challenged to imagine their own roles in an AI-powered society, considering both their future employment and their personal goals. During the 2022-2023 school year, our module was used with 437 stud ents across five schools and seven teachers in the southern and eastern United S tates. The teachers worked with  middle and high school students in a mix of foundational computer science courses and English Language Arts courses.

## Summarized results

A major goal of this entire module is to support students in seeing themselves as someone who will be successful in an AI-powered career in the future. We want to help students develop the awareness that AI will likely be a part of their future whether they end up as a computer scientist, teacher, or veterinarian. Looking at the exercises in the final lesson individually showed that students were engaged and thinking about how AI is, and can be, used in the world and in professions. Students were describing how the models could to help their selected professional, w ith 32% of students even creating their own or tail oring existing models to be even more effective.  Student s also skillfully applied the models to solve their  own problems. To give a holistic example, we share the story of Corey  (pseudonym). While there are hundreds of similar vignettes, Corey’s story exemplifies how students engaged with the module and made sense of AI. Corey’s story Corey, an 11th grade student taking Honors English in the Eastern  United States, selected “other” as their gender, and identifie s as non-Hispanic or Latino. Corey had a bit of a background  in AI, as they explained that they already knew  that AI learned from examples. After high school, C orey plans to get a doctorate in Entomology. In the final activity, Corey selected to help the Drama Director. First, Corey recommended that they use a model to read emails from students and families to identify themes and i nterests for next school play. Second, the Drama Director could use a model to rea d and sort plays into categories to narrow the sele ction process. For the global problem, Corey addressed the spread of antisemitism. Corey felt that a model could use a similar process to the Sexism Detector they had analyzed earlier in the module. “By training a model to recognize common antisemitic words or tropes, it co uld help social media managers understand where the se ideologies are spread most and perhaps how to decre ase or better contain the spread of said ideas.”  Corey described the value of AI models to make the world a better place  and explained how AI could be used in a variety of settings, showing that they understood A I to be flexibly used for myriad purposes. In Corey ’s own words, “During this module, I learned about how AI is trained & taught as well as AI's applications in real life.”

## Discussion

From the overall student responses, and the ongoing  analysis of the implementations, we have evidence that the students were not only enjoying the final lesson  (with an average interest score of 2.48 on a 0-4 scale), but also internalizing the content and applying it to the re al world. Students were able to explain how the models worked and found ways that AI models could help both profe ssionals and themselves. Many of the students found  ways to connect with the material through their choices and were able to explain how AI models can help themselves and others to perform their jobs, do day-to-day activities, and fix global problems. We credit much of this success to the grounding in self-determination theory and our deliberate design to allow for autonomy, competence, and relatedness.

At the end of the module, the students shared with us their thoughts and lingering questions about AI. One student expressed, “I wonder how much it will grow and progress. And I know it will impact my life more and more the older I get.” Responses like these sho w that the students were thinking about the impact of AI on their futures, and wondering about changes. This su ggested that students were engaging with the module material and that the module aims were met. As AI i s a growing field, questions like this are importan t. These students will grow up in a future that we have yet to understand, and their wonderings about it now ar e the first step in helping them be prepared for this future.
