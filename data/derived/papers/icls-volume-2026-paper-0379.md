# What is CT Integration and Why does it Matter?: Educator and Researcher Perspectives

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
What computational thinking (CT) integration is and why it is important are often not defined in research and practice. This poster examines researcher and educator definitions of CT integration and their motivations. We found that educators and researchers described CT based on concepts (e.g., problem decomposition) and solution-oriented capabilities. They focused on CT integration as cross-disciplinary. Respondents grounded their rationale for CT integration in building competencies and literacies and school improvement and reform.

Computational thinking (CT) can support students as they live, learn, and work in today’s technological world (Mills et al., 2021). Education systems at the district, state, and national level have recognized the importance of CT, highlighting it in district learning progressions (Coenraad et al., 2021), state mandates (Maryland State Department of Education, 2018), and national standards (CSTA et al., 2024; NGSS Lead States, 2013). CT integration has become a key mechanism for students to be introduced to CT skills (Dunbar et al., 2025). Yet, while CT integration is being used and researched at the classroom (Coenraad et al., 2022; Rich et al., 2020), school (Israel et al., 2022), and district (Coenraad et al., 2021; Dunbar et al., 2025) levels, what CT integration is and why CT is being integrated with subject-area content is often minimally defined or missing. In some cases, the fact that CT is relevant across subject-areas is taken as a given within its definition (e.g., Barr & Stephenson, 2011), and in other cases, defining CT integration revolves around the level at which CT and subject-area content are integrated (e.g., Coenraad et al., 2022; Waterman et al., 2020). To create more clarity for teachers being asked to integrate CT with their subject-area content and bound what CT integration is, we examine data from researchers and educators to explore two research questions: (1) What does CT integration mean for educators and researchers? and (2) Why do educators and researchers integrate CT with subject-area content? Theoretical frameworks We define CT according to Barr and Stephenson’s (2011) framework for integrating CT which focuses on three interrelated dimensions: CT concepts, capabilities, and dispositions. CT  concepts include core ideas such as data designing and implementing solutions. Finally, CT dispositions  emphasize the values and attitudes that support CT practice, such as confidence with complexity. This framework was used to answer our first research question investigating “what” educators and researchers perceived to be CT involved in integration. There are differing motivations for CS and CT. Vogel, Santo, and Ching (2017; 2019) identified seven visions for CS education centered on (1) economic s and workforce, (2) equity, (3) competencies and literacies,

(4) civic engagement, (5) technological, scientific, and social innovation, (6) school improvement, and (7)

personal agency and joy. While distinct, these visions are often referenced together, demonstrating multiple areas of impact that individuals feel CS and CT can have for students. This framework was utilized in answering our second research question concerning “why” educators and researchers integrate CT. We analyzed a survey of researchers and educators identified by our research team and practice-side partners as experienced in CT integration. Twenty-nine people (16 educators, 13 researchers) completed the survey. Respondents were asked two questions about CT integration: (1) In your work, what do you mean by integration when you say CT integration? and (2) Why, if at all, do you think integration of CT with subject-area content is important?. The survey data were analyzed deductively using a codebook focused on what CT integration means in terms of concepts, capabilities, and dispositions and why participants thought CT integration was important developed from the theoretical frameworks above. The coders also utilized an “other” category that was analyzed inductively to unpack its meaning and subsequent patterns to add codes, such as cross-disciplinarity. Two researchers coded all responses. Disagreements in coding were discussed to reach 100% agreement. 2563 What CT means to educators and researchers We examined the CT concepts, capabilities, and dispositions respondents referenced when describing CT integration. Among concepts, problem decomposition (8 of 29), algorithms and procedures (5 of 29), simulation (5 of 29), data analysis (5 of 29), and abstraction (4 of 29) were most frequent. Many respondents cited the technical aspects of CT: “there are places to decompose, recognize patterns, use algorithmic thinking, abstract, debug, reason with data, and think about your thinking.” Capabilities emphasiz ed designing solutions (9 of 29), using diverse learning strategies (6 of 29), and modeling and systems analysis (5 of 29), with less attention to testing and debugging (3 of 29), reflecting and communicating (3 of 29), and group problem solving (1 of 29). These counts show respondents associated CT integration with creating and applying solutions more than with collaborative or evaluative practices. Dispositions were rarely mentioned, with only one instance of comfort with open-ended problems: “It [CT integration] means incorporating more open-ended problem-solving opportunities within the realm of their core content.” Overall, educators framed CT integration as structured problem solving and design, with little focus on dispositions or collaboration as was seen by Rich et al. (2020). Cross-disciplinarity During coding, we found respondents  frequently mentioned cross-disciplinarity when defining CT integration. While not part of the original framework from Barr and Stephenson (2011), which takes  the integrated nature of CT as a given, it was mentioned by 23 of 29 respondents. Yet, the nature and depth of cross-disciplinary connection differed between respondents. Some respondents expressed a shallow cross-disciplinary connection of applying CS in context by stating, “ Using CS in discipline specific learning activities. ” Conversely, other respondents cited a necessary depth and synergy between CT and subject-area content, for example stating, “I like to frame it as "mutual-supportiveness" - that CT helps students learn disciplinary content (e.g., math, biology) while disciplinary content provides context for the use of CT concepts/skills.” This range is potentially why prior work in CT integration has illuminated levels of integration (Coenraad et al., 2022; Waterman et al., 2020), and why the nature of that integration and its connection with students needs further unpacking. Why educators and researchers integrate CT Educators and researchers highlighted six of the seven CS visions as motivation for CT integration (all besides citizenship and civic engagement) without substantial differences between the two participant groups. Most commonly, respondents highlighted competencies and literacies as their main motivation for CT integration (25 of 29). Additionally, respondents commonly cited school improvement and reform (14 of 29), economic and workforce development (8 of 29), equity and social justice (7 of 29), and personal agency, joy, and fulfillment (6 of 29) as important motivators for their CT integration. Highlighting both competencies and literacies and economic and workforce development, one respondent shared, “ It prepares students for [the] 21st century problem solving world. Even if students do not…go into a CS career..LIFE requires we think and work in a CT way. The earlier they learn the CT concepts the better.” Educators and researchers most often described CT integration through technical concepts and solution-oriented capabilities, with little emphasis on dispositions or collaboration. The prominence of problem decomposition, algorithms, and designing solutions shows CT framed as structured problem solving, while frequent references to cross-disciplinarity highlight its role in supporting disciplinary learning. Educators and researchers grounded their rationale for CT integration in the need to build competencies and literacies for students and school improvement and reform. This demonstrates a current focus on discrete skills and alignment between skills and subject-area content. By considering how CT integration is defined and ensuring it does not take just a skills focused approach, researchers and educators have an opportunity to expand conversations of CT integration and the related literacies to connections between CT and focal topics in today’s schools and that have been part of school reform efforts such a s socio-emotional learning and portrait of a graduate or durable skills. Looked at individually, the percentages of respondents mentioning specific components of the Barr & Stephenson (2011) framework is low. Because many responses were broad rather than practice specific, these counts taken discretely may not reflect the full range of participants’ understanding of CT integration. Additionally, this study is limited by its small sample based on self-selection as to whether to answer the survey, but points to the need for future research on how to foster dispositions and collaboration and deepen cross-disciplinary connections. 2564 Barr, V., & Stephenson, C.
