# Scaffolding Knowledge Building Processes through ONA-Supported Collective Reflective Assessment

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study designed a learning analytics-supported reflective assessment that utilizes ordered-epistemic-network-analysis (ONA) tool to scaffold students’ reflection on their epistemic actions by visualizing their knowledge building (KB) discourse moves. KB, supported by Knowledge Forum, emphasizes students’ collective contributions to community knowledge advancement. Previous research indicated that learning analytics-supported reflective assessment can effectively foster students’ metacognition and regulation; this study extends to ONA-based reflective assessment. Data were obtained from two classes of elementary-school students (n = 86) engaging in collective inquiry into Earth sciences; experimental students experiencing ONA-based reflective assessment. Quantitative analysis indicated that students in a designed KB environment enriched with analytics-supported reflection showed improved domain understanding and social interactions. Qualitative analysis showed how students engaged in the KB journey and how ONA-supported reflective assessment promoted their collective inquiry. This study contributes to knowledge-building pedagogy by demonstrating that reflective assessments on students’ discourse as epistemic engagement can promote collaborative knowledge development.

## Introduction

Knowledge building is a major approach in computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) that focuse s on fostering a knowledge community where students coll ectively inquire and create knowledge (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2014). In knowledge-building classr ooms, students take up collaborative responsibility  for advancing community knowledge, including continued pursuit of inquiry, sustained idea improvement, and enduring theory revision. Knowledge building/knowledge creat ion is supported by collective inquir y on Knowledge Forum – with students engaging in epistemic actions and spe cifically discourse moves to advance their knowledge. In CSCL, collaborative inquiry can  be supported by learning analytics (LA) that provi de visualization of students’ learning proc ess and their learning (Wise, Zhao, & Hausknecht, 2014). LA-supported reflective assessment in KB, where students used vi sualization of KF work to reflect on what they have  inquired and what can be inquired further, has been shown ef fective for identifying  knowledge gaps and promising directions for engaging in productive knowledge-building (Tong & Chan, 2021; Yang et. al., 2020). Different KF analytics tools such as word-clouds (Resendes et al., 2015), KBDeX (Knowledge Bu ilding Discourse Explorer) (Oshima, Oshima, & Matsuzawa, 2012) have emphasized  development of ideas. There is relatively limited work focusing on students’ reflection on their knowledge building discourse moves as epistemic actions, how students engage in discourse moves in contributing to knowle dge building processes. As a typical learning analy tics method, ordered-epistemic network analysis (ONA) (Shaffer, Collier,  & Ruis, 2016) has been employed by researchers to investigate the temporal patterns of  students’ epistemic actions in knowledge-building environments (Zhang et. al., 2024). This study aims to investigate further the roles of reflective assessment using learning analytics tools, including ONA visualization, in supporting students’ knowledge building. Knowledge building for collective knowledge advancement Knowledge building is defined as a community-based collective learning process highlighting sust ained theory generation and idea improvement (Bereiter & Scardam alia, 2014). Knowledge building focuses on collecti ve knowledge creation within a learning community and aims to improve the community’s knowledge, with individual understanding being a byproduct of this process. Like experts engaged in knowledge creation, students in a classroom community create novel and coherent explanations that expand the boundaries of shared knowledge and add value to the classroom community (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2010). The focus of KB pedagogy is to create environments that are  likely to promote the emergence and improvement of new ideas under the guidance of a series of principles (Scardamalia, 2002),  that emphasize knowledge improvement processes, complex problem-solving and knowledge construction, and the development of students’ creativity and higher-order thinking skills (Hong et al., 2015). Students need to take responsi bility for collective knowledge advancement  (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2014), which requires students to reflect and regulate their inquiry processes. Central to knowledge building is Knowledge Forum® (KF), a computer-supported collaborative learning environment develo ped to provide spaces for collective knowledge building, where students engaged in formulating problems, building o n ideas, co-constructing explanations, drawing on authoritative  sources, revising their theories, reflecting and synthesizing their ideas.  These epistemic actions,  manifested as discourse moves, have been  a major area of inquiry and analysis in KB research. Typica lly, these discourse moves are examined by research ers such as in depth of inquiry (Hakkarainen,  Lipponen, & Järvelä, 2002), ways of contribution (Resendes, 2015), and knowledge-sharing, knowledge-construction, and knowledge-creation coding schemes (van Aalst, 2009). Analytics-supported reflective assessment in knowledge building Reflective assessment requires students to engage i n metacognitive activities involved,  reflecting on what they know and need to know, and employing regulatory strategies, including making plans, monitoring processes, and reflecting on their understanding (Scardamalia, 2002).

Previous studies on knowledge-building suggested that reflective assessment can help students focus on inquiry goals and improve their knowledge-building discourse (Tong & Chan, 2021; Yang et. al., 2020). Electronic portfolios were utilized to scaffold ref lective assessment in KB, in which students evaluat ed and reflected on their performance (Lee, Chan, & van Aalst, 2006). In recent years, learning analytics has been embedded into reflective assessment as they can pro vide visualization of students’ perform ance on KF. Knowledge Connection Analyzer (KCA), a web-based assessment tool, candisplay quantitative data on students’ KF participation and qualitative information from t ext-based tables (Yang et. al., 2016). Through these quantitative and qualitative data, students reflect ed two questions embedded in KCA: Are we a communit y that collaborates? Are we putting our  knowledge together? It has been suggested that KCA can scaffold students’ reflective assessment and improve their achievement  (Yang et. al., 2016). Knowledge Building Discourse Explorer (KBDeX), a social network analysis tool, c an visualize the connections of collaborative ideas  and discourse on KF (Oshima, Oshima, & Matsuzawa, 2012). KBDeX-supported reflective assessment can help students visualize the keyword networks of their KB discourse, allowing them to reflect on what has been inquired about and identify promising directions (Yang et. a l., 2020). Idea-friends maps using KBDeX were designed to show students the relations among concepts in KF no tes for students to reflect on current inquiry and making linkages (Feng et. al, 2019).

Previous studies on LA-supported reflective assessment in KB mainly focused on the reflection of what ideas were improved (content), while few studies sc affolded students to reflect on how ideas were improved (epistemic actions). Scardamalia (2002) suggested t hat we not only need to focus on the idea improveme nt-but also emphasize students’ engagement in improving their ideas collectively; focusing on the process through which students take responsibility for collective knowled ge advancement (Oshima et al., 2019). As a commonly used learning analytics method, ordered-epistemic network analysis (ONA) has been used by researchers to investigate temporal patterns of discourse. Some researchers em ployed ONA to analyze temporal patterns of students ’ epistemic a ctions on KB and gained a deep understanding of stu dents’ engagement in collective knowledge development (Zhang et al., 2024). However, most cur rent studies utilize ONA primarily by researchers, rather than by students, to self-evaluate their learning processes. We conjecture th at when appropriately used, ONA visualization can scaffold students’ reflection on discourse moves as epistemic actions. U nderstanding the discourse moves could help with how ideas improve and facilitate students’ community awareness and community knowledge advancement. Therefore, we designed a learning analytics-supported reflective assessment design that involves KF6 analyses as well as ONA visualization to scaffold student s’ reflections on their learning processes. This study aims to investigate the effectiveness of  the designed ONA-supported reflective assessment. The research questions are: ● RQ1: Do the students using analytics-supported reflection have better domain understandi ng and social interactions than those only use KF6 analysis-supported reflection? ● RQ2: How did the students engage in reflection activities and community knowledge advancement?

## Research participants and context

This study was conducted at a primary school in Shenzhen, China, involving 86 fifth-grade students (two classes) participating in a science inquiry on Changes to the Earth’s surfac e. The course lasted four weeks, with two lessons each week, each lasting 45 minutes. A quasi-experimental design approach was adopted; the experimental class had 44 students, and the comparison class had  42 students. The inquiry processes were conducted on the Knowledge Forum® (See Figure 1).

The notes have “build-on” connections under the same discussion topics. This study treated sepa rate notes without arrow links to other notes as separate discussion topics.

Pedagogical design and analytics-supported reflective assessment (experimental class) This study employed an instructional design adapted  from previous research (Tong & Chan, 2016; Yang et. al., 2020), to encourage students to engage in collectiv e inquiry practices in the KB environment. The peda gogical design was based on several KB principles, includin g community knowledge, idea improvement, epistemic agency, collective responsibility (Scardamalia, 2002). This pedagogical design scaffolded students to co-construct ideas advancing community knowledge, emphasizing im provable ideas, sustainab le inquiry, and collective reflection. Specifically, analytics-supported reflective assessment is incorporated to support knowledge building. Overall design Phase I:  Developing a co-constructive inquiry culture and generating initial  questions and diverse ideas (Lessons 1 to 2). At the start of the course, all students were encouraged to write down their initial questions and ideas regarding Changes to the Earth’s Surface on Knowledge Forum. They worked in the same view (same webpage) on KF, post ing their questions and ideas and build-on others’ ideas and questions.

Phase II:  Deeping their ideas and inquiry (Lessons 3).  In this phase, students were organized into small groups to engage in advanced inquiries on various sub-topics (e.g., How are earthquakes formed? What are the causes of volcanic eruptions? What imp act does wind have on the surface of the Earth? What impact does precipitation have on the E arth’s surface?). Students worked as opportunistic groups as they could work in their specialized groups as well as contribute to different groups building up collective work (Zhang et al., 2009) Phase III:  Conducting experiments (Lessons 5).  They were encouraged to make an experimental plan a nd conduct hands-on experiments (e.g., Simulating a volcanic eruptio n using mashed potatoes and tomato sauce and simulating Fault Earthquakes with Soil.) to deepen their understanding of Changes to the Earth’s Surface.

Following Phases II and III, students wrote many KF posts, and an analytics-supported assessment was conducted (see description below) Phase IV:  Integrating knowledge and summarizing the whole pro cess (Lessons 6 and 8). In Lesson6, each group conducted a presentation to s hare their inquiry process and key findings to the whole  class. This week, students discussed the connections among  sub-topics in the classroom during the presentations. At the end of the course, all the students were required to contribute an individual synthesis note to reflect how their domain understanding  changed during the collective inquiry practice. Next, all the students were encouraged to synthesize what they had learned and what they wanted to inquire further on KF at the class level with the synthesized scaffolds. Analytics-supported reflective assessment design After Phase II and Phase III (in lesson 4 and 7), t he student groups were scaffolded to engage in grou p reflective assessment and writing group reflection worksheets on KF writing. Researchers showed students differen t KF6 analytics tools on social interactions as well as ONA visualization. The KF6 analyti cs tools included the number of notes each student created, the network of who r ead whose notes, and the network of who build-on whose notes.

The ONA visualization (Figure 2) was produced first  by analyzing students’ KF discourse coded into epistemic actions based on their discourse moves (T able 1). A modified scheme was developed, adapting from other coding schemes, and the ONA visualization ill uminated the temporal transitions among the epistemic actions. The reliability of the coding scheme has been examined in previous studies (Zhang et. al., 2024). Students were shown visualization s of their KF work using KF6 analytics tools and ONA visualization in conjunction with selected KF posts. Student reflection was scaffolded by a worksheet co ntaining five guided questions: (1) What did we inquire? (2) What do we plan to further inquire about? (3) Have we participated in the collective inquiry (from the visualization of KF participation and social interactions)? (4) Have we contributed to the collective knowledge development (from the visualization of ONA)? (5) Which epistemic actions should we increase in the next inquiry? ## Data collection and data analysis

Domain knowledge: pre-post tests Before the course, students in both classes were as ked to complete a pre-test to assess their understanding of the subject matter related to the Earth’s surface. The pre-test lasted 20 minutes and included 16 multiple-choice questions related to Changes to the Earth’s Surface. After the course, all students took a post-test that lasted 40 minutes and consisted of multiple-choice questions, true/false questions, and short w ritten answers. T-tests were conducted to compare the pre-test and post-test results in both the experimental and comparison classes to analyze the effectiveness of the pedagogical design.

Social interaction: social network analysis The “who read who” and “who built on whose notes” matrices derived from the Knowledge Forum log data were transformed into social networks to characterize students’ social interactions. In the “who read who” network, the nodes represent students, while the  edges indicate which students read each other’s Kno wledge Forum notes. In the “who build-on who” network, the nodes likewise represent stude nts, and the edges indicate which students built on each other’s Knowledge Forum notes. In soc ial network analysis (SNA), some important measurem ents of nodes can capture social interactions of individual participants – weighted degree and closeness centrality (Knoke & Yang, 2020). In this study, weighted degre e means how many times one student read/built-on others’ notes. Closeness centrality denotes the distance from one student to other students in the network. Mann-Whitney U tests were employed to compare those SNA indices between the experimental and comparison classes. Student engagement in analytics-supported knowledge building ONA visualizations were produced based on KF discou rse coding for epistemic action, and student worksh eets were collected for examination.  After the reflective assessment, interviews were conducted with several students in experimental class. The interview transcript and  reflection worksheets were collected to analyze th eir engagement in ONA-supported reflection activities. A qualitative anal ysis was conducted to examine how students generated initial ideas and inquiry interest in Phase I, deepened collective inquiry in Phase II and Phase III, and synthesized community knowledge in Phase IV.

## Result

RQ1: Did the students in the experimental class have  better domain understanding and social interactions than those in the comparison class after instruction? RQ1-1 Domain understanding The independent samples t-tests showed a significant difference in pre-test scores between comparison class (M = 12.26, SD = 2.013) and experimental class (M = 10.27, SD = 3.378), t (84) = 3.335, p <.001. This indicates that students in the comparison class possessed a higher level of domain understanding than those in the experimental class prior to the intervention. The t-test results for post-test scores displayed significantly higher levels of domain understanding than those in the comparison class (M = 79.45, SD = 8.990; M = 72.10, SD = 13.288; t (84) = 3.021, p <.005). Additionally, students in the experimental  class achieved significantly higher scores in short-written answers than those in the comparison class (M = 20.36, SD = 2.114; M = 17.00, SD = 4.030; t (84) = 4.874, p <.001). Answering short written questions requires s tudents to have a deeper understanding of the conte nt; this indicates that students in the experimental class attained a deeper level of knowledge. RQ1-2 Social interactions based on SNA We examined students’ social interactions using SNA  indices of “who read who” networks and “who build-on who” networks in the community. The Mann-Whitney U test was applied to compare the SNA indices (weighted indegree, weighted outdegree, and closeness) between students in experimental class and comparison class (Table 2). In Phase I and Phase II, before the first round of ONA-supported reflective assessment, there are no significant differences in the SNA indices between the two clas ses. The students in the two classes had no differe nt engagement in contributing to the community before the designed intervention. In Phase III, after the first round of ONA-supported reflective assessment, students in the experimental class showed significantly higher weighted indegree and weighted outdegree both in the “who read who” network and the “who build-on who” network. That suggests that students in experimental class using ONA-supported reflection tended to read more others’ id eas and reply to others’ ideas. Students in the experim ental class exhibited significantly higher  closeness centrality than those in the comparison class in the “who buil d-on who” network. In Phase IV, after the second roun d of ONA-supported reflective assessment, students in the ex perimental class showed significantly higher closen ess in the “who read who” ne twork than those in the comparison class. That indi cates, after the ONA-supported reflective assessment, students’ social interactions demonstrated increased closeness. RQ2: How did the students in the experimental class engage in knowledge advances and ONA-supported reflection?

We presented the key events from students KF discou rses, students’ reflection worksheets during the re flection activities, and interviews after the reflection act ivities to analyze student engagement in community knowledge advancement and in ONA-supported reflective activities. Theme 1: Generating initial questions and ideas In Phase I, students posted their ideas and questions on KF reg arding Changes to the Earth’s Surface  on KF to foster a culture of improving community ideas. They  expressed interest in natural phenomena that can c ause changes to the terrain, such as earthquakes, volcan oes, wind, and rain. At the end of the phase, four promising questions were identified based on discussions on K F (e.g., How are earthquakes formed? What are the causes of volcanic eruptions? What impact does wind have o n the surface of the Earth? What impact does precipitation have on the Earth’s surface?).

Theme 2: Seeking preliminary explanation and engaging in deep theory building. Students interested in the same questions engaged i n collective inquiry on KF. For example, for the qu estion, “Why did the volcano erupt?” one student explained, “Because of crustal movement.” and another student added that “Under immense pressure, hot magma will break throug h the weak crust. ” Through their collective efforts, students deepened their understanding of those questions. Theme 3: Conducting science experiments or synthesizing community knowledge Some students conducted scientific experiments to g ain a better understanding of the Changes to the Earth’s Surface. For example, students interested in the volcano sim ulated the volcano eruption employing mashed potatoes and tomato sauce. Other students create Mi nd Maps to synthesize community knowledge discussed earlier (such as the changes brought by volcanic eruptions to the surface and the impact of volcanoes on humans). Theme 4: Analytics-supported reflective process During collective inquiry processes, the students e ngaged in two rounds of reflection activities. They  reviewed their discussion by looking back at the notes poste d in the previous week and reflected on their parti cipation in KF writing by analyzing the visualization of the KF participants’ histogram, social interactions, and ONA figures of how they take epistemic actions.

After being shown the ONA figure, the students disc ussed the features of the visualization. They successfully find the nodes’ sizes, lines’ thicknes s, and lines’ direction on ONA figures bearing diff erent meanings. The primary students realized that the no des mean their different epistemic actions (i.e., q uestions, explanation) in KF writings, the nodes’ size means if they continue the same epistemic actions after the previous actions, the lines’ direction means the temporal transition of the epistemic actions, and the lines’ thickness means to what degree the transition is important. Third, the students in each group analyzed the ONA figure of their own sub-question and the visualization of KF participation and social interactions, and then, reflected on th eir epistemic actions and participation in KF writing.

Then, they wrote the reflection worksheets that inc lude “what we inquired” (e.g., the reason for the volcano’s eruption), “what we plan to further inquiry” (e.g., how to prove the reason for the volcano’s eruption), “have we participated in the collective inquiry (from the visualization of KF participation and social interactions)” (e.g., we engaged in the collective inquiry by posting not es and reading others’ notes), “have we contributed to the collective knowledge development (from the anal yzation of ONA)” (e.g., we improved ideas and generated further questions after post initial ideas), and “which epistemic actions should we increase in next inquiry” (e.g., we should increase synthesize actions).

To scaffold students’ reflection on their ONA analysis, four sub-questions were provided in the reflective worksheets. The scaffolding reflection sub-question “Did we generate new questions based on the existin g ideas?” was intended to help students think about community  knowledge as something improvable rather than repetitive and un-growing. The following interview excerpt shows how one student reflected on the ONA figure and the scaffolding question:  “ Regarding last week’s ONA figure, we have very few Further-Questions and Improved Ideas. We have been studying the old questions. Some old  questions have already had answers, but there were still many peers who keep discussing tho se old questions.... I think it's because our class mates didn't read others’ notes....” From the above excerpt, we found that this student analyzed the ONA figure and identified the gaps (“have very few Further-Questions and Improved Idea;” “many peers who keep discussing those old questions”). This demonstrates useful insight as the student can  now see that they keep discussing old questions re peatedly and propose very few further questions. Based on his analysis, the student appeared to reflect on the reason behind the gap (“our classmates didn't read others’ notes”). This suggests that the visualization of epistemic actions is necessary to foster knowledge advancement intended with the scaffolding reflection question “Did we ge nerate new questions based on the existing ideas”.

The scaffolding reflection sub-question “Did we synthesize diverse ideas?” was intended to help students become aware of the importance of rise-above. The following reflection worksheet excerpt s hows how students reflected on the ONA figure and the scaffold questi on: “Regarding last week’s ONA figure, we improved the existing ideas, but we didn’t synthesize diverse id eas. We need to post more notes on KF and should sy nthesize ideas....” From the above excerpt, we found that the students analyzed the ONA figure and identified their deficiency (“we didn’t synthesize diverse ideas”). Based on the analysis, they proposed the improvemen t on epistemic action (“We need to post more notes on KF  and should synthesize ideas”). This suggests that the visualization of epistemic actions is necessary to promote students to synthesize diverse ideas. Theme 5: Sharing and diffusing community knowledge After conducting scientific experiments and drawing  mind maps, students who inquired about each questi on shared their knowledge in the class. They continued to refine and integrate ideas to deepen and diffuse community knowledge. For example, after listening to other cl assmates’ presentations, one student further raised  a new question, “ What are the connections and differences among the effects of wind, precipitation, volcanoes, and earthquakes on terrain?”

## Discussion and conclusion

This study examined the role of analytics-supported reflective assessment using ONA visualiza tion and guiding questions to scaffold students to reflect on their discourse moves on KF and modify these actions in s ubsequent inquiry stages. The quantitative results indicate t hat students in the experimental class, who engaged  in ONA-supported reflective assessment, demonstrated better domain understanding and social interactions based on SNA compared to those in the comparison class. Specifically, students in the experimental class achieved higher scores in domain understanding and exhibited a deeper unde rstanding of knowledge than those in comparison cla ss. Additionally, they read more notes from their peers  and built-on more of their classmates’ notes, sugges ting an increased awareness of connecting with others and a  greater contribution to community knowledge develo pment following the ONA-supported reflective assessment activities. The qua litative analysis revealed how students in the experimental class engaged in knowledge advances integrated with the ONA-supported reflective assessment. The students analyzed their ONA figures and identified their deficiencies, noting a need for more epistemic actions related to Further Questions after existing Ideas, Improved Ideas after original Ideas, and Synthesizing diverse ideas.

Consistent with prior studies in knowledge building (Tong & Chan, 2021; Yang et. al., 2016), we found that reflective assessment can effectively assist s tudents in reflecting on their inquiry processes, o rienting future directions, and adjusting knowledge-building strategies. This study shows that ONA-supported reflective assessment can help students reflect on their KB in quiry process by providing visualizations of the te mporal patterns of their epistemic actions. This study als o highlighted the design illuminating key themes fo r students’ knowledge-building trajectory that could be enriched with ana lytics-supported assessment. With the ONA-supported reflective assessment, students actively engaged in metacognitive and regulation activities pointing to further inquiry. This study contributes to knowledge-building pedagogy by demonstrating that reflective assessments on students’ epistemic engagement can promote collaborative knowledge development.
