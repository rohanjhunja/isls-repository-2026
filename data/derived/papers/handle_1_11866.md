# Exploring Students’ Interdisciplinary Collaborative Learning Outcomes and Processes with Varying Degrees of Knowledge Heterogeneity

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Interdisciplinary collaborative learning (ICL) is key to 21st-century education. Knowledge heterogeneity (KH) plays an important role in interdisciplinary collaborative learning, but few empirical studies investigated how different degrees of knowledge heterogeneity shape the ICL in detail. This quasi-experimental study investigated how various degrees of knowledge heterogeneity affect student groups’ ICL outcomes and processes. A total of 48 undergraduates participated in this study. Group solution reports and verbal discussions were collected and analyzed. The findings showed that groups with a higher degree of KH performed significantly better than those with a lower degree of KH in adopting disciplinary insights. Different behavioural patterns were identified in knowledge integration, problem-solving, and coordination during ICL.

## Introduction

The 21st-century challenges, such as climate change,  require different ways of thinking and the development of new skills (Kidron & Kali, 2015).  A ddressing these challenges necessitates knowledge, skills, and methods beyond the boundaries of a single discipline (Schmidt, 2008). Given the complexity and time required to develop expertise in even a single discipline, undergraduate students need interdisciplinary collaboration to tackle complex problems that span multiple fields (Mansilla & Dura isingh, 2007). In ICL, students can benefit from KH, which refers to the variety of knowledge, know-how, and expertise that an individual can access th rough their group (Rodan & Galunic, 2004). This heterogeneity provides valuable learning opportunities and serves as a driving force for change, fostering the development of students’ knowledge and skills (Vuojärvi et al., 2024). KH plays a crucial role in knowledge integration because nonredundant and diverse information encourages the combination of ideas, leading to innovative and improved solutions (Tsai et al., 2015). However, KH is not always conducive to knowledge integration in ICL. When knowledge het erogeneity is too high, gaps in shared understandin g between new information and team members’ existing knowledge can complicate coordination and hamper effective knowledge integration (Zhang, 2023). Its impact varies depending on the degree of differe nce between disciplines. Although existing research has explored how KH influences the collaborative learning process (Zhang & Wang, 2021), there remains a critical gap in unde rstanding how different levels of heterogeneity aff ect both learning outcomes and processes. Therefore, this study investigate d how higher and lower degrees of KH shape the quality of interdisciplinary collaboration and learning. The research questions  include: (1) What were the differences in the quality of the products between groups with higher and lower degrees of knowledge heterogeneity? (2) How did behavioural patterns in interdisciplinary collaborative learning differ between groups with higher and lower degrees of knowledge heterogeneity?

## Context and learning tasks design

A quasi-experimental research design was employed in this s tudy. Two classes participated in the study, undergoing higher and lower degrees of KH, respecti vely. Forty-eight second-year undergraduates from various disciplines participated in this study. Among them, 22 students were from Class A and 26 from Class B. Students in both classes were randomly assigned to form various multi-disciplinary groups of five or six members. Students used their personal mobile devices while engaging i n face-to-face discussions and simultaneously documenting their group solutions on a collaborative learning platform, Google Docs. The lesson about social inequality and poverty lasted 110 minutes, consisting of a 60-minute introduction by the instructor and 50-minute student activities. All students in both conditions first spent 20 minu tes reading materials to acquire diverse knowledge. In the lower KH condition, reading materials provided knowledge fro m social, economic, and environmental disciplines, as the course primarily focused on these three disciplines. In the higher KH condition, reading materials consisted of knowledge from social, econo mic, environmental, political, ethics and philosophy, and anthropological disciplines. These chosen disciplines  were designed to provide a comprehensive range of knowledge about social inequality and poverty. The following task was a collaborative learning activity in which students shared their insights and integrated knowl edge to solve complex real-world equality problems in 30 minutes. During this period, each group wrote an in terdisciplinary report in response to two questions: “Should we focus on economic growth and development or prio ritise environmental justice when addressing social inclusion?” and “How can we reconcile the potential  conflicts between economic progress and environmental injustice in the pursuit of social equity?”

## Data collection and analysis

The group reports were collected as the final produ cts of their ICL. Additionally, audio data of group verbal discussions during the tasks were gathered. To address the first research question, a rubric developed by Su et al.

(2024) was used to assess the quality of the group reports (). The assessment rubric comprised four dimensions:

purposefulness, disciplinary grounding, advancement through integration, and critical awareness. Each dimension contained two sub-dimensions, with disciplinary grounding containing three. Performance in each sub-dimension was evaluated on a 10-point scale across three levels: naïve (0-3 points), intermediate (4-6 points), and mastery (7-10 points). The final score for each dimension was calculated by summing its sub-dimension s cores. Two authors independently scored three group reports (33%), and the inter-rater reliability (Cronbach’s Alpha) for the four dimensions was.939,.716,.729, and.774, res pectively. The two raters discussed any discrepanci es in scoring, and the one r ater evaluated the remaining six groups. Then Mann-Whitney tests were conducted to compare the quality of the group reports. To address the second research question, each group’s verbal discussion was first segmented into utterances, which served as the unit of analysis. Each utterance as a unit of analysis was then coded using a scheme adapted from Claus and Wi ese’s (2019) interdisciplinary learning model with behavioural indicators (see Table 1). Two coders in dependently coded the verbal discussions with good intercoder reliability (Cohen’s Kappa =.701). In total,  619 utterances were coded. Subsequently, Epistemic  Network Analysis (ENA) (www.epistemicnetwork.org, Shaffer et al., 2016) was conducted to model and visualise the cooccurrence of ICL patterns under the two conditions. The stanza size was set to four, an appropriate value for synchronous discourse data (Ruis et al., 2019), mea ning the co-occurrence of collaborative learning codes was analysed within every four utterances.

## Results

To compare the quality of ICL products between the two conditions-higher and lower degrees of KH-MannWhitney U Tests were conducted. There is no significant difference between the two c onditions in overall performance (U = 8.000, p =.730), “Purposefulness” (U = 9.500, p =.905), “Integration” (U = 15.000, p =.286), and “Critical Awareness” (U = 14.000, p =.413). However, groups with a higher degree of KH (M = 18.000, SD = 2.345) performed significantly better in “Disciplinary Grounding” than those with a lower degree of KH (M = 12.500, SD = 3.873, U = 1.500, p =.032). This indicates that groups with higher KH considered a broader range of disciplinary perspectives, providing stronger reasoning for incorporating diverse viewpoints and more credible sources of information.

Content analysis and Epistemic Network Analysis w ere conducted based on coding to unpack the ICL behavioural patterns of groups with higher and lowe r degrees of KH. Figure 1 presents the comparisons of ENA networks between the two conditions. The red lines indicate stronger behavioural co-occurrence under the higher KH condition, while the blue lines represent stronger connections under the lower KH condition. The red rectangle highlights the centroids (mean position of the proj ected points) of the groups with higher KH, and the blue rectangle represents the centroids of the groups wi th lower KH. Along the X-axis (MR1), a Mann-Whitney test revealed that the two conditions were  statistically significantly  (U = 0, p =.020, r = 1.000) in terms of the distribution of projection points.  Groups with lower KH demonstrated a strong tendency to explain ideas whi le incorporating diverse perspectives and stakeholder considerations at the early stage of discussions. These groups also more frequently analysed problems and developed problem-solving strategies, offering more suggestions and evaluative feedback compared to groups with higher KH. In contrast, groups with higher KH frequently proposed and explained their ideas with specific suggestions  for problem-solving. Unlike their lower heterogeneity counterparts, these groups tended to integrate know ledge later in the discussion-specifically when evaluating others’ contributions and presenting their own proposals-rather thanduring the initial phases of discussion. ## Discussion and limitations

This study investigated the ICL outcomes and processes of student groups with higher and lower degrees of KH. The results showed groups with a higher degree of KH significantly outperformed those with a lower degre e of KH on Disciplinary Grounding. This finding suggests that higher KH helped groups incorporate a broader range of perspectives, supported by strong reasoning for involving those perspectives and credible information. This is likely because heterogeneous knowledge encourages o ut-of-the-box thinking (Zhang, 2023), leading to  more diverse problem-solving approaches as heterogeneity increases. Thes e results align with previous research indicating that individuals with access to more div erse knowledge are more open to different viewpoint s (Hsu & Lim, 2014). Additionally, groups with lower KH tended to integrate knowledge and early in discussions, providing explanations as they initiated new topics. In contr ast, groups with higher KH integrated knowledge later in the discussion, particularly when proposing their own i deas and evaluating others ’ contributions. This could be due to the higher coordination costs that arise from a lack of shared cognition and overlapping knowledge between team members’ existing knowledge and the new knowledge being introduced (Ye et al., 2016). Also, groups with higher KH required more time to coordinate diverse ideas and perspectives before integrating them effectively. This highlights an inherent trade-off in ICL: while greater disciplinary diversity fosters inno vation and a more comprehensive approach to complex issues, it also i ncreases the challenge of overcoming and coordinati ng differing perspectives (Crowley & O’Rourke, 2020).  Secondly, in problem-solving, groups with lower KH more frequently analysed key concepts in the problems and negotiated strateg ies with suggestions, while groups with higher KH were more likely to directly propose solutions during idea exchanges with less analysis of the problems in detail. It is possible that managing multiple st reams of unfamiliar information increased cognitive load (Kirschner et al., 2018), leading to the direct pro blem-solving instead of thorough analysis before addressing the problems. With integrative behaviour, team members function a s “connectors”, specialising in integration and implementation to leverage the knowledge contributi ons of their peers (Pennington et al., 2020). This finding is supported by research indicating that KH enables students to combine new ideas with existing  knowledge (Hsu & Lim, 2014).

There are several limitations in this study. First, there was no pre-test, and differences in students’ prior knowledge and skills could not be controlled, which  may affect the validity of the findings, despite all students being randomly assigned to groups. Additionally, the sample size was relati vely small, and the unit of analysis was the group when comparing the quality of collaborative learning products between conditions. Future research could benefit from a larger sample size to compare ICL outcomes across different group conditions.
