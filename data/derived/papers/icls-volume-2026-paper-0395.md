# Understanding Play and Inclusion through Interaction Analysis

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this poster, we present interaction analysis from video data of a playful learning episode in an inclusive elementary classroom. We found that, as a methodology, interaction analysis revealed the ways that adults’ instructional moves expansively position children’s ideas as valid contributions. We aim to spark conversation in the Learning Science community around intersections of dignity, inclusion, and playful learning.

The purpose of our poster is to work towards understanding the utility of interaction analysis to highlight and understand the intersections of dignity, inclusion, and playful learning. We use interaction analysis of one playful, inclusive classroom episode, and present the analysis with transcripts. Our poster discusses the potential and limitations of interaction analysis towards pedagogical and methodological innovation, inviting the Learning Sciences into conversation. Therefore, our guiding question for this poster is, how can interaction analysis reveal how adults support inclusive participation in play-based learning? Framework We build on scholars who advocate for humanizing and centering learners’ voices by arguing that play is dignityaffirming work (Lee et al. 2025). Espinoza and colleagues (2020) define educational dignity as “the multifaceted sense of a person’s value generated via meaningful participation in substantive intra-and inter-personal learning experiences that recognize and cultivate one’s mind, humanity,  and potential” (p.19). Play empowers, supports agency, and values children’s ideas– ultimately positioning all learners as worthy and capable human beings (Lee et a., 2025; Lee & Jasien, 2024). Our poster explores the participation and dignity of diverse students within playful learning in inclusive classrooms. We take cultural, political, and relational stances on disability (Annamma & Morrison, 2018; Kafer, 2013; Leonardo & Broderick, 2011), meaning we understand disability as an intersectional identity that is constructed through relations between people and within cultural and political contexts. These stances are grounded in our intersectional identities as educators and, for Author 1, in her identity as a disabled and neurodivergent person. Play shifts cultural, political, and relational ways of participating within schooling, which can reimagine how disabled students are positioned and whether there is space for their expansive, intuitive participation.

The episode presented in this poster comes from a video recording of a play-based lesson in a mixed-age 1st and 2nd grade classroom (6-8yrs). The science lesson in this episode focused on the structure and function of flowers. Students were split into groups of 5-6 children and tasked with creating and performing a dance demonstrating plant pollination. We used free-standing cameras and clip-on microphones to record the interactions.  The study site features inclusive classrooms, where disabled students participate in inquiry-based learning activities alongside their peers in general education classrooms. While the entire class did this activity, as a case study, we focused on one group of children because of video permission restrictions and the diverse identities of children in the group, which included an autistic child. This group was brainstorming how to create a dance that represented the parts of a tomato plant flower with the support of an adult (undergraduate student researcher). We used interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to iteratively analyze our transcripts and discussed emerging themes as illustrative of our findings (Braun & Clarke, 2022), including how an adult in the episode supported diverse forms of participation.

We present an analysis of an episode within our video data to illustrate our findings (student pseudonyms are inspired by tomato plant varieties). The episode begins with Tiger noticing a group of his peers dancing, bumping, and swaying across the room as they brainstorm dance moves. Tiger puts his arm around Blue and says, “I wanna do this” as he holds onto him and leans away, mimicking the other students’ movements. After doing the dance for 10 seconds, Tiger adds a playful verbal chant in time with the tempo of their swaying bodies: “hot dog, meatball, co-ca co-la, woo, woo”. Beginning at line 1, we see the interactional move where he evaluated Tiger and Blue’s dance as a worthy contribution. He positions them as competent and contributing participants. When he gestures for them to move closer, he is taking a stance (Du Bois, 2007) of physically and intellectually including them in the play. When he re-enacts their dance in the invitation (line 8), he demonstrates his attention to and understanding of their offering; this positions the playful food chant, “hot dog, meat-ball, co-ca co-la, woo, woo,” and swaying as valid and valuable to the collectively constructed dance. The adult does not criticize or stop Tiger. This is interesting because Tiger’s chant could be understood as a verbal stim (echolalia) and could be positioned as off-topic or not part of the activity. By not interrupting Tiger’s playful food chant, which can be understood as an expression of disabled ways of knowing, we see the adult as resisting a dehumanizing practice of controlling students’ language and play. Instead, he makes sense of Tiger and Blue’s movements in connection to the parts of a flower, saying the dance was “like the Anther, right?”(line 9), which connects their movements to the learning goal and expresses an inclusive stance that affirms their participation as competent and valid. Interaction analysis helped us to understand the students’ participation, not only in how the adult responded to Tiger, but also how the students responded to the adult. When Blue responds, “oooh yeah,” (line 3), we hear an enthusiastic agreement. Then, Tiger and Blue continue their bumping and swaying food chant dance, taking up the adult’s invitation to include their movements in the larger group’s dance. Towards the end of the episode, Tiger’s rhythmic verbal expressions transform through iterative practicing from “hot dog, meat-ball, coca co-la” to “toma-to, toma-to, toma-to plant,” again demonstrating a willingness to transform their intuitive participation into actions that could integrate in the group. At the end of the lesson, when each group presented their dance to the class, all of this was part of their final dance. As a field, we must attend to inclusive education, not only in designing innovative pedagogies, but in analyzing and uplifting the learning in these playful spaces. While policies and structures have begun shifting towards inclusion in name, pedagogical and interactional practices lag behind, leaving educators in need of tools, frameworks, and supports to forward dignity and meaning-making. We hope this poster will spark the learning sciences community to consider the intersections of dignity, play, inclusive education, and intersectional disability justice, and, specifically, to deliberate on the potential and limitations of interaction analysis in this work. In the process of conceptualizing this poster and in conducting the interaction analysis, we reflect on how playful activities could be participatory and dignity-ing spaces for facilitating the participation of diverse and historically marginalized students in inclusive classrooms. We see potential in the way that play explicitly shifts how school operates, including in expanding what is considered valid participation. By using interaction analysis to detail the learning within play, we push back against the controlling instructional practices used with disabled students and move towards pedagogical innovation in inclusive education While we can conclusively use interaction analysis to reveal the interactional moves of adults evaluating, positioning, and affirming the participation of diverse students in playful learning, we still do not know the students’ perspectives on whether and how they felt included, dis/abled, or dignified. Thus, guided by our commitments to center the most impacted (Invalid, 2017), we invite the Learning Sciences community into conversation with us towards potential methodologies and pedagogies that meaningful ly center the perspectives of disabled students.
