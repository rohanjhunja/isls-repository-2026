# “It’s aligned with my personal beliefs, i can do this”: How Science Educators Navigate Tensions Between Official and Counter Scripts of Science and Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how informal science educators transform their lived experiences of migration, activism, and encounters with marginalization into pedagogical commitments focused on social justice. Drawing on Holland and Lave’s history-in-person and Gutiérrez’s third-space concepts, we explore how educators’ histories serve as mediational resources that shape teaching philosophies and reconfigure who belongs in science. Using semi-structured interviews with community scientists from Daphnia, an after-school science organization serving marginalized youth, the analysis traces how educators navigated and re-authored official and unofficial scripts about legitimate science and participation. Findings reveal that educators mobilize personal histories to create hybrid pedagogical spaces emphasizing freedom, flexibility, and epistemic justice. These third-space negotiations obscure boundaries between scientific and community knowledge, transforming science into a relational, participatory practice rooted in care and belonging. By conceptualizing informal educators as historical actors, this study highlights their critical role in social transformation.

In the field of science education, ongoing questions about who belongs, whose knowledge is valued, and the purposes science serves remain central (Bang et al., 2013; Carlone & Johnson, 2007; Tan et al., 2013). Despite many years of reform and attempts for culturally responsive teaching, science education continues to prioritize dominant epistemologies while marginalizing the cultural, linguistic, and emotional experiences that shape how people understand and engage with science (Tan & Calabrese Barton, 2018). In today's political climate, where educators face the pressure to present science as neutral and detached from social issues. Such framing ignores the social, historical, and political factors that influence both teaching and learning, as well as the lived realities that shape how educators and students engage with science, thereby limiting the transformative potential of science education.

Informal science education programs play a crucial yet often-neglected role in transformative possibilities for science education. These community-based programs are flexible and relational, offering opportunities to challenge prevailing narratives about who can be a scientist and how science is practiced. Informal science environments allow educators to enact justice-oriented pedagogies, integrate sociopolitical issues, and support marginalized students in redefining what it means to do or to belong in science (Tan & Calabrese Barton, 2018). However, informal science educators frequently operate on the boundaries of institutional legitimacy, encountering limited opportunities for professional development and fragmented career paths (McLain, 2017). Despite these challenges, many educators draw on their personal histories, experiences of migration, activism, and feelings of exclusion to reimagine science as a practice centered on care, belonging, and justice. Understanding how these educators transform their personal experiences into teaching commitments is crucial for recognizing how educational spaces can serve as sites for social change (science education rereimagined as a transformative and justice-oriented approach/tool/practice). At a time when science education faces growing pressures to depoliticize the curriculum and limit discussions of systemic inequalities, these identity negotiations become even more critical.

Educators’ personal histories are not incidental to their teaching. They form the foundation of their pedagogical commitments. The ways they teach, care, and design learning environments are deeply connected to how they negotiate meanings, tensions, and participation in  their identity development. Teachers’ identities as science educators influence how and why they teach science and shape the relationships they develop with students” (Zhai et al., 2024, p. 25, referencing Madden & Wiebe, 2015, and Moore, 2008). The formation and development of science teacher identity are shaped in part by educators’ sociocultural and contextual experiences. 745 This highlights their active role in the construction and negotiation of self, while emphasizing the importance of social interactions, relationships, and meaning-making in shaping identity (Zhai et al., 2024). Informal science educators’ professional identities are similarly “complex, pluralistic, and fluid” (McLain, 2017, p. 129). These identities are not fixed traits but dialogical and contested processes through which educators continually position and reposition themselves within broader organizational and historical struggles. Such lived experiences become mediational resources through which educators redefine and re-author what science is, what purposes it serves, and how participation in science can become a vehicle for social change. In this way, identity development is both personal and political, a process of negotiating belonging, purpose, and justice in science learning spaces. As Zhai et al. (2024) emphasize, teacher preparation programs, learning environments, and pre-existing identities interact to shape these ongoing processes. Identities that develop in response to historical struggles can also become sources of politicized care (McKinney de Royston et al., 2017), critical positional praxis (Rodriguez et al., 2023), and justice-oriented pedagogy (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020), each of which expands the purposes and possibilities of science education. This paper situates informal science educators as “historical actors” whose teaching is both a continuation of lived struggles and a design for alternative futures through hybrid third spaces (Gutiérrez et al., 2019). Drawing on Holland and Lave’s (2000) concept of history-in-person and Gutierrez’s third space and hybridity, we examine how personal trajectories and broader social histories intersect in their pedagogical philosophies. By studying how informal educators mobilize their lived experiences to challenge dominant narratives about legitimacy, belonging, and purpose, this study highlights how they author new transformative scripts of science education that privilege justice, care, and community engagement.

## Theoretical framework

Identities are not static personal traits or fixed categories of membership. Instead, they are relational, contested, and produced through dialogue within practices (Avraamidou, 2014, 2019; Zhai et al., 2024). Holland and Lave

(2000) argued that “the creation and development of subjectivities, even those marginal to power, is made possible

because cultural forms are not only tools for positioning the other but also tools for positioning the self” (pp. 1920). Through stories, pedagogical approaches, or professional routines, people “self-author” themselves in dialogue with the cultural and historical voices that address them. Moreover, “subjectivities and their more objectified components, identities, are formed in practice through the often collective work of evoking, improvising, appropriating, and refusing participation in practices that position self and other” (Holland and Lave, 2000, p. 29).

This study conceptualizes informal science educators’ pedagogical commitments as emerging from historically situated identity-making processes, what Holland and Lave (2000) called history-in-person. Historyin-person illustrates how ongoing social struggles (what they called enduring historical struggles), such as colonialism, racism, gender inequality, and migration, have shaped and therefore are consequential to individuals' identities, which are continuously reshaped through local contentious practices, defined as the daily conflicts and negotiations of lived experiences.

For informal science educators, migration histories, family activism, or experiences of marginalization are not background factors but active mediational resources through which they story their teaching philosophies. History-in-person invites us to ask: how do large-scale historical struggles become lived, felt, and re-authored in the daily practices and commitments of science teaching? It also demands attention to the generativity of struggle. How educators’ identities, developed in response to inequity, can become sources of politicized care (McKinney de Royston et al., 2017), critical positional praxis (Rodriguez, Mirra, & McNeill, 2023), and justice-oriented pedagogy (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020) that open opportunities to reinvent science educational practices and approaches. In this way, educators' stories serve as dialogic spaces where the past, present, and imagined futures come together; their teaching is both a re-enactment of inherited histories and a design for alternative futures. Attending to these histories in person allows us to investigate how personal trajectories intersect with broader enduring struggles to shape the pedagogical philosophies and commitments enacted in after-school science learning environments. However, to understand how these histories-in-person transform into or take on pedagogical approaches and commitments, we draw on Gutierrez et al.’s (1999) concept of the Third Space, which becomes visible when different, often conflicting activity systems overlap. In these third spaces, diverse histories, roles, and mediating tools–including, but not limited to, language and cultural norms –coexist. Further, it is within the third space that official scripts and unofficial counterscripts collide. Official scripts include dominant norms, curricula, and institutional logics; and unofficial counterscripts refer to participants’ individual knowledge, experiences, languages, affective views, and orientations towards justice. Gutiérrez and colleagues (1999) highlighted that teachers may support hybrid activity in classroom settings. In this work, we identify informal science educators as playing a similar role. In an after-school science program with educators of diverse backgrounds, the intersection of the official scripts of dominant, academic science and the unofficial scripts introduced through educators' more expansive and critical views of science 746 creates a third space. This space invites potential transformation towards new meanings, roles, interactions, and relationships within science. In these spaces, “learning is organized so that ruptures are points of negotiation rather thandisruption. This negotiation opens up the possibilities for alternative voices to become part of the official curriculum” (Gutiérrez et al., 1999, p. 294).

Local contentious practices are seen as third spaces where scripts and counterscripts interact, shaping how participants engage in official spaces. According to Holland and Lave (2000), local contentious practices refer to the situated, everyday struggles and interactions that occur in specific times and places. These practices are shaped by broader historical and structural forces but are realized and mediated through local, immediate contexts. Local contentious practices are the sites where individuals en gage in conflict, negotiate meanings, and participate in the production of cultural forms and identities. From the perspective that new hybrid activities emerge from tensions between normative practices as official spaces and alternative counterscripts as unofficial spaces, community scientists’ histories-in-person become essential mediating tools that support the creation of those hybrid activities. Thus, third spaces represent both (1) places where individuals interact and (2) where they may negotiate the tensions and the enduring struggles that live in persons as they encounter institutional structures to produce new hybrid forms of activity, participation, and meaning. Together, history-in-person, local contentious practices, and third-space hybridity frame pedagogical commitments as a mutual constitution of history, person, practice, and alternative future possibilities. History provides meaning and the backdrop for the  struggles educators carry. Local contentious practices become the sites where historical struggles play out in everyday practice. Third spaces provide the context for the activity in which those histories emerge to be contested and transformed. Said another way, the third space is where local contentious practices play out, and there is a possibility of hybrid forms of activity. Guided by the concepts of history-in-person (Holland & Lave, 2000) and third space hybridity (Gutiérrez et al., 1999), this study investigates how informal science educators’ lived experiences function as mediational resources for developing justice-oriented pedagogical commitments in after-school programs. To address this purpose, the study centers on the following guiding research question: How do informal science educators’ histories-in-person, including experiences of formal science learning, migration, and marginalization, shape or embody their pedagogical commitments in after-school science programs as they describe, negotiate, and enact them?

## Context and methods

The preliminary findings were collected through semi-structured interviews with informal science educators, known as community scientists, at Daphnia. This non-profit organization worked with K-12 youth who are often excluded from and marginalized in  scientific spaces. Community scientists facilitated inquiry-based science programs, such as observing live samples under microscopes in various settings: during the school day or after school, at schools, community-based organizations, or in their own lab spaces. In Daphnia programs, youth voice and lived experience are foundational to science learning. Through their teaching, community scientists aimed to create spaces that support science identity development (Carlone & Johnson, 2007), give youth agency, foster a sense of belonging, and enable recognition of their role as social actors (Gonsalves, 2014; Rahm et al., 2014). In addition to their youth-facing role, community scientists also wrote culturally responsive, locally relevant curricula and built relationships with neighborhood partners. Over the past two years at Daphnia, we conducted participant observations and ethnographic field notes.

The analytic framework integrates narrative analysis through the lens of scripts and counterscripts (Gutiérrez, 1995; Gutiérrez et al., 2019). Scripts represent dominant, official narratives and expectations about science and science education, while count erscripts are alternative, resistant, or re-authored narratives that educators construct in response to exclusionary or limiting official scripts. This heuristic highlights how power, intersubjectivity, potential shared understanding, and knowledge are constructed, contested, and negotiated in teaching and learning spaces (Gutierrez et al., 1995, p. 445). Analytically, we examine (1) how educators navigate official and unofficial spaces, (2) how their history-in-person shape their negotiations of scripts and counterscripts and their pedagogical philosophies and practices, and (3) how their narratives highlight broader challenges and tensions in science education. Pedagogical commitments are therefore interpreted through how community scientists made them visible. Pedagogical commitments are interpreted through how community scientists described, resisted, or  redefined the dominant and self-authored narratives.

The scripts and counterscripts were coded relative to a particular official or unofficial space. For example, official “general science community” scripts include neutrality, credentialism, the science PhD trajectory, traditional schooling norms and approaches, and deficit framings of students. Official “organizational Daphnia” scripts reflect institutional pedagogical norms/approaches of interdisciplinary programs. Counterscripts focus on educators’ resistance and reframing of these dominant narratives by emphasizing care, activism, or community knowledge.

747 Since our data is derived from interviews rather than observations, local contentious practices are inferred from narratives. In our analysis, local contentious practices are operationalized as the narrative moments where educators described negotiating tensions between institutional expectations and their own lived pedagogical commitments. These moments make visible how enduring historical struggles become embodied in everyday pedagogical decisions and identity work.

## Data analysis

To show how informal science educators negotiate their histories-in-person within their institutional and community settings, this section presents two cases from six semi-structured interviews conducted with Daphnia’s community scientists. Each case highl ights a distinct trajectory and set of pedagogical tensions that explain how educators mobilize their lived experiences as mediational resources. Through these narratives, we explore how educators navigate contradictions between institutional norms and pedagogical commitments, creating hybrid “third spaces” where their identities and pedagogical philosophies continue to evolve. CASE-1: Bernadette Bernadette’s relationships to science and education were marked by continuous moments of tension, transformation, and relief. Referencing her experience in K-12 education, Bernadette shared, “I did not like science in high school.” We position Bernadette’s disinterest in science as not rooted in deficit views of herself or her abilities, but as responses to structural exclusion and alienation. We understand Bernadette’s disconnection from science in high school as fundamental to her history-in-person, and lived experiences that inform her draw towards justice-oriented, relational, and pedagogical commitments. When recounting her post-secondary experiences and her time with Daphnia, Bernadette illuminated the tension between what may be offered through academia and the inaccessibility of/within academic institutions themselves. In reference to internship opportunities provided in her undergraduate experience, Bernadette said, “I did not really understand the concept of [them] because I am a first-generation college student and all these new ideas were being introduced to me,” illustrating how not all students entering undergraduate studies hold the same academic, personal, and professional experiences –an experience particularly salient for those who are the first in their immigrant families and communities to enter college. She made visible a dominant script of academic spaces, one that privileges those who have been previously exposed to knowledge to navigate academic institutions, ultimately shaping both the preparedness of incoming students and their sense of belonging within higher education.

In opposition to community educators who hold interdisciplinary science degrees like hers, Bernadette shared, “...the other community scientists have PhDs that might not mean a thing, but they are taken more seriously,”  as individual teams at Daphnia contribute to fundraising for their own programs. Her noticings allude to a chasm within Daphnia, separating those with assumed expertise through their PhDs and community educators who may not hold the same credentials, and shaping the continuation of individual programs. Further, her acknowledgment of these academic markers in relation to Daphnia reflects how the organization remains a place that both (a) adheres to the  dominant script of leveraging academic credentials and (b) exposes staff, and consequently youth participants, to information that may aid in navigating academic institutions. This tension lays the groundwork for the creation of a third space, where competing stances around what is considered knowledge, what is considered expertise, and what are aspirational pedagogical choices in science, broadly, and in Daphnia programming, specifically, meet. But it is Bernadette’s noticing of the inequities embedded in academic institutions and youth  organizations that equipped her to analyze and resist dominant scripts found across and between academia, teaching, and science. Through her interdisciplinary science undergraduate major, Bernadette was introduced to science as social justice with ties to concrete, real-world scenarios. During our time, she shared a memory of a course field trip  hosted at a canal near where she grew up. Students were then tasked with sampling the water and conducting microbial analysis. Reflecting, Bernadette said, “It was really interesting to hear…that, like a personal aspect” and “how science affects my community and me.” This exposure in her post-secondary trajectory to socially embedded science p resented a counterscript to a dominant script of science upheld as objective, decontextualized from social issues,  and detached from personal narratives. Instead, the course invited Bernadette to envision herself and her communities within science. Her memory of this experience contributed to her reorientation toward what science is and what its purpose could be, in which one’s personal and communal experiences become legitimate sources of scientific knowledge. We name this coming together of dominant and counterscripts of what science is and does as a  third space, one that recognizes and validates the presence of all community members and the knowledge relevant to their communities, namely those who have been historically marginalized and harmed through science. Bernadette was recommended by an advisor in her program for a summer internship at Daphnia, returning as an intern for the two following summers  and then as a staff member. On her initial experiences with  Daphnia, she said, “What was striking about that experience was the freedom and the informal learning setting, both for myself as an intern and the students she worked with.” 748 She emphasized she had fun exploring different science  topics of her choosing and “figuring out how [to] apply my learning in school in the internship.” Further, it was also through the internship that how Bernadette saw herself in education shifted. Explicitly, she shared, “I had always  said I would never work with kids,” but it was how Daphnia felt like “community service” that Bernadette realized  she loved teaching. Her initial rejection of working alongside young people transformed into a pedagogical  commitment to serve the community, highlighting the political and ethical dimensions of teaching. Bernadette  continued by adding that at Daphnia, youth participants are encouraged “to think about what they’re doing rather  than…this is what you’re learning, this is what I’m expecting of you.” Her emphasis on freedom, choice, and making the thinking of both herself and young learners visible through Daphnia’s programming represents a  counterscript to the dominant script found within formal schooling, where classroom practices may stifle youth curiosity, demand a standardization of learning, and position educators as primary epistemic authorities. The  pedagogical practices of Daphnia that offer interns, educators, and young people autonomy in a science learning environment, despite traditional classroom practices, represent the making and continuation of a third space. Coupled with their pedagogical commitments, Bernadette and her programmatic team at Daphnia resisted  b uilding community partnerships rooted in extractive, transactional practices. When reaching out to programmatic partners and schools, Bernadette shared that her team is often told, “we don’t want you, like, outsiders coming…into our space,” signaling community exhaustion and reflecting a dominant script where partnerships of informal  learning settings have been grounded in unidirectional approaches that do not honor community partners’ asks nor needs. It was these continued responses that “triggered that transition of, like, really trying to understand our  community and why it is that we really want to work with them.” This shift animates a counterscript where  Bernadette’s team (a) accepted and honored the community partners’ refusal to work together by shifting the team’s own orientation to building relationships, and (b) placed responsibility on the team to get to know the communities  they are a part of and would like to grow alongside. Bernadette and her team re-authored their practices to value  community autonomy and showed an epistemic transformation to develop programs with communities and not for communities, demonstrating what may be accomplished through a third space contesting settled and reimagined relational practices.

CASE-2: Katia Katia first heard about Daphnia in 2021 while pursuing her PhD in Curriculum, Instruction and Leadership. After volunteering a few times and “falling in love” with the organizational concept, she became a Community Scientist two years ago. Recently, she tr ansitioned to a Lead Community Scientist role, which includes teaching, coordinating Daphnia’s visits to schools, and maintaining her team’s strategy and partnerships. Her academic journey from an undergraduate Biology and Ecology focused degree in Brazil,  to research in science communication, and currently in social sciences, situates her between the scientific and social sciences communities. The movement across disciplinary, institutional, and cultural boundaries positions  her as both an insider and an outsider of science education.

Reflecting on her background, she shared that her study of “science science” dates back to her undergraduate training and early research years. But she later became drawn to “science communication,” which she described as “every form of communicating scien ce from journals to radio podcasts and museums, [and] being in touch with or communicating science to the general public or society.” This reveals early tensions of a third space between the epistemic authority of formal scientists and the relational commu nicative approaches Katia began to follow as a Community Scientist. Her dual positionality exemplifies local contentious practice that allows her to discuss both the power and limitations of the epistemic authority of credentialed scientists. From the offi cial script perspective, her comments point to a belief that scientific knowledge is generated and communicated by scientists, making it essential for “outsiders” or the public to understand. Katia’s motivations were rooted in her passion for science and academic training; she sought to provide learning opportunities for young people often excluded from science education, which was closely aligned with Daphnia’s mission. Katia emphasized her commitment to democratizing access to science, stating, "Let's make science accessible because science is amazing, and it would be awesome if everyone could have access to this knowledge." In Daphnia school visit programs, Katia articulated, “I want them to understand at least, or have a sense of what ecosyst ems are, depending on where they are at.” She followed the Daphnia script of making science "accessible" by identifying the learners' starting points, while still relying on a mostly positivist approach that transmits fixed knowledge from "scientists" to "learners." However, Katia’s identity as a scientist and communicator did not always align with Daphnia’s institutional counterscript of providing resources and opportunities for marginalized young people in science, countering the historically-prevalent script of who  does and does not belong in formal science learning spaces (e.g., schooling, higher education). Katia learned to teach at Daphnia first through training in the organization's mission–inquiry-based and culturally-responsive teaching approaches, followed by observations of her colleagues’ teaching and extensive practice. Initially, she noted that the focus was on the message “we have 749 microscopes for you, and we are going to tell you all about microscopes.” However, she now hoped the programs would have more personal significance and “a meaning for you [the students].” She identified shifts in how and why microscopes were used, showing how local contentious practices can stabilize counterscripts as official organizational scripts. She also emphasized a positive shift in science communication from “deficit to participatory” research, "bringing people’s knowledge... and mak[ing] it meaningful so it’s useful for them, not only for scientists." Challenging the deficit model, this statement called for a shift from unidirectional approaches to knowledge acquisition to participatory knowledge co-production. This shift aided ongoing efforts towards epistemic justice and rightful presence, in which marginalized students not only access but also actively contribute to scientific knowledge and practice. Katia inhabited a hybrid space where she redefined her identity as a scientist and communicator, questioning who qualifies as a science doer and knower at Daphnia. Her past academic and professional experiences illustrate the tension between teachers' current and ever-shifting identities, presenting opportunities for identity development or transformation (Zhai et al., 2024). At this early third space, identity negotiations inherited from official scripts of science disciplines begin to meet with the lived realities and justiceoriented goals of informal science education at Daphnia. Katia’s multilingualism and flexibility in her pedagogical choices animated the tensions between official science scripts and Daphnia counterscripts. She reflected that her practice was evolving from "we [scientists] have all the knowledge" to "listening more to the students" and "customizing the lessons as much as possible." In her practice, she was committed to surfacing “what do [students] think, what knowledge do they have,” and to questioning how Community Scientists could meet them “where they are.” K atia's teaching practice evolved beyond mere flexibility, embracing her identity as a scientist, her personal beliefs, and her ability to speak Spanish. She felt confident in her work because it aligned with her values. She stated, "I cando this," reflecting her belief t hat her skills and identity enhanced her teaching. Her multilingualism, particularly her relationship with Spanish, also invited youth to express their ideas freely, as seen with Spanish-speaking students who often mentioned the "gusarapo,"  an insect from the Dominican Republic. Her teaching approach promoted inclusion, in contrast to  official science scripts that favor rigid methods, treat students as passive learners, and prioritize English as the primary language of science. She culturally re-authored a hybrid third space within Daphnia’s pedagogical commitments, embracing students’ knowledge, questions, and linguistic diversity as assets rather than challenges to overcome, aspiring for epistemic justice. Yet despite her flexibility and multilingualism, Katia often struggled to enact participatory pedagogy. In one of Daphnia’s programs, a short, one-time, school-day program, she faced difficulties in “managing her  own teaching goals and expectations” within the program structure. She recognized her flexible teaching style as “the best [she] cando.” While she strived for a “big impact,” she also “want[ed] to give them all the information that [she] has ”, indicating how she remains tethered to dominant scripts of knowledge transmission and limited transgressive, participatory practices. In an anecdote, Katia also described Spanish-speaking immigrant students who “hate science” and “don’t like this class” because their school science teacher spoke no Spanish. When she expressed her love for science (“Science is amazing”), students responded by asking “questions about very basic things, like what is a cell?" She thought the students “obviously needed that, someone listening to them and just giving them that basic support,” highlighting how historical, institutional inequities of formal schooling remain pervasive, and may limit transformative practice in Daphnia programs. Katia’s story demonstrated the frictions between critiquing and reproducing dominant scripts (access to knowledge and the transmission of knowledge) as she iteratively developed her pedagogical counterscript (participatory science oriented toward epistemic justice). Katia engaged in local contentious practices and third-space negotiations where the authority of scientists, formal schooling, and institutional logics collided with pedagogical commitments and cross-disciplinary history-in-person (scientist, science communicator, educator, social science PhD student, and multiling ual). Her language and flexibility became key pedagogical tools in refiguring Daphnia’s program structures and authoring her identities as a science educator. As she navigated contradictions within Daphnia’s professional community, her hybrid philosophies of meaning-making and participation became visible and transformative, showing how Daphnia moves beyond access to create inclusive and agentic pathways into science.

“As teachers narrate their stories, they negotiate and renegotiate a fluid self to understand and define who they are.” (Zhai et al., 2024, p.16) Informal science educators' pedagogical commitments emerge from ongoing negotiations between their "history in person" and institutional structures of science. The constellation/convergence of lived experiences, including personal struggles and migration histories, serves as an active mediational resources that shape how science educators teach, what they value, and how they position science in relation to justice and belonging. These temporal narratives, which link past struggles with present pedagogical commitments and future aspirations, constantly inform and transform how informal science educators approach their work and practice. As Zhai et al. (2024) remind us, “science teacher identity 750 development is an ongoing process of producing, reproducing, and transforming” (p.19). From this lens, identity development is not viewed as linear but as a contextual and dialogical process. The contextual and dialogical nature of this identity work manif ests in resistance that aligns with Third Spaces (Gutierrez et al., 1999), unscripted social environments where official scripts and unofficial counterscripts interact productively and where local contentious practices flourish.

This process of identity transformation manifests most clearly when informal science educators confront and resist the “monologic script” of traditional schooling and science teaching, which privileges objectivity, dominant discourses, and values about legitimate knowledge and power. In this process, both Bernadette and Katia actively constructed counterscripts to official curricula. They emphasized freedom and flexibility, representing counterscripts that value relational learning and community relevance over rigid, teacher-controlled (top-down, expert-driven, and unidirectional) disciplinary values. Both educators’ reflections/narratives show how informal science spaces loosen the boundaries of who can act or speak as scientists. They created environments that invite marginalized students' participation and bring local community knowledge into science learning spaces, which are otherwise excluded from traditional academic settings. Informal science educators transform their personal experiences with cultural and institutional challenges into lived expertise, creating resources that challenge monologic scripts. By leveraging their firsthand knowledge of linguistic marginalization and academic exclusion, they resist the silence surrounding cultural discourse in traditional science teaching. When they position themselves as multilingual communicators and align with their communities, they expand participation and overcome barriers that typically exclude English learners, recently migrated students, and first-generation students in post-secondary education. Third spaces are hybrid environments that foster freedom and flexibility, enabling informal science educators to improvise and engage with the cultural and linguistic resources students bring to learning. Within these spaces, a recurring contradiction emer ges as educators simultaneously resist and reproduce official scripts while negotiating them through their history-in-person. It is precisely at these points of tension that embodying both script and counterscript allows for the most significant transformations of Third spaces. Zhai et al. (2024) explains “the frictions or tension s between teachers' existing identities and new identity components are opportunities for their identity change or transformation.” (p.19) For example, when Katia invited students to share what they already knew about science or asked them to bring samples  from their own neighborhoods, she transformed science authority from something validated exclusively in formal “natural sciences” academic research communities to something co-authored in the community context. In parallel, Bernadette and her team were in tent on ensuring that their practices with young people and the surrounding communities are built collaboratively and resist extractive, relational practices. By intentionally valuing students' cultural knowledge and honoring community members' refusal to engage with Daphnia as an organization, both educators challenged traditional power dynamics and democratized the learning process. Through these hybrid practices, informal science educators treat science as a “tool” for social justice. Such hybrid activity systems enable authentic interaction and social heteroglossia, where multiple voices and forms of knowledge coexist. Such pedagogical commitments redefine science as a collective and dialogical practice, challenging the idea that scientific knowledge originates from “elite” institutions. Here we see a shift in experience in/with science from traditional academic science to professional practice, more rooted in collaboration and built on communal relevance. This shift from academic to professional identities in these cases also reflects McLain’s (2017) notion that informal science educators' identities are “complex, pluralistic and fluid” (p.129). Learning becomes generative as informal science educators integrate new experiences and values into their identities, transforming not only themselves but also the organization’s official script.

## Significance

This study investigates how informal science educators shape their identities through a dialogical process, drawing on their experiences to develop evolving, justice-oriented pedagogies and political identities. As informal science educators narrate their stories and illustrate their fluid selves, they engage in continuous negotiations between their "history in person" and institutional structures of  science, linking past struggles with present pedagogical commitments and future aspirations. This research extends existing literature beyond formal science educators by demonstrating how informal science educators' temporal narratives constantly inform and transform their practice within Third spaces. The significance of this work lies in revealing how these hybrid activity systems enable authentic interaction and social heteroglossia, where multiple voices and forms of knowledge coexist, ultimately redefining science to be a collective and dialogical p ractice that challenges elite institutional knowledge production and shifts to prioritize building thoughtful relationships among historically marginalized educators and young people alike. Most importantly, this research demonstrates that learning becomes  generative as informal science educators integrate new experiences and values into their identities that counter dominant scripts around academia and science. This process positions informal science spaces as critical sites that reimagine who belongs in science 751 and how scientific knowledge can serve movements towards social change. Furthermore, the study highlights how informal programs create environments where belonging is actively cultivated rather than assumed. Critical change in education emerges from critical connections rather than critical mass; that is, transformative education depends more on the quality of relationships and pedagogical commitments than on the number of participants or resources. Moreover, Hunter and Kenimer (2023) also call for further research into how place, content, and agency influence the identity development of informal science educators and their ability to navigate challenges within their professional and institutional contexts. Building on these calls, our research highlights critical directions for future inquiry into how science educators' identities evolve through temporal processes that link past experiences, present practices, and future aspirations. We forward that future research should examine how informal science educators' history in person transforms into identities in practice, particularly for communities historically marginalized in science, such as women of color, and how these transformations occur across diverse settings, from formal classrooms to informal spaces in communities. Such investigations would deepen our understanding of the interplay between systemic and organizational structures that impose official scripts and the relational, participatory conditions that foster counterscripts.
