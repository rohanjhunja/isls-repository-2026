# Student and Family Experiences in a Student Success Program in Higher Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
First-generation students often experience increased challenges in higher education institutions even though a variety of supports are offered. This case study explored the experiences of family members and first-generation students in a non-matriculated general education program. The students had many positive and challenging experiences, while their family members expressed gratitude for the opportunity and were concerned for the well-being of their student. The principles learned from this program could help other student success programs.

## Introduction

First-generation undergraduate students are often highly motivated and committed to changing the world (Haney, 2020; Stone et al., 2016). They also bring resilien ce, optimism, proactivity, and other strong qualiti es (Hands,

2020) to their learning environment. Unfortunately, these students frequently exper ience increased challenges in

their learning. Even with financial support, tutoring, advisement, and appropriate course loads, these students tend to have higher dropout rates (Moreno, 2021). Instit utions offer a variety of interventions and support  for first-generation students. Examples include mentoring (Mc Gonagle et al., 2014; Ortega, 2018; Plaskett et al., 2018), advisement (Barber et al., 2021; Bennett et al., 2020; McGonagle et al., 2014), financial support (Hurwitz, 2020; McGonagle et al., 2014; Walton et al., 2020), and many other programs and interventions. Some research includes student perception s of interventions and rarely perspectives from the students’ family. Some  students’ perceptions are portrayed as student satisfaction (Kuo, 2014; Kuo & Belland, 201 6), improved self-efficacy (Zhou et al., 2020), or improved student o utcomes (Joosten & Cusatis, 2019). However, research lacks understanding of how the interventions are received by students and how interventions align with the students’ educational goals. These perspectives are essential to ensure the programs are optimal. Research has shown a consistent correlation between student satisfaction and student performance (Kuo, 2014; Kuo and Belland 2016; Dhaqane & Afrah, 2016; Martirosyan et al., 20 14), and efforts need to be made to ensure that stu dents are satisfied with the interventions offered and their perspectives are addressed (Jones & Cole, 2002; Patton, 2006). Purpose and research questions The purpose of this research was to explore the exp eriences and perceptions  of first-generation students in a student success program in higher education. Additi onally, this research explored the questions, conce rns, and experiences of family members of these students. This research was guided by the following research questions:

1. What are the students’ experiences in the program?

2. What are the students’ perceptions and feelings of the support they are being offered?

3. What are the experiences and concerns of family members of students in a student success program?

Theoretical frameworks The student success program was guided by two frameworks: self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan, 2012) and social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1977). Self-determination theory focuses on promoting students ’ interest in learning, valuing education activities, behavior re gulation, motivation, and confidence in their own a bility and capacity. Social cognitive theory suggests that lea rning occurs in a social context with dynamic inter action. The theory considers a person’s past experiences which factor in whether  behaviors will occur, with the goal of understanding how people regulate their behavior through control and reinforcement to achieve their goals.

## Context

Through an existing research-practice partnership (RPP; Penuel & Gallagher, 2017), the student success program sought to build supports for students, especially in their knowledge, conf idence, attitudes, emotional well-being, broad creativity skills, career readiness, and community building. This program was developed and implemented at a large, private university in the western United  States. The first two years of the program include d firstgeneration students enrolled in a general education program fo r non-matriculated students who want to take university classes to improve their GPA and potenti ally matriculate into a university. Students in the program were part of a cohort, enrolled in the same courses  each semester, and participating in the same wrap-around services provided in the program. This included access to instructors, peer mentors, teaching assistants, peers (each other), mental health services, career readiness, experiential learning activities, and family support. One key area of the services included highly trained peer mentors modeling successful academic practices while building relationships of trust.

## Research methodology

This research used a qualitative exploratory case study (Stake, 1995) to explore two  cohorts of students in a student success program. Each cohort had 11 student s, for a total of 22 students over two years. Qualitative data was collected from pre/post interviews, focus groups, surveys, and observations with each cohort. Interview data was also collected from 6 family members of these students. Data were analyzed using thematic network analysis (Attride-Stirling, 2001) to understand the experiences of the students and t heir family members. Trustworthiness was established through peer debriefing, member checking, and triangulation.

## Results

Across the data from the two cohorts of students, t here were positive and negative elements the studen ts experienced in the student success program as they participated in the many parts of it. The main themes from this data was categorized into two global themes: (1) the positive, good things about the student success program and (2) the negative or challenging things about th e student success program. Some of the positive, go od things they mentioned included opportunities to grow and p ush themselves, exci ted to meet new people and make connections, overcoming fears and nervousness, and building personal accountability. Some of the negat ive and challenging things included being uncomfortable approaching and talking to an instructor, pressure from internal and external expectations, a heavy academic workload, and being scared of the unknown. The students had many good things to say about thei r experience in the student success program. They were all grateful for the opportunity and the suppo rt provided so they could work hard and be successf ul. But on the flip side, in the second semester the students felt the mentors were micromanaging them and not allowin g them the autonomy they hoped for. This was a tensio n between the students and the mentors, with the me ntors and administrator overseeing the study hall and oth er activities reminding the students that t heir job was to do well and become a matriculated student. But the students longed for some autonomy within th e study hall time. The students expressed the student success program was helpful and worth their time. They were excited  for the future, for themselves and for future generations. They expressed that their time right then was a cha nging point in their life and the future of  their family. Most students expressed that they were doing well a cademically, even though they struggled with some subjects. One stude nt said, “So academically pretty good. I feel like I'm going to fall in the same ranges as in last semester, so hopefully I'll have almost all A's and one B or B m inus.” As the academic year went on, the students became closer and helped each other. The family members who were interviewed all said si milar things, (1) gratitude for the experience thei r student was having, (2) worried about their students’ well-being, generally, and (3) high hopes and expectatio ns they had for their students’ future. The family mem bers were all very proud of their student, while ex pressing worry and concern for them. Many of the family members had not ever attend college and what their student was doing was very foreign and unknown to them, so thei r anxiety for their student was high. But in the ne xt breath they were proud and grateful. Overall, they were very emotional, had high expectations, and were very proud.

## Discussion and conclusion

The students participating in the student success p rogram had a range of experiences, both positive and challenging. They worked hard and achieved many academic milestones, with all who applied to be matriculated undergraduate students being accepted to the univer sity they hoped to attend. Even with tensions and c hallenges in the program, the students were upbeat and hardwo rking. They were focused on their goals. Even their  family members, who experienced a range of emotions and expressed concerns, were hopeful for their students’ success. The different elements of this student success prog ram and the experiences of these students provides good principles for what a student success program should be. The second cohort experienced a more ref ined program as it changed in the second iteration.  The people who designed and implemented the student  success program also grew, through their experiences with the students and family members. They learned so many of the good and the bad of what to do and what did not wor k as well. They too were very focused on the success of the students. The things learned were definitely helpfu l to this university  but could be taken and applied at another university in a student success program aimed to support students.
