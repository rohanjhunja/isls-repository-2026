# Teen Sensemaking and Incidental Online News Encounters: A Small Stories Approach

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how teens engage in sensemaking processes when encountering news through their social networks and social media feeds. Using a small stories approach and debriefing participant-collected social media artifacts during interviews, we show how an initial information encounter reflects one’s network and algorithms. We then follow that information encounter through subsequent, related small stories to discover how and why teens pursue additional related information, whether on or offline. Findings show the importance of identity, family and other adults, and social interactions in the sensemaking process, and also support the concept of sensemaking as an ongoing process of revisiting an earlier small story and building upon it. The findings have implications for both how parents and teachers support teens as they navigate these sensemaking processes and how future researchers might use small stories to support research with teens in digital environments.

## Introduction

Teenagers in the United States, much like their glo bal peers, are well-established as frequent and heavy users of social media (Anderson et al., 2023). Their social media use includes the expected social  and entertainment activities, but their online experiences are broader. Social media serves as a major news source for teens (Lenhart et al., 2009), and although teens may not be actively seeking ne ws they nonetheless have agency in this context (Swart, 2021). The news that teens encounter and the choices that they make when encountering that news online have the capacity to influence their worldview. Their sensemaking processes in this context shape their developing identities, their belief systems, and the way they interact with the world. In this study, we interrog ate these sensemaking processes as teens encounter news via s mall stores on social medi a and in turn share small stories about these encounters through media diaries and interviews.

## Background

Online news encounters  can be intentional or incidental. Incidental encounters  are governed by multiple environmental and personal factors, including ident ity, motivation, social networks, and media systems  (Weeks & Lane, 2020). Many online news encounters involve small stories. Small stor y is a term used to describe a comprehensive array of narrative forms that differ from traditional and personal experience narratives (Bamberg, 1997; Georgakopoulou, 2007). Small stories may be fragmented or incomplete, and may be informal or anecdotal. As a form of narrative research, small stories nonetheless consider  the emergent, sequential, and temporal nature of interactions (Georgakopoulou, 2007). By widening the concept of story to include brief, self-contained units such as are typical of social media posts, research ers can gain insights into micro-level identity, belief-shaping, and sensemaking experiences (Georgakopoulou, 2017). As highlighted by Garner (2023), how people engage in social media can be interpreted from a small story perspective by looking at the posts and interactions in an online space. In this study, we expand the notion of social media  small stories to incorporate not only “breaking ne ws” style posts by individuals (Page 2010), but also the breaking news posts that individuals encounter. Additionally, we extend beyond the virtual spaces where social media-based small stories occur to encompass people’s reactive and reflective stories when recounting the moments that they encountered online news. In this study, we adopt Weick’s (1995) sensemaking framework, with the caveat from Sandberg and Tsoukas

(2020) that all sensemaking necessarily reflects context.  Although Weick examined sensemaking in

organizational interactions, social media environments offer similar opportunities to observe how people interact with and understand their world. From Weick (1995), we note that sensemaking has sev en characteristics, of which we draw directly on four in our analysis: (1) It relates to identity construction; (2) It occurs retrospectively;

(3) It is social;  and (4) It is ongoing.  Small stories and sensemaking work well together in the inquiry process.

Small stories offer the ability to see the complex and at times contradictory ways people engage in id entity work as they offer for consideration narratives of their experiences in the world (Bamberg, 2004). Collectively, multiple small stories develop portraits of their tellers who are effectively reflecting on lived experiences. When sensemaking occurs via small stories, such as on social media, there is an implied intersubjectivity. Intersubjectivity is the shared understanding that is negotiated among c onversational participants. It is not inherently present whenever there is interaction  (Dennen et al., 2023), although social interactions  are part of working toward intersubjectivity. When information is negative or considered of low personal value, sensemaking activities decrease (Stieglitz et al., 2017) and individuals are less likely to strive for intersubjectivity. It is through the deliberate actions of people in social spaces – actions that might include discourse as well as sharing and liking

– that can lead to intersubjective sensemaking (Fuchs & De Jaegher, 2009). The social nature of sensemaking is

further reflected in the contextual elements that determine  what narratives will be told and how they will be told (de Fina, 2008). In other words, the listener is a key participant in the sensemaking process. Finally, the ongoing nature of sensemaking reflects how people continuously process the narratives of their lives, with temporal context influencing how stories are told (Mishler, 2006).

Purpose and research questions The purpose of this study is to investigate how teens encounter, interact with, and ultimately understand the news they encounter online, using small stories and sensemaking as guiding frameworks. The research questions are:

1. How do teens learn about news through their social media feeds?

2. How do teens make sense of news encountered through their social media feeds?

3. When and how do teens value sensemaking when encountering news through their social media feeds?

In this short paper, we focus on a single small story from three participants, selected for their typicality. All three reference a major news story occurring during the data collection period, the Israel-Hamas War.

## Method

Participants in this study are ten teenagers recruited from a high school in a medium-sized city. Participants ranged in age from 13-18. Three female participants are focused on in this pa per. Data were collected during OctoberDecember 2023. Participants kept daily media diaries for one week,  and then debriefed the diaries during an interview. The media diary consisted of a brief survey about that day, 3-5 screenshots from their social media feeds that day, and a brief video recording about that day. Data analysis for this paper focuses on the screenshots and the related small stories told during the debri ef, with whole interview transcripts used to situate fi ndings in the broader context. First, participant descriptions were generated, foc using on identity characteristics, interests, and attitudes shared during the interviews. Second, small stories were identified in the interview and extracted for further analysis alongside the corresponding social media screenshots. Finally, the researchers collaboratively and iteratively reviewed the small stories to describe the participants’ sensemaking process.

## Participant 1: Bella

Bella is 15 years old. On social media, Bella uses Instagram, TikTok, and Snapchat, and has strong opinions about algorithms. She uses a news app, but admits that she just glances at headlines due to a lack of time. Bella indicated seeing content about the Israel-Hamas war, but did not share artifacts about th e war itself. Instead, she shared multiple artifacts about genocide in the Congo. In her feed, she encountered a TikTok er who used the IsraelHamas War to draw attention to the humanitarian crisis in the Congo. One shared artifacts was a screenshot that suggested the viewers create and share a video usin g a particular filter to raise money to support people in the Congo. Bella indicated that she followed through and share d it, which led the interviewer to ask whether she trusted that the money would really be donated. In response, Bella shared the following small story: Bella:  I actually recently had a discussion about this. Ev en if they aren't necessarily giving the money, it only takes a person approximately 15 seconds to create a video and post it. And it doesn't even have to be p ublic, it could be private, so it really doesn't take that much time out of a person' s day, or even effort to create this to most likely help someone in need.

Bella continues to talk about the discussion she had with her friend, and how she vie ws the situation through the lens of her own religion. Later she also reflects h ow her understanding is shaped by her parents, whom  she describes as “super religious.” While Bella follows the prominent news story, she has engaged with and developed concern about a parallel story.

## Participant 2: Charlotte

Charlotte is 14 years old. She mainly uses Instagra m, YouTube, and Pinterest to get in touch with clos e friends and to learn things related to hobbies. She shared that she tries to stay away from “negative” or “sad” information, and wants a positive online experience. However, she explained that she was frequently exposed tonews on world and local politics, terrorism, and healthcare throu gh her friends’ posts and automatically generated f eed. She shared a small story of encountering news about the war and later sharing it with her teacher. Although the news exposure occurred via television and not social med ia in this instance, the small story nonetheless pr ovides an example of incidental exposure followed by sense-making processes. Charlotte:  I was staying over with my grandparents pretty much  all week, last week, and they like to put the news on in the morning. So, I would wake up, and then I would sit on the couch and either be on my phone or be working on my crochet project and listen to the  Gaza news. And I would watch the news and then I come up with questions and then I'd go to school, a nd I asked my teacher who majored in world events, like, what she thought abo ut it, and like, why they were doing it. And I learned a lot about the history of their battles. And how this isn't just a new thing, and how we're reoccurring i t is and why it's, I guess, different, and in some cases, more severe than it has been past. In conversation with the interviewer,  she connects the story with her teacher to make sen se of the news she encounters. She grew more interested in the topic after exploring it with a social connection.

## Participant 3: Kala

Kala is 16 years old and uses social media in what she describes as the "stereotypical way," occasionally sharing to her Stories on Instagram and commenting on posts. She also uses TikTok, where she mostly consumes entertainment content but also sees political conte nt. Specifically, she recalled seeing several posts abo ut the removal of the U.S. Speaker of the House, the Israe l-Hamas War, and ways to raise money for certain causes by using specific filters.  Although she has incidental online exposure, Kala explained that she intentionally does not watch the news because she feels as though it has become too biased. When she sees posts or notifications abouts current events that interest her, she will look up information on her own and then ask her father. Kala:  With the Gaza stuff and a lot of the political stuf f, my dad and I are on opposite ends of the spectrum. And I think a lot of times, with stuff li ke this, the truth, per se, is in the middle. I do my own research, and then he does his own research, and we talk about it. I sat downstairs with him today, in fact, and I talked with him for about an hour about politics and the Republican candidate for the 2024 election. [...] So I do fact check with my own news and artic les and stuff that I read, but a lot of my conclusions come from conversations with my dad. With Kala’s short story, we see how she navigates t he complexities of current events and her awareness  of bias and conflicting interests. She acknowledges that her social media feed is showing her news that is from a particular perspective that has been tailored to her interests. Kala’s story also highlights how she relies on her father to help her make sense of the information she is consuming.  Even though she does engage with fact-checking activities, her critical insights are gained once she becomes an active participant in conversations with her father.

## Discussion

Our three participants in this paper, like the others in the larger study, were either news-avoidant or only casually encountered news through their feeds. Some followed  news sources in their feeds, but those were typica lly associated with special interests such as local eve nts or environmental issues. Fo llowing a news source and actively reading news from these sources are different activities, and their primary reasons for using social media were related to social relationships and entertainment, not staying informed about news or learning. For these teens, a combination of network connectio ns and social media algorithms ensured their awareness of the prominent news story about the Israel-Hamas War. Teens are aware of their points of exposure, as can be seen in the case of Charlotte who does no t directly follow news. In contrast, Bella’s story provides an example of how an individual encounters news and th en might find themselves motivated to learn based o n that incidental exposure, as described by Stroud et al. (2022). The teens’ experiences reaffirm s the news-finds-me attitude documented in other studies (Gil de Zúñiga et al., 2017), and also suggests that incidental exposure is not the same thing as accidental exposure (Thorson, 202 0). Accidental exposure implies a randomness to the  news encounter, but still typically reflects underlying relational and algorithmic choices that led to the exposure. Sensemaking was a choice, and teens in this study equally shared instances when they chose or chose not to further investigate a topic. When teens were interested in sensemaking, they engaged in behaviors such as seeking additional online information (e.g., learning more or fact-checking), although further sensemaking also could occur through algorithmic activity (e.g., seeing related content in their feed). The teens’ choices about what topics to pursue and how to pursue them reflect the ir developing ident ities – partly tied to the families and communities that have raised them, and partly tied to the interests and causes that are personally and  socially relevant. In this way, their sensemaking processes integrate identity construction and social interaction, along with both retrospective and ongoing work to revisit  the small stories of their news encounters. As they do this retrospective and ongoing sensemaking work, they generate new layers of small stories that document their understanding across time. Families remain an important part of the exposure and sensemaking process, much as they are for pre-teens (Notley et al., 2022). In terms of sensemaking, the involvement of family and other adults extends well beyond exposure. Teens look to adults as models and conversation partners in this realm. The value of sensemaking is evident when t eens make personal connections to a small story.  Personal connections might seem to imply the development of echo chambers, but people have agency as individuals to transcend their contexts in the sensemaking process (Ancona, 2012). Personal connections are important because they reflect representativeness in news (Vraga & Edgerly, 2023). For example, Bella, when discussing news exposure, frequently referenced the religious, immigrant household that she lived in. Personal connections that increased the value of sensemaking also included school experiences and co mmunity events. For example, teens were excited to see school topics presented in non-school spaces, as if this added to their importance or relevance.

## Implications

This study has implications for how educators help teens develop their sensemaking skills and foster dispositions that enable teens to become savvy in this realm.  Educators might consider how information literacy could be developed throughout the K12 curriculum, incorporating more lessons and guided exploration of how information is presented on  social media and the complexities of how this information can be v alidated. This topic is challenging to address in schools given the forbidden nature of teachers engaging with students on topics related to social media (Dennen et al., 2020), but it nonetheless represent s an area of need. Second, students could be taught how algorithms function and  consequences of echo chambers. Addressing these topics in the curriculum might open pathways for teens to seek teacher support as part of their incidental exposure sensemaking processes. Although this goal may feel difficult in the current political climate, efforts in this direction could support learning and communication for all involved as they explore ways of relating to and understanding online conten t and of managing the emotional and ethical issues that may arise through these news encounters. This study also has implications for how small stor ies may continue to be explored through narrative research. In this study, small stories help to illuminate how the experiences of teens on social media are dynamic and shift depending on the platform and the content. Small stories illuminate facets of the participants’ identities, which is critical in understanding teen behavior and develop ment. Additionally, small stories lend themselves especially well to social media research, where scrolling and consuming form a narrative that is often fragmented, non-linear, and fleeting. ## Conclusion

These three teenagers and their small stories provide a b rief glimpse of how sensemaking occurs in online news encounters. From a learning sciences perspective, this study offers insights into the cognitive pro cesses of sensemaking, with implications for improving the connection be tween school-based learning experiences and incidental, naturalistic learning opportunities that arise in digital environments. The use of small stories opens the potential for further consideration of  how narrative fragments of experience contribute to the development of an individual’s identity, beliefs, and knowledge.
