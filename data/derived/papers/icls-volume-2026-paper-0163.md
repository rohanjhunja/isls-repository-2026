# Teacher as Designers: Using Robotics to Integrate Computational Thinking and Science

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how middle school science teachers designed and implemented robotics-based units that integrated computational thinking (CT) practices following a professional development (PD) program positioning them as curriculum designers. Drawing on Waterman et al.’s (2020) and Coenraad et al.’s (2022) integration frameworks and the project’s CT framework (Bernstein et al., 2025), we analyzed teacher interviews, implementation logs, lesson plans, and student materials from six teachers across twelve units. Findings show that teachers implemented CT at multiple integration levels, engaging students in algorithm design, decomposition, abstraction, pattern recognition, and data practices. Teachers explored CT practices to establish foundational understanding, enhanced CT– science connections through scaffolds, and guided students to exhibit disciplinary ideas through programmable mode ls and extend understanding by collecting and analyzing data. These findings illustrate how positioning teachers as informed designers supports CT-integrated science curricula. Importance Computational technologies are essential for problem solving and collaboration in both professional and personal contexts (NRC, 2014). In STEM fields, these technologies have transformed practice through abstraction, algorithms, decomposition, modeling, simulation, and data analysis enabled by computing (Puttick et al., 2024; Weintrop et al., 2016). Preparing students to participate in such transformations requires the development of computational thinking (CT) practices, defined as “solving problems, designing systems, and understanding human behavior, by drawing on the concepts fundamental to computer science” (Wing, 2006, p. 33). Such preparation positions students to become producers, not merely consumers, of computational solutions. Yet despite growing calls to integrate CT into K –12 science classrooms, teachers face persistent challenges in designing learning tasks where CT meaningfully supports science learning (Cassidy & Puttick, 2022). Teachers are rarely positioned as designers of curricula that combine science, engineering, and computing (Margot & Kettler, 2019), leaving limited evidence on how to support them in planning, adapting, and enacting such curricula in authentic ways (Lee et al., 2020). Conceptual framework Computational Thinking (CT) Prior research offers important frameworks for conceptualizing CT integration. For example, Waterman et al. (2020) introduced a continuum of CT integration in science education, distinguishing between levels where CT exists (CT concepts, skills, and practices already present in the lesson can be called out or elaborated upon by the teacher, often without technology use), enhances (development of additional tasks or lessons that serve to deepen the disciplinary concept while making explicit connections to relevant computing ideas), and extends (redesign or expand lessons so that computing or CT practices become central to students’ engagement with disciplinary content). More recently, Coenraad et al. (2022) added the category of exhibit, which emphasizes students’ representing science knowledge through computational models. These frameworks provide a robust lens for describing the variety of ways CT can interact with disciplinary science learning. Engaging with robotics enables learners to develop CT practices while investigating core scientific phenomena, positioning robotics as a powerful context for integrating disciplinary learning with computational problem solving (Cassidy & Puttick, 2022). Through robotics, students apply key CT practices such as problem decomposition, abstraction, algorithmic design, and iterative debugging. These activities also support engagement in core science practices, including modeling, experimentation, and data collection and analysis (e.g., Sullivan & Hefernan, 2016). For the Robots in Science project, we adopted the PRADA framework (Dong et al., 2019), which emphasizes pattern recognition, abstraction, decomposition, and algorithms. To better align with the needs of 1297 science and robotics integration, we extended PRADA by including a fifth practice, Data Practices (Weintrop et al., 2016). In our framework, pattern recognition refers to identifying similarities among and within problems. Abstraction involves stripping away unnecessary details to develop a generic solution or representing a complex system through a simplified model or visualization. Decomposition is the process of breaking down data, processes, or problems into smaller, manageable parts. Algorithm design c oncerns the creation of an ordered sequence of instructions to solve a problem or perform a task. Data practices include collecting information, analyzing it to generate insights, and representing it through graphs, charts, words, or images. Professional development for teachers-as-designers Positioning teachers as designers acknowledges their capabilities as effective curriculum developers who draw on both disciplinary knowledge and classroom expertise (Penuel & Gallagher, 2009). This approach recognizes teachers’ pedagogical knowledge, their professional judgment, and their ability to innovate in response to their unique contexts (Voogt et al., 2019). When teachers design and/or adapt their curricula and instructional practices, they put disciplinary goals, student learning needs, and classroom resources at the forefront of implementation (Puttick et al., 2025). Professional development that supports this role emphasizes strategies such as active learning, practice-based inquiry, and collaborative design, which create opportunities for teachers to engage directly with new ideas while situating them in the realities of classroom practice (Darling-Hammond, 2017; Tucker-Raymond & Gravel, 2019). Also, time is needed to be allotted for deep play with new technologies and resources (Koehler et al., 2011). Thus, teachers’ curriculum adaptations are shaped by their wisdom of practice and responsiveness to student needs (Calabrese Barton et al., 2021).

Professional development for teachers-as-designers Positioning teachers as designers acknowledges their capabilities as effective curriculum developers who draw on both disciplinary knowledge and classroom expertise (Penuel & Gallagher, 2009). This approach recognizes teachers’ pedagogical knowledge, their professional judgment, and their ability to innovate in response to their unique contexts (Voogt et al., 2019). Thus, teachers’ curriculum adaptations are shaped by their wisdom of practice and responsiveness to student needs (Calabrese Barton et al., 2021). This study investigates teachers as curriculum designers, with particular attention to how they navigate the continuum of CT integration into middle school science. The guiding research question is: In what ways do science teachers use robotics to integrate CT into their science units? Robots in science professional development Teachers participated in professional development (PD) delivered in both virtual and in-person formats (Bernstein et al., 2025).  Early sessions were conducted online due to pandemic restrictions, followed by an in-person workshop. The PD spanned three to five consecutive summer days, with continued support during the school year to assist classroom implementation.

The Robots in Science project aimed to develop teachers’ content knowledge, pedagogical expertise, and technical skills to design and enact CT-integrated science units using robotics. The PD was guided by three core principles: (1) Curriculum Analysis and Integrated Unit Design: T eachers examined their existing curricula to identify opportunities to incorporate CT and robotics, aligning these with science standards and disciplinary models; (2) Robotics and Technology: T eachers developed fluency with robotics kits and programming tools to support student learning, and; (3) Computational Thinking: T eachers engaged with CT as a set of crosscutting practices, identifying where these practices appeared in their curricula and how to extend them. Because this study focuses on CT integration, we describe the CT-related activities in greater detail. Teachers were introduced to the project’s CT framework (PRADA + Data Practices) through a visual poster highlighting key practices. Rather than prescribi ng specific strategies, facilitators prompted teachers to consider how these practices could be recognized, strengthened, and extended within their classrooms. Facilitators modeled each practice using real-world examples, and teachers analyzed curricular t opics to identify existing CT opportunities.

To support engagement in the online setting, we incorporated hands-on “unplugged” CT activities. These included writing step-by-step PB&J algorithms, constructing LEGO ducks to explore abstraction, completing decomposed Mad Libs tasks, identifying patterns through jigsaw puzzles, and collecting and analyzing household data (e.g., shoe or car types). These activities emphasized core CT practices in accessible, low-tech contexts. Following these activities, teachers reflected on the CT practices they enacted and identified parallels within their own curricula. They then engaged with two exemplar CT –science activities: a robotics-based investigation of light reflection and absorption and a robot design task focused on inertia. Finally, teachers designed and built physical robot prototypes using craft materials and the Hummingbird Robotics kit. This process functioned as both a feasibility test and a collaborative design critique. Teachers shared their prototypes, exchanged feedback, and identified the CT practic es embedded in the building and programming process. They then developed full instructional units and participated in structured feedback sessions to refine how CT was integrated into their designs.

## Participants

Thirteen middle school teachers participated in the project’s PD over three years. This study focuses on six teachers who attended multiple sessions and provided the most complete datasets. These teachers taught science or engineering and each implemented two units, resulting in 12 total implementations. Data were collected through multiple sources to examine teachers’ perspectives and classroom enactments of CT with robotics. Primary data included post-implementation and PD interviews and implementation logs. These data captured instructional approaches, design and implementation decisions, and strategies used to support student engagement with CT and robotics. Teachers also shared lesson plans and instructional materials developed for their units. The level of detail in these materials varied, as we intentionally allowed teachers to submit only what was required by their districts. Teachers also provided selected student work and classroom videos, which provided insight into how students engaged with CT practices.

## Data analysis

Data were analyzed using a deductive-inductive approach informed by prior research on CT (e.g., Dong et al., 2019, Bernstein et al., 2025). First, two researchers conducted consensus coding (Cascio et al., 2019) to identify interview and log excerpts capturing teachers’ CT enactment, assessment, student engagement, learning outcomes, and instructional design. Following this initial phase, which established teachers’ intentions for curricular design and implementation, one researcher applied the integration-level frameworks from Waterman et al. (2020) and Coenraad et al. (2022) to characterize the degree and nature of CT integration in each activity. Inductive coding introduced an additional category, Explore, defined as activities that engaged students in CT practices without explicit links to disciplinary content and that built awareness and familiarity. This category replaces Waterman et al.’s (2020) Exist level. Additionally, we applied the PRADA + Data Practices (Bernstein et al., 2025) framework to ident ify the specific CT practices within each level of integration. Analytic memos (Saldaña, 2018) summarized each implementation and documented patterns in how CT practices co-occurred across integration levels. To enhance trustworthiness, another project res earcher provided critical feedback and participated in discussions about interpretations (Creswell & Creswell, 2017).  After coding and memoing, we compared data excerpts across participants to identify recurring patterns in how integration levels were enac ted. These cross-case comparisons informed the organization of the findings by integration level (Explore, Enhance, Extend, Exhibit). The examples presented reflect patterns observed across multiple teachers and units rather than isolated cases.

Teachers integrated several CT practices into their lesson designs, engaging students in activities that reflected varying levels of CT integration.  At the Explore level, teachers often drew directly on activities introduced during PD, even though the project team had not intended for these specific activities to be implemented in classrooms. Several teachers, for example, began their units with the PB&J activity, in which students provided step-by-step instructions for the teacher to make a peanut butter and jelly sandwich. Teachers believed this served as an engaging entry point, helping students recognize the need for precision in instructions and building awareness of CT practices. One teacher mentioned the PB&J activity helped students make “connections between [PB&J and] how they would decompose [their] project into steps.” Similarly, another adapted this idea by having students write and follow instructions for moving a pile of cardboard bricks across their classroom, emphasizing the importance of breaking down the problem into clear, sequenced directions. Both activities reflected algorithmic design because students had to generate ordered instructions, and decomposition because they had to break down a larger task into smaller, manageable steps.

To further familiarize students with CT, teachers integrated vocabulary-focused lessons using the project poster and real-world examples. They described this as valuable in helping students identify when they were engaging in CT practices. Teachers explicitly pointed to the CT practices on the poster during class discussions, reinforcing student engagement with algorithmic design and decomposition even if students did not always use the formal vocabulary. One teacher stated, “Students should be aware becaus e this type of thinking is important for their future…It’s not about memorizing the vocabulary but practicing the thinking.” While Explore-level activities emphasized recognition and awareness of CT, Enhance-level activities shifted toward scaffolding student thinking and slowing down the design process to make CT practices more explicit. Several teachers implemented engineering journals that prompted students to record their ideas, explain design choices, and identify which CT practices they were using. This reflection prevented students from jumping directly into tinkering and instead supported connections between robotics tasks and underlying science concepts. 1299 Teachers also used pseudocode as an intermediate step before programming, asking students to outline the logic and sequence of their intended program. This engaged students in algorithmic design while also fostering abstraction as they identified which aspects of the science concept their robot was meant to model. Others had students complete exit tickets about which CT practices they engaged in and explained how. Extend-level activities, in turn, moved beyond scaffolds by positioning CT as integral to authentic inquiry and experimentation. Teachers designed activities in which robots became tools for conducting and analyzing experiments. In these lessons, students moved beyond applying CT practices in isolation and instead integrated them into authentic inquiry processes, allowing robotics to serve as a bridge between CT and science learning. For example, one teacher had students design robots that could hit balls of different sizes and masses to explore Newton’s third law. Students engaged in decomposition by breaking down the experimental setup into smaller, testable steps, and in algorithmic design by programming the robot to carry out a repeatable sequence of actions. They then engaged in data practices by collecting measurements of how far each ball traveled and analyzing the resulting variation. Through this process, students engaged in pattern recognition by comparing outcomes across trials and identifying regularities that supported conceptual understanding of force and motion. Abstraction was also critical, as students had to strip away extraneous details to focus on the essential relationship between mass, force, and motion.

Exhibit-level integration pushed students to use robotics to represent  and communicat e abstract disciplinary concepts.  T eachers guided students to design and build robots that modeled abstract  scientific concepts. These activities required students to externalize disciplinary knowledge through robotic representations. For example, one teacher had students use robotics to represent and explain the invisible structure of matter, transforming abstract concepts into tangible, programmable models. Students programmed LED lights to blink in patterns representing electron orbitals and used servo motors to simulate rotational motion. These models simplified the complexity of atomic motion into manageable and observable components. Another teacher used robotics to help students practice balancing chemical equations. Students designed and coded robots to signal whether a chemical equation was balanced or unbalanced by using tri-colored LED lights to represent states of equilibrium. Th e teacher explained that “as they programmed, they had to think through how the dial would indicate if the equation was unbalanced or balanced.” This robotics activity served as a performative model demonstrating students’ understanding of chemical principles. Throughout the building and programming process of their robots, students engaged in algorithmic design by creating sequenced instructions to control and operate their model. Decomposition was evident as they broke down a broad science concept into smaller parts that could be represented mechanically. Finally, abstraction played a central role in Exhibit-level work because students had to strip away extraneous details of a complex biological or physical process and highlight only the most important elements that could be modeled with a robot. In doing so, they translated disciplinary ideas into robotic representations and made abstract concepts more visible. This study demonstrates that teachers were able to successfully design and implement unit plans that integrated CT practices through robotics into their existing units. The level of integration varied across teachers as well as the CT practices emphasized in classroom activities. These findings highlight the importance of PD  that intentionally scaffolds the distinctions between integration levels while also foregrounding specific CT practices. An additional insight from this study concerns the role of CT vocabulary. Teachers differed in their views on whether explicit CT terminology should be introduced to students. This responds directly to Waterman et al.’s

(2020) open question about whether making CT explicit benefits students or serves primarily as a teacher-facing

design tool. Like Waterman et al, teachers resisted approaches that reduce CT to memorization of terminology. Instead, they emphasized the importance of students practicing CT through meaningful engagement  in the Explore-level tasks to build awareness of CT practices before connecting them to science content. This study also contributes to the  discussion about the affordances of robotics for CT integration. Robotics kits provided teachers with flexible pathways for integrating CT across multiple levels.  At the Extendlevel, students used robotics to collect, represent, and analyze data which allowed students to identify meaningful patterns and refine their understanding of scientific phenomena, which addresses Coenraad et al.’s (2022) concern about data practices not going beyond the Exist-level. At the Exhibit-level, teachers moved  beyond assessing content understanding by engaging students in creating computational abstractions (Weintrop et al., 2016). These robotic representations made invisible science concepts tangible and interpretable. These uses demonstrate how robotics can serve not only as a vehicle for CT skill development but also as an expressive modeling tool that deepens disciplinary understanding.
