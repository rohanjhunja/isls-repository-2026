# Work in Progress: Training STEM Graduate Students to Collaborate with Stakeholders to Address Socio- scientific Issues

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Socio-scientific issues (SSIs) are complex, real-world problems that arise at the intersection of science and society. SSIs cannot be solved by one discipline alone and should engage stakeholders as part of the problem-solving process. As STEM graduate students are prepared to be scientists and address SSIs, it is imperative that they are trained to collaborate with stakeholders. This scoping review examines how STEM graduate students are trained to collaborate with stakeholders to address SSIs.

Socio-scientific issues (SSIs) are complex real-world dilemmas between society and science, often accompanied by ethical, political, and social ramifications (Sadler, 2004; Viehmann et al., 2024). Some of the most prominent topics of SSIs include the environment (e.g., climate change), genetics (e.g., genetically modified projects), health (e.g., infectious diseases), and local issues (e.g., Tribal water conflict (Rozance et al., 2020)) (Viehmann et al., 2024). Given the complexities of SSIs, it cannot fall on any one discipline alone to solve these problems. Thus, it is important to incorporate stakeholders as part of the problem-solving process. One way to do this is by expanding the boundaries of scientific norms through incorporating other disciplines, a nd utilizing multidisciplinary, transdisciplinary, or interdisciplinary approaches. In this paper, we focus on the transdisciplinary approach(TDA) and how it can be used to train graduate students to address SSIs. The TDA is defined as the collaboration between scientists and stakeholders, working across and beyond their respective disciplines and areas of expertise (Choi & Pak, 2006). Stakeholders may include non-scientists or non-academics (e.g., advocacy groups, politicians) and members of the community affected by SSIs. At the graduate level, individuals are being trained to become experts in their field, honing their scientific knowledge in preparation for their future careers. However, traditional graduate programs in STEM do not train scientists prepared to collaborate with stakeholders.  Thus, we are interested in the ways in which graduate students are being trained to be transdisciplinary; specifically how they are learning to collaborate with stakeholders to address SSIs. This scoping review explores the following question: How are STEM graduate students trained to be transdisciplinary as to collaborate with stakeholders to address socio-scientific issues?

This paper reflects a sub-set of literature identified for a larger meta-synthesis study focused on how STEM interdisciplinary graduate programs attend to broadening participation in their efforts to train students to address community issues. We adopted the systematic approach to search and select  literature as described by Ong and colleagues (2024). The literature works described and discussed here represent a specific theme of incorporating stakeholders into interdisciplinary  or transdisciplinary graduat e training. W e refer to the literature presented in this paper as part of a scoping review, described by Grant and Booth (2009) as, a type of review which “provides a preliminary assessment of the potential size and scope of available research literature. It aims to identify the nature and extent of research evidence” (p. 101). Literature works for this paper were included if they were peerreviewed, published between 2010-2025, based in the United States, included STEM graduate students, described how stakeholders were part of student learning, and described specific socio-scientific issues (e.g., climate change, misinformation). This resulted in 6 relevant literature works within our broader set that  spoke to our question (Cagle et al., 2025; Haak et al., 2022; Hall et al., 2018; Killion et al., 2018; Lekies & Moore, 2020; Rozance et al., 2020).

## Preliminary findings

In this section, we describe the different ways graduate students were trained to be transdisciplinary, specifically how they were being trained to collaborate with stakeholders. Courses and immersive or experiential learning environments Five of these articles described incorporating stakeholders into course curriculum. (Cagle et al., 2025; Haak et al., 2022; Hall et al., 2018; Lekies & Moore, 2020; Rozance et al., 2020).  These included engaging students in 2782 conversation with stakeholders involved in specific socio-scientific issues, such as invasive species (Haak et al., 2022), addressing misinformation to the public (Cagle et al., 2025), climate change (Lekies & Moore, 2020), and societally engaged research (Rozance et al., 2020). In addition to hearing first-hand accounts of SSIs and their impact on communities, students were also asked to research specific SSIs and lead their own presentations (Cagle et al., 2025) or participate in role playing case studies  so that students can reflect on the perspectives of stakeholders (Hall et al., 2018).  Many of these courses incorporated an immersive or experiential learning environment (e.g., Cagle et al., 2025; Haak et al., 2022; Killion et al., 2018; Lekies & Moore, 2020; Rozance et al., 2020) in which students  had opportunities to actively work or  communicate with various stakeholders. To supplement presentations and class discussion, field trips served as an immersive and experiential opportunity for participants to engage with community members and other stakeholders. This ensured that students learn firsthand how SSIs were impacting the community, and how students could use their disciplinary knowledge  and collaborate with stakeholders to address them (Killion et al., 2018; Lekies & Moore, 2020). Setting themselves apart from just going on field trips. Rozance et al.’s (2020) required graduate fellows to engage in “research and information needs voiced by their societal partners [stakeholders]” (p. 6, Rozance et al., 2020). Student outcomes Generally, most students started the course with a deficit perspective of the role of stakeholders  in science (Haak et al., 2022; Hall et al., 2018; Killion et al., 2018). Several students reported that stakeholders should not be part of the research process, with one sharing they “felt that scientific researchers should purposefully exclude the public’s voice in defining or conceptualizing the socio-environmental problem because ‘it is the right thing to do’” (p. 13, Hall et al., 2018). However, post-learning experience, most students reported a greater understanding of the need for “meaningful engagement between scientists and the public … characterized by the inclusion of the public’s local expertise and knowledge” (p. 14, Hall et al., 2018). Immersive and experiential learning opportunities appeared to play a significant impact on students’ learning and understanding of the need to collaborate with stakeholders.  Rozance et al. (2020) reported that students who participated in their fellowship programs “gained direct experience in building and maintaining relationships with their societal partners and shifting research design, methods, timing and outputs as the project developed based on shifts in their partners’ needs” (p. 6). One fellow even acknowledged that  “Science is only one tool in the toolbox. To be an effective actor in society, scientists have to accept this” (p. 6). The six papers presented here shows that STEM graduate students’ exposure to collaborative research and efforts with stakeholders when addressing SSIs were primarily through course work and immersive/experiential learning environments. Courses incorporated presentations from stakeholders, group discussions about SSI s, or roleplaying scenarios where students consider how they would collaborate with stakeholders.  Immersive and experiential learning experiences provided graduate students opportunities to meet and discuss SSIs with local community members outside of the classroom. I ncorporating stakeholders in TD environments served as a mechanism for students to expand their conceptions of who counts as an expert in addressing SSIs. Through these different interventions, STEM graduate students’ perceptions of how stakeholders should be engaged shifted. Prior to engaging with stakeholders, STEM graduate students often held beliefs that emphasized the importance of STEM over other disciplines and perspectives. This may reflect enculturation into norms that emphasize  the idea that STEM knowledge is “objective,” and therefore more “correct” than other ways of knowing. Intentional interactions in courses  with stakeholders challenged these ideas and  led to the students recognizing that stakeholders play important roles in conceptualizing research methods and taking action to address SSIs. That is, without considering stakeholder perspectives and active collaboration, science ’s ability to address SSIs in a meaningful and impactful way is limited.

Given the reports that students had shifts in their perceptions and understandings of the role of stakeholders in science, more research should be conducted to incorporate and expand  such experiences across more graduate programs. In incorporating stakeholder interactions, efforts should be made to institutionalize these approaches to ensure that all graduate students are knowledgeable about the role of stakeholders in science and addressing SSIs. Although class discussions grant a safe space where students  can theorize about how they may interact and collaborate with stakeholders, the literature indicates that immersive and experiential learning offers direct experience in working with stakeholders. Thus, more programs should consider incorporating these opportunities to existing course curriculums and graduation requirements. 2783 Cagle, S. M., Anderson, A. A., & Kelp, N. C. (2025).
