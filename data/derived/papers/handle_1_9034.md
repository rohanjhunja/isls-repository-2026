# The Hidden Meaning of Physical Interaction With Exhibits: The Relevance of the Instrumented Activity Situation Model

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Authors discuss the relevance of The Instrumented Activity Situation (IAS) model in analysis of visitor–exhibit interactions. Observing visitors playing with exhibits in science museums raises questions about the relevance of the actions they undertake. We argue that visitors’ conceptual understandings of scientific phenomena are seeded via rapid iterative cycles of explorative co-attuning between processes of instrumenting and instrumentalization. In our analysis we have demonstrated how the adapted IAS model helps to analyze visitors’ interactions with exhibits.

## The potential significance of the work: the science center context

Exhibits are designed to provoke a series of antici pated physical manipulations that guide the visitor  through potential physical actions with the object. Ideally, as a result, the visitor discovers the exhibit effect and further explores the scientific phenomena. Research provide s evidence of visitors’ signification of their phys ical experiences. Some researchers interpret visitors’ c ontinued repetitions of purposeful attempts to achi eve the exhibit effect along with their exploratory behaviors as pre-symb olic perceptions and actions (van Schijndel, et al., 2010). Under this paradigm, the process of conceptualization can be envisioned as a negotiation between naïve (embodied) and cultural (verbalized) ways of perceiving phenomena. This negotiation can be seen as a dialogue that celebrates both perspectives while seeking reconciliation between them (Abrahamson, 2017). The theoretical and methodological approach: towards an understanding of visitors’ epistemic practices The Instrumented Activity Situation model highlights the intermediary status of instruments (here: exh ibits). It orchestrates the multiple relationships which bind together the three elements constitutive of instrumented activity situations: 1) subject (visitor), 2) object (goal), and 3) instrument (exhibit). The use of an instrument is indicative of the triad and of the multiple interactions which  result from it, forming the common core characteri stic of instrumented activity situations as a class. Accord ingly, an exhibit becomes an instrument only when a  visitor (subject) “has been able to appropriate it for himself – has been able to subordinate it means to his ends – and, in this respect, has integrated it with his activity” (Verillon & Rabardel, 1985, p. 85). Precisely at this moment, when an exhibit is treated as a tool (instrument), the visitor becomes a designer of the action in his or her own right. In the ISA model, the instrument mediates the subject–object interaction. In the case of the visitor–exhibit interaction, the exhibit is the instrument for the effect-object. Thus, the adapted ISA model focuses on the subjec ts’ intentionality – their goals and actions – with rap id iterative cycles of explorative co-attuning desi gn interpretations. The exhibit design plays a mediating role between the instrument and the object. Design is hence understood as the concept of an artifact (exhibit) posited by a designer, that is interpreted by a user. Thus, the exhibit as an instrument of ac tion is conceptualized equally by its designer and the user (visitor). Visitors’ action is thus the (re)interpretation of the artifact, (r e)discovering an exhibit’s design. Possibilities for action are discovered by a visitor in real-time. Often, we can observe tension between the exhibit ICLS2022 Proceedings 2174 © ISLS effect and the visitors’ goal (i.e., what the user wants to do with the exhibit at the moment). Usually, visitors’ goals are multiple, provisional, and change during the interaction – they emerge or get updated as the visitor goes along, constantly (re)developing sensory-motor schemas. Through exhibits, the science center aims to model a particular kind of scientific practice. This practice enables visitors to experience the type of agency that occu rs during a design process. Therefore, in this paper, we invest igate 2 cases of the visitor–exhibit interaction as  a design situation.

Case Swirling Sands: a case of high confluence between the designers’ and visitors’ designs The Swirling Sand exhibit consists of three slowly spinning disks covered in fine-grained sand (Figure 1). As the disks turn, visitors candraw in the sand using their hands or props, such as paintbrushes, scraper tools, and small scoops, available at the exhibit. Visitors can also change the speed of the disk’s rotation usin g a small knob. The motion of the rotating disks and visitors’ fingers produces a variety of intricate circular patterns and shapes. The three workstations also add a social di mension to the interaction through which visitors c an observe and re-mix each other’s work. The data exce rpts presented below are from a young female adult visiting the Swirling Sand exhibit for the first time. Using a flexible protocol, the authors engaged participants in using the exhibit. The interactions were videotaped for subsequent analysis. Once the researcher asked the participant to use the exhibit, no additional instr uction or cues were provided. The participant decid ed by herself what to do and how long the interaction should take. After she ended her interaction with the exhibit, the participant (“Pola”) was shown a video recording of her interaction and was asked to describe her actions and goals. If the participant omitted certain actions, the researcher asked additional questions, such as “What were you doing at this point?” and “Why?” The goal of the semi-structured stimulated recall protocol was to examine the participant’s conceptualization of her goals and actions. Pola used the exhibit for approx. 5.5 min. She was the only person interacting with the exhibit during that time. An analysis of the interaction is presented in the table below. Pola spent almost 80% of intera ction time exploring the exhibit and its various fe atures. She began using the exhibit with some sand on the plate and a relatively high rotation speed. After 5 min., Pola decided to start making patterns using the exhibit as a tool to achieve the effects predicted and intended by the designers.

## Conclusions

In our analysis of these two examples, we have demonstrated how the adapted ISA model helps to analyze visitors’ interactions with exhibits. One direction for further discussion is the evaluative value of the model. Is the degree of intuitiveness of an object equivalent to the ext ent of the confluence between designers’ and visitors’ effects? Conversely, does dissimilarity of designers’ and vi sitors’ effects entail a design failure, or rather successful discovery? How does the visitors’ signification of their physical experience change according to the f ormal or informal context? Such discussion holds promise for implications for exhibit design and facilitation.
