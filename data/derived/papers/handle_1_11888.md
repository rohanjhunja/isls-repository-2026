# The Role of Free Movement in the Physical Space for Productive Disequilibration in Collaborative Simulations

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examined how City Settlers, a simulation that is mapped onto the physical environment, impacts students’ interaction and learning, both within and between groups. In particular, we examined how the open-ended nature of the game and students’ ability to freely move in the space, helped generate productive disequilibrating events. Analysis of group discourse showed that when a member of the group physically leaves and comes back, or a member of another group physically moves to their group, candisequilibrate group discourse towards new productive discourse around the game’s science, civics, and strategic concepts.

## Introduction

Research highlights the significance of physical spaces in shaping students' cognition and learning (Brooks, 2010; Tondeur et al., 2015), particularly within computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL), where room layout, technology, and pedagogy jointly influence outcomes (Ellis & Goodyear, 2016). Distributed intelligence suggests cognition is shared among space, participants, and technologies (Tissenbaum et al., 2022; Pea, 1993). Embedded phenomena exemplify this distributed view by immersing students in simulations to deepen science understanding (Jaeger et  al., 2016; Moher, 2006).  While the physical space can be an important facili tator of learning, its correlation with the ability of students to move wi thin the space can be equally as important. Researc hers have found that the physical and material settings can shape students’ free movement, interactions, and play, which can enhance agency, collaboration, and information exchange (Ha rtikainen et al., 2022; Zeng et al., 2023). In spatial and technology-mediated environments, the ability to move between public visualizations and private spaces fosters new modes of knowledge sharing, community b uilding, and improved communication (Tissenbaum & Slotta, 2019). Foundational across such socially mediated learning environments is the idea that students construct knowledge by interacting with their peers,  and a key part of this learning process is disequilibriation. Disequilibriation, challenges students  to re-examine their presently held understandings by inte grating new information from others through the exchange and negotiation of ideas (Piaget, 1977). I n open-ended, sociallymediated learning environments, peer interactions c an support disequilibration and learning, but they also risk unproductive disruptions such as off-task talk or interruptions. In contrast, productive  disequilibration refers to disruptions that challenge existing understandings and open new possibilities for collaborative inquir y and idea generation at both individual and group levels. Alt hough prior research has established the va lue of distributed intelligence and embedded phenomena in supporting learning, fewer studies have closely examined how physical movement within these environments actively contrib utes to the collaborative process. In particular, t he role of student mobility in fostering productive disequilibration —a mechanism central to conceptual change and group learning—remains underexplored. In response, this paper exam ines how productive disequilibration manifests itself in an immersive, embedded simulation called City Settlers, which allows for free student movement within the space, and contains both private territory (Scott & Carpendale, 2010) for individual, within group, and across group interactions, with a particular focus on: RQ1: How does the physical environment work jointly with  the digital environment to affect students’ interaction s with each other? RQ2: How does students’ free movement in the physical space catalyze their productive disequilibrium?

## Methods

Gameplay and mapping to the physical space In this study, students participated in City Settle rs, a whole-room immersive simulation that transformed their classroom into a shared fictional land, with teams managing cities mapped onto distinct p hysical zones (Figure 1). Teams of 3-4 students made strategic decisions about resources, buildings, and alliances, with physical movement between zones being critical due to resource inequalities. This paper looked at the third day of a weeklong camp, with students already familiar with the game's mechanics and team  members, but assigned new city locations. Students were given no specific instructions and minimal researcher intervention apart from two reflection pauses (Figure 2, grey bar). Within City Settlers, there are no defined win conditions, rather, cities are free to choose what it means for them to succeed (e.g., the biggest city, the richest city, the cleane st city), and whom they would like to work with, or against, to achieve these goals (Kumar et.al, 2021).

## Participants and data collection

Seventeen ethnically diverse middle-school students participated in a 1 week-long STEM summer camp study, with fourteen consenting to audio and video recordi ng (the rest placed in Green City with no recording), demographic factors were not specifically analyzed.

Analysis Gameplay was analyzed using video content logging (Goldman, 2007), focusing specifically on Red City a s representative of both within-group and across-group interactions. Researchers employed a discours e coding scheme adapted from Peppler et al. (2013) to classify student interactions into four distinct categories (Kim et.al, 2021): Game Action Talk (A) (discussions about game actions, like managing resources), Game Metrics Talk (M) (utterances related to scoring or game metrics), Science Talk (S) (conversations about scientific concepts, such as pollution or ecosystems), and Off-Topic Talk (O) (comments unrelated to gameplay or science). Additio nally, instances when Red City students moved from their t able or were approached by students from other citi es were recorded to examine the role of physical movement; non-game-related movements were excluded. Interrater reliability for discourse coding reached Cohen’s Ka ppa = 0.71 (Gisev et al., 2013), with disagreements  resolved through researcher discussions.

A second analytic approach utilized grounded theory  (Strauss & Corbin, 1990) to categorize across-group interactions, particularly instances when stu dents moved into other cities’ territories or inter acted away from their designated city areas. This analys is identified three event types: Strategy events  (Se) involved collaborative planning or agreements; Trading events (Te) encompassed resource trade attempts or successful exchanges; Wandering and Checking events  (WCe) consisted of students' movements withou t explicitly stated intentions or destinations. Students’ announced intentions or subsequent discussions helped researchers categorize these events. Interrater reliability for event coding yielded Cohen’s Kappa = 0.66, again resolving disagreements collaboratively. To evaluate the impact of these across-group events on collective thinking and productive disequilibration, researchers analyzed Red City’s d iscourse before and after each event. Productive disequilibration was indicated by occurrences of (A), (M), and (S), signifying active cognitive engagement and integration of new information (Peppler et al., 201 3). Adapted linkography (Goldschmidt, 2014) further illuminated the progression and relationships among  these productive events, clarifying how student mo vements effectively supported collaborative learning by facilitating productive disruptions (Figure 2 link 1 and link 2).

## Findings

To understand students’ free movement during the game, we examined the Red City’s students’ actions. Student A, B and C’s time spent at the table, moving across the room, and their movement patterns (e.g., moving from Red City directly to another city) across the time were relatively similar. Student D’s movements in t he room mainly occurred in the second half of the game and were mostly wandering and moving around the room. We also looked at events between Red City and other  cities (95 total interactions), involving strategy, trading, or wandering; Red City students visited ot her cities 44 times and hosted interactions 51 time s, with strategy events typically lasting longer than tradi ng or wandering events. The Red City’s students left their territory for three reasons: 1) making plans or future agreements with others (Se-38 occurrences); 2) trading with others (Te-24 occurrences); and 3) wandering and checking on o thers (WCe-39 occurrences). Ses generally were of a longer duration than Te and WCe. Red City and Yellow City  primarily interacted through strategic agreements regarding shared forest resources, resul ting in 32 events (14 strategy Se, 4 trading Te, 17 WCe), with Red City students frequently traveling to Yellow Ci ty’s territory. Red City  and Blue City’s interaction mostly involved earlier agreements to supply lumber, enabl ing Blue City to operate a polluting clay mine, tot aling 25 events (13 Se, 8 Te, 6 WCe), mostly occurring at Red City’s territory. Red City and  Green City’s interactions centered around resource lending due to Green City’s scarcity, resulting in 26 events (8 Se, 7 Te, 13 WCe), evenly split between the two territories. Red City and Pink City had minimal interactions, mainly basic resource trading, leading to only 11 events (3 Se, 5 Te, 3 WCe), predominantly at Red City. After event analysis w e looked at students' collaborative discourse when in Red City’s territory, focusing on within and acr oss group interactions. Analyzing Red City students' discourse revealed differing participation: Student A contributed the most utterances (346), followed by Student B (281), Student C (230), and Student D (90). Se and Te consistently contained productive discourse— (A, M, S)—indicating active engagement and cognitive processing. WCe were less reliably productive, with some lacking discourse entirely. Overall, Se generated the highest average productive discourse, surpassing Te and WCe.

It is worth noticing the WCe events. Student C frequently wandered to Yellow City without  clearly communicating findings, making these initial movements seemingly unproductive. However, later events showed she had acquired valuable strategic insights about Yellow City, such as their lack of clay as a barrier to running a lumber camp. Student D similarly wandered without e xplanation, partly due to disagreements within Red City regarding pollution strategies. However, his wander ing became beneficial, as he returned with critical  bidding information; for instance, he accurate ly informed Red City that Yellow City was bidding " 5 lumbers," allowing his team to strategically adjust their bid to "6" and successfully outbid Yellow City. Although specific wandering events were  no t individually identifiable as productive, these mo vements collectively facilitated Student D’s deeper strategic contributions. This demonstrates that seemingly aimless wandering can be indirectly productive, enabling students to gather crucial strategic intel ligence, reshape their group's bidding strategies, and enhance collaborative decision-making despite initial perceptions of unproductiveness. Free movement, physical space and serendipitous collision We observed that students’ free movement in the physical space enabled serendipitous collisions, where students encountered each other unplanned (Malinin et al., 2 016). In this version of City Settlers, serendipito us collisions occurred when students from different cities arrive d at the same place simultaneously, identified by o verlapping events in Red City’s territory (Figure 2 light pink color highlighted events: a-k). Of the 11 serendipitous collisions in Red City, collision “f” (Figure 2) is notable, involving Event 55 (Se between Red and Green) and Event 56 (WCe between Red and Yellow). During this collision, a Y ellow City student observed Red City giving food to Green City during a negotiation. Although Yellow Ci ty had previously requested food and been refused, seeing Red City agree to Green City’s strategy influenced Yellow City’s approach. Later, in Event 68, Yellow City asked Red City for food again but was still refused. This  likely marked the point where Yellow City realized  Red City would not help them, prompting a shift in strategy to seek alternatives for accessing shared forest resources. The WCe event in collision “f” played a key role in this strategy change, demonstr ating how serendipitous collisions can enable productive disequilibrium. We also found that physical proximity influenced students' free movement patterns and serendipitous interactions: although i nitial visits from all cities were frequent, Wander ing and Checking events decreased sharply with greater dist ance from Red City, as evidenced by fewer v isits from Blue and Pink Cities compared to Yellow and Green. Addit ionally, location-based territories facilitated privacy, enabling strategic negotiations and productive collaboration.

## Discussion

The findings of this study highlight the intricate ways physical and digital environments interact to  facilitate students’ collaboration, particularly through the facilitation of free movem ent and productive interactions. By examining how these elements foster productive dise quilibration in City Settlers, we can better unders tand the role of free movement  in promoting dynamic knowledge construction.  In terms of understanding how disequilibrium can be productive for students in wh ole-class immersive simulations, we aimed to address tw o central questions: RQ1: Our analysis  showed that the integration of physical and digital  spaces shaped the dynamics of student interactions in significant way s. The open-ended nature of the City  Settlers simulation, mapped onto the physical space, organically generat ed frequent opportunities for students to have prod uctive discussions and events. Event series showed us some strategies that students set at the beginning of the game had more impact on future event s and triggered complex productive discourse (i.e.  Se at the beginning of link 1 and link 2). We found th e event sequences that generated rich, productive disc ourse typically began with strategic interactions (Se) and can be catalyzed by wandering and checking on others (WCe), and through serendipitous collisions. These productive interactions relied on  distinct territorial boundaries. Groups of student s occupy separate physical spaces, enabling a sense of owner ship, which is also enhanced by free movement. Simultaneously, the digital simulation dynamically updated content in response to student actions, pro viding visible, shared information across these physical t erritories. When effectively integrated, this inter play between physical territories and responsive digital environ ments can create common reference points, aligning students’ attention, fostering collective and distributed intelligence, and mutually reinforce productive collaboration. RQ2: The location-based setting and free movement facilitated group t erritory establishment, privacy, meaningful intergroup negotiations (e.g., Red City and Yellow City’s strategic resource sharing deal that relied on these spatial dynamics), enabled wondering and checking events, and serendipitous collisions. When multiple events occur simultaneously at the same location, the likelihood  of productivity increases for the events involved.  The convergence of several events at the same time make s it more probable that productive discourse will e merge, which opens new possibilities for collaborative inquiry and idea generation (leading tonew events), even if some of the events (WCe) included students remaining silent and not directly contribut ing. However, as long as other students at the same location are engaged in produc tive discussions, those who passively listen or obs erve may still benefit from the discourse. By taking in info rmation shared by their peers, these students are i ndirectly exposed to ideas that could challenge or reshape their t hinking. This convergence of events creates a great er chance of fostering productive disequilibrium through these interactions.
