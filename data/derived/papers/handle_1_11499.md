# Investigating Coordination in Professional Learning Communities in Higher Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The purpose of this study is to describe four faculty learning communities (FLCs) coordinators’ strategies, challenges, and how they perceived their roles. Data include (i) FLC coordinators’ reflective reports; (ii) in-depth interviews; and (iii) videotaped reflective discussions. Findings revealed five themes: the composition of the FLC, the importance of a pre-existing familiarity between the FLC members, the time constraints, prior pedagogical knowledge, and issues relating to reflective processes.

## Introduction

While a growing number of studies have investigated  Professional Learning Communities (PLCs) as a form  of professional development at the primary and secondary education levels (e.g., Margalef & Roblin, 2016), research on Faculty Learning Communities (FLCs) in higher education has been relatively limited (e.g., Myllykoski-Laine et al., 2023). Studies (e.g., Bedford & Rossow, 2017; Cox, 2004) show that FLCs can significantly enhance faculty development, with evidence suggesting improvements in both student and faculty learning and contributi ng to institutional transformation (Favre et al., 2021).

Although under-researched, FLCs have much to offer as a model of professional learn ing. Several researchers have documented FLCs as an effective way to develop innovative teaching practices (e.g., Cherrington et al., 2018; Cox, 2004; Louca et al., 2023). Similarly, research has also highlighted the central role of coordinating PLC’s work (e.g., Margalef & Roblin, 2016; Nehring & Fitzsimons, 2011). A coordinator is responsible for (a) coordinating the PLC members’ collaborative work, (b) promot ing a PLC working environment that support s professional growth and development, and (c) coordi nating and supporting the PLC group practices to generate new (reflective) knowledge about the PLC members’ teaching practice. This is an interpretive case study (Yin, 2017) seeking to describe ways in which four faculty coordinated four different FLCs in a university in Cyprus, focusing on the coordinators’ perceived roles, actions, and needs. Design and procedures This study is part of a larger project funded by the Cyprus Research and Innovation Foundation seeking, among others to investigate the characteristics of produc tive and sustainable FLCs. Following an interpretive case study approach (Yin, 2017), this study involved 4 instructors at a university in Cyprus who acted as coordinators of four different FLCs in various departments. Two of the coordinators had (some) form al pedagogical background and prior experiences  working in PLC coordination. All FLC coordinators were also undergraduate program coordinators, and their FLCs consisted of instructors teaching in the programs  they coordinated  (with the exception of one FLC that included 2 instructors from other disciplines interested in the topic addressed by the particular FLC).

Data for this study consisted of the coordinators’ reflections and reports throughout the year and of indepth focus interviews at the end of the year. We also collected reflections of the coordinators and their discussions during the coordinators’ reflective meetings. Using  discourse-based approaches and open coding techniques (Strauss & Corbin, 1998), we analyzed all data, looking for characteristics of the coordinators’ roles, the strategies used, and the challenges experienced. Each coordina tor was treated as a separate case, and using the constant comparison approach (Glaser, 1965) we identified common patterns.

## Findings

Despite small deviations in terms of their speciali zation, in all four FLCs, the coordinators highlighted the fact that coordination of their FLC was relatively easy due to the uniformity in the composition of the groups. Having similar experiences from teaching in the same progr am and with the same students turned out to be a powerful tool for supporting a culture of professional learning, communication, and development. That the coordinators were also coordinating the respective program where FLC members were teaching, was also supportive of the FLC work. All coordinators were in a long-lasting collaboration with the members of the FLC, although this was mostly done on a one-on-one basis. In all FLCs (except for FLC 4, which had two members from two other Departments), a collaboration between the members existed before the formation of the FLCs (although in a less organized manner).

Time was also a common, multifaced theme identified. All coordinators suggested that the main problem faced throughout the year was the availability of t ime. Despite some instructor flexibility in higher education, finding a common time to meet turned out to be a struggle for the coordinators, especially d uring the duration of teaching months.  Further, all coordinators reported that they had difficulty f inding enough time to reflect and prepare between the FLC meetings, which was essential for the outcome of the meeting. One of the key differences between the coordinators  was the fact that the coordinators in FLCs 1 and 3 had formal pedagogical knowledge, whereas the coordinators in FLCs 2 and 4 did not have any. At the same time, the coordinator of FLC 1 also had long-term experience in coordinating teacher PLCs, which was also part of her research interests. We suggest that this probably led to several differences observed in the data collected. The coordinators of FLC 1 and 3 described the work of their FLC as a research-based process based on a repeated process of reflecting on data collected by the memb ers’ teaching practice. This resulted in the transf ormation of the group’s initial aim. Their background knowledge  and experiences most likely helped the two coordin ators adopt elements that helped them act in a flexible. On the contrary, the work of the coordinators of FLCs 2 and 4 was described as more technical, in the sense of simply applying tools provided without any adaptations. Reflection was also different in the two groups. Re flection time in FLC 1 was described by the coordinator as an official part of the meeting. It was designed to be a formal, collective process tha t included a personal aspect and a whole group discussion. In FLCs 2 and 4, the coordinators did not structure refle ction as a formal part of the meeting. Rather, reflection was an informal process. These coordinators suggested, however, that several members’ personal reflections were pow erful outcomes of the FLC process, helping them identify issues related to their own needs and make decisions about modifying their teaching practices in the future.

## Discussion

Our findings provide insights into the efforts to promote  sustainable PLCs in higher education. One of the obstacles that possibly contributes to the slow growth of the implementation of FLCs in higher education settings might be related to the lack of pedagogical backgro und knowledge of the FLC coordinators. In the study, background pedagogical knowledge or research interes ts in education supported the FLCs coordinator’s work in various ways, helping them to be flexible and adapt ive, utilize reflection as a tool for professional growth of the FLC group members, or minimize the time needed for preparation for the meeting. A systematic effort to empower FLC coordinators might be an important initiative for p romoting sustainable FLCs. This process should be possibly focused on e.g.,  providing pedagogical knowledge and experiences to FLC coordinators, supporting their needs for coordination, and providing regular opportunities for FLC coordinators to help them develop knowledge and skills necessary for coordinating their PLC meetings, learn about new tools that can support their role, and reflect with peer coordinators on strategies and solutions employed.
