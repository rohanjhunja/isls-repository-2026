# Connecting Chemistry Understanding: An Integrated Framework for Concretizing Abstract Visual Representations

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Visual representations play an important role in students’ understanding of abstract concepts in technical fields like chemistry. However, it can be difficult for students to understand how these representations relate to one another, to underlying scientific concepts, or to the physical world they represent. Education research has explored this issue extensively, proposing approaches such as concreteness fading intended to progressively ground abstractions to concrete experiences; however, these may be theoretically inadequate for domains like chemistry that depend on maintaining (not fading) flexible ties among a triad of three key dimensions: abstract formalisms, scientific concepts, and the physical world. In this conceptual paper, we present a novel, integrative framework that draws inspiration from fine-grained constructivist, multimedia, grounded, and embodied learning theories with the explicit goal of concretizing students’ triadic understanding of chemistry concepts.

## Introduction

Chemistry is the study of matter, its structure, and its transformations, and modern chemistry education is built on particulate models of matter (Taber, 2012). Johnstone (1991) describes a  triadic relationship in chemistry between (a) observable entities in the physical world (e.g., liquids, solids, gases), (b) the structure and interactions among invisible submicroscopic particles (i.e., atoms, molecules, and ions), and (c) visual representations depicting formal, mechanistic models (e.g., symbolic chemical equations). A challenge related to learning how to explain phenomena using chemistry  concepts inherently stems from a paradox: chemists explain the observable world in terms of particle organizations and interactions of the unobservable world. This conceptual paper extends Stieff & Wi lensky’s (2003) work to present an instructional framework that integrates components from finegrained constructivist, multimedia, grounded, and embodied  learning theories. This framework focuses on ways to create and maintain attention to chemistry’s triadic relations and provide design considerations for instructional sequences that could help students concretize and, therefore, understand chemistry concepts more flexibly.

## Theoretical background

Visual representations are important  tools for helping students understand abstract conc epts in chemistry education. One reason is that these representations are used to reify and communicate information about abstract mechanistic models that cannot be directly observed (e.g., by depicting particles within a closed system, a student can see how their behavior affects the internal pressure of a closed container). In doing so, visual representations use iconicity to make these concepts more “concrete” (Ausubel, 1964). Johnstone’s triadic model (Johnstone, 1983, 1991) categorizes observable phenomena as macro, submicro, and symbolic categories of observation  for chemistry education  (see Figure 1). The macro category of observation involves phenomena that are  readily observed without external aids. For example, the to p circle in Figure 1 depicts an oral syringe (used to administer liquid medicine to an infant) that candemonstrate the inverse relationship between the internal pressure and volume of a closed system. This is a macro observation; as volume decreases, pressure increases (i.e., one of the ideal gas laws, Boyle’s Law). The macro category of observation is not intended to be a category of representation; instead, it describes the lens chemists use to make observations in the physical world. This lens is distinct from untrained observation and is developed reciprocally but distinctly, with the submicro and symbolic categories of observation. The submicro category of observation indexically represents invisible phenomena. These representations are based on extant theory and do not often map to the observable world directly; instead, they map to features of some causal-mechanistic model that explains macroscopic behavior. The left circle in Figure 1 depicts molecules as collections of spheres and their motions as red arrows. These specific dep ictions intend to communicate the shape and relative size of the molecule (based on the electron clouds that surround each atomic nucleus) and that those parameters relate to the frequency and force with which they collide with the container (i.e., h ow kinetic molecular theory conceptualizes pressure).

The symbolic category of observation involves representations that depict or predict relationships among chemical phenomena. These are often used to summarize, evaluate, or predict relationships; they can also describe features of causal-mechanistic models. For example, the right circle in Figure 1 d epicts two ways to summarize the inverse relationship between the pressure and v olume of a  gas in a  closed container. One is a graphical depiction that plots the effect that modifying the volume of a syringe has on its internal pressure. T he other is a mathematical representation used to predict the result of a change in volume on a change in pressure. The submicro and symbolic categories of representation are complementary. For  example, Figure 2 depicts Boyle’s law using submicro representations to depict molecules within a closed syringe. As the volume of the syringe decreases from the left to the right side of Figure 2, the reader can observe the effect that reducing the syringe volume has on the frequency with which molecules collide with the walls of the container, resulting in higher pressure compared to the left image. Thes e submicro representations emphasize context-dependent causal-mechanistic information (i.e., information specific to the mole cules that are depicted and the conditions they are under) while st ill maintaining the invariant structural components  of symbolic representations (an example of which is shown below the two syringes in Figure 2). This symbolic representation summarizes the inverse relationship between the pressure of a gas and the volume of its container. It can be modified to predict the resulting pressure after a change in volume to any given closed system  (if all other conditions are held constant). This symbolic representation minimizes context-dependent information while emphasizing the invariant structural components of the pressure-volume relationship for a gas. While the symbolic representation does not describe the effect o f a volume change on any one system, it does represent the underlying relationship between the pressure of a gas and volume of its container that can be applied generally to any gas in any closed containe r. Thus,  t he submicro and symbolic representations are complementary and together summarize, analyze, and predict observable chemical behavior (Johnstone, 1991). We see that the affordances symbolic and submicro category representations offer for learning are complementary. Specifically, submicro representations can support a deep understanding of  a concept in a particular application context (Taber, 2013). However, students find it challenging to apply th eir knowledge beyond the context where it was learned (i.e., it i s difficult for students to transfer it;  Tuckey & Selvaratnam, 1993). Symbolic category representations can support a more abstrac t understanding of a concept that can be applied in many contexts (Taber, 2009). However, students find it challenging to understand the deeper conceptual meaning of abstract representations (i.e., developi ng a robust, grounded understanding of the concept;  Bain & Towns, 2016). Therefore, depending on the task, chemists may leverage either or both types of representations to explain or interpret phenomena at the macro category (Kozma et al., 2000). If a chemist were asked to explain the effect that reducing the volume of a closed contain er would have on its internal pressure, they would likely leverage either submicro or symbolic representations to explain this observable change. The explicit goal of a chemistry education research subfield involves understanding how students draw connections among multiple visual representations (e.g., Gilbert & Treagust, 2009; Kozma & Russell, 20 05). Although Johnstone’s triadic model functions at the  submicro and symbolic level, students often struggle with relating these to the ir experience (Bodner, 1991). This may be because the macro category does not describe students’ everyday experiences, instead, the macro category describes a model of the  experience that makes certain assumptions or removes details. In fact, across the three levels of representation, none can be understood through direct observation alone. A student cannot explain how the particles inside t he syringe affect internal pressure without understanding the submicro and symbolic representations that support this explanation. For example, if the reader were to open the oral sy ringe in Figure 1 halfway, pressing a thumb tight against the opening would create an approximately c losed system. The reader could then decrease the vo lume of the syringe to feel resistance as the pressu re inside increases. While this demonstration prese nts one way to experience the relationship between  the pressure and volume of a gas directly, a causal mechanistic model is needed to explain how and why this phenomenon occurs. Only when s tudents are trained with symbolic and submicro category representations  can they observe this demonstration as a chemist would (i.e., Figure 2: leveraging both a generalized symbolic description and a submicro model of matter behavior to explain a causalmechanistic model for the phenomenon). This creates a hermeneutic cycle where each leve l of observation informs the other and, therefore, uncovers increasi ng levels of nuance to the underlying structural fe atures of the causal-mechanistic model. One problem such a hermeneu tic relationship poses for instruction is that the cycle must begin with one of these three related levels of observation. The most common approach to teaching these categories of observation among traditional textbooks and courses begins with symbolic or submicro category representations. This follows a similar logic to the tradition in mathematics education of teaching “formalisms first ” (Nathan, 2012). This involves organizing learning a nd understanding abstract concepts using abstract representations in the classroom bef ore applying this knowledge tonew or application contexts. In doing this, inst ructors focus on students’ ability to interpret the ir experiences through formalized representations like the symbolic or submicro category representations described above. The logic for these approaches is that students cannot observe the world in terms of the macro category until they can imagine it in terms of submicro and symbolic representations. Other research argues that instruction should begin  with the macro category and become increasingly abstracted (Johnstone, 1983; Lin et al., 2016). These instructional sequences prioritize students’ interpretation of the world in terms of macro category observations before interpreting them in t erms of submicro or symbolic category observations. The logic for these approach es is that students are more familiar with the worl d in terms of the macro category and can subsequently relate their understa nding of submicro and symbolic representations with respect to these observations.

In what follows, we will argue that instruction should start wit h legitimate embodied experiences. Because the macro category of observation involves interpreting the world as a chemist does (i.e., using particulate models of matter to engage with causal-mechanistic reasoning), macro category observations are still an abstract interpretation of students’ experiences. Therefore, we present an integrative framework that argues that instruction should begin with a legitimate embodied experience in the world to ground such abstract observations. Research has defined expertise in chemistry educati on as having the ability to flexibly translate amon g multiple visual representations (Davenport et al., 2008). Therefore, we present a framework that integrates research that has been demonstrated to support such deep understanding. We first take a fine-grained constructivist epistemological perspective. In doing so, interpreting students’ understanding of the underlying chemistry concept depicted by multiple visual representations involve s understanding how they relate. Multimedia Learnin g Theories support students in understanding how multiple repr esentations relate to one another and to target concepts. Lastly, Grounded and Embodied learning theories argue that legitimate embodied experience supports deep conceptual understanding.

Inspirations for a new framework Fine-grained constructivism Recent chemistry education research has conceptuali zed understanding in fine-grained constructivist principles (Rodriguez & Towns, 2021; Spitha et al., 2024). diSessa’s  (2018) Knowledge in Pieces (KiP) framework defines scientific knowledge as coordination classes in whi ch a complex system of small pieces of knowledge co mbine to describe a larger scientific concept. diSessa ar gues that to develop a robust understanding of a ta rget concept, students must understand the smaller pieces that constitute the larger concept, including how these pieces integrate to describe the concept.  Granted, novices will see  the relevance of pieces of knowledge differently t han experts (Chase & Simon, 1973; Chi et al., 1981); thus, we argue novice students must integrate the individual knowledge pieces.

Koedinger and colleagues’ (2012) Knowledge-Learning-Instruction (KLI) framework presents design considerations for instruction to support understanding different types of knowledge pieces based on complexity. Instructional design, they argue, should align with the knowledge type for students to learn each knowledge piece. Students must engage in explicit and verbally mediated processes using supports like self-explanation and explicit instruction to understand a concept. For example, t he behavior of gases  can be decomposed into several knowledge pieces; Boyle’s law (Figure 1) is one such piece. This inverse relationship between the pressure of a gas and the volume of its container can be represented in different ways to different ends using macro, submicro, and symbolic representations. Only when these representations are  integrated (according to KiP)  can a student develop a deep and flexible understanding of Boyle’s law. Multimedia learning theories While the fine-grained constructivist and expertise literature has focused on knowledge more broadly, Multimedia Learning research focuses on understanding how visual repres entations relate to one another and relate to the target concept. It is important to consider that students learn bes t from multiple visual representations when they understand how the representations relate to one an other (i.e., they have developed connectional under standing; Rau, 2017) and that these processes are verbally mediated (Mayer, 2009; Schnotz, 2002). Students have developed connectional understanding when they understand how one representation’s features relate to another. According to multimedia learning theories, studen ts understand connections among multiple representations once they have extracted t he relevant knowledge pieces from multiple representations and integrated them into a cohesive mental model (Ainsworth, 2006). While multimedia learning research recognizes that students must make connections among multiple representations, they do not always consider these connections rooted in experiential learning. Grounded cognition (Barsalou, 2008) argues that the most effective way to construct a coherent  mental model for a target concept is to connect to experiences that are often embodied (Varela & Thompson, 1991) Grounded and embodied learning theories Grounded and embodied learning research applies pri nciples from grounded and embodied cognition to educational contexts, arguing that the mind evolved  to simulate and predict bodily actions in the worl d (Wilson, 2002). Perception and action are important components of  thought (Goldstone & Barsalou, 1998)  such that generalizable and transferrable knowledge becomes e ncoded in formal systems of symbolic and diagrammat ic notation that attain meaning by virtue of being gro unded in analogies and metaphors that stem from rea l-world experiences (Lakoff & Johnson, 2008). Consequently, learning environments that ground abstract formalisms and novel concepts in concretized experiences improve l earning (Nathan, 2014). Moreover, grounding formalisms and concepts through embodied experiences promotes a deeper understanding of the concept in context (Fyfe et al., 2015) and a more flexible understanding that may transfer across applications (Goldstone et al., 2010). In practice, recent research in mathematics education offers a promising new direction that could support students in developing both a deep and transferrabl e understanding of the underlying causal-mechanistic chemistry models these visual representations depict. Theories of grounded and embodied learning (Nathan, 2014) describe the importance that contextual factors (li ke experiences of initial learning) play in develop ing a deep understanding of how to connect the invariant structural components of the model to our experiences in the world (Barsalou, 2008). In essence, because knowledge is grounded in perception and action, observing or performing these physical actions grounds a deep understanding of the structural and functional relationships in experience. In research, participants have demonstrated how exp eriences can tether perceptions (Clement et al.,

1989) to subsequent concepts and learning (Nathan & Walkington, 2017). Thus, according to theories of grounded

and embodied learning, the goal of using multiple v isual representations is to tether students’ knowle dge about them to experiences and to the underlying causal-mechanistic model. In doing so, this produces a robust, experience-based, and cohesive conceptual model. Unfortunately, deep understanding is inflexible and situated in the context where it was learned, making it challen ging for students to apply to novel contexts and ap plications (Kaminski et al., 2013).

Grounded embodied learning in practice: Concreteness fading Concreteness fading  is a theory of instruction supported in the researc h literature for effectively grounding conceptual understandings of symbolic representations in mathematics while also supporting students’ application of these concepts in different ways (Fyfe et al., 2014; Gentner et al., 2003; Goldstone & Son, 2005).  The general idea of concreteness fading is that students should start with an embodied experience and work with increasingly abstracted versions of the representation until all  contextual information is abstracted and the stude nt is left to symbolically represent underlying structural and fu nctional relationships.  In a recent review, Fyfe & Nathan

(2019) defined concreteness and abstraction on a continuum. We draw from this theoretical perspective to define

abstraction as the extent to which the representation has become idealized. Concreteness is determined by the perceptual richness and the number of contextual factors that surround the representation. Concreteness fading advances Bruner’s (1966) three-step progression for learning abstract concepts into a theory of instruction that emerges from the groun ded and embodied mathematics education research lit erature (Fyfe et al., 2014). This progression involves lear ning relationships among observable (i.e., experien tial) phenomenon and their abstract representations. The idea is that students learn from rich contextualized (concrete) experiences to eventually understand how symbolic s ystems (i.e., abstract formalisms) relate to the underlying concepts (Fyfe et al., 2014; Goldstone & Son, 2005).

For instance, in chemistry, understanding the invariant structural component of Boyle’s law (i.e., that the pressure and volume of a closed container at consta nt temperature are inversely proportional) could be grounded in a real-world context while also affording a robust enough understanding to transfer across contexts (e.g., when Boyle’s law is integrated with other simple gas laws to result in the ideal gas law). The explicit epistemological goal for our use of concreteness fading is to scaffold the development of flexible and deep understanding. However, this prog ression involves fading the concrete and contextual ized representations during the instructional sequence to shed or “leave behind” the original situated experi ences that provide the initial grounding so that  these perceptually rich associations do not “weigh down” the abstractions and interfere with their role in knowledge transfer. While fading can also occur in a chemistry lesson that involves multiple types of visual representations, it is far  from the goal. Chemistry learning, in contrast to some math education, must continue to activate multiple representations to describe concepts in problem-solving contexts. The integrated framework This section presents a series of recommendations and actionable steps for implementing an integrated framework designed to maximize the affordances and minimize the limitations of each theoretical perspective (see Figure 3). First, we argue that instructors should identify the key and underlying structural relation ship for their target concept. In the case of Boyle’s law, this is the inverse rel ationship between pressure and volume  resulting from particle collisions. After identifying the target concepts, instructors must create a way to experience the target concept (i.e., ways for students to ground their understandin g of this abstract model through an embodied experience that includes sensorimotor feedback rather than purely visual feedback). This experience must be designed with the explicit intention for students to subsequently gen erate a cohesive situational model. For Boyle’s law, this experience can be as simple as using a syringe to feel its internal pressure increase as the student decreases its volume.

Next, we suggest that instruction focus students’ interpretations on the submicro and symbolic categories of observation.  This includes focusing on the aspects of the embodi ed experience and focusing on how they can be abstracted as submicro and symbolic visual representations. This will invoke the principle of concreteness fading with a separate fading progression used for each. For example, students could use a pressure se nsor to measure the change in pressure that results in a volume change.  This data can be represented symbolically as a graph and with the notation of changes as a mathematical formula. Thei r embodied obse rvations and measurements can also be represented as submicro representations depicting the particles' behavior w ithin the syringe. For both representations, the fading progressions are tethered to the same initial experience. Nonetheless, it is still possible that the impact o f the embodied experience could not potentiate and consolidate in students’ memory. Thus, we recommend  using connection-making supports (Rau, 2017). These supports are instructional scaffolds that help construct each knowledge piece into one coherent mental model. For example, self-explanation prompts can help students reinforce their understandings by anchorin g their attention to how components of their experience map onto the submicro and symbolic representations during each step of the fading progression. This will help students dra w connections among features of each representation  (e.g., by answering: How does the behavior of the particles i n the simulation relate to the graphical depiction of changing pressure and volume?) as well as drawing connections between each representation and the underlying structural relationship that the concept depicts (e.g., by ans wering: How does the graphical depiction of changin g pressure and volume relate to your initi al experience with the syringe?).  Embodied activities connect students’ feelings from their experience to their thoughts about the experience (i.e., macro observations). This paper aims to extend grounded and embodied learning to the field of chemistry education by exploring whether and how the grounded and embodied research literature can improve the design of instructional activities and materials for supporting students in making and maintaining connections from multiple representations of chemistry concepts. In this framework, a deep understanding is the result of students’ ability to make bidirectional connections among multiple representa tions (Stieff & Wilensky, 2003). Implementing this framework that extends grounded and embodied learni ng to chemistry representations leads us to two tes table hypotheses. Testing these hypotheses will help theoreticians, researchers, and practitioners consider, identify, and put into practice techniques for connecting chemistry concepts using multiple representations. Hypothesis 1 This integrated framework supports students’ deep understanding of the target concepts (i.e., designing instruction to begin with concrete and grounded embodied experiences before moving to more abstract depictio ns of the phenomena leads to a deeper understanding of the concept) than any other instructional sequence. Evidence from mathematics education research has demonstrated tha t when students’ understanding is  grounded in concrete experiences, they develop a deeper yet flexible und erstanding of the target concept compared to any ot her sequence of instruction (McNeil & Fyfe, 2012). Howe ver, whether these results should apply to chemistr y education research is unclear. Therefore, empirical  work must evaluate whether and how concrete and gr ounded experiences can affect students’ understanding of t he concept. In doing so, we advocate that future researchers consider investigating the implementation of this i ntegrated framework to complement the role that vis ual representations play in chemistry education. That is, students must start with an experience that is then faded with explicit scaffolds to connect prior knowledge, paying partic ular attention to the affordances of the macro, submicro and abstract representations. We hypothesi ze (H1) that implementing this framework will lead to mor e robust conceptualization as evidenced by students’ ability to explain bidirectional connections among multiple representations than traditional teaching of visual representations of chemistry models. Hypothesis 2: Given that this integrated framework supports stude nts in understanding the connections among represen tations when connection-making supports are included. We posit that impleme nting this integrated framework not only helps students develop deeper, connected understandings by reinforcing the connections among knowledge pieces but also engenders a more generalized understanding tha t helps students develop more flexibility in their application such that they better transfer across contexts. We hypothesize (H2) that in practice, students will show better near and far transfer of chemistry concepts as is evidenced by their ability to learn related future material than students learning from more traditional sequences. Next steps This integrative framework is a promising start addressing some challenges in chemistry education; we hope this makes undergraduate chemistry more accessible to al l students. This conceptual framework is designed to help research and practice for teaching and learning in chemistry with more than just visual representations. Our future research will empirically test  our earlier hypotheses using a combination of randomized contro lled trials with qualitative assessments. We will recruit from an undergraduate population with a large enrollment in introductory chemistry courses. An experimental design will allow us to evaluate whether this integrated framework improves educational outcomes compared to instruction that does not include embodied experiences. Additional qualitative interviews and assessments will highlight how students’ insights benefit when connections between observations are grounded.
