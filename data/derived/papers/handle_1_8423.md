# Facilitating Productive Struggle in Science Education: The Possible Benefits of Managing Scientific Uncertainty During Sensemaking

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Utilizing students’ scientific uncertainty as a resource for deep learning opportunities has been introduced for productive sensemaking in science education. However, productively managing the emerging uncertainty is challenging for teachers and students in that they are often frustrated by or ignore the uncertainty during sensemaking. It also remains unclear what drives or obstructs sensemaking when students are learning science concepts, and when the process advances or recedes. This paper aims to suggest a conceptual framework to understand how uncertainty management can facilitate students’ sensemaking so that they can experience productive struggle. To develop the framework, this theoretical paper explores sources and types of scientific uncertainty, and how the management of these uncertainties does or does not promote the productiveness of students’ struggle during sensemaking. Lastly, a framework is proposed to translate the theoretical constructs into classroom practices.

## Introduction

An extensive research strand emphasizes the importa nce of struggling experiences in developing a deep understanding of science concepts, phenomena, or so lutions. Among them, to name a few, are impasse-dri ven learning (VanLehn et al., 2003), productive failure  (Kapur, 2008), and productive struggle (Warshauer,  2015). The underlying ideas across these theoretical approaches to confronting impasse are consistent regardi ng its prerequisite: the recognition or identification of the impasse, gap or inconsistency in the current kn owledge structure offers opportunities to move one step for ward, and then, the subsequent intentional and effo rtful struggling to overcome the challenges yields productive learning outcomes. In a similar vein, utilizing students’ scientific uncertainty as resources for deep learning opportun ities has been explored in science education (Beghetto, 2017; Manz, 2018). Practices of science (e.g., Next Generation Science Standards [NGSS], 2013) inherently involve dealing with uncertainty. When students are require d to engage in scientific practices to make sense of sci entific phenomena, identifying and exploring what t hey are uncertain about candrive their process of sensemak ing (Odden & Russ, 2019). However, recognizing and maintaining emerging uncertainty is challenging for  students; they are often cognitively overwhelmed o r even emotionally frustrated, and thus tend to ignore the  uncertainty (Chen, 2022; Jordan & McDaniel, 2014). Instructional supports are thus needed to gear stud ents’ struggling toward productive management of th eir scientific uncertainty when engaged in sensemaking during science learning. Researchers have explored the process of sensemaking in order to identify when and with what students may struggle and offered frameworks for how teachers can support the sensemaking process. They have identified a series of non-linear phases of sensemaking (e.g.,  inconsistency or gap recognition, generation of ex planation, and resolution; Odden & Russ, 2019). However, it remains unclear what drives, obstructs, or resumes the process of sensemaking and when the process advances and recedes. To uncover this issue, this theoretical paper aims to suggest a conceptual framework to understand how uncertainty management can facilitate students’ sensemaking process so that they can experience productive struggle. Sensemaking and scientific uncertainty Odden and Russ (2019) defined sensemaking as “the p rocess of building an explanation to resolve a perc eived gap or conflict knowledge” (p. 187) and identified what elicits and sustains the process of sensemakin g (e.g., perceived gap as an entry condition, lack of discus sion as constraints, or satisfactorily resolved gap  as an exit condition). Building on this previous research, thi s paper suggests four phases of sensemaking: phenomena representation, knowledge gap recognition, exploration, and application and evaluation of potential resolution. Along with the four phases of sensemaking shown in  Table 1, this paper highlights iterative and effortful uncertainty management with which students are engaged during sensemaking and explains the (meta-)cognitive engagement involved in uncertainty management durin g each phase. In this manner, this paper advances a n understanding of the role of students’ uncertainty management in driving or impeding sensemaking relat ed to scientific phenomena, and of when and how instructional strategies can be implemented during sensemaking. To unpack the relationship between uncertainty management and productive struggle, this theoretical paper distinguishes desirable uncertainty from undesirable uncertainty and posits how managing the  two types of uncertainty can cause either curiosit y or frustration through productive or unproductive struggle as illustrated in Figure 1. In addition to the discussion on desirability of uncertainty for productive struggle, two major sources of scientific uncertainty, content knowledge and epistemic knowledge, are introduced.

Sources of scientific uncertainty Uncertainty in learning is defined as a subjective experience of being aware that one has insufficient or incoherent knowledge, or ability to make sense of phenomena, s ituations, or solutions (Chen, 2022; Jordan & McDan iel, 2014). The feeling of uncertainty is subjective because it is experienced differently between individuals depending on the degree to which individuals react to novelty, complexity, unfamiliarity, ambiguity, and lack of  clarity. Individuals’ ability to understand the given topic also interacts with these causes of uncertainty to spark students’ subjective uncertainty (Silvia, 2010).

In science classrooms, there are two major sources  of scientific uncertainty against which students experience novelty, complexity, etc. during science learning: Content and epistemic knowledge. In other words, content and epistemic uncertainty can be distinguished in terms of what is known and how it is known. Content uncertainty can be defined as individual’s subjective feeling that they are unaware of or unconfident about the terminology, principles, or theories being discussed (e.g., “What theory can explain why the small ball fall faster than the big one with the same weight?”), while epistemic uncertainty refers to individual’s subjective feeling that they are unsure about “how we know what I know and why we believe it” (Duschl, 2008, p.269; e.g., “How can I know that the air makes the difference in the speed of falling balls?”). Types of scientific uncertainty Additionally, content and epistemic uncertainty can  both fall into additional categories determined by  their desirability: Desirable and undesirable uncertainty. Regarding the types of uncertainty in this vein, researchers used different but similar terms, such as good and bad uncertainty (Beghetto, 2017) and productive uncertainty (Manz, 2018). The authors concur with these distinctions of uncertainty in that they address that not all uncertainty is detrimental or beneficial. Nevertheless, this paper prefers to frame uncertainty based on its desirability. This is in part because this paper distinguishes uncertaint y from uncertainty management which Beghetto (2017)  and Manz (2018) did not. It is the productive uncertainty management that can make even “bad” uncertainty desirable at some points. The desirable uncertainty is desira ble because it concerns the knowledge which is cruc ial to ICLS2022 Proceedings 1118 © ISLS sensemaking of the topic and it emerges at an appro priate time (e.g., emerging during the phenomena representation rather than interrupting the process of reducing other crucial uncertainties). Engaging in productive struggle through uncertainty management While it is individual differences that makes uncertainty desirable or undesirable at the beginning of sensemaking during science learning as stated above, the productive management of both types of uncertainties can encourage students to engage in productive struggle and spark curiosity for learners to yield productive learning outcomes. Productive struggle  involves the process where students grapple with t heir understanding of the presented information and actively work through the struggle to make sense of the problematized phenomenon and advance their own understanding (Warshauer, 2015). In this theoretical paper, we adapted the Fredricks’s et al. (2004) framework of examining school engagement to represe nt student engagement at the level of a learning ev ent. With this framework, this paper distinguishes produ ctive struggle from unproductive struggle regarding  three dimensions of engagement: behavioral, emotional, and cognitive engagement (See Figure 1). This framework not only helps construct a multifaceted understanding o f individual differences during uncertainty management but also suggests instructional strategies for productive uncertainty management in multifaceted ways. Gee (2017) emphasized that the combined effects of behavioral, emotional, and cognitive engagement facilitate the process of resolving the recognized uncertainty. For example, Gee identified a) well-ma naged attention to what students fail to make sense and b) interests to what they are paying attention to and c) intentional caring for the uncertainty as crucial factors of meaningful learning experiences (i.e., productive struggle). On the other hand, students may disengage in their learnin g when they lose their attention, feel uncontrollab le, or get cognitively overwhelmed partially due to their perc eived inability to understand or due to persistent failure (D’Mello & Graesser, 2012; Silvia, 2010).

Outcomes of uncertainty management Desirable uncertainty can spark curiosity when it is productively managed, but it can also frustrate learners when it unnecessarily persists unresolved (D’Mello & Gra sser, 2012). Similarly, undesirable uncertainty can  also frustrate learners when it is, for example, too complex to resolve, but it can also lead to students’ curiosity when it is strategically managed (See Figure 1). It should be noted that productive uncertainty management is not easily achieved and thus needs the combined effort of a st rategic approach utilizing student, peer, and teacher support (Chen, 2022; Jordan & McDaniel, 2014). A strategic approach will include, for example, (collaborativel y or individually) raising, maintaining, and reducing un certainty through questioning, comparing, or genera ting explanations about issues of uncertainty at an appr opriate time. One means of translating this approac h into practice is discussed in the following section.
