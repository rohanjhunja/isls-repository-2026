# A Womanist Analysis of an Asset-Based Anti-Gun Violence Curriculum

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Researchers collaborated with a social justice organization to co-design a curriculum for elementary and middle school youth to create an interactive map highlighting community assets as an exploration towards how they might combat gun violence. The curriculum was designed around Asset-Based Community Development (ABCD) to engage students in asset mapping for social action. However, the researchers noted a lack of guidance for addressing the systemic underpinnings of gun-violence. In response, the researchers applied a womanist lens—which reflects Black women and women of color’s everyday strategies for ending all forms of oppression—to examine how it might shape reflection and iteration on the curriculum. Analyzing curricular resources and codesign notes, the findings highlight the importance of fostering relationships to redistribute power, center dialogue for engaging in tensions, defining a collective communitarian approach with learners, supporting learners in storying their communities, and engaging in care and wellness as resistance.

## Introduction

Social justice education aims to, “enable individua ls to develop the critical analytical tools necessa ry to understand the structural features of oppression and their own socialization within oppressive systems ” (Bell, 2016, p. 4). Researchers have emphasized that the d esign goals of the work should be “fundamental soci al transformation” (p. 566), which directly engages wi th transforming relations between individuals, comm unities, and social institutions (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016). The work is a collaboratio n between researchers and an educational organization, Digital Interactive Visua l Arts & Sciences (DIVAS) for Social Justice, who d evelops informal learning contexts for youth to engage in social justice education such that they are contributing to social action projects with tangible impacts in their local environment. Situated within a larger collaboration examining how justice-centered education with technology can address structural inequities in society, the codesign team— involving university researchers and organizational  leaders in DIVAS —created an Anti-Gun Violence Asset-Mapping Project. Given the prevalence of gun violence within the local community, the main objectives were for youth to understand gun violence as a public health  issue and use community mapping and multimodal too ls to highlight community assets that are important for preventing gun violence. In framing the curriculum, researchers and partners from a local, social justi ce organization drew from asset-based community development (ABCD), which facilitat es community members in solving problems by identifying and capitalizing on individual, institu tional, and associational strengths in their commun ity (Kretzmann & McKnight, 1996; McKnight & Kretzmann, 1 993). However, it is a strategy for community mobilization (Mathie & Cunningham, 2003) rather than an analytica l tool for addressing historical and systemic forms of domination—a necessary practice in social action work. Acknowl edging that the executive director of DIVAS is a Black woman from the community, this pap er draws from Phillips’ (2006) conceptualization of womanism to explore how we address oppressive forces in social justice education. As a social change perspective, womanism centers the everyday experiences and efforts of Black women and other women of color to “better the world aroun d them for all of humanity” (Black Metropolis Resea rch Consortium, n. d.). In the past, DIVAS has implemented curricula that reflects characteristics of womanism, such as their work on the Black Housing Project, in which learners gathered personal stories around Black homeowners in their neighborhoods and engaged in conversations about what makes a house a home. The work was intended to provide a counternarrative around who is able to  own homes in the city, explore diverse pathways to homeownership, and engage in documenting the various stories of people in their communities. Because we had already started se eing characteristics of womanism embedded within th e organization’s work, we decided to analyze the current curriculum and our codesign work to explore the research question: How do the characteristics of womanism, applied as an analytical tool for an a sset-based justice-oriented curriculum, provide pedagogical insights on development of the curriculum? We engaged in iterative, deductive analysis using w omanism as a lens to explore the codesign and curricular artifac ts. The paper offers insight in how womanism helps conceptualize pedagogical structures for social justice education.

## Background

Asset-based community development (ABCD) Asset-based community development (ABCD) emerged in oppos ition to dominant need-based approaches and deficit framings of social problems that exist in a community (Mathie & Cunningham, 2003). Deficit approaches take the position that minority students and families perform poorly academically because they l ack normative proficiencies needed to excel in schools. These approaches also assume that the parents and community members of minority students are apathetic about their educational needs (Yosso, 2005; Ladson-Billings, 2007). To counter this approach, ABCD highlights the strong suits of a community and its members, recognizing the capacities and capabilities of the individual, associations, and institutions that make up the community (Kretzmann & McKnight, 1996; McKnight & Kretzmann, 1993; Mathie & Cunningham, 2003). The three core tenets of ABCD are that it is asset-based coming from the community’s assessment of their individual, associational, and institutional level assets, it’s internally focused, drawing on the strengths and capacities of its assets t o develop solutions, and it’s relationship driven building and rebuilding connections within the comm unity to mobilize work (Kretzmann & McKnight, 1996). With these in mind, Mathie and Cun ningham (2003) assert that ABCD forges communal participation where members use their social capita l–“the potential su pport and assistance that stem from the social relationships they have cultivated or inheri ted through family or class membership” (p. 479) –to leverage their social and economic capacities in advancing their goals. Although ABCD provides a useful orientation, we fou nd it did not offer direction for engaging with systemic inequities. For example, unlike theories l ike community cultural wealth –which describes the cultural knowledge, practices, and relations possessed by ma rginalized groups (Yosso, 2005) –ABCD lacks approaches that support a critical contextual and historical anal ysis of structural discrimination answering questions, such as “in what ways do institutional powers sustain strat egies of oppression in nondominant communities?” and “how are government affiliated local i nstitutions implicated in the structural oppression  of the community?” We question if the sole focus on assets might diminish the depth of the harm caused by systems like racism or classism and how they affect community members. We explore how educational design of an asset-based, anti-gun violence curriculum situates problems in connection to learn ers’ communities, enabling them to critically addre ss it from all angles by applying an analytical lens that refl ects the identities of the founders of DIVAS and  centers Black women and women of color’s methods of social transformation: womanism. Womanism Initially introduced by Alice Walker (1983), womani sm is a social change perspective that is “rooted i n Black women’s and other women of color’s everyday experiences and everyday methods of problem solving in everyday spaces, extended to the problem of ending all forms  of oppression for all people, restoring the balanc e between people and the environment/nature, and reconciling human life with the spiritual dimension” (Phillips,  2006, p. xx). Considered a spiritual movement, womanism focu ses on transforming  energy–whether that be mental, emotional, physical, material, social, and environmental energy–through first envisioning a desired outcome, then “going back to the level of thought and feeling to transmute originating conditions in ways that lead to to th at outcome” (Maparyan, 2012, p. 51). Five characterist ics that are central to womanism are that it is: (1) antioppressionist—focused on liberation across all intersections of o ppression in humankind, (2) vernacular— centering everyday people and experiences  that cut across hierarchical demarcations of societ y, (3) nonideological—functioning in a decentralized and pluralistic way that prioritizes inclusion and relations across people rather than a single way of thinking or being, (4) communitarian—promoting collective well-being for all humans, living/non-living things, and earth as the goal for social change, and (5) spiritualized—recognizing that humans, the environment, and the material world are all interconnected (Phillips, 2006). Womanism offers a way to conceptualize the type of environment needed to eng age in antioppressionist work. It can complement ot her antioppressionist pedagogical approaches like Black  feminism (Collins, 2000) or disciplinary specific ones like data feminism (D'ignazio & Klein, 202 3) to consider sociocultural contexts of the learni ng. This paper explores adopting a womanist perspective when addressing community issues from an asset-based approach.

## Codesign and research context

This paper builds on an ongoing participatory actio n research (PAR) with an educational social justice organization, in which community members are core contributors to the knowledge-production process (Bergold & Thomas, 2012) and community empowerment and mutua l learning is centralized (Cornwall & Jewkes, 1995). The organization, DIVAS for Social Justice, is located in a large metropolitan city and dedicat ed to empowering youth of color through media art and technology in after-school programs, summer camps, and intergenerational projects that address community challenges while educating about the cultural heritage and strengths within their communities. Some of the researchers have been work ing with the organization since 2018 exploring the design of justice-oriented technology education. In the current work, the team (and authors of this paper) consists of the Executive Director of DIVAS who lives in one of the  neighborhoods where programming runs and identifie s as a Black woman of Afro-Caribbeandescent; a DIVAS researcher who identifie s as a Black woman of African-Americandescent and was previously a researcher at the university who graduated and continued her involvement with DIVAS; and three researchers from a private un iversity who identify as a mixed race (Black and Wh ite) American woman, a Black American woman, and a Ghanaian woman. The codesign of the Anti-Gun Violence Asset-Mapping Project evolved over two years, beginning in Nov. 2021 when DIVAS received funding to implement an anti-gun violence curriculum. We begandesigning the curriculum for an initial pilot implementation in Feb. 2022 in partnership with a community center  that runs after school programs. However, the implementation fel l through due to challenges related to recruitment and facilitation. In response, the Executive Director r an a subset of the activities with another organiza tion in June 2023 where we were unable to get the proper IRB approval for data collection. Instead, we took the year to expand on the curriculum for a year-long after school program starting in the Fall of 2023.

## Data collection and analysis

In this study, we report on an analysis of the code sign process and the curriculum design. The data co nsists of: the codesign notes from Nov. 2021-Nov. 2023, the seven lesson plans and curricular resources developed for the initial implementation in Februa ry 2022, the facilitator guidelines designed to hel p educators understand the philosophy of the curriculum and to localize it to their context, and the curriculum outline developed to guide the year-long implementation. Researchers performed thematic analysis of data sources deductively (Braun & Clarke, 2012), drawing on theoretical constructs from Phill ips’ (2006) womanism. After familiarizing themselve s with the curriculum, researchers iteratively coded the d ata based on five womanist characteristics —antioppressionist, vernacular, nonideological, communitarian, and spir itualized—to elucidate possible analytical themes from a perspective rooted in BIPOC women ’s everyday social change-making practices (Phillips, 2006). They collectively organized the codes p ulling out the quoted excerpts from the data source s in relation to the five characteristics, clarifying their choices and resol ving disagreements. Then they discussed the insight s from this categorization layering in additional codes such as: antioppressionism as an everyday practice, mutual a id and self-help through community organizations, engaging both  community challenges and sociopolitical underpinnings, tensions between local and elites, tending to both individual and collective, and subverting power dynamics through storytelling. After reviewing the data, five prominent womanist practices were identified from the codesign process, providing deeper insight into the curriculum. Curriculum overview In codesigning curriculum, the authors relied on te nets of ABCD elements to create a unit outline that  they organized around six goals for youth to: (1) develop a greater understanding of gun violence as a health crisis and how it affects communities of co lor; (2) learn the fundamentals of community mappin g; (3) develop a clear understanding of community assets by focusing on economic development in addition to health and wellness; (4) develop technical skills using open source material s for the final prese ntation of assets; (5) develop critical thinking skills through researching community assets that are effective in preventing gun violence; and (6) develop skills in self-care (e.g., yoga, mindfulness, meditation, and affirmations). The intention was to have learners create simple podcasts with the audio files of interviews with individuals in their communities, and annotate  a digital Google Map with the audio, photos, and short videos  they captured. The initial implementation was divi ded into seven lessons that could span multiple days if needed: L01 Intro to the Anti-Gun Violence Asset Mapping Project, L02 What is Community Mapping?, L03 Gun Violence as a Public Health Crisis, L04 Introduction to Podcasting, L05 Elected Representatives, L06 Anti-Gun Violence Organizations, and L07 Developing Inte rview Skills. The curriculum was designed to have additional work sessions in which learners would continue to gather and organize data on their assets including interviews, photogra phs, and videos, and use them to annotate the map o f their community. The curriculum left space within its str ucture for facilitators (who are recruited from the  local community) to localize the curriculum to the context and interests of the learners, draw on culturally relevant and timely examples to support the educational goals, and develop learners’ art and literacy skills and practices. In the original implementation, we attuned the curriculum with a focus on stress management but identified opportunities to expand how we cared for the learners within the longer curriculum identifying where individuals fro m the community could discuss trauma, yoga, meditation, and breathing techniques within various lessons.

## Findings

The analysis highlighted five womanist practices that can help us conceptualize important pedagogical structures for justice-oriented education: (1) fostering relationships to redistribute power, (2) centering dialogue to engag e with tensions, (3) recognizing and naming the “collective” with learners, (4) supporting learners in storying their communities, and (4) engaging in care and wellness as resistance.

## Finding 1: Fostering relationships to redistribute power

Womanism identified the importance of relationships  for engaging with systems of power within our code sign work and for engaging learners with systems of powe r in their justice education. Relationships between research institutions and community organizations can be cha llenging to ensure equitable support and participat ion. The researchers had to contend with the historical harm s of research on (and not with) communities of colo r, and the positioning of a wealthy university within a city p lagued by gentrification and unequal distribution o f resources. We found that the growth of our relationship was key to our success managing various needs within the work. In light of working in the presence of political and h istorical inequities, “Womanist activism does not f ocus on the confrontation of institutional structures so much a s on the shaping of thought processes and relations hips” (Phillips, 2006, p. xxx). Throughout our 5yr partnership, the collabora tion grew through focusing on shared visions for the community and the support of one another as people. This included consistent dialogue around the goals of the work, supporting each others’ professions, cooking and sharing food in community, engaging in care, and voicing appreciation. Many educational collabor ations embed power dynamics at varying levels that the collaborators have to contend with. Our team’s relationship enabled us to harmonize (Phillips, 2006) as we worked on scholarly contributions and centered the community work while staying critical of potential harms. In the curriculum, relationships were also central to how we viewed engaging learners with systems of power. We created a multilevel approach intended to connect across individual change agents, community-based organizations, and local government representatives  to address gun violence. The curriculum examined h ow power is distributed across various people within t he community and focused on building relationships to coordinate around that power. We considered how you th’s voices could be included in current  budgetary conversations at the governmental level so “that th e students’ voices can be heard” in authentic ways,  which involved knowing pertinent information like a “reco rd of where that money is going” (referring to an a rticle of money allocation to the local government for develo pment work, Notes [12/13/21]) or having the person “in charge of the money distribution [be] at the final presentation” (Notes [12/13/21]). A womanist approa ch draws our attention to how we can “harmonize and coordina te” across different actors in order to create together with people from diverse groups (Phillips, 2006, p. xxvx i). From a pedagogical perspective, this emphasizes  the importance of learners’ building relationships as they reflect on power held by various community members.

## Finding 2: Centering dialogue to engage with tensions

Our second finding identified the importance of dialogue for engaging us and the various stakeholders and learners in the complexities of doing justice-oriented work. First, while we would not consider g un-violence a deficit— because it is not an internal characteristic of any community but a consequence of systemic inequities—there was a strain at times attempting to simultaneously enga ge with the assets and challenges. The tension presented itself when we shared the title of the curriculum with principals, teachers, and funders. Concern was often voiced over the directness of engaging with a sensitive topic, which prompted discussions around the asset-mapping and community storytelling approach that inevitably led  to understanding and support from the various stak eholders. Due to the prevalence of these discussions, we talked about changing the title but upon reflection dec ided not to; the executive director stated, “Having the challeng e in the title is important. We aren’t operating in  a utopia, so showing that real challenges can be addressed through this type of approach is important” (Notes [9/28/23]). Womanism enabled us to identify the importance of t he dialogue that these tensions generated as part o f the antioppressionist work. Specifically, as we explore  learning across all stakeholders, this dialogue pr ovided an opportunity for reflection and sharing of our philo sophy of holding both the harms and assets together  in conversation.

A second tension in which womanism helped identify the importance of dialogue was surrounding the multidimensional nature of assets. Assets were posi tioned in the curriculum as an avenue to support antioppressionist agendas—i.e. in the unit outline we identify how learners w ill, “develop critical thinking skills through research of community assets that are effec tive in preventing gun violence” focusing around economic development, health, and wellness. We noted the complexities in understanding assets that could promote wellness when looked at from different angles. Reflecting on  her past work, the executive director discussed th e need to take time to explore the different ways an asset ca n be beneficial to the community, and discuss how t hings can promote sustenance beyond just enjoyment. For examp le, she reflected how “in past activities students didn’t understand what they were missing —[they] took pictures around their neighborhood of t hings that they liked which ended up being fast food restaurants” (Notes [7/14/21]). Additionally, we discussed how a deli that provides access to fresh food in the neighborhood is also a smoke shop (Notes [11/8/2023]). We identified that without a dialogue around perspectives on assets, the learner s might not grow in their own perceptions of welln ess in their communities. Conversations around equitable economi c assets are equally complex with the presence of s mall businesses not always being an indicator. As the executive director pointed out, “the kids need to be cognizant of the fact that there are companies that aren’t hiring Black people even when Black people make up a large portion of the community in which the business is situated”  (Notes [11/8/23]). Defining assets is not necessar ily a straightforward process and requires a critical dialogue. Womanism’s emphasis on decentralization with dialogue for harmonizing across perspectives in ways that can also accommodate disagreement and conflict (Phillips, 2006) helped us orient the goal for our facilitators to s upport learners’ growth in conceptua lizing assets without overriding or negating their perspectives.

A third tension was surrounding the curricular flexibility for facilitators; particularly, “having instructors figure out where they have room to add their voice to [the curriculum] and not override the foundation  of what we’re doing” (Notes [10/25/23]). We had particular values integrated into our approach to social justi ce, which created strain when facilitators wanted to put thei r spin on activities in ways that did not align wit h our vision. For example, one facilitator with a user-experience (UX) design background reviewed the mapping activities and wanted to have learners use the map to identify all the problems in their community so they could design solutions. The facilitator was unintentionally orienting the a ctivity towards deficits rather than as sets. On one hand, womanism has a non-ideological stance and supports pluralistic views (Phillips, 2006); on the other hand, we felt the asset-based perspective was central to our antioppression ist work. Womanism has been acknowledged appearing paradoxical  and logically inconsistent, yet also stands by “not  need[ing] to resolve internal disagreement to function effectively.” (Phillips, 2 006, p. xxv). We realized that ongoing dialogue to reconcile differences such as these was how we could support facilitators in leveraging their backgrounds and ideas in the work while engaging them with our own ideals ingrai ned in the activities. As Phillips identifies, “[d] ialogue is a means by which people express and establish both co nnection and individuality. Dialogue p ermits negotiation, reveals standpoint, realizes existential equality, and shapes social reality. Dialogue is the locale where both tension and connection can be present simultaneously” (Phil lips, 2006, p. xxvii). In response, we recognize th e need to create greater opportunities for this dialogue with facilitators so we can learn from one another in the ways we are conceptualizing the curricular activities and goals.

## Finding 3: Recognizing and naming the “collective” with learners

Our third finding notes the importance of collabora tively defining a collective (communitarian) approa ch. To start, in Lesson 1, rather than positioning gun violence as an issue endemic to the local neighborhood, we framed gun violence more broadly as “a public health issue ” using local and national data. We hoped to highli ght the interconnectedness of the issue for overlapping communities, engage in the womanist practice of mutual aid, and develop a shared commitment to social justice through solidarity (Vakil & Vossoughi, 2015). To emphasize local collective, the curriculum highlights the “history of the area” (Notes [6/14/23]), “pictures of the local community,” “examples of how local communities came together in  the past to do things” (Notes [9/28/23]). In this, we also noted the importance of the learners being able “to  see someone they know in the work being done” (Not es [9/28/23]). From a womanist perspective, the collective (communitarian) was positioned as an everyday practice of the community (vernacular) and as something that is accessible to them. Additionally, the activities had learners define wh at it means to be a communitarian from their perspectives. In Lesson 1, learners are asked to re spond to the discussion prompts: “What does it mean  to be a change agent in your community? What kinds of contr ibutions to their community can create change on a small and large scale?” We continued having discussions s urrounding asset-based orientation towards a community issue asking: “What questions are the ones that can  help probe them into thinking about their communit y spaces as ways to combat gun violence?” (Notes [8/4/21]). Womanism points to the importance of bringing in le arners’ everyday experiences (Phillips, 2006) as part of how they come to understand what the collective means and how communities “come together to solve problems, to celebrate togethe r” (Notes [9/28/23]). Learners’ perspectives were intended to be part of how we explored the goa ls for social change that reflects the womanist ide al of commonweal, which is the state of collective well-being for ultimately all people (Phillips, 2006).

## Finding 4: Supporting learners in storying their communities

Womanism drew our attention to the central role of dialogue and storytelling with and about communitie s. As a method for social transformation, dialogue is central to womanism. As stated by Mapayryan (2011), “to recognize, claim, and utilize the power of the word is a basic womanist social change modality” (p. 58). The curriculum was designed to engage learners in exploration of stories within their communiti es and storying of their communities both as a whole and in relation to gun violence and  resilience. From a critical angle, learners were facilitated in examining power dynamics involved in storytelling about gun violence. In the unit outline, we codesigned lessons set to explore “how the stories are told about gun violence,” “how the stories are present ed in the media,” “the different narratives that we are told about gun vio lence,” “the narratives that we have told ourselves surrounding gun violence,” “how data is used to tell the story of gun-violence in non-dominant communities,” and “critique how issues are framed and how that makes us underst and solutions to problems.” From an antioppressioni st, womanist lens, interrogating narratives was intended for learners to organize and label various types of oppression embedded in the rhetoric around gun violence.

Our critical discussions around storytelling also examined the role that data played. A womanist framing of data allows us to see it as a tool to understand  and oppose oppressive forces (antioppressionist). On the other hand, data can help learners identif y and understand collective well-being and lack thereof. We wanted learners to be able to use data to examine questions like, “What constitutes a public health issue? What does it look like in communities of color vs. in other communities? What  are the public health issues that we face?” and “How does gun violence affect the community’s health” (unit outline). However, using data wasn’t straightforward; we posed questions such as, “how do we take an asset-based approach to understand data?” (Notes [11/1/23]). We identified how there were inherent framings we would be engaging in depending on what data we shared. For example, we identified the dichotomy between, “highlighting the  number of hungry people versus highlighting the [neighborhood] community garden”  (Notes [10/18/23]). We also examined the various us es of data from “understanding structural marginalization that leads to higher gun violence and crime” to “data on assets including community organizations and how they present their impact” (Notes [10/18/23]). In order for learners t o have agency over the data and storytelling with the data, we recognized a need to build data literacy skill s such as “graph reading,” “identifying trends in data for health around gun violence,” and “being able to make claims about gun violence based on data.” (unit outline). We recognized that engaging in dialogue around the various types of data supports examination of how learners could represent and story their communities. Importantly, we wanted learners to be able to gain agency in telling stories. We ask learners to ident ify, “Who are the key people whose story you want to cap ture” (unit outline) as a way of recognizing their power in telling stories about their communitie s. Womanism frames storytelling as a practice throu gh which learners' exploration and telling of their own story can be an avenue towards collective wellbeing (communitarian), similar to how Black women come together at the kitchen tab le to “share the trut hs of their lives on equal footing and learn through face-to-face conversations” (Mapayran, 2011, p. 59). This h ighlighted opportunities for learners to engage in collective work, while still engaging wit h their pluralistic views. For example, one of the outputs we designed for a lesson on community was having youth  create “individual and collective definitions of w hat a community is and who their communities are composed  of.” In addition, we discussed having participants  learn how to discuss the anti-gun violence “project [with others] so they can talk with their families to choose things to put on the map” (Notes [10/2523]). From a womanist perspective, this highlights the potential for stor ytelling as justice work through a collective practice (communitarian) that creates inclusive connections between community members in ways that document different perspectives (nonideological).

## Finding 5: Engaging in care and wellness as resistance

Our final finding highlights the importance of care  and wellness as a practice of resistance against o ppression. Framing gun violence as a public health issue also frames it as negatively impacting the commonweal (P hillips, 2006). As articulated by Phillips (2006), “[i]n the current social context, many forms of infirmity are the result of oppressive conditions and process” (p. xxiv). We are in no way reducing or equating the realities of gun violence to infirmity. Rather, we appreciate how womanism in vites addressing the role of societal and environmental structures in public health issues and focusing on healing society. For example, two lessons within the unit focus on learners understanding how gun violence affects the community’s health and researching community assets for prevention of gun violence —particularly community organizations and practices focused on health and wellness. One example includes having yoga facilitators discuss with youth where s tress lives in the body when faced with trauma. Centr alizing self-care practices mirror how womanists “use prayer, ri tuals, meditation, collective visualizations, and a host of other mean s to draw spiritual energy toward social, political, and even physical problem solving and healing” (Phillips, 2006, p. xxviii). By highlighting local community-and self-care efforts, the curriculum also reflects the vern acular (or “everyday”) and how “the soul of womanism is grassr oots, identified with the masses of humanity” (Phil lips, 2006, p. xxiv). In other words, a womanist framing of the curriculum offers an empowering lens to addressing the impacts of gun violence on the community by focusin g on common human concerns (e.g., safety or belongi ng) that connect across different groups and the “do-it-yourself” mutual aid and self-help practices within the community. It was important to ground the facilitat ion through a mothering orientation in which they w ere able to “promote [a] positive/safe environment” where th ey were encouraging community-building for youth to be taken care of as they engage with a sensitive topic  like gun violence. Rooted in African cultural hist ories, motherhood as a womanist method of social transformation, includes behaviors rooted in caretaking, nurturance, dispute resolution, and education (Phillips, 2006).

We also noted the concern for the wellness of learn ers as they investigated societal inequities that a re challenging to change. The executive director posed  the question, “How do we write [the curriculum] so  that someone doesn’t feel defeated? But also wri te it so we address the issues head on” (Notes [9/1 /2021]). In future discussions, this was reframed as, “How do we balan ce the critical with the hopeful?” (Notes [9/28/23]). Discussing an issue as devastating as gun violence can be difficult for learners and risks leaving lea rners with feelings of hopelessness. Womanism points us toward s the importance of everyday collective efforts wor king towards creating a foundation of care attending to mind, body, and spirit to support the learners (Phillips, 2006).

## Discussion and conclusion

The womanist analysis draws attention to various pe dagogical structures and characteristics important for social justice education. First, it guided how we could st ructure learning objectives around an asset-based approach grounded in relationships. The womanist analysis identified how relationships were a method to redistribute power both in our codesign work and in the ways learners were facilitated in their community work. From a le arning design perspective, the importance lies in engaging learners in discussions about various roles in society and how power is implicated for enacting an antioppressioni st agenda. Learners should be able to critically an alyze who they need to connect with and why as they gain agency in doing social action work. Second, the womanist framing highlighted the opport unities for dialogue to impact learning and community well-being both in terms of how we forefront integration  of pluralistic thought and coordination around differences. It was apparent the importance of facilitating inclusive discussions that bring in learners’ and community members everyday experiences while engagi ng with differences. For example, while the asset-based approach centralizes identification of assets (Kret zmann & McKnight, 1993) it does not  offer guidance when faced with different perceptions of assets or asset s which might have both benefits and harms. Womanis m identifies how acts of “harmonizing and coordinatin g involve figuring out how to make disparate elemen ts work together” (Phillips, 2006, p. xxvii). From a pedagogical perspective, scaffolding should therefore support sharing, reflection, and negotiation around a range of persp ectives. Importantly, dialogue should facilitate gr owth in our understanding, not just for the learners, but acros s everyone involved —i.e., ourselves, the administrators, facilitators, community members, government represe ntatives, etc.—through exploring differences in ideologies to handle the multifaceted and evolving nature of being antioppressionist. Third, the analysis exemplified the pedagogical str uctures for working with the collective. The “collective” is referenced across womanist literatu re in how problems are framed (i.e., the “collective struggle” or “collective plight” (Hudson-Weems, 2006, p. 50-51), how actions are engaged in (i.e., how we “collectively make sense” (Phillips, 2006, p. xxvii) and “collectively construct, affirm and maintain” (Collins, 1996, p. 9), and how goals are framed (i.e., reaching a state of “collective wellbeing” (Phi llips, 2006, p. xxiv). In educational design, it draws attention to developing an understanding of who the “collective” encompasses includin g perspectives of who is impacted by similar issues a nd who is working in solidarity with us. The vernac ular characteristic surfaced activities that elevated stories o f everyday people highlighting the historicity and accessibility of narratives for collective action. A focus on these narrative aspects offers a venues for countering fatalistic attitudes as learners recognize how people in their communities have enacted change. Fourth, it centered narratives in ways that facilit ated learners in critically examining them and constructing them. It was essential for learners to exp lore the power that stories hold, including how the y are framed, who is involved, and how data is integrated in these representations, and gain agency in telling their own stories and the stories of others. Restorying is a practice researchers have identified for supporting youth’s ability to counter dominant narratives and recognize their own power in tel ling stories about themselves, their experiences, and their communities (Thomas & Stornaiuolo, 2016). Our analysis found sto rying a pertinent practice to support as learners engaged in their social action work and worked with people in their communities. Fifth, wellbeing and care surfaced as a form of res istance to the societal inequities the learners wer e engaging with. It arose in coding t he wellness practices of yoga, meditation, and brea thing, and were the only instances where we identified the spiritualized dim ension of womanism.  When engaging with stories and data focused on topics of harm, death, and tragedy, it is essential that efforts to facilitate a “politicized womanist care” (Watson, 2018) lays the foundation of the learning community. Beauboeuf-Lafontant highlights that curricular choices are not what defines caring —it is about the orientation of the educator towards  developing connections with their students, advocating for them, and provi ding the support they need to eventually care for t hemselves (Beauboeuf-Lafontant, 2005). Future work will continue to examine how we can centralize this work. The work in this paper is limited by not yet includ ing an analysis of the implementation, which is the next step. The goal is not demarcating the best pro cess for justice education, but to “ start where you are ” and move “ one step forward ” (Phillips, 2006, p. xxv). Our womanist analysis i dentified characteristics of various pedagogical supports for relationship building, dialogue, collectively building, and storying that are central to the antioppressionist work and will continue to evolve as we move forward.
