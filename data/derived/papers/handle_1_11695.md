# Organizing Ensemble Learning: Representational Infrastructure in a Youth Ensemble Devising Process

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Identifying when, how, and why an artistic leader uses representational infrastructure is a promising method for investigating how ensemble learning in arts disciplines is organized. This study explores how one teaching artist designed and used representational infrastructure in a youth ensemble theatrical devising process, generating numerous opportunities to develop participant agency, and ultimately resulting in the creation and performance of an original play.

## Introduction

There is a growing body of literature in the learni ng sciences on ensemble learning within artistic di sciplines (Ćosić, 2024; Hollett et al., 2022; Leander et al., 2023, Vogelste in, 2020). To better understand how artistic ensembles learn to perform togeth er, it is necessary to pay closer attention to how leaders of such groups design for and facilitate ensemble collective activity ove r time. Identifying when, how, and why an artistic leader uses representational infrastructure—“representational forms and the embodied practices associated with them ” (Ma and Hall, 2018, p. 510) —is a promising method for investigating how ensembl e learning in arts disciplines is organized. This study explores how one teaching artist— who, as an arts educator and leader of a group of young theatre makers-designed and utilized representational infrastructu re throughout an ensemble devising process, generating numerous opportunities to develop participant agency, and ultimately resulting in the creation and performance of an original play.

## Methods

The researcher observed a 5-day theatre camp devising process at Southeastern Youth Theatre (S EYT), located in a southern US city. Jennifer (pseudonym), led the devising process and worked with her intern and thirteen youth theatre makers (ages 10-14). Data collection consisted of: 1) semi-structured interviews with Jennifer — before, during, and after the camp, 2) daily field notes of the devis ing process, and 3) artifact collection (e.g. photographs of co-created items for the show, copies of scripts, etc). A first  phase of analysis involved coding data for representations —anything visual that was created by Jennifer or co-created with the youth ensemble, including embodied representations. References to r epresentations in interviews and field notes, inclu ding explanations of when, by whom, how, and why they we re used, were also coded. Each representation recei ved a subcode, developed through open coding, identifying its type (e.g., script, e nsemble pose.) A second phase of analysis focused on “breakdowns” of infrastructure (Star & Ruhleder, 1996), or “tears” (Ma & Hall, 2018), where something goes wrong and things stop working smooth ly. Identified breakdowns and associated representa tions were then linked. A third phase of analysis focused on creating chronol ogical descriptions of representational infrastructure used throughout the devising process.

## Findings

Jennifer and the ensemble created and used five main representations which served as infrastructure —a devising guide (DG), blocked (embodied) scenes, a daily schedule, an ensemble contract, and a typed script.  Each of these representations and their associated practices worked together as organizational resources. The DG, a large piece of butcher paper Jennifer taped to the classroom wall on the first day of camp, is further described in this paper.

The Creation of the DG. On Day 1, during the idea generation phase of the devising process, Jennifer used the DG to collect and display student ideas about possible themes and characters for the play. She also used the right side of the DG to write down a co-generated scene list that served as both a play outline and a schedule for embodied scene creation. As seen in Figure 1, at the end of the first day's brainstorming session, Jennifer and the ensemble members had developed an outline of 9.5 scenes for a play based on the origin story of a Greek s ea monster. Once the DG had been filled with possible themes, characters, and scenes, Jennifer spoke to the ensemble directly in front of it, ackn owledging their hard work. She asked the students to engage in a call and response, providing them with phrases to Figure 1 The DG’s Scene Outline, Day 1 repeat. She began: “I'm a superstar!” They responded. “I did that!” They repeated again. “I'm proud of myself!” Finally, Jennifer directed the students to say the phrase, “I'm proud of you,” over and over, pointing their fingers at their fellow ensemble members while doing so.

Utilizing the DG. Later that afternoon, Jennifer facilitated the grou p's first “Drama Time”—a dedicated chunk of rehearsal time when the ensemble developed physical movement (blocking) and spoken dialogue for each scene. After the creation of Scene 0.5, Jennifer gathered the students over to the DG again. While notin g what the group had accomplished, she used a marker to physically cross out that part of the scene list. "We started with nothing," she said, "and now, we have somethin g." Over the course  of the next several days, each time the group completed a scene (up through Scene 5), Jennifer gathered them in front of the DG, crossed out the finished scene, and encouraged the ensemble members to celeb rate their hard work, shouting phrases such as, “Go me!” “I'm an actor!” “I'm amazing!” “I'm proud of myself!” and “I'm proud of you!” “Tears” in Infrastructure. Two tears in infrastructure were observed on Day 4 of the five-day devising process. First, Jennifer decided to make significant changes to the plot in order to make the storyline clearer, simplify the show, and get rid of repetition within  the previously agreed upon scenes. Jennifer wrote the new scenes on a piece of paper, a mini-devising guide (mDG), and taped it over the bottom of the DG's scene outline, thus replacing four original scenes. Soon after, ensemble members gathered in front of the DG to look at the new ending. The second tear occurred later that afterno on when the ensemble moved to a smaller classroom. Jennifer left the DG on the wall of the original room, but she did take the  mDG with her to the new space, though it went unutilized the rest of the day. Due to changes in schedule and locat ion, Jennifer ended up shuffling the ensemble to multiple spaces to finish creating the show's fi nal scenes. Eventually, the group completed scene c reation on the actual stage of the theater. Per usual, Jennife r led a celebration to recognize their accomplishme nt. “Did we do this?” she inquired. “Yes!” the group shouted back. “Is that the entire play?” They exclaimed, “Yes!” It was at this moment that Jennifer explained to the researcher that she did not bring the mDG with her, but if she had, she would have used it as part of this familiar routine.

The Destruction of the DG. On Day 5, the last day of theatre camp, Jennifer returned back to her original classroom. When the students arrived that morning, Jennifer told them, “I want us to look at something really fast.” She grabbed the entire DG, brought it to the students, and led them  in the familiar call and response, confirming once again that they had completed all t he scenes for the show. Then, Jennifer began rippin g up the DG. She handed its pieces to the ensemble  members to further destroy, while  simultaneously shouting, "We did it! We did it!" Reflection on the DG. A month after the devising camp ended, Jennifer shared her recommendations for teaching artists facilitating youth ensemble devisi ng processes. She mentioned the importance of havin g a representation like the DG: “Have the scenes on a piece of paper in the room…  so that they can see it, they remember, you can reference it every single time. T hey know how much they've completed. They know what 's coming next… Look at it before you block it [the scene]. ‘Okay, this is what's about to happ en in this scene that we're blocking.’ So remind them, ‘This is where we just finished in the story. This i s what we're about to do. ’ Have that piece of paper on the wall. That to me is very important. I didn't have one of those the first time I did it. And that was a big change for me. And incredibly helpful. And I'll never not have it.”

## Discussion and conclusion

Similar to the way that Sheridan et al. (2022) conceptualized “direction and suppor t” as means for developing learner agency in visual arts contexts, the DG  and its associated practices  was a means for developing learner agency in the SEYT context. When viewed along with other forms of representational infrastructure, Jennifer’s use of the DG can be understood as  a means of shifting agency between herself and her students. The DG, when used as part of a routine, served not only to move the ensemble forward in completing their show but also served to create moments where the ensemble  could name and celebrate themselves and each other as agentic thea tre artists, thereby developing aspects of the ensemble's social infrastructure, as well. This research contributes to our growing knowledge of ensemble learning, specifically focusing on the contributions of the artistic leader. Studying how one theatre teaching artist used representational i nfrastructure throughout an ensemble devising process provides in sights into how representations are co-created between leaders and students and how they function in a var iety of ways to organize learning. Specifically, th is study details the collective creation of an original work of art over time—a type of ensemble activity that requires further exploration. Furthermore, this study suggests, similar to Ma and Hall's (2018) work, that representational infrastructure interacts with social infrastructure, evolves over time, and marks representational transitions throughout an ensemble's artistic trajectory. Paying attention to who is involved in the creation of a representation, at what points in the creative process, and for wha t purposes allows learning scientists to better und erstand how agency is shared in ensemble learning contexts.
