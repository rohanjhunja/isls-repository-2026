# Ambitious Teaching for Struggling Students: Exploring Teachers’ Problems of Practice

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Ambitious teaching is essential for fostering deep mathematical understanding, yet many teachers encounter challenges in implementing such practices, particularly with struggling students. Drawing on data from professional learning community meetings of mathematics teachers in three middle schools, this study investigates teachers' problems of practice as they experience ambitious teaching practices in their classrooms. Findings reveal that teachers raised significant concerns about facilitating whole-class discussions. A detailed analysis identified key teachers’ problems of practice, including maintaining student engagement, eliciting diverse solution strategies, and protecting students from being mocked or labeled by peers when sharing incorrect solutions. These findings are discussed in the context of teacher learning processes, highlighting the importance of facilitators and teacher educators being responsive to teachers and aware of their problems of practice.

## Introduction and literature review

Ambitious teaching is widely established as essenti al in helping all students develop a deep understanding of mathematical concepts, procedural fluency, and adaptive reasoning (Lampert et al., 2010). Mathematics education researchers have reached a consens us on a set of ambitious and equitable instructiona l practices designed to support rigorous mathematical learning for all stud ents (Casa et al., 2024;  Lampert & Graziani, 2009; NCTM, 2014; Youngs et al., 2022). These practices include: (a) Engaging students in exploring and inv estigating mathematical ideas and concepts before providing explicit and direct instruction; (b) Implementing high-cognitive demand tasks that promote reasoning and problem-solving; (c) Engaging students in making connections between different mathematical representations, drawing on student' everyday experiences and intuitions; (d) P roviding opportunities for students to collaborate on mathem atical tasks in small groups; (e) Facilitating whol e-class mathematical discussions where students share diverse ways of thinking and reasoning. Despite strong academic consensus and empirical evi dence for the potential of ambitious teaching practices to support all students (e.g., Boaler & Staples, 2008; Cobb & Jackson, 2011; Horn & Garner, 2022; Kim et al., 2022), studies reveal that teachers often perceive these  approaches as inappropriate for struggling or low-achieving students (Anthony et al., 2018; Jackson e t al., 2017). The study provides a nuanced account of the problems of practice  (Babichenko et al., 2021; Horn & Garner, 2022; Lefstein & Snell, 2014)  that teachers encountered (or expected to encounter) when implementing ambitious teaching practices with struggling students. By offering detailed analyses of how teachers expre ss and understand these challenges in their own ter ms, we seek to help teacher educators become more responsive to these p roblems of practice as they emerge in professional development settings (Inouye & Gunshenan, 2023; Schwarts et al., 2021). Our research explore s teachers' voices on implementing ambitious teaching with struggling students through two phases. In the first phase, we quantitatively m apped teachers' voices regarding ambitious teaching for struggling students, f ocusing on the following research question s: RQ1) Which ambitious instructional practices do teachers tend to discuss in terms of c hallenges, risks, and problems when working with st ruggling students, and which they tend to discuss in terms o f advantages and benefits for struggling students? RQ2) What are the most prominent problems of practice teacher s identify when implementing ambitious teach ing with struggling students? In the second phase, we delved into the details of teachers’ dialogue about k ey problems of practice identified in phase 1, focusing on the following research questions: RQ3) How do teachers describe their lived classroom experiences of these problems? RQ4) How do teachers interpret these problems?

## Context

This study was conducted within a Research-Practice Partnership project  [Equitable Math Teaching in Heterogenous Classrooms ], aimed at developing pedagogies for teaching mathema tics in heterogeneous classrooms. In 2023-2024, three Israeli middle schools participated in this project. In each school, 6-8 7th-grade mathematics teachers participated in Professional Learning Communities (PLCs)  focused on introducing, experiencing and co-planning lessons which incorporated ambitious teaching practices. The PLCs were facilitated by the project researchers (the authors of this paper). The data includes 24 audio-recorded PLC meetings. Initial findings, presented here, focus on the analysis of the first three PLC meetings of each school.

## Data collection and analysis

The nine PLC meetings were audio-recorded. In the first stage  we carefully listened to the audio recordings, identifying episodes when teachers referred to ambitious teaching practices for students whom they perceived as struggling (a total of 28 episodes was identified). Each relevant  episode was fully transcribed and analyzed with the following guiding questions: (a) Which teaching  practice did the teacher(s) discuss?  (b) Did the teacher(s) view the practice as supportive of the struggling students, or did they bring up challenges or problems of practice that they expected to arise when enacting the practice? This analysis yielded an initial mapping of te achers' problems of practice while trying to implement different ambitious and equitable teaching practices. In the second stage, we delved into the content of the problems of practice that teachers most commonly mentioned. This stage involved conducting a discourse analysis to capture teachers' perspectives and the constraints they identified.

## Initial findings

Phase 1: Mapping teacher voices regarding ambitious teaching for struggling students Due to space constraints, we will report here only on the main patterns revealed. Teachers tended to mention the teaching practice of engaging students in making connections between mathematical representations and drawing on students' everyday experiences as particularly a dvantageous for struggling students. The advantages  of this practice were emphasized in  11 episodes, while its disadvantages were addressed  in 3 episodes. In contrast, teachers most frequently discussed challenges related to facilitating whole-class mathematical discussions, in relation to struggling students. Problems of practi ce associated with facilitating whole-class discussions with struggling students were brought up in 6 episodes. No episodes mentioned the advantages of this practice in supporting struggling students.

Phase 2: Problems of practice associated with facilitating whole-class discussions As our initial mapping revealed,  teachers tend to view facilitation of whole-classroom discussion as particularly challenging to enact in a way that will support str uggling students. Thematic analysis of the content of the 6 episodes addressing problems of practice associated with facilitating whole-class discussions, revealed three main themes: (a) The challenge of maintaining struggling stude nts' engagement during whole-classroom discussions;

(b) Lack of diversity in students' perspectives and  solutions; and (c) The vulnerability involved in m aking

(possibly incorrect) mathematical thinking public.

Through detailed micro-analytical methods, we further closely examined one representative example of teacher dialogue for each issue. This granular analysis provides valuable insights into the nuanced tensions and dilemmas that teachers navigate when attempting to facilitat e whole-class mathematical discourse. Due to word count constraints, the analyses are not presented here, but will be presented at the conference.

## Discussion

This study aimed to explore teachers' problems of p ractice when implementing ambitious mathematics tea ching in their classrooms, with particular attention to supporting  struggling students. Our initial findings challeng e the widely held assumption that teachers avoid ambitiou s teaching primarily because they view struggling s tudents as unable to handle high cognitive demand tasks (Ba ttey & Stark, 2009; Stylianou, 2010; Jackson et al., 2017). Our detailed analysis of three cases revealed the p roblems of practice teachers encounter: maintaining  student engagement, eliciting diverse solution strategies, and protecting students from being mocked or labele d by peers when sharing solutions. Particularly notable was ho w teachers' problems of practice reflected thei r protective stance towards students, extending beyond cognitive  support to address the social dynamics of classroo m participation, demonstrating their deep commitment to creating a safe and inclusive learning environme nt for all students. These insights have important implications for prof essional development and teacher learning. Just as ambitious teaching calls for responsiveness to stud ent thinking, our findings suggest that facilitator s need to be responsive to teachers' authentic problems of practice (Schwarts et al., 2021). Understanding these specific problems of practice can help teacher educators bet ter attend to and support teachers as they navigate  the real challenges of implementing ambitious teaching practices while developing their own responsiveness to teachers' experiences and needs.  While these preliminary findings are based on analy sis of a relatively small number of PLC meetings, they point to promising directions for future research and practice.
