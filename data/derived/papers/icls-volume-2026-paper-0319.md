# How Curriculum Mediates Teacher Noticing of Student Work in a Geometry Simulation

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how secondary mathematics teachers learn to interpret and respond to student work when their noticing is supported by curriculum materials. Curriculum is conceptualized as a mediational resource that shapes how teachers perceive, reason about, and act on students’ mathematical ideas. Using simulation-based interviews, twelve experienced geometry teachers engaged with student work under two conditions: one providing only student-facing materials and another including teacher-facing materials. Teacher noticing was examined as a process of interpretive sense-making, focusing on how teachers made sense of student ideas through the linguistic and representational features of the curriculum. A combined framework of professional noticing and systemic functional linguistics revealed that access to teacher-facing text supported teachers in contextualizing student reasoning within curricular goals and offering feedback that advanced conceptual understanding. The study highlights how teachers’ interpretive activity is shaped by the interaction of tools, language, and representations within a designed simulation environment.

School mathematics has long reflected broader societal conceptions of what it means to be doing and knowing mathematics (Lampert, 1990), and reform efforts have consequently relied on curriculum resources as vehicles for instructional change (Ball & Cohen,  1996). Yet even well-intentioned reforms underestimate how teachers engage with curricular materials in practice. Teachers may not develop a coherent curricular vision integrating both mathematical and pedagogical goals, nor trust that materials can effectively support those goals (Drake & Sherin, 2009). Drawing from Vygotskian (1978) perspectives on mediated learning, this study conceptualizes curriculum not as a static repository of content but as a mediational tool that shapes how teachers perceive, interpret, and respond to students' mathematical ideas. Remillard (2005, 2018) describes this as participation with the text, where teachers' interactions with curriculum both reflect and shape their instructional reasoning. Yet this participation depends on how transparently curriculum communicates its pedagogical intent (Davis & Krajcik, 2005). Kang and Kilpatrick (1992) observe that textbooks often stabilize dynamic mathematical activity into declarative knowledge, potentially distancing teachers from the exploratory nature of mathematics itself. Building on this view, the present study explores curriculum as a dialogic partner whose linguistic and representational structures mediate teachers' interpretive activity as they engage with student work. Teacher noticing, the ability to attend to, interpret, and respond to students' mathematical thinking (Jacobs et al., 2010), is the central construct examined here. Research shows that teachers often struggle to maintain a dual focus on the mathematics itself and on students as active mathematical thinkers (Lampert, 2001), tending to evaluate correctness rather than reason about the ideas represented in students' strategies (Kazemi & Franke, 2004). This challenge is particularly pronounced when students' thinking is expressed through multiple representational forms, where teachers are often less attuned to what those representations reveal about students' mathematical understanding (Dreher & Kuntze, 2015). By treating noticing as a form of mediated interpretation (Goodwin, 1994; Mason, 2002), this study shifts attention from what teachers know to how they learn to see and make sense of student ideas through the linguistic and representational resources of curriculum. This study explores the following research questions: ● How does access to teacher-facing curriculum materials, compared to student-facing materials alone, shape how teachers interpret students' mathematical ideas? ● How do the linguistic processes teachers use when interpreting and responding to student work differ across curriculum conditions?

Twelve experienced secondary mathematics teachers with an average of 15 years teaching mathematics and 10 years teaching geometry participated in 90-minute simulation-based interviews via Zoom in November– December 2024, all actively teaching geometry in U.S. public secondary school contexts. Simulation environments provide designed spaces where teachers engage with practice in controlled yet authentic ways (Shaughnessy & Boerst, 2018; Kennedy, 1999), making them particularly useful for examining 2086 how curriculum mediates teachers' interpretive activity. The simulation took place in LessonDepict, a storyboarding tool depicting classroom scenarios through graphical narrations, and consisted of three recurring phases: reading curriculum materials, observing a teacher pose a task, and interpreting student work (Figure 1), repeated across four geometry topics for a total of 16 student responses per participant. Figure 1 Three Phases of the Simulation: Reading Curriculum, Observing Instruction, and Interpreting Student Work Each interview began with participants reading curriculum materials introducing the mathematical topic and what students were expected to practice. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two conditions: Group S received only student-facing curricular text; Group ST received both student-facing and teacher-facing text. Materials came from Illustrative Mathematics (IM), a widely-used, open-source, standards-aligned K –12 curriculum (Illustrative Mathematics Certified, 2023). IM distinguishes between teacher-and student-facing goals in how they frame mathematical concepts: teacher-facing goals describe mathematical, pedagogical, and language objectives (e.g., “Comprehend that dilations take angles to congruent angles”), while student-facing goals are written in the first person (e.g., “Let’s” or “I can.”). These linguistic differences were analyzed using systemic functional linguistics (SFL), which examines how language constructs meaning through patterns of processes, participants, and circumstances (Hal liday & Matthiessen, 2004). The Transitivity system in SFL reveals how ideational meanings—our experiences of actions and thoughts —are represented in text. Different process types (e.g., Mental: think, consider; Material: draw, construct) reveal how curriculum frames mathematical concepts differently for teachers and students (Jeon & Herbst, 2023). In Phase 3, participants examined student work and responded to two interviewer prompts: (1) “What feedback would you provide for this student?” and (2) “How do you see this student’s work matching the objective?” Student work, designed by the author, while varied in content and representational form —whether visual, written, digital, or dialogic —collectively required teachers to make sense of students’ mathematical reasoning and evaluate its instructional potential. Teachers were not assessing correctness but were instead interpreting the reasoning evident in students’ work. Following Kane’s (2013) argument-based approach to validation, this variation grounds inferences about teachers’ reasoning in realistic and representative tasks and was intended to reflect the interpretive complexity teachers routinely navigate in classroom settings. Participants then observed a simulated classroom scenario in which a cartoon teacher posed a task connected to the curriculum goal before transitioning to student work displayed as if observed up close. Analytic framework The analysis combined two complementary frameworks: teacher noticing and systemic functional linguistics (SFL). Interview transcripts were segmented into idea units (Jacobs & Morita, 2002), and categorized by actor (student or teacher) and topic of conversation (Sherin & van Es, 2009). Student-related units revealed how teachers attended to and interpreted student thinking; teacher-related units revealed how they envisioned responding. A Mann–Whitney U test compared groups. Teachers in Group ST demonstrated higher average scores in both interpreting (0.96 vs. 0.73) and responding (1.07 vs. 0.82), though differences were not statistically significant. The pattern nonetheless suggests a meaningful trend: Group ST teachers interpreted student reasoning with greater specificity and connected feedback more directly to learning goals, while Group S teachers more frequently described student work in general evaluative terms or surface correctness. These results motivated a closer examination of how curriculum conditions shaped the linguistic processes teachers used (Figure 2). By attending to how the student used slope and coordinate representations to reason about geometric relationships, Joseph anchored his interpretation in the precise language of the teacher-facing goals, shifting from describing what the student did to articulating why those actions demonstrated understanding of how dilations preserve angle measure and parallelism. This reflects how access to teacher-facing curriculum text mediated the depth of teachers' interpretive reasoning in response to the same student work. This study offers a proof of concept for how curriculum functions as a mediational tool that shapes how teachers notice, interpret, and respond to student work. Teachers with access to teacher-facing materials attended more closely to conceptual reasoning and the language of geometric relationships; those with only student-facing materials focused more on procedural correctness. These findings underscore calls in curriculum design to make the pedagogical intent of instructional tasks more transparent to tea chers (Davis & Krajcik, 2005), specifically that the linguistic features of teacher-facing text, not just its content, functionally shape what teachers attend to and how they respond. The combined use of SFL and professional noticing frameworks also contributes to a broader conversation in the learning sciences about how professional learning is distributed across tools, representations, and activity systems, pointing toward future research on how designed environments mediate interpretive activity across p rofessional contexts.
