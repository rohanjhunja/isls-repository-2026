# Interrelation Between Leadership of Transactivity and “Equal” Participation in Collaborative Problem-Solving

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
“Equal” participation has been considered appropriate in transactive discourse, rather than imbalanced participation. This study investigates the interrelation between the leadership of transactivity and the equality of participation. Eight groups of three undergraduates participated in 45-minute collaborative problem-solving. Results suggested that not only equal participation but also a collaborative division of labor was crucial for collective and rotating leadership of transactivity. In contrast, cooperative division of labor was interrelated with dominant and fixed leadership of transactivity.

## Background and purpose

Transactivity, also known as reasoning based on reasoning by others, is a key to successful collaborative problemsolving (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995). Because transactive discourse is a social mode of knowledge co-construction (Weinberger  & Fischer, 2006), as Hong and his colleague  noted in Vogel et al. (2023),  “ equal” participation in transactive discourse through distributed engagement has been considered appropriate. Oshima et al. (2023) shed light on different patterns of the leadership of transactivity: rotating and fixed leadership. Rotating leadership refers to collective contribution, in which each participant takes the leadership in different scenes (Ma et al., 2016). On the other hand, within fixed leadership, the leadership is fixed to (a) specific participant(s), and the rest participate less. Ideally, the leadership of transactivity rotates among all participants, and all participants “equally” participate in the transactive discourse. Although “equal” participation has been considered appropriate for successful collaborative knowledge building (Vogel et al., 2023, p. 339), the interrelation between patterns of transactivity  and the balance of participation remains a matter of debate. Oshima et al.  (2023) suggested that participants who are not initially leading the transactive discourse might be able to take the leadership role and increase their participation through an intentional establishment of a division of labor, such as whiteboarding. However, in Vogel et al. (2023), Hong and his colleagues argued that more active and transactive discussion could lead to more diverse and unequal participation.

In this study, we conduct a social network analysis on co-located collaborative problem-solving to investigate the interrelation between the leadership of transac tivity and the balance of participation. W e hypothesize that  c ollaborative and rotating leadership of transactivity aligns with equal speec h participation, whereas imbalanced and fixed leadership corresponds to unequal participation.

## Method

The study involved eight groups (Groups A to H) of three undergraduate students, who participated in a 45-minute collaborative problem-solving (CPS) task, “ Rescue at Boone's Meadow ” (CTGV, 199 2). The problem was introduced through a 17-minute video that describe s the story of Jasper and his friends, finding an injured eagle in Boone ’s Meadow. The objective was to determine the fastest way to transport the eagle to a hospital  in Cumberland Town. Participants had access to a laptop for viewing t he video and a whiteboard for noting information and idea. Video and audio recordings were captured and transcribed. Based on the transcripts, we quantified the temporal trajectory of the leadership of transactivity using a social network analysis method  (Oshima & Hoppe, 2021; Chen & Poquet, 2022). In a transactive discourse, participants engage in synchronous use of words (Po pov et al., 2017), which in turn generates co-occurrence of words. Therefore, the temporal development of the transactive discourse can be roughly sketched by the trajectory of co-occurrence network of words (Oshima et al., 2023). In this study, we defined the leadership of transactivity as the commitment to the group’s discourse, meaning adding and linking new words to the group’s discourse. We used KBDeX (Oshima et al., 2012) to measure individual leadership in transactivity. Specifically, we c alculated the difference in total degree centrality between t he group’s network and the network after removing the words and links added by a specific individual.

Additionally, to test the equality of participation, we measured the amount of speech in milliseconds and conducted a chi-square goodness-of-fit test using a significance level of 0.05. The null hypothesis assumed that the expected value of the amount of speech is same among group members (i.e., speech participation was equal).

## Results and discussion

The results partially supported the hypothesis, with one group as an exception. As hypothesized, all groups with rotating leadership demonstrated equal speech  participation, while most groups with fixed leadership showed unequal speech participation  (Table 1). However, contrary to the hypothesis, Group F, despite having fixed transactivity, exhibited equal participation. Among the groups with equal speech participation (Groups A, B, C, D, H, and F), only Group F exhibited a fixed leadership of transactivity (Table 1). For example, in Group C, which demonstrated rotating leadership, there was no significant difference in the amount of speech among members (끫븤2 = 0.994, p = 0.61), and the leadership (colored areas in Figure 1(1)) was collectively shared by members (C1, C2, and C3). In contrast, Group F, despite having no significant  difference in speech participation (끫븤2= 0.889, p = 0.64), exhibited fixed leadership concentrated on member F3 (Figure 1(2)).  Group E also exhibited fixed leadership by member E1  (Figure 1(3)), but there was a significant difference in the amount s of speech among the three members (끫븤2 = 8.651, p = 0.01). Thus, results suggested that equal participation is often interrelated with collective and rotating leadership of transactivity; however, merely engagi ng equally can sometimes lead to dominant and fixed leadership.

We argue that when members participate equally, the  extent of the division of labor —collaborative or cooperative—determines whether the leadership of transactivity becomes rotating or fixed. A key distinction between collaboration and cooperation lies in the degree of division of labor (Dillenbourg, 1999). Collaboration typically involves horizontal division of labor, wh ere participants dynamically monitor and interact w ith each other (e.g., Miyake’s (1986) constructive interaction). In contrast, cooperation often features vertical division of labor, where tasks are divided into independent subtasks, solved individually, and then assembled. We found that members F1 and F2 actively participated in the conversation but contributed less to the transactive discourse than F3. A possible explanation is that the transactive leader F3 might have assigned subtasks, such as information gathering and organization, to the members as vertical division of labor. Encouraging horizontal division of labor through external scripts may effectively promote rotating transactive discourse, not only equal participation. This study opens the door to discussions on a paraverbal analytical lens  (Baker and Reimann, 2024, p.

276) for understanding transactivity and designing effective scaffolding. Interaction in co-located collaboration is

inherently multimodal (Enyedy and Stevens, 2022; Praharaj et a l., 2021). By analyzing paraverbal information, such as speech overlap (e.g., Nasir et al., 2021) a nd emotional synchronization (e.g., Nasir et al., 2 021; Aoyama Lawrence and Weinberger, 2022), it may be possible to gain deeper insights into transactive discourse. A limitation of this study is that the current meth od is unable to capture the quality of transactivity. For instance, both dialectic and dialogic transactivity  (Vogel et al., 2016)  may be detected similarly in terms of cooccurrence of words, making it difficult to distinguish between them. To address the limitation, in-depth qualitative analysis is essential.
