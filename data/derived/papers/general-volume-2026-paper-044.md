# LumiBuilder: A Chatbot-Building Tool for Hallucination-Aware AI Literacy

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract

Hallucinations, or confident but incorrect responses generated by large language models, present a critical challenge for young learners who increasingly turn to AI for schoolwork and everyday tasks. LumiBuilder is a web-based chatbot building tool designed to help children understand these limitations by constructing their own chatbots and testing how design choices influence model behavior. The tool integrates hallucination awareness scaffolds, including confidence displays, fact checking, model comparison, document verification, and repeated questioning, to support inquiry-based exploration of AI reliability. This interactive demonstration introduces LumiBuilder’s core features and the design principles that shaped its development. Participants will explore the tool, observe how hallucinations emerge through varied prompts, and discuss implications for AI literacy, youth and AI interaction, and the design of learning environments that promote verification, reflection, and calibrated trust in AI systems. The rapid integration of generative AI into educational practice underscores a pressing need for young learners to develop robust mental models of how large language models (LLMs) operate, including their limitations, uncertainty, and error modes (Long & Magerko, 2020; Lee et al., 2021). Hallucinations, outputs that appear fluent and authoritative yet are factually incorrect, pose particular challenges because they candistort reasoning, undermine trust, and often go undetected by novice users (Ji et al., 2023). Most chatbot interfaces obscure the underlying mechanics of generative models, offering few opportunities for learners to investigate why errors occur or how to verify AI-generated information (Ng et al., 2021). LumiBuilder was developed to address these challenges by shifting learners from passive AI use to active AI construction. Inspired by constructionist traditions that emphasize learning through building and debugging (Papert, 1980; Resnick, 2017), LumiBuilder engages learners in designing their own AI agents, specifying constraints, testing outputs, and iteratively refining their systems. Through this process, hallucinations become objects of inquiry rather than sources of confusion. The LumiBuilder sits within a larger AI literacy effort aimed at helping learners develop verification strategies, cultivate epistemic vigilance, and understand when and how to trust AI systems (Ng et al., 2021; Long & Magerko, 2020). By making AI behavior transparent and manipulable, the tool supports learners in building calibrated trust. This includes knowing when AI outputs are reliable and when additional verification is needed. This interactive demonstration showcases how design-based approaches can foster critical AI literacy skills that are essential for navigating an AI-mediated world. Moreover, the verification strategies LumiBuilder cultivates, such as source evaluation, cross-referencing, and evidence-based reasoning, may extend beyond AI-specific contexts. As learners increasingly encounter misinformation across social media, search engines, and AI-generated content, these practices could serve as transferable epistemic skills for navigating a broader information landscape. Future research should explore how hallucination-awareness scaffolds translate to other digital literacy contexts. Related work Efforts to define AI literacy have emphasized the need for learners to understand both what AI systems cando and what they cannot (Lee et al., 2021). Key competencies include recognizing uncertainty, probing system behavior, and understanding error sources. Research consistently shows that students often misinterpret AI fluency as correctness and lack systematic strategies to validate output (Leiser et al., 2024). Constructionism provides a framework for fostering deep understanding through the creation and debugging of computational artifacts (Papert, 1980; Kafai et al., 2020). Building on these foundations, the LumiBuilder enables students to author their own chatbots by defining purpose, personality, rules, and knowledge boundaries. Students experience firsthand how design choices shape AI behavior. Recent learning sciences research has explored how human–AI interaction shapes sensemaking, authorship, and epistemic agency. Recent work demonstrated how hybrid human–AI collaboration can support s ocially shared regulation in learning, while research on equity in AI education has examined how AI systems can either amplify or alleviate educational inequities (e.g., Atchley et al., 2024). Work in the field has emphasized the importance of studying learning in both humans and machines to advance our understanding of AI in education (Luckin, 2018). LumiBuilder extends this tradition by positioning hallucinations not as failures to avoid, but as opportunities to reason about model constraints, prompting structures, and verification strategies. LumiBuilder makes AI limitations visible and manipulable, allowing learners to systematically test, compare, and refine chatbot behavior while developing metacognitive awareness of when and how AI systems fail. These competencies align with broader calls in digital literacy research for teaching strategies such as critical ignoring and lateral reading to help learners evaluate information across online contexts (Kozyreva et al., 2023). Future work could explore whether the verification habits developed through LumiBuilder transfer to other information evaluation tasks beyond AI-generated content. LumiBuilder overview LumiBuilder is a web-based learning environment that enables students to design, configure, and test their own AI-powered chatbots while developing critical awareness of AI hallucinations and reliability issues (see Figure 1). LumiBuilder incorporates a set of hallucination-awareness scaffolds that make AI reasoning and uncertainty visible to learners. (1) Confidence Display communicates the model’s level of certainty, helping learners recognize that confident tone does not always signal correctness. (2) The Fact-Checking Panel retrieves information from reliable sources, enabling learners to compare AI-generated claims against verifiable evidence.

(3) Model Comparison presents responses from multiple LLMs side by side, highlighting variation across models

and prompting learners to consider why answers diverge. (4) Document Verification checks whether the chatbot’s response is grounded in a provided text, illustrating the distinction between generation and retrieval. (5) The Repeated Questioning Tool allows learners to ask variations of the same query, helping them detect inconsistencies and probe the stability of the model’s reasoning. These scaffolds support learners in developing habits of questioning, verification, and calibrated trust as they experiment with their self-designed chatbots. Structure of the Session Participants in this 30-minute interactive demonstration will engage in a focused, hands-on exploration of LumiBuilder, followed by a collaborative discussion on applying hallucination-aware AI education in their own contexts. Hands-on demonstration (15 minutes) The session begins with a brief orientation to LumiBuilder's interface and guiding pedagogical principles. Facilitators will walk attendees through the process of configuring a chatbot from scratch, including selecting a character role, setting behavioral rules, and uploading or defining the chatbot's knowledge base. Participants will then test their chatbot using LumiBuilder's diagnostic panel, which surfaces five hallucination-awareness features: Response Confidence, Fact Check, Document Verification, Model Comparison, and Repeat Question. For example, attendees might configure a science tutor chatbot, then use the Fact Check feature to evaluate whether the chatbot's explanation of photosynthesis is grounded in accurate information, or compare how GPT-4o and Claude Sonnet respond differently to the same student question. These guided explorations will illustrate how design choices, such as adjusting rules, constraints, or knowledge boundaries, directly shape the reliability and tone of AI-generated outputs. During this segment, attendees will have opportunities to try their own queries, observe how design choices influence chatbot behavior, and see how the tool makes inconsistencies and knowledge gaps visible. Facilitators will circulate to support exploration and highlight how these features align with learning goals around evidence-based reasoning and trust calibration. Attendees will also be encouraged to intentionally prompt their chatbots with edge cases or ambiguous questions to observe how hallucinations emerge and how the diagnostic features help surface them. By the end of this portion, participants will have experienced the full Use-ModifyCreate progression as students typically do in classroom settings. Interactive discussion (15 minutes) The second half of the session transitions into a guided conversation focused on instructional design and adaptation. Participants will discuss how LumiBuilder's scaffolds can be incorporated into research, design-based learning activities, undergraduate AI literacy courses, teacher preparation, or K-12 inquiry tasks. Facilitators will prompt attendees to consider implementation across varied infrastructures and learner needs, including strategies for helping students interpret confidence indicators, compare model behavior, and document verification workflows. To ground the conversation, facilitators will pose specific design scenarios, such as how a middle school teacher with limited internet bandwidth might adapt the model comparison feature, or how the tool could be integrated into an existing media literacy curriculum. Participants will also give feedback on LumiBuilder's features and offer suggestions for future extensions, fostering a collaborative exchange of ideas about how hallucination-aware tools can support critical AI literacy at scale. Discussion prompts will invite attendees to reflect on how verification practices cultivated through LumiBuilder might connect to broader information literacy goals in their own teaching and research contexts. This session is ideal for researchers in AI education, educators interested in preparing students for responsible AI use, and technologists designing next-generation learning tools.
