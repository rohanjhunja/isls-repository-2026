# Thrive Online: Preparing Learners for Success in Online Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The need for students to learn online continues to increase. Many students report feeling unprepared, lacking the skills and confidence to succeed. Efforts are needed to bridge a student’s transition from traditional learning environments to online. We developed Thrive Online, an asynchronous short course aimed to increase students’ knowledge, skills, attitudes, and self-efficacy to learn online. A pilot study evaluated the impact of Thrive Online with a sample of 28 students, with results reporting promising data this course can support students’ successful transition to online learning.

Online learning The adoption of online courses has temporarily or permanently increased due to the COVID-19 pandemic (Arum & Stevens, 2020), which relied on emergency remote teaching and learning (Hodges et al., 2020). Online classes are different, and when developed in haste, they may lack some of the traditional safeguards of traditional classes that help students. Indeed, some students in fully online classes report feeling more disconnected from their peers and instructors, feeling more responsibility to be self-directed, since they receive less help from th eir instructor and can also feel intimidated by the technological expectations of the online learning environment (Ho lley & Oliver, 2010; Otter et al., 2013). Students transit ioning from a traditional high school to college ma y feel disconnected from their peers in an online classroo m because they are unfamiliar with how to appropria tely connect to other learners.

In education, it is important to focus on assisting  students in learning how to learn (Barron & Darlin gHammond, 2008) and build not just content knowledge but also 21st-century skills. These skills will help learners navigate formal learning opportunities, cultivate new mindsets and learning habits (Fischer, 2000)., and meet the demands of changing technologies and job expectatio ns. Formal education should prepare learners with t hese skills and the desire to be a lifelong learner. In addition, it should enable them to be flexible as new expectations arise so the learner can be successful in whatever learning environment. This is a description of an e ffort to improve student learning capacity and self-efficacy to successfully learn in an online environment. Thrive online design In the last year, we have collaboratively designed and implemented the Thrive Online course to address the various needs of learners at a large private university in the United States. This course was designed to increase students' knowledge, skills, attitudes, and self-efficacy nav igating the online learning environment and is guid ed by Gagné’s principles of instruction (Gagné et al., 1992). The Thrive Online course is a 2-week asynchronous course. It was designed with five strategies: (a) creating engaging activities, (b) creating an environment that values the knowledge and experience of the learners, (c) regul ar communication with instructors and teaching assi stants (Tas), (d) manageable workload for the learning conditions, and (e) multiple means of processing the material to diversify and scale the learning materials that are relevant and applicable to the students’ learning needs. Using these strategies, the course material include: (a) orientation to the learning management system, (b) online learning technical skills, (c) learner wellness, (d) online learning strategies, (e) developing a growth mindset, (f) accessing campus resources to support online learning, and (g) connecting with instructors, TAs, and other learners.

## Methodology

We used design-based research (Barab & Squire, 2004; McKenney & Reeves, 2018) to frame our approach and develop the course, providing an opportunity to ite ratively tackle a problem of practice in online lea rning. We designed learning modules and resources with input from learners and pilot studied the implementation. We used mixed methods (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018) to cap ture the experiences, knowledge, beliefs, and attitudes of learners. This work is guided by the following research question: How do students’ perceptions, knowledge, and confidence with online courses change after participating in the Thrive Online course? ## Participants

Participants included 28 undergraduate and graduate  students who were enrolled in an online course at the university. Participants were randomly selected from over 20,000 students; participation was voluntary.

## Data collection and analysis

Data was collected from pre/post-surveys focused on  participants’ knowledge, attitudes, and self-effic acy. We incorporated a Short Instrument to measure Students’ Confidence with ‘Key Skills’ (SICKS; Bray et al., 2020).

Using results from the SICKS, students gained an un derstanding of their deficiencies and strengths in online learning and set goals accordingly. All data is informing future design changes and improvements. Quantitative data from the surveys was analyzed for differences from pre to post.

## Results and implications

The pre/post surveys provide a promising picture of the support that the Thrive Online course gives to students. The survey focused on student perceptions, knowledge, and confidence in connecting with other students online, the value of connecting with others online, value o f improving their knowledge and skills in online co urses, managing their mental health, setting goals, and ap plying the skills and strategies they have learned.  The percentage of students who were undecided, disagree d, or strongly disagreed about these different area s was higher before taking the Thrive Online course. There was an increase from pre/post in all areas for students who strongly agreed. After taking the Thrive Online course, 63% of the participants indicated that they strongly agreed they now know how to connect with other students in the online environment. And with that, 57% strongly agreed that it is important to connect with other students in the online environment. It is promising that with a short 2-week bridge cou rse students are noting positive changes. As a pilo t study and with iterative design we believe the Thri ve Online course will continue to be helpful for al l online students at the university. With a very small sampl e size in this pilot work though, we cannot make sw eeping claims, but as Glazier (2021) points out that to make human connection online, there must be intentional actions, whether you are an instructor, TA, or student. Thrive Online provides a means for this to happen. Thrive online next steps We continue to explore when the most valuable time is to expose students to these learning principles and techniques to successfully implement new knowledge and skills into their credit carrying online courses. Future iterations will include supports for the unique needs of marginalized student learners. We also want to know more about student confidence and ability to succeed in an online learning environment.
