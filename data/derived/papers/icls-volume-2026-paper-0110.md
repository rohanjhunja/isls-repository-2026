# Boundary Crossing for Pedagogical Transformation: How High-Coupling Encounters Enable Professional Development Across Maritime and Education Communities

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This action research study examines how education researchers and maritime faculty partnered to transform teaching practices at an Indian maritime institute, motivated by concerns about cadets' underpreparedness for sea. Through ethnographic observations, interviews, and analysis of six professional development workshops over nine months, we demonstrate that professional identity profoundly shapes pedagogical beliefs and constrains change efforts. Maritime faculty's seafaring identity dominated their teaching identity, requiring partnership approaches that honored rather than bypassed this reality. Our boundary-crossing analysis reveals that abstract pedagogical workshops stalled at identification, while high-coupling encounters using maritime-embedded boundary objects, role-switching, and co-designed lessons enabled coordination, reflection, and transformation. Four design principles emerged: using domain-specific boundary objects, redistributing authority through role-switching, framing problems locally, and integrating professional identities respectfully. This study contributes actionable insights for designing purposeful partnerships across specialized training contexts where practitioner expertise must anchor pedagogical innovation.

How do we create meaningful professional development when two professional communities with distinct expertise, priorities, and epistemologies must collaborate? This question lies at the heart of educational partnerships across specialized training domains where domain practitioners hold deep operational knowledge while education researchers bring pedagogical expertise. Such partnerships are increasingly necessary as rapid technological and regulatory changes demand continuous adaptation of training practices in fields from medicine to aviation to maritime operations (Pavić, 2023). Yet the challenge extends beyond simply conveying pedagogical best practices to practitioners. It requires building what we term a purposeful partnership: collaboration that honors the professional identities, contextual constraints, and legitimate knowledge bases of both communities while generating transformative change in teaching and learning practices. This study examines one such partnership between education faculty and maritime faculty at an Indian maritime training institute. The collaboration was initiated when the institute's leadership invited an education faculty team to examine teaching and learning practices and design a professional development program. This invitation was motivated by troubling reports that fresh cadets were underprepared for shipboard work and, in some cases, experienced serious distress at sea. These concerns echo international challenges in maritime education and training, where institutions face mounting pressure to adapt curricula while maintaining compliance with international conventions and meeting evolving industry needs (Gabedava, 2021). The institute sought context-sensitive support that could speak to maritime realities while strengthening instructional practice. Our nine-month action research study reveals that creating purposeful partnerships in such contexts requires more than good intentions or content expertise. It demands deliberate boundary-crossing design that enables learning to unfold at the interface of distinct professional practices. We document how maritime faculty's professional identity, deeply rooted in their extensive seafaring experience, profoundly shapes their pedagogical beliefs and practices. Their identity as seafarers dominates their identity as educators, creating both challenges and opportunities for professional development initiatives. Critically, we demonstrate that partnership approaches must engage rather than bypass this reality. Through analysis of ethnographic observations, interviews, and six professional development workshops, we show that abstract pedagogical sessions stalled at boundary identification, while high-coupling encounters using maritime-embedded boundary objects, role-switching, and co-designed lessons enabled coordination, reflection, and transformation. Four design principles emerged from workshops that successfully traversed professional boundaries: using domain-specific boundary objects, redistributing authority through role-switching, framing pedagogical problems locally within maritime contexts, and respectfully integrating both professional identities.

## Theoretical framework and literature

Professional development in specialized training contexts presents unique challenges that differ from traditional academic settings. Vocational and technical education teachers occupy a dual professional space, simultaneously maintaining identities rooted in their original professions while developing pedagogical expertise (Köpsén, 2014). Research shows that vocational teachers often prioritize their professional identity over their teaching identity, which significantly shapes their pedagogical practices and responses to professional development initiatives (Zhou et al., 2022). This dual identity may create peculiar tensions when external education experts design professional development programs without engaging the specific occupational cultures and knowledge systems that structure specialized training environments. Teacher professional identity is neither fixed nor singular but rather emerges through continuous negotiation between personal biography, professional experiences, and institutional contexts (Beijaard et al., 2004). Identity formation involves how teachers perceive themselves in relation to their work and how they interpret their roles and responsibilities (Day et al., 2006). For vocational teachers transitioning from industry to education, professional identity development is especially complex as they must integrate their established occupational expertise with emerging pedagogical knowledge while navigating institutional expectations that may conflict with industry norms (Shwartz & Dori, 2020). This process of identity negotiation becomes particularly salient when education researchers attempt to introduce pedagogical innovations that may be perceived as threatening established professional practices. To understand how learning unfolds when two distinct professional communities collaborate, we adopt a boundary-crossing framework (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011). Boundaries exist wherever different practices, perspectives, or expertise meet, creating both barriers and opportunities for learning. Akkerman and Bakker identify four learning mechanisms at boundaries: identification (recognizing differences between practices), coordination (establishing routines for working across practices), reflection (expanding perspectives through comparison), and transformation (fundamentally changing practices through collaboration). This framework enables us to analyze how maritime faculty and education faculty navigate their distinct epistemologies, values, and practices during professional development.

Critical to our analysis is the distinction between low-coupling and high-coupling boundary encounters. Low-coupling exchanges involve minimal shared activity, often featuring abstract knowledge transmission from one group to another with limited joint work or co-constructed artifacts. In contrast, high-coupling encounters embed learning in shared practice through boundary objects rooted in both communities' authentic work, role-switching that redistributes authority, and collaborative problem-solving around locally meaningful challenges. The boundary objects concept, introduced by Star and Griesemer (1989) and elaborated by Akkerman and Bakker, refers to artifacts, tools, or practices that are meaningful across different communities and facilitate coordination without requiring consensus.

Purposeful partnership across professional boundaries thus requires more than simply bringing people together. It demands deliberate design that creates conditions for genuine boundary crossing through high-coupling mechanisms that honor both communities' expertise while enabling transformative learning. This theoretical lens guides our analysis of how maritime and education faculty collaborated, what enabled or constrained their joint work, and under what conditions professional development moved beyond identification toward coordination, reflection, and transformation.

Given the challenges of creating purposeful partnerships between education researchers and specialized training practitioners, this study addresses three interrelated research questions:

1. How do professional identity and historicity shape pedagogical beliefs and practices in a specialized

training community?

2. What mechanisms enable or constrain boundary crossing when education researchers and maritime

faculty partner to transform teaching practices?

3. Under what conditions does cross-professional collaboration move from identification to coordination,

reflection, and transformation?

## Research design

This study followed an action research design to improve teaching and learning practices at a maritime institute through continuous professional development of maritime faculty. Action research is particularly suited for professional development contexts as it enables practitioners to systematically study and improve their own practice through iterative cycles of planning, action, observation, and reflection (Koshy, 2010; Carr & Kemmis, 2003). The reflective and cyclical nature of action research allowed us to design, implement, evaluate, and refine the professional development program based on ongoing feedback from participants and observations of collaborative encounters.

## Context and participants

The study involved all 19 maritime faculty members (MF) from an Indian maritime academy and five education faculty researchers (EF) from a social science institute. Among the MF participants, 14 held extensive seafaring experience, averaging 19 years at sea, including eight Captains and four Chief Engineers. Three MF were female faculty teaching physics, English, and serving as counselors, while 16 were male. Five MF taught foundational subjects without prior sailing experience. The MF teaching experience ranged from 1.5 to 24.5 years, with an average of 11 years. The EF team comprised two female and three male researchers aged 25 to 45, all working in a department focused on teacher education and professional development. Faculty members who attended the sessions and participated in data collection varied across workshops.

## Intervention and data collection

The collaboration commenced with a two-week ethnographic immersion where two EF researchers lived alongside cadet-students to understand the teaching, learning, and overall culture of the institute. This was followed by a baseline data collection phase involving classroom observations, focus group discussions with MF in three groups, and individual semi-structured interviews with MF conducted post-intervention. The professional development intervention consisted of six workshops conducted over nine months, ranging from abstract pedagogical theory sessions to hands-on maritime-embedded activities (see Figure 1). In addition to these, data sources included workshop session notes, informal conversation records during workshops, and post-facto analytic memos written by the research team. This multi-method approach enabled triangulation of findings across different data sources and time points. Professional identity shapes pedagogical practice (RQ1) Maritime faculty's professional identity remained anchored in their seafaring roles, which profoundly shaped their pedagogical beliefs and classroom practices. This identity-practice nexus emerged vividly through baseline observations and focus group discussions, revealing a coherent but pedagogically constraining belief system rooted in maritime training culture rather than contemporary educational theory. Transmission beliefs and the blank slate metaphor MF consistently articulated a transmission model of learning, viewing students as vessels to be filled with knowledge. As one faculty member expressed during a focus group discussion: "...they are totally fresh, it is like a blank slate. And then you have to put something..." (FGD, Baseline Group 1) This metaphor appeared repeatedly across data sources, paired with beliefs about "drilling" knowledge through lectures and practical demonstrations. Classroom observations confirmed practices aligned with these beliefs: faculty lecturing from the front while students sat in rows, with limited interaction or inquiry-based engagement. Teaching versus training: A critical distinction MF drew sharp distinctions between "teaching" and "training," viewing the latter as their primary responsibility. One faculty member asserted: "We provide training, and teaching is a subset of that training... because our training encompasses the classroom theory part and then to improve skills we have more focus on practical..." (FGD, Baseline Group 1) This discourse was met with strong affirmation by senior faculty, suggesting shared cultural norms. Faculty frequently expressed discomfort with the label " teacher," preferring "trainer," which reflected dominant institutional discourse and reinforced hierarchical group dynamics. Paradoxically, while emphasizing practical skills, MF also commonly stated that " actual learning happens at sea." Their institute-based role was framed narrowly as preparing students to pass examinations and instill discipline, creating a disconnect between stated values (practical learning) and classroom practices (exam-oriented lectures). Discipline, culture, and maritime hierarchy Discipline and compliance were cherished institutional values reflected in classroom management. Faculty maintained rigid discipline, sometimes through punitive measures such as "cleanship" (cleaning assignments) or "boiler" work to suppress dissent, mirroring shipboard authority structures. One warden explained: "Discipline is very important, as, if they go to the ship without being disciplined, it will become a problem." (Ethnographic Notes, Warden Interview) Students often appeared disengaged, with ethnographic notes documenting sleeping students, reluctance to participate, and limited questioning beyond exam requirements. 985 Seafaring identity hierarchy These beliefs and practices reflect how MF's dominant seafaring identity overshadows their educator identity. All maritime faculty with sea experience wore white uniforms with epaulettes denoting their former ranks and addressed each other by maritime titles (Captain, Chief Engineer) rather than academic designations.  Practical classes are led by facilitators who are also ex-seafarers, but  worked at lower ranks. They wielded much less power and respect in the institute. Faculty members without sea experience, who taught foundational subjects like physics, mathematics, and English, were often perceived as less qualified or relegated to supporting roles within the faculty hierarchy. This identity hierarchy manifested symbolically and practically throughout the institute.

Faculty frequently described their teaching role as " stress-free" and suitable for those nearing retirement, contrasting it sharply with the perceived intensity and importance of life at sea. Drawing from their seafaring past, they viewed it as their responsibility to " pass the baton" of professional knowledge to prepare the next generation for shipboard life. Their teaching methods closely mirrored how they themselves were taught: lecture-driven theory sessions supplemented by practical demonstrations, replicating the hierarchical and discipline-centered maritime culture. These symbolic practices and beliefs signaled that, despite adopting educator roles, their primary professional identification remained anchored in seafaring careers. As subsequent hierarchy.

Limited pedagogical training Almost all maritime faculty underwent mandatory short-duration courses before joining as instructors to comply with the International Convention on Standards of Training, Certification and Watchkeeping for Seafarers (STCW). Previously known as "Training of Trainers and Assessors (TOTA)" and currently the "Vertical Integration Course for Trainers (VICT)," both span approximately 10 days and aim to enhance teaching skills. However, the brevity and content scope limit meaningful change in faculty conceptions about teaching and learning. Although terms like inquiry-based or project-based learning are introduced, faculty interpretations remain selective and narrow.

This baseline revealed that changing practice requires engaging MF's dominant professional identity, not bypassing it. This realization fundamentally shaped our partnership design, as subsequent sections demonstrate.

Boundary crossing in practice: Low vs. high-coupling (RQ2 and RQ3) The collaboration between education faculty and maritime faculty evolved through distinct phases of boundary work, ranging from encounters that reinforced professional boundaries to those that successfully enabled crossing. Analyzing these interactions through a boundary-crossing lens reveals critical insights into what enables or constrains partnership across professional communities. A. Mutual orientation at the boundary Early engagements comprised ethnographic observations, focus group discussions, and classroom visits where both communities assessed each other's legitimacy and established initial positions. In one early focus group that maritime work is fundamentally "training," with "teaching" as a subset within it, emphasizing the primacy of practical skill formation and prior seafaring experience. The immediate and emphatic endorsement by senior MF operated as boundary work delimiting identity, competence, and authority within the community. Such moments functioned as symbolic boundary-making that policed distinctions between trainer and teacher, seafarer-experienced and non-seafarer staff, and operational versus academic knowledge, thereby establishing conditions for what would count as credible pedagogical change. B. Low-coupling failures Several professional development sessions elicited participation but limited discussion or durable uptake. Topics included andragogy, situated cognition and its implications to education, and the importance of visuospatial reasoning in STEM education. Post-intervention interviews rarely referenced these sessions unprompted; when probed, some MF reported difficulty connecting abstractions to maritime routines. Analytically, these meetings stalled at identification, where differences in language and emphasis became visible, but boundary objects, codesigned tasks, or maritime-specific anchoring were insufficient to move work into coordination or reflection. A particularly illustrative example occurred during a theory-heavy session on ICT integration without hands-on components, which prompted heated debate but little agreement. Absent shared tasks or local exemplars, 986 participants remained at identification, surfacing differences without negotiating shared procedures. In effect, these sessions remained about the academic idea of pedagogy rather than shared activity located in maritime systems and problems.

C. High-coupling successes By contrast, a set of sessions actively traversed practice boundaries by embedding pedagogy within maritime artifacts, roles, and settings, consistently generating animated exchange, durable recall in interviews, and concrete intentions to adapt practice.

Inquiry with maritime content:  EF facilitated an inquiry-based session using a maritime curriculum topic while MF assumed learner roles, experiencing the pedagogical approach firsthand. This role-switching created a boundary object where maritime content met inquiry pedagogy, enabling MF to experience student perspectives while maintaining domain legitimacy. The session enabled coordination through shared procedures and sparked reflection on what engaging theory instruction might look like, with several MF commenting in interviews that they had not previously considered such approaches possible within their time and syllabus constraints. MF-led demonstration lesson:  In workshop 5, an MF taught a lesson on learning and memory linked to maritime contexts while peers and EF observed cadets' responses. The lesson functioned as a boundary object, recognizable as training yet open to pedagogical redesign. Post-lesson debriefing supported reflection on discourse moves, questioning techniques, task design, and assessment practices. Critically, having an MF lead preserved seafaring legitimacy while redistributing pedagogical authority, enabling coordination around shared observation and reflection on practice.

Co-designed activity on ship structures: In workshop 6, two EF and one MF collaboratively planned and cotaught an activity-based session on structural features of ships for cadets, while other MFs observed. The jointly produced lesson operated as a strong boundary object rooted in maritime content but designed with pedagogical principles. Co-teaching enacted coordination by requiring real-time negotiation of roles and pacing. Debriefing enabled reflection on questioning strategies, evidence of student understanding, and classroom management. Positive reception from both cadets and observing faculty indicated seeds of transformation through a locally owned, legitimate model that integrated both communities' expertise. ICT tools with maritime tasks:  Hands-on exploration of digital tools, including AI applications, GeoGebra for visualizing ship stability calculations, maritime simulations, and classroom response systems (Kahoot, Plickers), anchored in maritime examples, supported coordination through shared tool use. MF reflected on fit to constraints such as time, class size, and connectivity, identifying pragmatic adaptations for large-group, examoriented contexts. Several MF later reported in interviews experimenting with these tools, demonstrating initial transformation of practice.

Synthesis of design principles Across high-coupling workshops, four design features repeatedly enabled boundary crossing: (1) maritime boundary objects that anchored pedagogical concepts in authentic domain practice, (2) role-switching that redistributed authority while preserving seafaring legitimacy, (3) local problem framing that made pedagogical choices consequential within maritime realities, and (4) respectful integration of both professional identities rather than privileging one over the other. These patterns align with Akkerman and Bakker's mechanisms of coordination, reflection, and transformation, demonstrating that purposeful partnership requires deliberately designed high-coupling encounters that honor both communities' expertise while creating hybrid practice spaces for joint learning.

Evidence of change in MF practice (RQ3 Impact) Post-PD interviews revealed shifts in MF pedagogical discourse and professional identity, particularly among faculty who actively engaged in high-coupling workshops. These changes manifested as a renegotiation of their dual professional identity, with teaching identity emerging more prominently alongside their established seafaring identity.

Emerging teacher identity MF named T3, who had previously emphasized that teaching was merely "a subset of training" during baseline focus group discussions, demonstrated a marked shift in how he positioned himself professionally. When asked about his teaching experience post-PD, he stated: "I feel teaching is in my DNA. I enjoy teaching. I enjoy speaking. Grandfather was also there. Father was also there. Uncle was also there." (Post-PD Interview, T3) This reflection on his familial teaching legacy represented a significant departure from his earlier stance. T3's newfound appreciation for teaching as a distinct professional identity, rather than simply an extension of his seafaring role, suggests that PD activities created space for him to reconcile these dual identities. Self-awareness and critical reflection Beyond identity shifts, MF demonstrated increased pedagogical self-awareness. T3 critically evaluated his own practice, acknowledging, "I tend to speak more than what is required... and that is a drawback also in teaching," comparing himself with teachers who "finish their syllabus on time and are getting good results." This reflexivity, largely absent at baseline, indicated emerging capacity for pedagogical reflection. Other faculty reported experimenting with specific pedagogical approaches introduced during high-coupling workshops. One mathematics faculty discussed using GeoGebra for visualizing ship stability and relating hands-on construction activities to constructivist principles. Another faculty applied distributed cognition concepts from the bridge simulation workshop to redesign communication exercises for cadets. These changes emerged not from adopting EF language wholesale, but from hybrid practice encounters that legitimized pedagogical reflection within maritime contexts. By anchoring pedagogical innovation in authentic maritime problems while preserving MF's seafaring legitimacy, high-coupling workshops enabled faculty to integrate new practices into their evolving professional identities.

Core argument This study demonstrates that purposeful partnership across professional boundaries requires high-coupling boundary work that honors existing professional identities while creating hybrid practice spaces for transformation. Our findings show that effective professional development in specialized training contexts succeeds not by replacing practitioner expertise with pedagogical theory, but by co-constructing locally 988 legitimate pedagogies grounded in authentic domain practice. The contrast between low-coupling workshops that stalled at identification and high-coupling encounters that enabled coordination, reflection, and transformation underscores a critical insight: boundary crossing is not accidental but requires deliberate design that integrates both communities' expertise while redistributing authority respectfully. Contributions Theoretical contributions:  This study extends boundary-crossing theory by demonstrating how specific design features operationalize Akkerman and Bakker's (2011) learning mechanisms in professional development contexts. We show that professional identity functions simultaneously as constraint and resource: maritime faculty's seafaring identity initially resisted pedagogical change, yet high-coupling designs that honored this identity enabled transformation by legitimizing pedagogical reflection within maritime contexts. This aligns with research on vocational teacher identity, showing that professional identification shapes pedagogical practice (Zhou et al., 2022) and extends it by demonstrating how partnerships can engage rather than bypass this identity hierarchy. Our work contributes to third space scholarship in teacher education (Laughlin, 2021) by specifying mechanisms that enable hybrid practice encounters in specialized training contexts where practitioner expertise must remain central.

Practical contributions:  We identify four actionable design principles for cross-professional professional development: using domain-specific boundary objects, redistributing authority through role-switching, framing pedagogical problems locally, and respectfully integrating professional identities. These principles offer guidance for creating what He & Bagwell (2022) term "hybrid spaces" where university-based education researchers and practice-based professionals can generate horizontal forms of knowledge through sustained collaboration. The distinction between low-coupling and high-coupling encounters provides designers with a heuristic for evaluating partnership activities.

## Implications for partnering with purpose

A purposeful partnership requires more than aligned goals or good intentions. It demands deliberate boundarycrossing infrastructure that respects both communities' expertise while creating generative third spaces (Christophersen et al., 2024; Laughlin, 2021). High-coupling design principles identified here are potentially applicable across specialized domains where practitioners hold deep operational knowledge: medical education, engineering training, military instruction, or emergency response preparation. In each context, transformation becomes possible when pedagogical innovation is embedded within authentic practice rather than imported from outside.

## Limitations and future directions

This study examined a single site with EF researchers analyzing their own collaborative practice, raising questions about transferability and researcher positionality. Future research should investigate the long-term sustainability of pedagogical changes, the impact on cadet learning outcomes, and the transfer of these design principles to other maritime institutes and specialized training contexts. Comparative studies across domains could identify common mechanisms and context-specific variations in boundary-crossing professional development. Also, although we saw some shift in teaching beliefs and practices, we feel that it is necessary to have a continual professional development program to see sustained and significant changes. Creating meaningful partnerships between education researchers and specialized practitioners remains a persistent challenge across training contexts. This study demonstrates that transformation becomes possible when partnerships move beyond surface-level collaboration toward sustained boundary crossing through highcoupling encounters. By honoring maritime faculty's professional identity while creating hybrid spaces for pedagogical innovation, education, and maritime communities co-constructed locally legitimate practices that neither could have developed alone.
