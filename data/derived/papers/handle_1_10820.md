# Epistemic Games at the Frontier: A Characterization of Emerging Science and Engineering Practices

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Expert practices in frontier science and engineering domains may provide insights for the design of K12 STEM education models. In this short paper, we present a novel characterization of the design process of a frontier engineering sciences (ES) innovation – the Paperfuge – through the lens of epistemic forms and games (EFG). The Paperfuge is a low-cost redesign of the centrifuge, making healthcare more accessible in the developing world. We offer a temporal decomposition of the prototyping journey of the Paperfuge, identifying six distinct design episodes. We present two episodes, underscoring the significance of ‘prototype-based imagining’ as the signature epistemic game played during the episodes that led to design breakthroughs. We briefly discuss the novel features of this game and its possible design implications for K12 STEM education, particularly for the design of makerspaces (MS).

## Introduction

Disciplines such as robotics, materials science, bi oengineering, and machine learning/AI recruit and r emix the practices, concepts, and methods of several domains  (physics, chemistry, biology, cognitive neuroscien ce, and mathematics) to handle the complexity of modern scientific challenges. These interdisciplinary endeavors, which blur the conventional boundaries between science and engineering practices, fall under the broad umbre lla of engineering sciences (ES). Engineering and science pedagogies must be reconfigured significantly to promote these emerging practices (Chandrasekharan & Nersess ian, 2021). To design education models that can sup port these practices, work is needed to understand and u npack aspects of ES practices, particularly how the se frontier research spaces function as fertile ground for inno vation and discovery. Seminal work in this directio n has shed some light on the nature of the interdisciplinary practices (Chandrasekharan & Nersessian, 2015, 2021) as well as the cognitive mechanisms underlying innovation and discovery based on these practices (Chandrasekharan, 2009). However, as ES practices are complex and evolving, it is still unclear how these practices might be mo deled to support K12 educational objectives in a way that al lows the practices to be incorporated within educat ion frameworks.

In 2012, the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS) were introduced in the US to reform the science and mathematics curriculum. With a focus on making  these subjects more interdisciplinary, the framework calls for introducing “engineering design” practices in K 12 science and mathematics classrooms (NRC, 2012). Although there is encouragement from both research and policy to introduce integrated STEM in K12 in a variety of contexts (classrooms, labs, makerspaces, after school programs, librar ies, summer workshops etc.), designing pedagogies that support the engineering dimension of STEM practices has thus far received little explicit attention (Chandler et al., 2011), much less the ES dimension s. Motivated by this gap in the literature, we characterize a frontier ES innovation — the Paperfuge – through the lens of the epistemic forms and games (EFG) framework, proposed by Collins and Ferguson (1993). We then briefly outline the potential implication of the analysis for the design of STEM-rich learning environments in K12, particularly makerspaces (MS). Collins and Ferguson (1993) proposed the EFG framew ork as a way to understand the knowledge-building processes that experts use across scholarly disciplines, from physical to social sciences. They suggested that though individual disciplines have their theor ies, concepts, and methods to understand and approa ch problems, generalizable strategies and structures can be found across disciplines. According to them, “Epistemic forms are target structures that guide inquiry. Epi stemic games are general purpose strategies for ana lyzing phenomena to fill out a particular epistemic form” (Collins and Ferguson, 1993,  p. 1). They identified various types of epistemic games common across several scientific disciplines such as stage models, hierarchies, primitive elements, system-dynamics models, and axiom systems. They noted that  children, while making sense of their immediate surroundings, are already engaged in playin g simpler versions of these epistemic games to buil d their understanding of everyday experiences. Scientists, on the other hand, play more elaborate, complex, and constrained versions of these simple games to make sense of the phenomena of their interest. This observation is significant, as it has important instructional desi gn implications for education. Current education mo dels focus more on teaching conceptual structures (concepts and theories) and there  is less emphasis on teaching cross-discipline approaches (high-level knowledge structures like epistemic games). They posited that introducing these games early will help learners acquire more general-purpose strategies to systematically guide their in quiry process. Hence, characterizing the epistemic forms and games of frontier disciplines could help us bet ter understand the nature of practices in those discipl ines. The insights could potentially help to design  education models for introducing learner-accessible versions of those games early on.

## Methodology

The Paperfuge is an ultra-low-cost, portable, hand-powered, paper-based centrifuge device designed by Saad Bhamla and colleagues at the Prakash Lab at Stanford (Bhamla et al., 2017). It is highly versatile and can be used for sample preparation to perform several diagnostic assays, including the detection of diseases such as malaria and measuring hematocrit value to determine if some one is anemic. It exemplifies a case of ES that tac kles a critical need with an unconventional, ingenious, and resourceful solution. The design of the Paperfuge is inspired by a popular toy known as a whirligig, a circular disk-shaped toy that works by twisting a thread and then moving two handles in a push-and-pull motion.

In this paper, we characterize the nature of the Pa perfuge design process, highlighting the complex and iterative building processes that mark frontier eng ineering sciences. We engaged in reflexive thematic  analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2019), temporally decomposing the Paperfuge prototyping journey to identify six distinct design episodes (critical events). We reconstructed the ep isodes based on the secondary sources — manuscript, supplementary material and videos. The primary data  sources for the analysis were the manuscript and i ts supplementary material (Bhamla et al., 2017). Publi cly available video data (Stanford, 2017) helped co rroborate and add finer details to the episodes. We provide a  theoretical characterization of the episode segmen ts, through the lens of EFG, shedding light on our research question: How did scientists and engineers engage in epistemi c games while building the Paperfuge?  Analytic memos helped us to iteratively refine the analysis, by explicating the moves and constraints. An important caveat here  is that the analysis might inadvertently portray t he prototyping steps as linear and unidirectional, but we acknowledge that the team’s everyday actions and decisions during the development of the Paperfuge were likely iterative and recursive.

## Findings

Across the design episodes, we observed different t ypes of epistemic forms, games, and moves that the team engaged in during their design journey. Following is a  brief overview of the six episodes of the Paperfuge design journey: Episode 1: The team explored everyday objects for design inspi ration and selected the whirligig toy as the potential base design to build a paper-based centrifuge; Episode 2: They conducted a series of exploratory experiments to understand how the radii of the Paperfuge disk impacts the RPM; Episode 3: They constructed an experimentally validated theoretical model of the s ystem, characterizing the whirligig as a non-linear and non-conservative oscillatory system; Episode 4: They optimized the design and settled on the disk r adius of 50 mm, which generated a relative centrifugation force (RC F) of 10000g, sufficient for sample preparation in a time duration comparable to that of the conventional cen trifuges; Episode 5: They added safety features to the design and developed custom assa ys; Episode 6:  They extended the base design of the Paperfuge for different applications like nucleic acid separation, for iden tifying different parasites, etc. Due to the space constraints of this short paper, we selected two such episodes (Ep isodes 3-4) that we consid er to have significant implications for both the characterization of frontier ES practices as well as for the design of MS in K12. Building an experi mentally validated theoretical model involved capturing the dynamics in a set of differential equations, solving them using numerical methods, fitting the model using the geometric properties of the prototype and  experimental observation, determining the unknown parameters in the model — twisting parameter and drag coefficient, simplifying the model by making assumptions, and verifying their assumptions with e mpirical results. In summary, the theoretical model  was validated via the data generated from the experimen tal prototypes to arrive at an experimentally-validated theoretical model of the system.

Theoretical Characterization:  To c haracterize the whirligig system, the team initiate d the epistemic game of building a coupled model, with the goal of filling out the coupled model template (form). This is similar to the game played by physicists to generate mathematical models of physical systems/phenomena. In the case of the Paperfuge, the primary goal was to achieve pred ictive power over the system’s behavior, to make de sign breakthroughs in a local context. Unpacking the complex  details of the modeling process is beyond the scope of this analysis. However, we found that the identifie d moves were in general agreement with the model pr oposed by Redish and Kuo (2015) to illustrate mathematical  modeling by physicists. These moves include: selecting features of the physical system to model; identifyi ng key variables, parameters, and recruiting approp riate concepts from literature to represent the physical system in terms of “mathematical structures” (the Paperfuge team started with the Lagrangian equation); making assumptions and mapping the measurements into mathematical structures to express the “physical –causal” relationship between the quantities; proces sing the mapped mathematical structures i.e. solving the num erical equations; interpreting the result about the  physical system, and evaluating whether the result “supports the original choice of model when compared to observations” (Redish and Kuo, 2015, p. 568). In this iterative cycle, the physical prototypes su pplied two critical data points. First, they were used to derive the values of empirical paramet ers. Second, they were used to verify if there was fidelity between the theoretical model and the experimental results. Episode 4: Optimizing the design The team optimized the design for their focal funct ion (relative centrifugation force), generating exp erimental predictions. The team settled on the disk radius of  50 mm, which generated an RCF of 10000g, sufficien t for sample preparation in a duration of time comparable to that of the conventional centrifuges. Theoretical Characterization:  The team engaged in a game of design optimization, driven by the template for an optimized design (form). To do this, they leveraged the predictive power of the coupled model to optimize their key metrics: relative centrifugation force and RPM, satisfying other constraints. Moves here involve mathematical optimization techniques (to predict how the system will behave with a given set of initial parameters values). Computational resources and mathematical techniques are leveraged to productively constrain the multiparameter dynamic system, to achieve a design breakthrough. Note that while the coupled model is the key artifact that is tested for different parameters, its constr uction is not possible without the tight coupling b etween the data generated via the physical prototypes (experimental  observations, geometric parameters) and the mathem atical model. T he game still is ‘tinkering (recruiting optimization techniques, changing parame ter values) with the coupled model (virtual artifact generator),’ to predict optimal designs. This episode led to a s table prototype, which was optimized, to embed the critical functional specifications.

## Discussion and pedagogical implications

In the above episodes, we foregrounded the centrali ty of building, manipulating and probing with proto types (“prototype-based imagining”) to arrive at a real-world system that solves a complex challenge. In ou r account, we construe prototypes more broadly,  comprising both the real-world physical system (physical prototype) and its simulated instantiation (virtual prototypes) generated via the computational/simulation models. Also, building, manipulating, and probing are intertwined in the se nse that these encompass a wide range of making, ti nkering, design, and inquiry moves. We found that building a nd manipulation of the ‘coupled model’ (episode 3-4) was powerful as it virtualized the physical system, unl ocking the predictive potential in the design space  that was previously inaccessible. Building a coupled model has also been observed in other ES cases (Chandrasekharan & Nersessian, 2015; 2021; MacLeod and Nersessian 2013). We foregrounded ‘prototype-based imagining’ as the key epistemic game played by the design team to achieve their design breakthroughs. This is possibly among the signature epistemic games played by scientists in other ES disciplines. It is important to highlight some distinctions betw een the nature of games played in non-ES science practice and the games in the ES case of the Paperfuge. These distinctions are critical to the design of educational interventions that seek to support prot otype-based imagining. Prototypes (physical, simulated, c oupled) are ‘performative’ models. They provide knowledge by ‘acting out’ parts of the phenomena under investigation. They are built to perform, and are created out of active  mechanism elements. This makes them unlike, say,  a crossproduct matrix form like the periodic table, which doesn’t perform, and is not made up of active mecha nism elements. Extending this view, non-ES model-building games, involving the construction of static representations (like texts, graphs, equations), can be considered descriptive, as these models do not act out phenomena. In STEM education, several  research programs have designed performative models  to facilitate students’ exploration of phenomena and engagement in computational modeling practices (Blikstein et al., 2016; Wilensky, 1999). Designs for interventions involving PBI and related engineering practices can build on this work, adding ways for students to build and use models to test prototypes.

The case we outline here only briefly captures the nature of the EFG in the building of such performative models, which seem set to dominate emerging STEM pr actice. A significant amount of work lies ahead to understand the ways in which the building of such performative models leads to discoveries and innovations, and how such an understanding can help design education al interventions that seek to embed and augment thi s practice. Among the candidate pedagogical practices that could be extended to address this problem, makerspaces (MS) stand out as the most promising, as many of the frontier practices share the ‘building’ feature with MS (For a list of constructs in MS, see Sinha et al. 2021). The analogical leap to craft ‘learner appropriate’ versions of MS experiences – incorporating the design principles advanced by scholars in MS, augmenting new epistemic games (such as ‘prototype-based imagining’) – could be a powerful way to reconfigure the MS learn ing experiences to support diverse ways of knowing, particularly the i mplicit and performative knowledge generated by ES.  Such MS designs would unlock MS’s potential to “be a sandbox to support frontier ES practices” (Date, 2022). The landscape of MS design possibilities foregrounding engineering – augmenting existing and emerging computational technologies (such as VR and AI) with  hands-on crafts – is limitless. An exciting time lies ahead for learning sciences and STEM education.
