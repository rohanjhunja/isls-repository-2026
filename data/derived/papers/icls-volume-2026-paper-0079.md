# Dialogues with Objects: Learning through Relational Attunement in Making

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
By engaging participants in an expansive example of open-source sharing practice, this study explores how dialogical encounters with others’ designs can inform more just and contextually grounded pedagogies in making. Building on a long-term design-based research partnership among a museum, schools, and community members, the study invited learners to engage with artifacts that carried the histories, relationships, and aspirations of youth makers. Analysis of dialogical sequences—moments of response and counter-response between people and artifacts—identified three forms of dialogical responsiveness: attending to contextual detail, negotiating conflicting representations, and sustaining dialogue through uncertainty. Findings suggest that engaging with materially and historically rich artifacts cultivates empathy, critical interpretation, and ethical imagination, reframing making as a practice of listening and responding to the social worlds that materials embody.

“My dream project is about showing the history of the [Museum]. I hope to create a timeline starting from the first creation of the [Museum] up to now and what things we might do in the future… I really want to be able to educate others, show the struggles in the process and details of creating something like the [Museum] today, and the main reason is to teach history and that it isn't easy to create stuff like that. But also, I want people to be inspired with different ideas from the timeline and remember, just persevere and you could do anything if you believe in yourself and through hard working.” When Hannah, a seventh grader, shared her “dream project,” I was in the audience at a community meeting within a design-based research partnership among community members, her middle school makerspace, and a local science museum. Over the winter and spring of 2023, she designed a 3D model of a future museum makerspace that communicated her team’s vision of a youth-and community-centered space. At the center of her model was a cardboard bench grounded in her sense of what making should stand for: remembrance, care, and perseverance. Months later, when these artifacts were shared in a new learning setting, that bench became the starting point for a different kind of conversation—one between people and artifacts. Making activities turn materials intonewly imagined objects (Shea, 2022). Through building and refining, these objects externalize individuals’ ideas and intentions (Wargo & Morales, 2020). Sharing artifacts and processes is central to maker communities and to “open source” learning (Brahms & Crowley, 2016), creating opportunities to learn from others’ work by converging participation trajectories (Halverson & Peppler, 2018). Yet such learning is often limited to technological or functional replication (Tz ou et al., 2019; Vossoughi et al., 2016). Objects made through human activities are more than the technologies they adopt or the functions they serve; both are entangled with shifting political, economic, and environmental conditions. Broadening open-source practices beyond “kits” to include the contexts of making can foster consequential learning (Jurow & Shea, 2015).

This study examines how people engage with artifacts made by others —specifically, how learners respond to a portfolio of designs created by youth in a maker project. The intervention was designed to invite dialogue with these artifacts, positioning participants as  “listeners.” By engaging participants in an expansive example of open-source sharing practice, this study explores how dialogical encounters with others’ designs can inform more just and contextually grounded pedagogies in making. In this study, I treat open-source sharing not simply as open code or freely shareable kits in a narrow technical sense, but as an intentionally shareable archive of process artifacts, annotations, and design traces that allow learners to enter into relation with earlier makers. I conceptualize learning here as the development of dialogical responsiveness—an orientation to listen and respond to the voices embedded in materials and others’ creative work. Grounded in theories of dialogicality (Shotter,

2006) and relational attunement (Vossoughi et al., 2020), I aim to extend our understanding of what it means to

learn through interpretation across contexts by treating artifacts as active participants in dialogue. Accordingly, 680 this article asks: How do people engage in dialogues with artifacts to understand the contexts and social meanings embedded in others’ designs?

Material practices and learning Making involves rich material practice that allows people to inquire about their world through materials and to act upon such inquiries (Shea, 2022). Drawing from sociocultural perspectives of situated learning, I view these inquiries and actions as socially mediated processes in which meaning is constructed through relationships —not only among people, but also between people and materials. While theories of situated learning have long emphasized learning as “relations among people… in, with, and arising fr om the socially and culturally structured world” (Lave & Wenger, 1991, p. 50), materials themselves are also saturated with sociocultural relations that shape how people perceive and position themselves within broader social structures. In Kabayadondo’s (2016) study of Zimbabwean medical workers designing a bottle-feeding prosthetic device, participants’ reactions to the prototype revealed how the object reflected local social relationships, particularly gendered ones. The prototype became part of the negotiation process inseparable from the human activity through which the interaction unwra pped the object and it “[became] loaded with assumptions and understandings of patterns of social relations that are echoes of and projections onto the sociocultural milieu” (pp. 166–167).

Working with materials while recognizing the social relations embedded in them can open opportunities for imagining new possible futures. Shea (2022) showed how community educators’ repurposing of discarded materials enabled young people to inquire with re sources grounded in local histories and struggles. Rather than circulating finished goods for consumption, these educators created “material possibilities” (p. 2) rooted in community knowledge and justice-oriented pedagogy.  Together, these perspectives foreground the idea that objects carry traces of human intention and social life, and that learning with materials therefore involves responding to the social worlds they contain and reproduce. Dialogicality and attunement Having recognized that the affordances of artifacts are shaped by historical and sociocultural contexts, the question remains: how do humans engage with these affordances in ways that produce learning? Kabayadondo

(2016) reminds us that an object’s affordances are not bestowed by the user but always already exist; people take

agency by framing and reframing these affordances through different ways of  seeing. This raises an important concern for learning scientists: how might educators and learners cultivate ways of seeing that move beyond the allure of “sexy digital technologies” to attend to more equitable educational futures (Vossoughi et al., 2016, p. 212).

Shotter (2015) offers one lens through the concept of dialogicality—the idea that human understanding emerges through responsive exchange. Tracing the roots of communication to early parent –infant interaction, he argues that human beings are “dialogical at  birth” (p. 9). Knowing, in this view, is not the possession of an individual mind but a process of  attuned responding to the voices, gestures, and materials that surround us. This resonates with the new materialist notion of distributed agency (Hood & Kraehe, 2017), which views humans as co-actors with material worlds rather than autonomous agents who control them. Relational attunement (Vossoughi et al., 2020) provides a complementary perspective on how such responsiveness unfolds in learning environments. In their studies of making and tinkering spaces, Vossoughi and colleagues observed how educators and youth coordinate their actions through affective, embodied, and ethical forms of alignment. Taylor (2020) extends this notion by identifying a second layer of attunement that arises between humans and artifacts: when two students worked with a map to design a bus tour, their engagement revealed an attunement between embodied experience and the “grid” epistemology of spatial representation. The map, in turn, gained new meaning through their use.

These examples illustrate how attunement operates both relationally and materially. Whether between people, or between people and artifacts, such interaction involves listening and responding—a dialogical process through which understanding develops. In the contexts described earlier, community educators attuned their material practices to the historical and living realities of Haveston (Shea, 2022), while Zimbabwean medical workers reframed a prototype’s use through kukuya-kiya—a form of creative adaptation grounded in local social understandings (Kabayadondo, 2016). These cases together show that dialogicality and attunement are not abstract ideals but lived, material practices through which people engage with the sociocultural and ethical dimensions of their worlds.

681 Taken together, the perspectives of material practice, dialogicality, and relational attunement view learning as a socially mediated process through which people make meaning by listening and responding to the voices embedded in materials and their histories. I operationalized these ideas through the design of a  structured intervention that invited participants to engage dialogically with artifacts intentionally layered with the histories, relationships, and values of youth makers. Situated within a larger DBR partnership, this study was designed as an exploratory case to make dialogical engagement with artifacts visible under structured conditions.

## Context, setting, and research design

This study builds on an ongoing design-based research (DBR) partnership among community members, a community-based organization, a local museum, two school makerspaces, and my university. The partnership aims to create equitable learning environments by centering youth as co-designers of their learning. In an earlier phase, young people designed and built prototypes, journals, and 3D models envisioning a future museum makerspace that reflected their community values and personal aspirations  (Feng, 2024). These artifacts, layered with the youths’ histories, ideas, and social worlds, became the materials for the present study. Situated within an undergraduate  critical making and tinkering course at the university, this study extended the partnership’s inquiry by asking how learners understand the social meanings embedded in existing materials. Having witnessed the entire creation process of these artifacts and worked closely with the youth designers for a year, I sought to design a context that would allow me to observe how new participants build their own relationships with these materials —how they enter  intonew dialogues with artifacts already shaped by others’ histories and intentions.

As a guest instructor of the course, I facilitated a workshop that invited undergraduate students to encounter and respond to the youth-made materials as part of our collective exploration of what makes maker education “critical.” The session began with a short lecture introducing sociocultural and justice-oriented perspectives on making, followed by a 45-minute collaborative building activity. Students were shown a digital portfolio that I assembled from one of the youth design teams’ materials. The portfolio included everything the youth created—photos, videos, maps, and journals —but no narration or interpretive text from me, a deliberate design choice to preserve the youth’s voices. Eleven participants were asked to “recreate a model of the youth’s design that stays as close to the original design ideas as possible.” They worked in two teams using accessible materials (cardboard, Play-Doh, markers, tape, paper, LEGO pieces, etc.) and later shared reflections in a group dialogical interpretation analytically visible. At the same time, because they were junior-and senior-level education majors, their engagement also carried pedagogical significance for how emerging educators might learn to practice ways of seeing, listening, and responding that matter for justice-centered educational work. Data consisted of fieldnotes, video transcripts, and photographs documenting participants’ processes and products. I conducted participant observation while facilitating the session; the course instructor simultaneously recorded complementary fieldnotes from another vantage point. After class, we synthesized our notes and discussed emerging patterns. I conducted a brief member check with participants to confirm our initial interpretations of their actions and comments.

Data were analyzed through thematic coding (Miles et al., 2014). I first identified recurring moments in which participants’ talk and material actions reflected interpretive engagement with the artifacts, then traced how these interactions unfolded across turns of talk, gesture, and material manipulation to illuminate the dynamics of dialogical responsiveness  (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). The unit of analysis  was the dialogical sequence—moments of response between participants and the artifact. Each sequence captured how participants listened, interpreted, and replied to the artifact’s material cues  to reveal patterns of dialogical responsiveness and relational attunement. Because participants encountered artifacts carrying the layered traces of a real youth design process, the workshop offered an analytic window into how learners may build relationships with others’ making across contexts. “These must be toddler tables”: Dialogical responsiveness to contextual detail During the workshop, the artifacts’ voices were made audible through participants’ responsive listening—their capacity to notice, interpret, and materialize the youth designers’ intentions. These voices were carried through the design of the portfolio —it  preserved the youth’s layered authorship with photos, maps, models, and journals being presented together, retaining their messiness and overlapping contexts. The portfolio’s abundance encouraged multiple entry points and levels of interpretation, enabling artifacts to “speak” in their own ways. Throughout the session, participants listened to these voices through different strategies: some lingered over the youth’s handwritten journals to identify what mattered most to the makers, while other compared 2-D layouts and 3-D models to make sense of how design ideas evolved. The following episode from Group A captures one such moment of dialogical engagement.

(1) Artifact: The 3-D model on the screen shows a small cluster of tables

and chairs.

(2) Kim: (Rolling a lump of Play-Doh and pressing it flat, she begins

shaping miniature tables, matching colors to the photos of the 3-D model.)

(3) Betty: (Scrolls through the youth’s online portfolio, then pauses on

the journal images, running a finger over a highlighted line.)

(4) Artifact: Under “toddlers/younger kids section,” the journal page

reads, “There is a section for toddlers to stay & enjoy the makerspace w/ things appropriate for their ages.”

(5) Betty: How sweet! They planned a spot just for little kids! (Flips a

few pages until she reaches the 3-D model.)

(6) Artifact: In the 3-D model, the wall beside the tables is labeled “Kids.”

(7) Betty: These must be toddler tables— they should be short! (Taps on

Kim’s Play-Doh surface.)

(8) Kim: (Pinches the legs shorter with both thumbs.) Like this?

(9) Betty: Exactly. Wow! You think they’re really young?

(10) Kim: (Nods, still smoothing the miniature table.)

(11) Researcher: They’re middle-school students, actually, but they had design

meetings with families with little kids before making these.

(12) Betty: Ah, that explains it. It’s obvious they’ve hung out with kids.

In this sequence, the artifact initiates the dialogue: the youth’s highlighted journal line projects a n ethical stance about care for younger children. Betty’s recognition and vocalization (lines 5 to 7) re-animate that intention—she hears the artifact as a speaking partner, not as a static instruction. Kim’s embodied modification of the table (line

8) becomes a material reply, translating textual empathy into physical design. The conversation unfolds further

when Betty connects her interpretation to the youth’s lived experiences (lines 9, 12), extending the artifact’s voice beyond the page to the imagined people behind it.

Through this exchange, participants shifted  from decoding to responding. Their learning resided in attuning to the affective and social dimensions inscribed in the youth’s work. Responsive listening thus became both the process and the outcome of learning: participants learned to act with empathy toward the unseen makers by hearing and extending the voices embedded in their artifacts. In this way, the portfolio’s layered artifacts made the youth makers’ prior design reasoning available for new learners to interpret, extend, and respond to. “Let’s incorporate everything they envisioned”: Collective relational attunement Across teams, they found that the youth designers’ materials presented multiple and sometimes conflicting representations of the proposed makerspace. These mismatches posed a practical challenge for participants who were trying to reconstruct “the right” version. In response, one team’s work crystallized dialogicality at a  metalevel: they reframed the problem and negotiated shared interpretive norms about how to listen to the artifacts as expressions of the youth’s thinking.

Across the three episodes, dialogicality unfolded as a way of relating to the material world. Whether the artifacts spoke through abundance, contradiction, or silence, participants treated them as living interlocutors. Participants learned through dialogue with one another, the materials, and the traces of the youth makers’ intentions embedded in the materials.

This study extends prior work on dialogicality (Shotter, 2015) and relational attunement (Vossoughi et al., 2020) by showing how these practices can occur between people and artifacts  as participants’ dialogue with the artifacts constitute forms of  responsive understanding. They learned to listen for what the artifacts were saying, and keep listening when the message was partial or uncertain. Such responsiveness transforms making from a task of technical replication into an ethical and interpretive practice. The findings also echo Kabayadondo’s

(2016) argument that interaction with objects can load it with new assumptions about social relations. Here,

participants’ small design decisions  each reopened the social meanings that the youth had embedded in their artifacts. Through material engagement, learners reactivated histories of care, community, and labor that might otherwise have remained invisible. Working with a recognition of these sociocultural relations enabled participants to imagine new possible futures for the space and for themselves as makers. Learning, therefore, appeared as a capacity for dialogical responsiveness. Participants learned to approach material traces as invitations —t o hear, question, and extend the meanings others left behind. This capacity is central to equitable and justice-oriented making practices because it shifts attention from perfecting objects to understanding the relationships and histories they carry. Designing for learning in this way means creating conditions where materials retain their complexity, contradictions, and gaps, allowing learners to practice the difficult work of listening across difference, context, and silence. This study offers an analytic window into what a more expansive open-source pedagogy might make possible. In this view, “open source” does not refer only to making designs available for technical replication. It also means making available the layered traces of a design process —histories, intentions, and unresolved meanings—so that others can enter into dialogue with them.
