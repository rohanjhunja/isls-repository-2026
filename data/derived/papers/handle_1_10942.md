# Who Gains? Who Loses? STEM Outreach Facilitators Negotiating Power Relations While Expressing Their Critical Agency

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study followed and analyzed the practices of a group of young Arab instructors (YAIs) as they facilitated STEM outreach activities in Arab schools throughout Israel. Using the construct of Figured Worlds, it unpacks the social construction and negotiation of power relations in the classroom, highlights conflicts between the YAIs and teachers, and the ways the YAIs enacted their critical agency.

## Introduction

Many studies highlight the importance of dialogic classrooms in which students can collectively engage in critical thinking, reasoning, and discussions, while enacting their epistemic agency and  appropriate epistemic practices. However, the dominant positioning of teachers in the classroom often hinders the enactment of students' agency. We present preliminary findings from an ethnographi c study that followed a group of young Arab instructors (YAIs) in Israel (N=31), who facilitated STEM outreach activities in Arab schools around the country, reaching ~50,000 Arab students a year as part of th eir participation in a Gap-Year Program between their high school graduation and undergraduate work. The Gap-Year Program is run by Alrowad for Science and Technology (Alrowad for Science and Technology, 20 11). Alrowad is a grassroots non-profit organization founded in Israel in 2011 by a group of Arab scientists, engineers, a nd educators  that aims to improve science and technology education for Arab students  as a way to enhance the social and economic status of this community, which has strong roots in the  socio, historical and political contexts this community has experienced (Peleg & Waxman, 2011, pp. 19–46). Apart from working as instructors in the organization, the YAIs participated in a year-long (180 hours) professional development (PD), in which they are trained and mentored by core organization members in STEM content, practice, and pedagogy, as well as le adership and social awareness and activism. The YAIs are explicitly encouraged and guided to cultivate egali tarian and dialogic power relations with the studen ts in their outreach science activities, and nurture students’ enactments of epistemic agenc y. This approach often creates conflicts with prevalent school norms and power relations. Arab citizens (within the 1967 borders of Israel) form 21% of Israel's population. About 50% of this population lives below the poverty  line (Israel Central Bureau of Statistics, 2020). T his, t ogether with strong roots in a collectivist culture, as well as political and historical circumstances, have led to schools that mostly reflect hierarchical, authoritative power relations between teachers and students and teacher-centered instructional approaches (Dkeidek & Issa, 2021). Our working assumption was that the negotiation of often conflicting power rel ations between teachers and YAIs stems from the intersection of two distinct figured worlds of teaching and lear ning science.  A figured world is “a socially and culturally constructed realm of interpretation in which particular characters and actors are recognized, significance is assigned to certain acts, and particular outcomes are valued over others” (Holland et al., 1998, p. 52). The research questions were: (1) What is the nature of the power relations negotiate d during the outreach activities led by the YAIs in the presence of classroom teachers? (2) How do the YAIs negotiate their critical agency (i.e., their ability to foster critical awareness and engage in acts of social transformation (Basu et al., 2009)?

## Procedure

The data for the current paper were drawn from participatory observations during five full days of STEM outreach activities facilitated by the YAIs in schools during the 2022-2023 school year. The participants were 5 male and 8 female  YAIs, all Muslims, aged 18-19, from the same Arab town in northern Israel. Each observation was documented by a thick description that documented what the YAIs, the students, and the teachers were doing in each activity  using time stamps, the exact utterances of the YAIs, teachers, and the students, in which the participants addressed one another, expressed conflicts, etc... After each observation (~5-7 hours), the first author read her handwritten documentation, added clarifying information, and typed them into  a research journal that was uploaded to Atlas.ti. Episodes that reflected interactions between the YAIs and the teachers were marked for further analysis, which closely examined enactments and negotiations of power relations.

## Findings

We present an illustrative episode drawn from one of the sessions during a science day at an elementary school located in an Arab village. The school mainly serves a Muslim, low-SES population. The activity took place in a K6 classroom (27 students). The students were engaged in a hands-on activity (1.5 hours), in groups. Each student received a kit of a model catapult, built a catapult that could be  taken home after the activity, and conducted guided experiments to understand its mechanism. Heba (pseudonym, a first-year female YAI) led the activity, and the classroom teacher was present. The classroom teacher occasionally left the classroom for long periods of time. The students were immersed in the activity. Some stood, others were sitting, talking to each other as they worked. Heba moved between tables, helping the students build the catapult, and conduct a series of experiments with the device, occasionally showing relevant video clips, explaining, and asking questions. About 45 minutes after the session started, when the classroom teacher was not present, another teacher entered the classroom. She noticed the laid-back atmosphere, got angry, and started to scold the students loudly: "Do not take any more ((pieces)). Only one piece of wood. Do not take any more. Sit in your chairs. Why are you standing?". Heba moved to the front of the class, saying: “My dear students, let’s continue the experiment. I will give you the next set of instructions.” The work continued. Heba noticed two students who were not working and were disturbing others. She approached them and asked for their assistance in distributing the equipment. They complied and stopped bothering others. The teacher, however, scolded them as they distributed the equipment: “Come on, count ((the equipment)) before you distrib ute. Get going! Sit down!” Instantly, Heba again moved to the front of the class, and addressed the students collectively, providing guiding instructions for a series of experiments with the catapult. After three similar attempts by the teacher,  the teacher moved near the door and stood next to it, chatting with a colleague until the end of the activity.

## Discussion and conclusion

The teacher was interpreting the situation through the figured world of a traditional classroom, in which students must sit quietly and speak only when authorized by the teacher. By contrast, Heba was acting within a reformed figured world of teaching in which the students are autonomous and participate actively in the learning process. Heba had tonegotiate power relations with the teacher and assert her leadership role if she wanted to facilitate the activity as she believed it should be  done. She expressed her resistance in three ways: (1) moving to  the front of the class, to the “teacher ’s place” in the classroom; (2) addressing the students quietly and respectfu lly, in clear opposition to the teacher’s scolding; and (3) continuing to facilitate her planned activity. Her treatment o f the students who interrupted the lesson flow  by asking for their assistance instead of scolding them adhered to a pedagogy that aim s to empower students rather than control them. We consider Heba’s active resistance, her insistence on claiming (and reclaiming) control over the activity, and the explicitly respectful way she approached the students as enactments of critical agency.  These moves allowed her to facilitate a lesson according to her ideals. However, her resistance strategy could not have created a lasting effect in this case since the teacher was alienated. Indeed, the teacher stepped aside, effectively allowing Heba to run the activity as she wanted. However, her moving to the door and her constant chatting with other colleagues until the end of the activity communicated that she was not part of the activity and that this was not “real” schoolwork.
