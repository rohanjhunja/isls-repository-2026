# Conceptualizations and Mechanisms of Teachers’ Learning to Notice

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This symposium brings together a set of posters that focus on science and mathematics teachers’ learning to notice. Teacher noticing is a core construct of teaching, and refers to teachers’ attention, interpretation, and decision-making amidst the “blooming, buzzing confusion” of classr oom interactions. The goal of the session is to examine different ways “learning to notice” is conceptualized and unpack mechanisms that drive teachers’ learning to notice. Symposium overview: Goal, structure, and highlights Teacher noticing, a core construct of teaching (König et al., 2022; Weston & Amador, 2023), broadly refers to teachers’ attention, interpretation, and decision-making of classroom interactions (Jacobs et al., 2010; Sherin et al., 2011). With the growing recognition of noticing as a core practice of teaching, there is an expanding line of research that seeks to understand how to support teachers’ learning to notice (Gaudin & Chaliès, 2015; Santagata et al., 2021). Prior research shows that well-designed professional learning opportunities can foster teachers’ learning to notice students’ thinking and teaching in interaction with student learning (e.g., Tekkumru-Kisa et al., 2018; Warshauer et al., 2023). As this knowledge has grown, it has prompted scholars to investigate what constitutes learning of noticing. In this symposium, we pause and ask: How do we think about what constitutes learning to notice? While efforts to support teachers’ learning to notice is evolving, less is understood about the mechanisms of teacher learning to notice (Walkoe & Luna, 2020). Some research investigates how video tagging tools focus teachers’ attention on particular features for further analysis (e.g., Walkoe et al., 2020). Others have considered how different kinds of video clips are selected and situated in professional learning and how collaborative analysis of particular types of clips can shape teachers’ learning (e.g., Sherin & Dyer, 2017). Recent work has pointed to the role of discourse as a mechanism of teachers’ learning to notice (van Es et al., 2024). However, there is more to be understood about how teachers come to notice complexities of teaching and learning, and designs to better 2318 support that. Going beyond the descriptive account of noticing, in this symposium, our work collectively explores: What drives teachers’ learning to notice? All in all, the goal of our session is to examine different ways “learning to notice” has been conceptualized, and unpack the "mechanisms" that drive teachers’ learning to notice. Using multiple methods such as participatory design research approach and typological analysis of artifacts of teaching, these studies document shifts in teachers’ learning to notice, factors that contribute to teachers’ learning of noticing, and the design choices in facilitating teachers’ learning this complex practice. T he studies identify mechanisms of teachers’ learning to notice such as coding schemes that serve as epistemic mechanisms and collective sensemaking of the complexity of teaching through analysis of classroom videos in professional learning contexts. While some papers zoom in on noticing subprocesses that different video processes afford, others seek to understand teachers’ learning to notice when teachers worked on making links to socio-ecological issues relevant to their lives. Together, these papers advance theory on teachers’ learning to notice, while also considering the situated and relational features of teacher learning. At the same time, they offer methodological tools and frameworks to advance research on learning to notice across math and science. This 90-minute structured poster session will consist of a brief introduction to the session by the session co-chairs (5 min), followed by the interactive discussion of 10 posters (60 min), 10-minutes of commentary from a discussant, Adam Lefstein from the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, who brings expertise on teachers’ learning in professional development. We will conclude with 15-minutes of questions and discussion. The discussant will consider commonalities and differences across these studies, surfacing the considerations and tensions for supporting teachers’ learning to notice and mechanisms that drive learning of noticing among math and science teachers across the trajectory of teaching careers. This exploratory study used a participatory design research approach within an intensive professional learning context. Data collection utilized standardized noticing tasks, complemented by reflective journals documenting participants’ evolving interpretive frameworks. Analysis combined quantitative tracking of orientation patterns with qualitative mapping of transformative processes. Analysis revealed three primary mechanisms driving teachers’ learning to notice strengths. Participants demonstrated systematic shifts from deficit-based toward strength-based noticing practices. Second, collaborative meaning-making facilitated frame shifting through structured protocols that created social spaces for negot iating alternative interpretations of student thinking. Third, reflective reframing supported progressive integration of strength-based orientations through systematic comparisons of deficit-based and asset-oriented perspectives. The iterative design revealed how these mechanisms operated synergistically, with cognitive restructuring mediated by socio-cultural practices of professional discourse and collective analysis. By functioning simultaneously as analytical tools and pedagogical scaffolds, structured coding schemes support the cognitive work of frame identification while creating social spaces for collaborative meaning-making. This dual functionality bridges individual cognitive transformation and collective professional development, revealing how participatory desi gn research can systematically develop teachers’ capacity for strength-based noticing. The study contributes methodological insights for designing equity-oriented professional learning experiences that leverage the sophisticated interplay between cognitive and socio-cultural dimensions of transformative practice. Poster 5: Attending, interpreting, and shaping: How varied ways of working with video connect with subprocesses of teacher noticing Jennifer Richards, Miriam Sherin, Sarah Larison 2322 Research has documented how video-based environments can provide rich opportunities for teachers’ learning, including learning to notice student thinking (e.g., Gaudin & Chaliès, 2015; Santagata et al., 2021). Further, advancements in video-related technology have expanded how teachers can engage with video — moving beyond watching and discussing videos to filming and editing their own clips. In this poster, we extend a prior exploration of how teachers noticed student thinking as they engaged in multiple video processes (Richards & Sherin, 2025) by specifying ways in which these video processes map onto posited subprocesses of teacher noticing, contributing to understandings of potential drivers for noticing. The framework we animate specifies several interrelated subprocesses of teacher noticing — attending, interpreting, and shaping (van Es & Sherin, 2021). “Attending” refers to what teachers focus on in classrooms, acknowledging that humans cannot pay attention to everything. “Interpreting” refers to how teachers reason about classroom events. “Shaping” refers to what teachers do in order to support their noticing during or after instruction. While these subprocesses dynamically interact with and influence one another, we think they also reflect important opportunities for learning in their own right. Data come from three iterations of an online, video-based course for early elementary teachers in the United States on mathematical argumentation (Lomax et al., 2017). Twenty-nine in-service teachers participated in the course, in which they engaged in four sequential video processes — recording video in their own classrooms, watching and reflecting on the video, editing clips to share in the course, and interacting with others around their clips. Data sources included interviews and think-alouds with teachers about these video processes and video annotations on clips. Our prior analysis explored the affordances and constraints of each video process for teachers’ noticing of student thinking (Richards & Sherin, 2025). Here, we build on this prior analysis to characterize the noticing subprocesses that different video processes seemed to afford. When teachers recorded video in their classrooms, all three noticing subprocesses seemed to be in play as teachers iteratively focused on student ideas that required attention, sought to make sense of them in real time, and shaped interactions to test working interpretations and gather more information. Watching and reflecting on the video tended to afford continued “interpreting” and “shaping,” as teachers primarily used the video as a tool to make sense of and gain more evidence about student ideas that had intrigued them. Editing clips, in contrast, afforded “attending” with some further “interpreting” as teachers selectively identified moments of student thinking to make visible to colleagues. Finally, when interacting with others around clips, all three subprocesses were again in play as teachers highlighted specific student ideas to consider, discussed varied interpretations, and at times reviewed the video to seek more information. These findings suggest potential implications for targeted video-based designs to facilitate specific noticing subprocesses (e.g., cultivating teachers’ attending by providing multiple opportunities to edit clips). Further research could deepen understandings of how engaging with varied sequences of video processes drives teachers’ learning to notice over time. Poster 10: A learning to notice for equity framework Jose Mendez, Elizabeth A. van Es Descriptive accounts of teachers’ noticing for equity contribute to theorizing the complexity of teachers’ in the moment attention and sensemaking to advance responsive and equitable instruction (e.g. Louie et al., 2021; van Es et al., 2022). What is less understood is how teachers learn to notice for equity. Teacher noticing for equity is characterized by teachers ’ attention to multiple dimensions of the classroom environment including linguistic resources, power dynamics, relational interactions, social i dentities, and sociopolitical narratives (van Es et al., 2022). We theorize that learning to attend to equity involves the ability to name these classroom dynamics and see them as interconnected (e.g., Rubin & van Es, 2025). The second dimension involves the development of an interpretive practice, which entails continuously engaging with evidence to better understand and support students’ needs (Sherin & Russ, 2017). The third dimension involves embodied engagement, drawing on recent noticing-as-embodied c onceptualizations that highlight the nuanced and multi-sensory aspects of noticing (Mason, 2002; Scheiner, 2021). Embodied engagement of one’s noticing includes both actions, such as shaping, to position oneself to notice more expansively, and choosing to ignore particular aspects of classroom interactions that are less generative for student learning (Louie et al., 2021; van Es et al., 2022). Embodied engagement that disrupts inequities is a reflection of developing one’s noticing for equity (Scheiner, 2021). We qualitatively analyzed data from a research practice partnership that explored whether and how co-designing and analyzing practical measurement data could provoke a mathematics teacher’s learning to notice for equity (Mendez & van Es, 2025). We adopt sociopolitical perspectives to theorize teachers’ learning to notice as tied to their personal histories and the power-laden environments they teach in (Louie et al., 2021). Symposium overview: Goal, structure, and highlights Teacher noticing, a core construct of teaching (König et al., 2022; Weston & Amador, 2023), broadly refers to teachers’ attention, interpretation, and decision-making of classroom interactions (Jacobs et al., 2010; Sherin et al., 2011). With the growing recognition of noticing as a core practice of teaching, there is an expanding line of research that seeks to understand how to support teachers’ learning to notice (Gaudin & Chaliès, 2015; Santagata et al., 2021). Prior research shows that well-designed professional learning opportunities can foster teachers’ learning to notice students’ thinking and teaching in interaction with student learning (e.g., Tekkumru-Kisa et al., 2018; Warshauer et al., 2023). As this knowledge has grown, it has prompted scholars to investigate what constitutes learning of noticing. In this symposium, we  pause and ask: How do we think about what constitutes learning to notice?

While efforts to support teachers’ learning to notice is evolving, less is understood about the mechanisms of teacher learning to notice (Walkoe & Luna, 2020). Some research investigates how video tagging tools focus teachers’ attention on particular features for further analysis (e.g., Walkoe et al., 2020). Others have considered how different kinds of video clips are selected and situated in professional learning and how collaborative analysis of particular types of clips can shape teachers’ learning (e.g., Sherin & Dyer, 2017). Recent  work has pointed to the role of discourse as a mechanism of teachers’ learning to notice (van Es et al., 2024). However, there is more to be understood about how teachers come to notice complexities of teaching and learning, and designs to better 2318 support that. Going beyond the descriptive account of noticing, in this symposium, our work collectively explores: What drives teachers’ learning to notice?

All in all, the goal of our session is to examine different ways “learning to notice” has been conceptualized, and unpack the "mechanisms" that drive teachers’ learning to notice. Using multiple methods such as participatory  design research approach and typological analysis of artifacts of teaching, these studies document shifts in teachers’ learning to notice, factors that contribute to teachers’ learning of noticing, and the design choices in facilitating teachers’ learning this complex practice. T he studies identify mechanisms of teachers’ learning to notice such as coding schemes that serve as epistemic mechanisms and collective sensemaking of the complexity of teaching through analysis of classroom videos in professional learning contexts. While some papers zoom in on  noticing subprocesses that different video processes afford, others seek to understand teachers’ learning to notice when teachers worked on making links to socio-ecological issues relevant to their lives.  Together, these papers advance theory on teachers’ learning to notice, while also considering the situated and relational features of teacher learning. At the same time, they offer methodological tools and frameworks to advance research on learning to notice across math and science. Two coders coded every response, and discrepancies were resolved.

We highlight three findings: 1) Cases support PSTs to attend to details about students and their actions,

2) Cases and prompts can be designed to support PSTs to make wide-ranging equity-oriented interpretations, and

3) Case prompts elicit solutions that include in-the-moment actions, changes for future instruction, and advocacy-based actions including gathering data and research. We offer frequency counts across the dataset, PST quotes, and case comparisons. As an example, we explore how a subtle difference in prompts influenced PSTs’ breadth of interpretations in two similar cases focused on group dynamics: In one case, 83% of responses offering equityoriented interpretations focused on a general imbalance in group dynamics; in the other, PSTs reasoned about a general imbalance in group dynamics (39%), as well as the roles of friendship (46%), language (43%), prior school experiences (24%), and gender (15%).

Cases have significant potential for supporting PSTs’ noticing for equity including details of specific students’ participation (Wager, 2014) and more critical dimensions of equity (Graham & Roth McDuffie, 2023). Poster 10: A learning to notice for equity framework Jose Mendez, Elizabeth A. van Es Descriptive accounts of teachers’ noticing for equity contribute to theorizing the complexity of teachers’ in the moment attention and sensemaking to advance responsive and equitable instruction (e.g. Louie et al., 2021; van Es et al., 2022). What is less understood is how teachers learn  to notice for equity. Teacher noticing for equity is characterized by teachers ’ attention to multiple dimensions of the classroom environment including linguistic resources, power dynamics, relational interactions, social i dentities, and sociopolitical narratives (van Es et al., 2022). We theorize that learning to attend to equity involves the ability to name these classroom dynamics and see them as interconnected (e.g., Rubin & van Es, 2025). The second dimension involves the development of an interpretive practice, which entails continuously engaging with evidence to better understand and support students’ needs (Sherin & Russ, 2017). The third dimension involves embodied engagement, drawing on recent noticing-as-embodied c onceptualizations that highlight the nuanced and multi-sensory aspects of noticing (Mason, 2002; Scheiner, 2021). Embodied engagement of one’s noticing includes both actions, such as shaping, to position oneself to notice more expansively, and choosing to ignore particular aspects of classroom interactions that are less generative for student learning (Louie et al., 2021; van Es et al., 2022). Embodied engagement that disrupts inequities is a reflection of developing one’s noticing for equity (Scheiner, 2021). We qualitatively analyzed data from a research practice partnership that explored whether and how co-designing and analyzing practical measurement data could provoke a mathematics teacher’s learning to notice for equity (Mendez & van Es, 2025). We adopt sociopolitical perspectives to theorize teachers’ learning to notice as tied to their personal histories and the power-laden environments they teach in (Louie et al., 2021). We also theorize learning as a progression that can support teachers and teacher educators formatively documenting changes in teachers’ noticing over time (e.g., van Es, 2011). Data included transcripts of co-design meetings to develop a survey as a form of practical measurement data and data discussions of iterative cycles of survey implementation.

This poster will present a framework for learning to notice for equity that consists of four levels. Each level is characterized by shifts in noticing along the dimensions of attending to equity, interpretive practice, and embodied engagement. As the case study teacher shifted in his noticing for equity, he came to see noticing as multidimensional and integrated, shifting frames to interpret events from an asset-and evidence-based stance. He also made connections to his practice and considered how to disrupt persistent inequities to create a responsive classroom through shaping noticing interactions focused on students’ ideas and patterns of participation. This framework moves from descriptive accounts of teacher noticing for equity to characterize the prog ression of learning to noticing from an equity lens over time. In doing so, this tool offers a framework to study teachers’ 2326 learning to notice for equity, while also providing guidance for designing experiences to support teachers’ expansive noticing.
