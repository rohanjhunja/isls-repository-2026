# AI-augmented Thinking: Exploring Human-AI Interaction in Generative AI-supported Programming Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAI) presents remarkable prospects for education, but its impact on students' thinking processes remains largely unexplored. This research explores the thinking processes of 20 students when programming supported by GAI. Twenty participants recorded hour-long programming sessions with GAI and submitted reflection reports on key interactions and challenges. We developed an AI-augmented thinking framework based on the screen recordings (including chat history with GAI). In reflection reports, students identified prompt skills, programming knowledge, and debugging skills as key factors influencing AI-augmented thinking in programming. This study contributes to the theoretical and practical understanding of students’ thinking processes when supported by GAI.

## Objectives and significance

ChatGPT and similar generative AI (GAI) technologies are transforming education by enhancin g teaching and self-directed learning (Zhu et al., 2023). Despite the benefits, they also bring risks related to academic integrity and overreliance on AI. To make good use of AI in education, students must develop an appropriate mindset that GAI should be used to augment rather thandiminish their thinking. In programming education, while AI tools promote students' computational thinking by assisting with programming  tasks and providing personalized instruction (Yilmaz & Karaoglan Yilmaz, 2023), the in-depth analysis of learners' thinking processes rema ins rare. Scholars have constructed  new interpretations of the cognitive process in the  context of AI-enhanced environments, such as hybrid intelligence (Dellermann et al., 2019), augmented intelligence (Sharma, 2019), and hybrid-augmented intelligence (Zheng et al., 2017). However, most above terms remain theoretical, with limited empirical evidence. T his study aims to investigate  and construct a framework of  student thinking processes in programming learning with GAI's support. We aim to address the following research questions (RQs): RQ1: What characterizes student thinking processes in programming tasks with GAI, as demonstrated by their programming behaviours and interactive discourses with GAI? RQ2: What factors influence students’ thinking process in programming tasks with GAI based on their own reflections?

## Participants

To address the research questions, we recruited 20 students from a university in Singapore in the January Semester of 2024. To enrich the representative of participants, 10 participants (4 males and 6 females) were from an undergraduate course on Engineering Computation  in engineering major, and 10 (1 male and 9 females)  were from a master course on Learning Analytics  in education major. Students in both classes began learning programming less than a year in their program.

## Data collection and analysis

The students participated in a 60-minute programming session after classes, during wh ich they conducted individual programming activities with the support of GAI tools like ChatGPT. Participants were requir ed to screen-record their behaviours in the integrated development environments (IDE) and discourses in GAI tools and reflect on their experience using GAI afterward s. For the undergraduate students, the programming act ivity was designed by the research team and adapted from Exer cism (https://exercism.org/). Students were required to use Python to accomplish tasks such as linear search, bubble sorting, class creation, and text-based user interface. For the master students, the programming activity was a n assignment about data analysis using R. They were  free to select and analyse data to answer an educational research question the y were interested in.  In the end, all 20 participants submitted the screen recordings, and 16 of them provided the reflection reports. To answer RQ1, we adapted qualitative video analysis to analyse the 20 participants' screen recordings. The first author watched the screen recordings, generating codes regarding students' behaviour in programming IDE and discourses with GAI in order to draft a coding scheme. The coding scheme includes the questions asked, the context in which the questions were asked (e.g., while programming or studying new concepts), and how students evaluated and integrated the feedback from  GAI into their work. Using the coding scheme, anot her researcher coded a one-hour screen recording independently, evaluated their  comprehensiveness and appropriateness, and provided suggestions for revis ing it. Then the research team iteratively refined the coding scheme through discussion and consensus-building.

To answer RQ2, content analyses were conducted on 16 students' reflection reports, which included their responses to the questions including their expectations for GAI, GAI's benefit  and limitations, their future plan for using GAI. The content analyses followed a systematic process to identify common themes and patterns across the reflections.

## Results and discussion

In total, 2,297 events/codes were generated. Ground ed in the empirical data and content analys is results, by categorizing key activities and defining distinct phases, an AI-augmented thinking framework was developed. AIaugmented thinking describes an individual's competence to integrate AI with their own thinking by appropriately using AI, critically evaluating AI-generated answers, and applying AI outputs responsi bly. As a result, the final coding scheme has four main coding categories: (1) Question Formulation: humans describe their questio ns to generative AI tools. (2) Solution Development: huma ns and/or AI generate the solutions. (3) Solution A nalysis and Evaluation: humans evaluate AI-generated solutions and find their weaknesses and g aps; (4) Solution Refinement: humans revise the solutions by synthesizing human and AI input. Based on students' reflection reports, we found participants raised three factors that influenced their AIaugmented thinking process: prompting skills, progr amming knowledge, and debugging skills. First, 8 participants thought the literacy to prompt a good question is important to Question Formulation. RES3  argued that "The key was to prompt the GAI carefully, i.e., rather than pasting in the task and copy-pasting the resulting code". However, the ability to ask good questions often re quires programming kn owledge. Three participants found they lacked essential programming knowledge to well prompt ChatGPT. For instance, MSLS9 thought "This is due to my lack of background knowledge, wh ich leads to imprecise prompt writing". Programming knowledge is also important to Solution Development and Solution Analysis and Evaluation. RES12 pointed out that "When the outcome was not desired, GAI could not re-evaluate the code it wrote and make the corresponding changes. It would still require a human to do so". Nine participants highlighted that debugging is important to Solution Refinement. When debugging, students found  that "trying to debug the problem with the GAI usu ally does not work either (RES9)." Based on participants' reflections, although GAI ca n decrease the fear of programming, foundational programming knowledge rem ains crucial for effective Question Formulation, Solution Development, Solution Analysis and Evaluat ion, and Solution Refinement. lights the importance of balancing human agency and AI in learning while avoiding overreliance on AI.
