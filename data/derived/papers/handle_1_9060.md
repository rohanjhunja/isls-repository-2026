# Exploring the Diagnostic Process of Pre-Service Teachers Using a Simulation – A Latent Profile Approach

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Assessing individual students’ learning progress is a key task in teachers’ everyday life, which allows them to infer students’ zone of proximal development and offer adaptive support. Because of the complexity of the diagnosing in classroom settings, training pre-service teachers using standardized settings with adaptive support seems desirable. However, to design effective learning environments and offer adaptive support, a deep understanding of the diagnostic process is necessary. Thus, the present study investigates the diagnostic process of pre-service teachers in a video-based simulation by examining the occurrence of diagnostic activities and their relation to relevant learner prerequisites and learners’ diagnostic outcome. Findings suggest that describing and evaluating simulated students’ learning process is sufficient for an accurate diagnosis. However, only students with higher professional knowledge made further-reaching pedagogical decisions. Moreover, we identified three profiles representing pre-service teachers’ distinct engagement in diagnostic activities and suggest the implantation of different types of scaffolding.

## Introduction

Assessing individual students’ learning progress is  a key task in teachers’ everyday life. For instanc e, teachers gather information about students’ current understa nding of a concept or method, about students’ learn ing prerequisites in general, or about their current le arning processes. This information enables teachers  to infer students’ learning status and their zone of proximal development (Vygotsky, 1980), which in turn allows them to make adaptive pedagogical decisions, for instance, by adapting the conduct of a lesson, by scaffolding individual students’ learning processes (Reiser & Tabak, 2014; Wood et al., 1976), or by giving individual recommendations for further training (e.g., Südkamp et al., 2012; U rhahne & Wijnia, 2021). Thus, teachers’ ability to accurately diagnose students’ learning progress is vital for teaching quality and has been shown to positively impact student learning (e.g., Brunner et al., 2013; vande Pol et  al., 2013). Yet, prior research has also shown tha t teachers in general and novice teachers in particular, often struggle when diagnosing (e.g., Urhahne & Wijnia, 2021). Based on novice teachers’ struggles and their so-called practice shock, there has been a call for a ‘practice turn’ in teacher education. Consequently, researche rs as well as practitioners have emphasized the nee d to implement authentic learning opportunities, often called approximations of practice (Grossman et al., 2009), for pre-service teachers in higher education. Simulatio ns have been proposed as one such means, as they al low to offer a balance between cognitive demand and authen ticity (Codreanu et al., 2020). The general idea of simulations is that learners can engage in authenti c situations that represent real world demands and by that develop complex skills, which are based on and at t he same time exceed their prior knowledge acquired during their studies. Moreover, such simulations allow to adapt the complexity and course of the simulation b ased on learners’ individual learner prerequisites and proc esses. Thus, simulations allow for individualizing learning processes and for implementing adaptive learner sup port, which allows increased learning effects (Cher nikova, Heitzmann, Stadler, et al., 2020; Radkowitsch et al., 2021). Although adaptivity as a core feature of learning environments is not a new concept (see Plass & Pawa r, 2020) and contingency as a key aspect of scaffol ding is based on adaptivity, implementing adaptive support in scalable learning environments such as simulatio ns requires a deep understanding of i) participants’ learner prerequisites (e.g., Kramer et al., 2021) and ii) participants’ cognitive processes within such learning environmen ts (Stadler et al., 2019). With respect to diagnosi ng, most research has focused on outcomes of a diagnostic process (mostly diagnostic accuracy) (Urhahne & Wijnia, 2021) and the influence of various covariates such as content knowledge (CK) or pedagogical content knowledge (PCK) (e.g., Brunner et al., 2013; Kramer et al., 2021). However, participants’ diagnostic process in such s imulations, differences between their individual diagnostic process as well as the relation to various prerequisites are not yet well understood.

Results not only give novel insights into pre-service teachers’ diagnosti c process within simulations, but also provide essential info rmation for adapting such simulations to learners’ needs, for example by implementing contingent scaffolding (see further Plass & Pawar, 2020). Diagnostic skills and diagnostic process Educational assessment is a core task for teachers and is seen as an essential prerequisite for indivi dual support and adaptive teaching (Reuker et al., 2021). Although different terms are used to describe educational assessment and corresponding skills (e.g., diagnostic competence (Heitzmann et al., 2019; Herppich et al., 2018), assessment literacy (Xu & Brown, 2016)), conceptions agree that diagnosing involves gathering and integrating information in a goal-directed way in order to reduce uncertain ty with respect to a professional decision (Heitzma nn et al., 2019). The central indicator for teachers’ diagnost ic skills has long been the accuracy of the diagnos is, operationalized as the extent to which a teacher’s judgement matches a (more or less) objective measure (e.g., by using standardized tests or expert ratings) of a student’s true performance (Südkamp et al., 2012). Lately, research has shifted the focus on the diagn ostic process (e.g., Herppich et al., 2018) as its understanding generally is an important requirement for the implementation of instructional support (Engelmann & Bannert, 2021). There are different frameworks describing the diagnostic process and different activities therein. For example, the diagnostic process was described b ased on frameworks for teachers’ professional visio n (Goodwin, 1994; Seidel & Stürmer, 2014), in which i mportant cues of a specific diagnostic situation ar e first noticed and then processed internally to arrive at a diagnosis and an educational decision (Loibl et al., 2020). The professional vision paradigm assumes that for the deep processing of the noticed cues, teachers need to describe the specific information, explain the described inf ormation by linking it to professional concepts and  finally predict students’ learning by drawing conclusions (Seidel & Stürmer, 2014). Similar activities were described by Blömeke et al. (2015) for the conceptualization of their so-called situation-specific skills and for t he conceptualization of scientific reasoning by Fischer et al. (2014). In an attempt to unite these frameworks in the context of diagnosing, Codreanu et al. (2021) proposed four observable diagnostic activities: describing (verbalize noticed cues or information), evaluating (inferring a general pattern of behavior of the student), explaining (linking the respective information to professional  concepts), and decision-making (drawing conclusions about educational actions). Despite these numerous, mostl y overlapping conceptualizations of the diagnostic process, little is known about teachers’ engagement in different activities during diagnosing, about individual or situationspecific factors determining the engagement in specific activities, or about the relation between these diagnostic activities as process measures and outcome measures such as diagnostic accuracy. Learning with simulations Simulations are representations of real-world situations that allow learners to engage in relevant activities for the specific situation (Gegenfurtner et al., 2014). Thu s, simulations allow learners to apply knowledge to  realistic cases in standardized settings and by that to acquire practice-oriented knowledge (Kolodner, 1992). Additionally, simulations allow learners to focus on specific professional skills and to learn in a risk-free environment. A crucial point is that simulations reduce complexity of a sp ecific task for training, but at the same time appr oximate practice (Codreanu et al., 2020; Grossman et al., 2009). There is a vast amount of research showing the general effectiveness of simulations for learning but also a large amount of unexplained variance (e.g., Chern ikova, Heitzmann, Stadler, et al., 2020; Gegenfurtner et al., 2014). For example, Chernikova, Heitzmann, Stadler,  et al. (2020) found positive effects of simulation s on learning of complex skills such as diagnostic skill s in their meta-analysis. Effects of simulations we re higher if learners received additional instructional support during learning, yet the type of instructional support interacted with learners’ prior knowledge. Thus, adapting inst ructional support to learners’ prerequisites and/or  learning process seems beneficial (Plass & Pawar, 2020; Radk owitsch et al., 2021). This, however, requires a de ep understanding of the cognitive processes and of learner prerequisites that might influence the cognitive process. Beside their general positive effect on learning, simulations (and especially digital simulations) additionally allow to document cognitive processes in detail, for instance, based on logfiles or notes. The documentation of learning processes allows research ers to infer on and analyze cognitive processes tha t are involved during solving the task (Greiff et al., 2015), which is important for gaining a better understanding of the concept under research, e.g., diagnostic skills. Moreover, simulations allow to standardize the simulated situation ICLS2022 Proceedings 568 © ISLS in a way that reduces the possible influence of con founding variables (Herborn et al., 2018). This see ms particularly important in situations in which interaction between persons is simulated (e.g., interaction between a teacher and a student) and which are as “messy” as teaching situations in regular classrooms. For inst ance, the motivational or affective state of an unstandardize d student could affect a teacher’s judgement of the  students’ learning processes. Keeping constant the motivational or affective state of students during a training session in a simulation might thus help pre-service teachers wit h little experience to focus on the relevant aspect s of the diagnosis.

Influence of relevant learner prerequisites Learner prerequisites are likely to influence diagnostic processes as well as outcomes within a simula tion (e.g., Heitzmann et al., 2019; Kramer et al., 2021). There fore, investigating how learner prerequisites affec t the diagnostic process as well as and outcomes seems important when investigating diagnostic skills. So far, research on teacher education has largely focused on the role of professional knowledge. Particularly, CK, that is subject knowledge about facts, concepts, and methods, and PCK, that is knowledge about how to teach a specific content, taking into account subject-specific (mis-)conceptions and instructional strategies (Shulman, 1986) seems to be predictive of teaching quality and learning of stud ents (Baumert & Kunter, 2013). Less evidence exists  for the role of professional knowledge for diagnosing (see  Förtsch et al., 2018). Kramer et al. (2021) invest igated the influence of professional knowledge on the diagnost ic process and the diagnostic outcome in a simulati on and found that PCK enhances both, diagnostic process and the diagnostic outcome, but CK does not. Besides professional knowledge, Heitzmann et al. (2 019) suggest a number of other factors possibly influencing the diagnostic process and outcome such  as interest (e.g., Rotgans & Schmidt, 2014), self-efficacy (e.g., Klug et al., 2016), or motivation (e.g., Wig field & Eccles, 2000), which may be especially impo rtant in simulations as learning environments. As evidence of the impact of these learner prerequisites on the diagnostic process and outcomes is sparse and relevant learner  prerequisites might be target of specific interven tions, identifying prerequisites that affect how pre-service teachers engage in diagnostic activities seems relevant.

## Research questions

Based on the prior research presented above, the present study investigates diagnostic skills of pre-service teachers in a video-based simulation by examining the occurr ence of diagnostic activities and their relation to  relevant learner prerequisites and diagnostic outcome. More precisely, the following research questions are addressed: (1) To what extent are pre-service teachers’ diagnostic activities interrelated and related to their diagnostic outcome as well as their learner prerequisites? (2) Do pre-service teachers systematically differ in the extent to which they engage in different diagnostic activities? (3) To w hat extent do pre-service teachers with distinct pr ofiles differ with respect to their diagnostic outcome and learner prerequisites?

## Sample and procedure

We used data from two studies (NStudy1 = 60, NStudy2 = 84) that investigated pre-service mathematics te achers’ diagnosing. Data of 26 participants was excluded since the data coding process of Study 2 was not yet finalized. The included sample consisted of 118 pre-service ma thematics teachers (Nfemale = 76) from five German universities. Their age ranged from 18 to 43 years (Mage = 23.32, SDage = 3.59). In both studies, all participants first completed a questionnaire and test assessing their cognitive and motivational-affective learner prerequisites. Afterwards, participants diagnosed two simulated students’ mathematical argumentation and proof skills  using the video-based simulation.

Video-based simulation In the simulation, participants acted as intern who  was asked to diagnose two students with respect to  their mathematical argumentation skills in the context of a geometry proof. For that, pre-service teachers watched short, staged video clips about one-on-one teacher-student  interactions in which the teacher prompted the stu dent to share their thoughts about the task. Meanwhile, par ticipants were asked to take notes relevant for the  given diagnostic task. All notes were used to examine par ticipants’ diagnostic activities. After each clip, participants could decide on whether they wanted to further obse rve the respective student by watching another vide o clip. Participants could watch up to 10 videos. Once part icipants had decided to stop observing the teacher-student interaction, they were asked to rate students’ mathematical content knowledge, methodological knowledge, and problem-solving strategies. For more information about the simulation, see Codreanu et al. (2021). ICLS2022 Proceedings 569 © ISLS Instruments In both studies, we assessed participants’ diagnostic activities and diagnostic accuracy based on notes and ratings generated during the interaction with the simulatio n. Moreover, we used a self-generated test to asses s CK and PCK as well as a questionnaire to assess interest f or diagnosing, for geometry, and for mathematics ed ucation, their self-efficacy as well as their motivation focusing on the perceived task value and their expectancy to succeed. The diagnostic activities were assessed based on the notes taken by the participants during watching the videos. Notes were segmented content wise into sense units. We coded whether each segment contained on e or more of the diagnostic activities describing, evalu ating, explaining, and decision-making based on Cod reanu et al. (2021). Describing was coded when participants described observations  of actions (e.g., “She used the characteristics of angles in parallel lines without  being able to name them explicitly”). Evaluating was coded when participants inferred levels of knowledge or p atterns of behavior (e.g., “He has very big problem s with mathematical terms and concepts”). Explaining was coded when participants named reasons for the students’ actions or their level of knowledge (e.g., “Her methodological knowledge is already quite good since she tries to use the angles to establish a proof.”). Decision-making was coded when participants made recommendations for further pedagogical actions (e.g., “In order to imp rove the understanding, the basic characteristics o f the congruence theorems and quadrangles must be deepened.”). For each simulated student, counts for each activity were summed up for all notes taken during the simulation. We calculated the mean across both simulated students. We used the final ratings of the simulated students ’ mathematical content knowledge, methodological knowledge, and problem-solving strategies as indica tor for participants’ diagnostic accuracy. For each content dimension, participants rated two to three items on  a 4-point Likert scale with a total of 8 items per  simulated student. Ratings were compared to an expert solutio n. If participants matched the expert rating, they received 1 point. Participants’ diagnostic accuracy ranged from 0 to 16 points. Scores were rescaled to lie between 0 and 1. CK and PCK were each assessed using 8 items that were developed to fit the content of the video-based simulation. Participants’ interest was measured using 9 items (3 for each aspect) ada pted from Rotgans and Schmidt (2014), which were rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Self-efficacy was measured using six items adapted from Kunter et al. (2002), which were rated on a 5-point Likert scale. We assessed task value with four items that were rated on a 5-point Likert scale from Wigfield (1994). Expectancy to succeed was assessed with four items on a seven-point Likert scale adapted from Rheinber g et al. (2001). Cronbach’s alpha for all scales and tests reported above were acceptable (between.55 and.90). For all scales, scores were rescaled to lie between 0 and 1.

## Data Analysis

We used R version 4.1.2 for all analyses. To answer the first research question, we computed Pearson correlations between all diagnostic activities as well as the di agnostic accuracy, and learner prerequisites using the Hmisc R package. To answer the second and third research qu estions, we conducted a latent profile analysis (LP A) with the diagnostic activities as indicators. All indicators were z-standardized to increase the probability of convergence. We then estimated model fit for models with one to six profiles using the tidyLPA R package. The best fitting model was chosen based on relevant fit indices (AIC, corrected AIC, BIC, Entropy, and BLRT p-values) as well as content related interpretability. For AIC, corre cted AIC, and BIC, smaller values indicate better m odel fit. Entropy values should be close to 1. Next, we assig ned participants to the most likely profile. Finall y, we compared profiles with respect to relevant learner prerequisites and diagnostic accuracy.

## Discussion

As an overarching finding, our results demonstrate that assessing the diagnostic activities in a simul ation based on pre-service teachers’ notes is generally feasible and results in theoretically meaningful differentiations between activities and participants. Based on pre-service t eachers’ notes, we identified three distinct profil es that differ with respect to the amount to which they engage in the different diagnostic activities. These profiles allow for the implementation of specific scaffolding that addresses pre-service teachers’ needs and takes their actual cognitive processes into account (Engelmann & Bannert, 2021; Plass & Pawar, 2020). Compared to a “one scaffolding fits all” strategy, this should lead to better learning outcomes (Chernikova, Heitzmann, Fink, et al., 2020). On a more specific level, our results show that pre-service teachers mostly describe and evaluate when diagnosing a simulated student. Higher order activities such as explaining or decision-making occur only rarely and mostly in a subgroup of pre-service teachers. O n a first look, this is unproblematic, as only desc ribing and evaluating are significantly positively related to diagnostic accuracy. Explaining and decision-making do not seem necessary for making an accurate judgment about the students’ learning process. However, f rom a practical perspective, this is problematic because going beyond the mere diagnosis by inferring pedago gical actions from the diagnosis is the key challenge for adaptive teaching and individual  scaffolding of students (Reuker et al., 2021). Our results allow for some c onclusions about how those pre-service teachers who  make decisions differ to those who do not: decision-maki ng seems to be mainly related to pre-service teache rs’ professional knowledge (see Table 1), which is also  reflected in the finding that pre-service teachers  associated to the decisive profile seem to have more professio nal knowledge than participants associated to both other subgroups. Although results are only provided on a descriptive level, particularly the difference between profiles in their CK is interesting. These findings partially disagree with findings from Kramer et al. (2021) who found no relation between CK and diagnostic activities on an aggregated level. Yet, our findings suggest that CK and PCK are not equally important for all diagnostic activities, which could explain their null finding. Our findings related to CK, thus, suggest that pre-service teachers with  low professional knowledge need to be scaffolded t o make pedagogical decisions. For instance, knowledge prompts could be a suitable means to support pre-service teachers in their higher order diagnostic activities. Moreov er, the difference between the predictivity of the diagnostic activities for judgement accuracy (describing, evaluating) vs. for adaptive teaching (explaining, decision-making) suggests that future research should not only focus  on diagnostic accuracy (e.g., Südkamp et al., 2012), but understand diagnostic skills in a broader sense to include decisions based on the diagnosis and invest igate how different diagnostic activities are related to this broader conception. Interestingly, we identified only small difference s between the three profiles beyond the differences  in professional knowledge. Even the restrained profile, which engaged little in all diagnostic activities, was not much less accurate than both other profiles and was also not less motivated or self-effective. It is unclear, what exactly leads to the differences between participants’ engagement and motivational interventions appear only little suited. Future research should examine their partially smal ler values on interest. It might be that they are n ot as much interested in the topic of diagnosing and that an intervention addressing their interest could enhance their general engagement. However, the restrained profile captured more than half of all pre-service teachers who participated in the study and it could be that the group is rath er heterogeneous. Further qualitative analyses of the restrained ICLS2022 Proceedings 572 © ISLS group might enhance our understanding of how participants in this group could be scaffolded beyond knowledge prompts to also engage in higher order diagnostic activities. As always, this work is not without limitations. F irstly, the diagnostic activities were captured based on notes learners generated during the diagnostic process. Hence, conclusions of our work are limited to observable diagnostic activities represented within these notes. Pre-service teachers might differ systematically in the extent to which they noted down their observations and tho ughts and even might not be able to access all rele vant cognitive processes themselves since reasoning processes often happen unconsciously (e.g., Loibl et al., 2020). Yet, our analyses resulted in meaningful profiles w hich should be extended by further qualitative anal yses as mentioned above. Secondly, we group participants to the profiles based on the most probable profile membership. We did not (yet) consider that participants could h ave similar membership probabilities for the profil es. Future research should address this issue by comparing different methods for class membership (e.g., BGH method). In all, our study provides valuable insights into pre-service teachers’ diagnostic process and sugges ts more and less relevant starting points for adaptive scaffolding. Yet, more research is needed with respect to their ability to make inferences about pedagogical action s and how different diagnostic activities are relat ed to this ability. Besides, studies implementing the suggeste d scaffolds and longitudinal investigations focusin g on how pre-service teachers learn to diagnose in the long term are necessary. Altogether, we showed that pre-service teachers’ diagnostic process in simulations differ inter-individually and that their diagnostic activities are related to professional knowledge and to the diagnostic acc uracy and we emphasized the importance of taking th e diagnostic process into account when facilitating pre-service teachers’ learning of diagnosing.
