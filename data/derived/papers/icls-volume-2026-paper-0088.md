# Negotiating Tensions as Sites of Learning: Teacher-Child Interactions During Mathematical Play

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Play offers rich opportunities for young children to engage with mathematics, yet its classroom enactment often involves negotiating tensions between children’s autonomy and curricular expectations. Drawing on cultural-historical activity theory, this stud y proposes a framework for identifying teacher –child negotiations around rules, representational tools, and agendas and examines how such negotiation shapes opportunities for shared sense-making during math play. Our analysis identified three relational configurations that capture how these negotiations unfold: teacher-dominant coordination, parallel coordination, and joint improvisation. The framework provides methodological and pedagogical tools for examining and intervening playful math learning environments that center children’s agency and shared epistemic authorship.

Despite its well-documented benefits, play has been increasingly marginalized in U.S. elementary classrooms due to pressures from assessment-driven outcomes (Miller & Almon, 2009). Yet, for many educators, play is not just a pedagogy, but also a vision of joyful, meaningful learning. Ms. Conway, an educator in the present study, shared the following during an interview: “... I want them to learn, but I want them to love learning, because that will follow through forever … They'll remember that more than whatever it was that I taught them that day… And don't forget to stop and be silly.” This quote indicates Ms. Conway’s commitment to preserving children’s autonomy and joy in learning mathematics. Throughout the year, her classroom often reflected both pedagogical flexibility and her desire to support children’s mathematical growth, which aligns closely with the vision of playful mathematics learning described in the literature (Baker et al., 2021; Mardell et al., 2021). Yet the realities of classroom teaching are rarely ideal. Teachers must constantly navigate competing demands between hon oring children’s autonomy and meeting curricular expectations (Wood, 2009). It is within this everyday negotiation that the promise and tension of mathematical play unfold. Therefore, this study examines what happens when someone with a mindset like Ms. Conway enters children’s mathematical play. Informed by cultural-historical activity theory (Engeström, 2001), we present a framework for analyzing the tensions in teacher-child interactions from three dimensions. Specifically, we ask: How donegotiations around rules, representational tools, and agendas during math play shape opportunities for shared mathematical sense-making?

In this paper, we share three illustrative episodes that illuminate how tensions were negotiated in different relational configurations, shaping the opportunities for children’s mathematical sense-making, participation, and agency. By shifting the analytic focus from teaching strategies to interactional dynamics, this study contributes to deeper understandings of how mathematical play becomes, or fails to become, a shared site of inquiry.

## Literature review

Play and mathematics Research on children’s play points to its benefits for intellectual and socio-emotional development (Vygotsky, 1976; Copple & Bredekamp, 2009). Within this body of work, mathematical play offers rich opportunities for young children’s access “everyday mathematics” in meaningful, self-initiated ways, which lays a foundation for future mathematics learning. Children are exposed to multiple mathematical ideas through play, such as spatial reasoning and pattern making (Piccolo & Test, 2010), number sense (Ginsberg, 2006), and operations (Papandreou & Tsiouli, 2020). Zippert et al. (2019) also find that social interactions during play promote children’s verbal math explorations and knowledge construction. Such child-centered experiences counter the traditional math learning 764 that positions learners as passive knowledge receivers (Boaler, 2002), thereby offering a promising learning space valuing exploration and curiosity.

On the other hand, children need support for the intuitive understanding of mathematics that can emerge during play. Sarama and Clement (2009) challenge the view that intervention may harm children’s play and offer strategies that more knowledgeable adults can follow, such as altering materials and giving language to children’s play, to increase the quality of play through mathematizing. Van Oers (2010) further unpacks learning through play by highlighting the gap between children’s ‘personal understanding’ of their play and formal mathematics, arguing that guiding children to perform ‘authenticated mathematical actions’ while preserving children’s own schematic representations supports both mathematical understanding and identity formation. Teacher supporting mathematical play in classrooms While previous studies have established the benefits of play and how adults can enrich children’s math learning through play, bringing math play into formal classrooms remains challenging for many teachers. Teachers hold ambivalent beliefs about the pedagogical value of play, concerning whether learning through play can lead to specific learning goals (Lynch, 2015). Underlying this uncertainty is a central challenge that lies in navigating the tension between curricular expectations and children’s intrinsic interests (Wood, 2009). In response, scholars have proposed guided play as a pedagogical approach that aims to balance teacher guidance with children’s autonomy by situating learning within an intentionally designed context. Teachers are expected to subtly steer children’s attention toward particular learning goals while preserving children’s agency and sense of playfulness (Weisberg et al., 2016; Zosh et al., 2018). However, this framework often proves difficult to enact in practice. Teachers either take a much-laybacked stance due to the fear of undermining student agency and the  uncertainty about how to probe math thinking during play (Heshmati et al., 2018; Henson et al, 2021), or dominate the math play with teacher-led instruction, which results in imposing learning onto children’s play (Wickstrom et al., 2019). These practical challenges suggest the inherently relational and negotiated nature of guided play, which cannot be reduced to simple teaching strategies or student compliance. While the principles and practices of guided play are well-established, their enactment is dynamic. In classrooms, teachers and children continuously coordinate multiple (and sometimes competing) goals, priorities, and interests. Focusing only on whether t eachers intervene ‘appropriately’ or children maintain agency oversimplifies the interactional work of learning math through play. What remains unexamined is how teachers and children negotiate directions of mathematical inquiry moment by moment through interactions. These negotiations are not merely practical but epistemological, as teachers often decide whose agenda defines meaningful mathematical engagement. At stake is an enduring adult-centered orientation that positions teachers’ instructional goals a s the primary benchmark for ‘successful’ play. Such framing often casts children’s spontaneous ideas or moves that diverge from the goals as detours to be managed, therefore failed to capture assets that children bring to play (Keifert & Stevens, 2019). This narrow lens constrains how play is enacted in the classrooms, reducing it to the pursuit of predetermined outcomes rather than an outlet for children’s ways of knowing. Hence, understanding how teachers recognize, value, and cultivate children’s knowledge as assets is crucial for sustaining play as a space for shared mathematical inquiry.  In this study, we examined teacher– child interactions during small-group math play to trace how tensions are negotiated as they unfold. This focus sets up a more relational account of how inquiry is co-constructed in math play.

## Theoretical framework

Sociocultural view of math learning We situate this analysis in sociocultural perspectives of learning, where learning is a form of social participation that emerges through engagement with peers, adults, and the tools and practices available in a given context (Vygotsky, 1978; Lave & Wenger, 1991). Mathematical learning, in particular, with its multifaceted rules and practices, is a process of enculturation, where children acquire not only content knowledge and procedures, but also practices, symbolic conventions, and ways of reasoning valued in the mathematical community (Sfard, 2008). Social interactions are central to this process, as interactions leverage contextual affordances and shape the opportunities available for children to participate in mathematical practices. Additionally, diffe rent histories, experiences, and perspectives that children and teachers bring to any activity shape what they notice, value, and pursue. In particular, disciplinary perceptions shape what enculturated adults, such as teachers, interpret and act on as meaningful mathematical engagement (Stevens & Hall, 1998). This perspective foregrounds the importance of studying the dynamic interactions through which learning occurs, highlighting the role of teachers not merely 765 as transmitters of knowledge, but as collaborators who mediate opportunities for children to explore, articulate, and extend their mathematical thinking.

Teacher-child interactions as a joint activity Joint activity, as theorized by Rogoff (1994), underscores the mutual transformation that occurs when adults and children engage together in purposeful activity. Vossoughi et al. (2022) further expands this idea by conceptualizing pedagogical support as a relational, joint activity where teachers and learners come together for a collaborative, meaning-making process. By cautioning against the tendency to analyze learning through adult-or child-centered binaries, this orientation emphasizes the generative and contextual understanding of teacher-child interaction. Following this perspective, we conceptualize teacher– child interaction as a site of negotiation where both participants’ goals, and their interpretations and use of tools and rules evolve through ongoing coordination. This framing no longer positions teacher’s moves merely as control or guidance, but as relational responses to unfolding participation, and values children’s action as consequential to the collective development of ideas.

Figure 1 Analytic Framework for Teacher-child Interactions during Math Play To analytically trace how joint activity unfolds, we draw on Engeström’s (2001) third generation of activity theory, which positions contradictions and tensions between activity systems as potential drivers of new practices when handled constructively. Adapting key mediational components, tools, rules, and the object of activity theory to a micro, interactional level in the context of math play, we establish an analytical framework that helps identify possible tensions in the space (see Figure 1). This framework depicts the teacher and students as two interacting yet distinct ‘ systems’ represented by two overlapping triangles. Each triangle includes three nodes – representational tools (including physical materials and language), rules, and their immediate agendas – that mediates their own participation but also shapes one another  through ongoing interactions. For instance, a child’s agenda may shape how they use physical materials, while their use of materials may prompt shifts in how teachers interpret rules. While power dynamics may lead one system to dominate at times, we recognize b oth parties as analytically consequential  in directing and making sense of math play. By examining moments of convergence and divergence across the three dimensions, we aim to illuminate how mathematical play becomes or fails to become a dynamic site of relational and intellectual co-construction, and why.

## Method

This study draws from a three-year longitudinal project examining mathematical play in a racially and economically diverse public charter school in southeastern United States, where 55% of the students receive free and reduced lunch. The broader research investigates how math play in classrooms shapes children’s mathematics identities and teacher’s pedagogical practices. Each year, participating K-2 teachers engage in four professional development sessions designed to support teachers implementing play in their classrooms. During these sessions, teachers unpack curriculum standards, redesign curriculum lessons into playful activities with our research team and reflect on their playful instructions through video club. After each session, teachers implement the redesigned playful lessons for four days as playweek. While supported by the school leadership, participating teachers still work within standardized curricula and accountability demands outside of playweek, which potentially impacts how they interpret and implement math play within our project. 766 The data in present analysis comes  from Ms. Conway’s kindergarten classroom in year one. With a STEM Preliminary review of classroom videos across three participating kindergarten teachers identified Ms. Conway’s classroom as a rich site for examining teacher-child interactions due to her active engagement and generative questioning that frequently formalized or extended children’s mathematical play. Ms. Conway typically began playful lessons with a whole-group activity, followed by 30 minutes of small-group play centers. Children freely chose among three to four play centers with two rotations each day, followed by a teacher-led closing discussion. 360-degree cameras placed at the center of tables recorded Ms. Conway’s small-group interactions, providing continuous multimodal video data for analysis.

## Data Analysis

This analysis builds on an earlier coding process across nine play centers from geometry, counting, and addition unit. We segmented video data into 77 interaction episodes, bounded by active exchange with the same student(s), ending when Ms. Conway switched to different student(s). We applied open and axial coding (Corbin & Strauss,

2015) to identify patterns in teacher-child interactions. Three recurring themes emerged: maintaining the integrity

of curricular math, surfacing math in play, and exploring math through play. Throughout this process, we kept analytic memos to document tensions and negotiations (Saldaña, 2021). For the current study, we returned to the memo and video corpus, and conducted an interpretive analysis guided by our analytic framework (see Figure 1). We transcribed verbal and nonverbal interactions and content logged each interaction episode. We then adopted an interaction analytic approach (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to examine how representational tools, rules, and agendas were negotiated in moment-to-moment interactions. This involved identifying these elements for both teacher and children and tracing how they shifted or came into tension over the course of each episode. In the next section, we present three illustrative episodes that represent distinct relational configurations during the negotiation of tensions: teacher-dominated coordination, parallel coordination with diverging agendas, and joint improvised sense-making. These episodes are not intended to represent a linear progression, but to foreground how different ways of navigating tensions shape mathematical play differently. Dot paint: Rule enforcement overrides children’s strategies This episode occurred during the counting unit, where children worked in pairs and stamped dots with dot markers for their partner to count. When Ms. Conway first approached Chance and Terique’s table, they were both dotting on their own paper. She directed them to follow the rules of taking turns and prompted Terique to first count Chance’s dots. Since Terique double counted some of the dots, he ended up reporting 23 dots while Chance only dotted 21. Noticing Terique randomly counting dots without applying any strategy, Ms. Conway grabbed a pencil and put it next to Chance while Terique was still counting, then waited until Terique finished to raise the question of how to avoid double counting. Both children proposed their strategies (see Table 1, line 2-6), but Ms. Conway encouraged Terique to show his strategy at first, as his idea appeared to align with the standardized ‘mark-andcount,’ introduced to class earlier. Chance and Terique then demonstrated and advocated  their own strategies enthusiastically (see Table 1, line 7,8).

However, since their alternative enactments of counting rules  were in tension with the  mathematically correct ways of counting held by Ms. Conway, their ideas got overridden. Ms. Conway’s stance shifted from subtle control of the interaction toward unilateral decision making, evident in her grabbing pencil from an earlier stage and cutting off children’s lines to offer suggestive hints (see Table 1, line 11,14). When Terique changed his strategy name from “mark it” to “block” on line 8, Ms. Conway still followed up using the word “mark”, indicating a dismissal of children’s evolving ideas. She eventually took over the pencil from Chance to model ‘the right way’ of counting and prompted children to “try it”. This move closed the space for negotiation and positioned the rule of playing, which is also the rule of counting, as fixed and authoritative, marginalized children’s emergent strategies.

This episode exemplifies tension in the conceptualization and enforcement of rules. While both Chance and Terique actively engaged in proposing strategies, their ideas are not aligned with the institutionalized mathematical norm  for accurate counting. Ms. Conway therefore resolved this tension by reasserting the standardized rules and modeling the “right” procedure. This move ensured mathematical correctness but also closed down opportunities for negotiation, anchoring the episode at the teacher-dominated end of the relational 767 configuration spectrum. It also illustrates how even available tools in the play space (e.g. pencils) are given fixed, rule-bound functions in service of formal learning goals, which reveals how fragile children’s agency can be when rules are closely tied to specific instructional objectives. Square versus rectangle: Leverage language tensions for shared geometric inquiry Throughout the geometry unit, Ms. Conway actively invited children to articulate the difference between a square and a rectangle across multiple play centers. This open-ended question created a tension between the representational tools  children used to mediate their geometric understanding and standardized mathematical language, which demanded further interpretive work from Ms. Conway. The following two excerpts show how Ms. Conway leveraged the tension as opportunities for joint meaning-making and improvi sed with children’s language to build conceptual bridges between children’s intuitive ways of knowing and disciplinary ways of knowing.

Excerpt 1 happened during cutting shapes where children draw and cut geometric shapes (rectangles, squares, circles, and triangles) off the paper. Ms. Conway wove the question of distinguishing a square and a rectangle into Madelyn’s ongoing play (see Table3, line 3). Madelyn’s response, “smaller,” reflected an intuitive but mathematically imprecise idea. Ms. Conway treated this less rigorous statement as an entry point for deeper 769 mathematical reasoning. Building on Madelyn’s language, she created an obvious counterexample, a large square and a small rectangle, to gently disrupt Madelyn’s claim and encourage reasoning about geometric properties beyond size. In doing so, Ms. Conway a lso modeled a mathematical practice of testing conjectures. Hence, this moment created a brief alignment between the teacher and child around language, opening a shared inquiry space for refining ideas about shape properties.

This study examined how teacher –child negotiations around rules, representational tools, and agendas shape opportunities for shared mathematical sense-making during play. These three dimensions mediate both teacher and children’s own participation and influence one another when tensions emerge and are negotiated. Across three episodes, we identified three relational configurations: teacher-dominated coordination, parallel coordination, and joint improvisation, that describe how these negotiations unfolded, revealing different kinds of mathematical learning. These dynamics do not operate in isolation as tensions in one node influence others. For instance, the rule enforcement in Dot paint episode shaped tool usage, resulting in a much restricted discussion around counting strategy. We also are not suggesting these configurations as fixed categories associated with tensions: while tensions in rules led to teacher-dominated coordination in Dot paint episode, similar tensions might be taken up differently in other contexts. What matters is how participants orient themselves to tensions as interaction unfolds. This interplay underscores that the nature of scaffolding in mathematical play is interactionally produced in addition to predetermined curricular design or teacher intention. Our analysis also points to why these tensions might be handled differently. In the Dot paint episode, the tension in rules was tied to institutionally valued learning goals, counting strategies, which heightened the pressure to resolve through directive teaching. By contrast, tensions in the last two episodes arose from extensions of the curricular goals, which may offer more room for exploratory and flexible negotiations. This aligns with activity theory’s view of contradictions: they can be either suppressed or leveraged as generative openings for learning, depending on how participants orient themselves to them. It also highlights how institutional pressures and curricular expectations can quietly steer how tensions are taken up on an interactional level, privileging certain forms of mathematical engagement while constraining others. This analysis is not an evaluation of Ms. Conway’s teaching. As suggested across the episodes, Ms. Conway demonstrates active, responsive engagement with children’s diverse forms of mathematical play. Our goal is to illustrate the complex relational work involved in supporting children’s autonomy and agency while attending curricular expectations during mathematical play. We argue that tensions in math play are not disruptions but productive sites where mathematical learning is defined and potentially redefined through joint negotiation. The three-node analytic framework developed here carries both methodological and pedagogical implications: Methodologically, it provides a structured way to trace what tensions arise and how they shift through interaction. Pedagogically, it offers teachers a lens for noticing children’s framing as assets rather thandeviations from predetermined goals. This may serve as a starting point for valuing the brilliant mathematical capabilities children demonstrate when given meaningful space to participate. Joyful, intellectually rich math play demands pedagogical dispositions attuned to children’s frames and a reimagining of classroom authority through shared epistemic agency between teachers and young learners. By surfacing and working with tensions  rather than smoothing them over, educators and researchers can cultivate learning environments where mathematics is not simply delivered but co-constructed. Future research and professional development can build on this relational lens to explore how teacher’s and children’s perspectives come into synergy, shifting math play toward evolving forms of inquiry, improvisation, and shared authorship that broaden what counts as mathematical engagement.
