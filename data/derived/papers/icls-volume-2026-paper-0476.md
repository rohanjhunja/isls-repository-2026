# Investigating How Students Plan, Monitor, and Evaluate Learning When Working in Groups in Undergraduate Biology Labs

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Social metacognition, or the extent that group members working together are aware of and regulate the thinking of other group members, is associated with greater cognition and learning. However, it remains unclear to what extent undergraduate students engage in planning, monitoring, and evaluating their group’s learning in the context of biology labs. Here, we investigate to what extent groups engage in social metacognition through their peer-to-peer questions and characterize different types of questions during groupwork.

Metacognition, or the awareness and regulation of one’s thinking, is a critical component of learning, with students who engage in greater metacognition generally having stronger ability to grasp deep conceptual understandings of new ideas and concepts (Ewell et al. 2023, Stanton et al. 2021). Similarly, social metacognition – the ability of students to plan, monitor, and evaluate their group’s thinking when working with other students – has also been associated with higher levels of critical thinking and learning (Chiu and Kuo 2010, Halmo et al. 2022). Despite the importance of group work in STEM, however, there remains relatively limited work characterizing how often students engage in social metacognition when doing groupwork or examining the factors that may influence groups’ social metacognition.

Here, we conduct an exploratory study in the context of introductory undergraduate biology labs  to address the following research questions: 1) To what extent are students engaging in social metacognition in their peer-to-peer questions? 2) What types of social metacognition (i.e., planning, monitoring, or evaluating their thinking) are students most commonly engaging in? We focus upon peer-to-peer questions for both theoretical and practical reasons. First, questions asked by students to each other are often critical to the process of students’ learning (Commeyras 1995), and thus there have been several recent studies focusing on analyzing students’ questions to each other to characterize the impact on student learning (Tiffany et al. 2023, Dahl et al. 2025). Similarly, students’ questions to each other can provide a window into how these students are thinking in the moment, given the potential of such questions posed to other members of the group to challenge the thinking of other group members. Finally, we highlight how for practical reasons our exploratory study focused first upon these questions as a way to broadly characterize students’ social metacognition during groupwork before returning to the remainder of the group conversations, given that each of the lab courses we observed were several hours in length. We observed nine sections of a  second-semester introductory biology lab course at one university, with each section lasting up to four hours. For each lab, we video-and audio-recorded one group of three or four students who all consented to the study; all procedures were reviewed and approved by Chapman University Institutional Review Board. Groups were tasked with completing lab procedures relating to ecology and evolution and with gathering and analyzing data prior to answering several questions in the lab manual relating to the procedure. Following the observations, recordings were professionally transcribed. W e then read through the transcripts to identify all questions that students asked, reviewing the video recordings as needed. We removed all questions that students asked the instructor, leaving only students’ peer-to-peer questions. Next, two researchers independently read through the transcripts  and came up with a codebook through both inductive and deductive coding. First, we utilized the framework of social metacognition from Halmo et al. (2022)  to establish codes that corresponded with different phases of planning, monitoring, and evaluating learning, which are broadly aligned with the steps of metacognition  (Schraw and Moshman, 1995). We also inductively identified different codes for non-metacognitive peer-to-peer questions. Following this, the two researchers met to discuss and generate a consensus codebook. Finally, multiple researchers are independently reading and coding each transcript, meeting to discuss and resolve any discrepancies.

## Preliminary results

2857 We have coded 1,928 peer-to-peer questions so far, with ongoing analyses of additional transcripts. Our preliminary results reveal that most peer-to-peer questions are not social metacognitive in nature and are instead procedural questions where students focus upon on completing the lab protocol without evidence of any planning, monitoring, or evaluating when asking these questions. Similarly, students often ask each other off-topic questions (i.e., questions not related to the lab or underlying biological principles) during the lab. Despite this, we identify that social metacognitive questions represent approximately one-third of the overall set of questions that students ask each other while completing the lab. In addition, our preliminary results suggest that students’ questions mostly exhibit evidence of monitoring and evaluating their learning, with less evidence of students’ planning of their learning. We are further characterizing to what extent students ’ social metacognitive questions that monitor their learning are monitoring understanding, progress, or requests for information, following the framework from Halmo et al. (2022). We are continuing to code transcripts and will characterize the proportion of social metacognitive questions across different lab groups and will use these data to identify patterns in students’ social metacognitive thinking across lab contexts.

By characterizing the types and frequencies of social metacognitive  questions that students are asking in the context of an undergraduate biology lab course, our work will provide insight into the extent that students are planning, monitoring, and evaluating their learning as a group while engaging in lab  tasks and analyses. In addition, our preliminary results indicate that students are largely not engaging in social metacognition through their peer-to-peer questions during these undergraduate biology labs, suggesting that students may need to be intentionally prompted to engage in social metacognition. Our work thus generates new knowledge on how different factors may influence how groups learn and also has implications for instructional strategies to promote greater social metacognition.
