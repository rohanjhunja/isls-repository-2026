# MathCHOPS: A Platform for Developing Collaborative Higher Order Problem Solving in Mathematics

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We describe an exploratory pilot study and a collaborative platform designed to engage novice learners in higher-order thinking processes. The theoretical motivation for and facets of novel item design are described. The pilot study took place in a high-school enrichment classroom in Shanghai. Gain scores in higher-order problem solving tests were higher than those in a control classroom, but differences were not statistically significant. Pre-and post-measures of expert-like attitudes towards mathematics problem solving and perceptions of collaboration were also used, and overall differences were also not statistically significant. Preliminary evidence, however, suggests that higher perceived benefits/enjoyment of collaboration may have been offset by higher perceived challenges/costs of collaboration.

## Introduction

The use of exercises requiring higher-order thinking in novice-level STEM ins truction is sometimes deprecated on the grounds of not over-burdening the learner’s cognitive load (Paas, Renkl, & Sweller, 2004). Learning from worked examples, for instance, is demonstrably effective for certain kinds  of schema acquisition. Instructors, however, also recognize that “students run into difficulty when encountering proble ms that go beyond “plug ‘n chug” and require them to apply real-world skills such as approximating, making assumptions, estimating, or doing additional research to solve the problem” (Harper, Freuler, & Demel,  2006). Practice using such higher-order processes is, however, typically put off until more advanced stages, at whi ch point many students have dropped out of STEM pathways. Janssen and Kirschner (2020) have recently promote d a research agenda that combines cognitive load theory (CLT) and (computer-supported) collaborative learning (CSCL). The authors assert that, “to fully understand the complex ity of CSCL, it is necessary to conduct research that simultaneously studies the processes of collaboration, how these processes may be affec ted by the antecedents of collaboration [student, group, and task characteristics], and how these interactions, in turn, aff ect the consequences of collaboration [individual achievement, group performance, and perceived efficacy]” (p. 790). Janssen and Kirschner did not specifically call out how higher-order processing might be distr ibuted in collective working memory. However, complementary theoretical work, such as on socially mediated metacognition (Goos, Galbraith, & Renshaw, 2002), suggests some ways of managing the cognitive load of hi gher-order processing when students work together rather than alone.

This paper describes early results on using collaborative affordances to s upport higher-order problem solving in the mathematics domain. This work is motivated by prior research findings that collaborative problem solving (CPS) is most conducive for learning when (a) there is positive interdependence among participants (they really need each other to succeed) (Slavin, 1996, Johnson & Johnson, 2009), (b) tasks are challenging enough to be worth the transactional costs of collaborating (Kirschner, Kirschner, & Janssen, 2014), and (c) learning material is conceptual in nature, not just procedural (Mullins, Rummel, & Spada, 2011). The re mainder of the paper is organized as follows: we first briefly review work on collaborative learning, higher order thinking, and computersupported learning technologies. We then describe specific ways in which the CHO PS platform creates positive interdependence through task design. Methods and findings for an exploratory pilot study a re then presented, following which we discuss the study limitations and consequences for future work. Collaborative learning While early research on collaborative learning reported positive effects on indi vidual academic outcomes (Johnson & Johnson, 2009; Slavin, 1996), understanding the design features that support these g ains remains an area of ongoing research (Mullins et al., 2011; Chi & Menekse, 2015, Janssen & Kirschner, 2020). Learning gains have been explained in several ways. Collaborating groups have the potential to solve  problems that individual members would not be capable of solving on their own and to engage in socially-m ediated metacognition (Vygotsky, 1978; Goos et al., 2002). Additionally, students are given an authentic reason to a rticulate, explain, and defend their ideas, while simultaneously being exposed to the alternate ideas of their peers (Von Glasersfeld, 2013). Investigating the learning benefits of different modes of student engagement, Chi and Wylie (2014) found convergent evidence of the relative superiority of “interactive” (i.e., collaborative) activities over those which are passive, active, or involve individual knowledge construction. A more recent revie w listed additional student CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 52 outcomes positively affected by group work, including students’ academic self-efficacy, attitudes toward specific disciplines (e.g., STEM), quality of reasoning, and perspective taking (Chen, Wang, Kirschner, & Tsai, 2018). Notably, simply putting students together in groups and telling them to work together is not sufficient to observe the positive outcomes associated with collaboration (Gillies, 2016). Collabor ative learning needs to be structured. To this end, researchers have devoted extensive effort to studying the relevant components of collaborative task design. This includes attention to: (a) the task or problem to be addressed; (b) specific mode or manner of collaboration required; (c) the collaborative working environment/support tools; and (d) how these different elements interact with each other. In this paper, we focus on tasks that induce positive interdependence among students, which have been shown to support individual learning (e.g., Cohen, 1994). Rather thandirect the manner of collaboration, often referred to as collaborative scripting (Dillenbour g, 2002), tasks are structured by distributing information and responsibilities across group members. The task conte nt also favors conceptual material, which promotes mutual elaboration, rather than purely procedural materia l, which promotes task distribution (Mullins et al., 2011). In development of the CHOPS platform, we considered features of the working environment that have been shown to support collaborative meta-cognition (Jeong & Hmelo-Silver, 2016). Higher-order thinking The division of thinking skills into lower-order and higher-order groupings can be s een as a simplification of Bloom’s taxonomy (Bloom et al., 1956; Krathwohl, 2002), which, originally, provided a level-ordering of cognitive processes from remembering, understanding, and applying to analyzing, evalua ting, and creating. The first three of these are often taken to constitute the lower-order processes (or thinking skills) and the last three to constitute higher order thinking (HOT) (Stanley & Moore, 2013). Brookhart (2010) notes that various definitions of HOT emphasize either transfer, critical thinking, or problem solving to dif ferent degrees (and with a lot of overlap). Some researchers describe it as spanning critical, logical, reflective, metacognitive, and creative thinking skills that are developed when individuals encounter unfamiliar problems, uncerta in conditions, or new phenomena that require solutions that have not been seen before (Widana, 2017). In spite of these variable definitions, it is widely recognized that training skills beyond remembering and a pplying facts or formulas is critical for education in the 21st century (Brookhart, 2010; Moseley, Elliott, Gregson, &  Higgins, 2005; Thompson, 2008).

It has been argued that mathematics education relies too much on factual recall and the use of standardized algorithms, with insufficient effort to teach or asse ss higher order thinking (Kulm, 1990; Battista, 1994). Students learn to solve particular kinds of problems but may fa il to transfer these skills to nonfamiliar situations (Schoenfeld, 1988). Khan and Inamullah (2011) explored the levels of questions teachers asked during teaching at secondary level using Bloom’s taxonomy. The authors reported that, among 267 observations, 97% targeted lower-order processes (recall, comprehension, or application). Teachers emphasize lower-order questions in the classroom and in both summative and formative tests. One reason for this may be that lower-order questions are easier to write and score. Another reason is that higherorder questions may be seen as too cognitively demanding for novices. From the vantage point of cognitive loa d theory, searching through an ill-defined problem space may be seen as extr aneous cognitive load (Paas et al, 2004). The result of introducing such higher-order tasks would thus be lower overall learning of essential, lowerlevel schema. However, Andre (1979) suggested that focusing too much on lower order questions can impede learning: if you ask lower-order questions, the result will be lower-order learning. Some recent research suggests that it may indeed be possible to promote higher order thinking skills through collabor ation (Gokhale, 1995; MacKnight, 2000; Osborne, Byrne, Massey, & Johnston, 2018). Collaborative technologies CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 53 A large variety of technologies have been developed to enable students to interac t with academic content collaboratively. In some of these, collaboration is with computer agents, using natural language (e.g., Rosé, 2007; Ward et al., 2013). Students might ask questions that are answered by the agent or answer questions that are asked by the agent. In related work on learning-by-teaching pedagogies (e.g., Biswas et al,  2016; Lubold et al, 2019), students may either ask a virtual agent to answer questions and take qui zzes, to check its learning, or help the agent solve step-based mathematics problems, explaining each step to the agent. Human to human collaboration environments include asynchronous and synchronous systems. Asynchronous interactions, such as threaded discussion forums common in modern lea rning management systems, have been the subject of several studies (e.g., Rosé & Ferschke, 2016; Wise & Chiu, 2011). Co-Lab (van Joolingen et al., 2005) and Virtual Math Teams (VMT; Stahl, 2009) were two prominent examples of synchronous environments. In Co-Lab, students chat alongside simulations and labs about water  management, greenhouse effects, and electricity. VMT was designed to develop students’ mathematica l discourse while working together viewing, dragging and constructing geometric figures. While Co-Lab and VMT are wha t-you-see-is-what-I-see (WYSIWIS) platforms, others allow users to view or control differe nt console elements. For example, COLLaborative Edition, Compilation and Execution (COLLECE; Bravo, Duque, & Gallardo, 2013) is groupware that allows users to edit, compile, and run program code collaboratively but with local visualization and control. The CHOPS interface and innovative item types We now describe the CHOPS (Collaborative Higher Order Problem Solving)  platform, in which two students working at separate computers are paired to solve problems together. MathCH OPS requires students to share information and/or agree on a problem-solving strategy in order to successfully solve the problems through unique item types which will be described in more detail below. When students lo g into CHOPS to begin work on an available assignment, the system automatically pairs students who are in  the queue at the same time (pairing constraints, for example to achieve more homogeneous or heterogeneous pairings, can be included). After pairing, students may navigate through instructor-curated assignments, chat with each other  using text messages, and receive corrective feedback on their solution attempts. The limitation of com munication to verbal chat, for the time being, is a deliberate design constraint that forces students to verbalize what they know or see. It is an important feature of some CHOPS tasks that given information may be distributed differently between the students. We refer to these as jigsaw tasks  (after Aronson et al., 1978). When students receive different graphical information, for example, they must communicate to their partners by translating this information into words. Sending pictures or screenshots would undermine this key cognitive process. An example jigsaw task on quantitative reasoning is shown in Figure 1. The item includes a common problem statement (determining an average in a sample; see caption). In addition, each student receives a representation of some data to be used in solving the problem. As shown, each student’s graphic information is unique. Since it cannot be shared with their partner directly (e.g., by copying and pasting), each student must convert the graphical information into a verbal representation. Moreover, simple shortcuts like “I don’t have data for the row 50-59” will not work. As they will discover, one student has a partially censored tabular  representation, while the other has a partially censored bar graph. To answer this item together, the pair ed students will need to communicate effectively while translating data from one representation to another. In this type of problem, information needed for a solution is not given to students directly. Rather, they must select information from a set of options. Some of these options may be distractors, i.e., infor mation that is not in fact necessary for the solution. Importantly, students are given a limited number of information r equests. That is, they may be allowed to select only one value when two are needed. Since the pair have two opportunities between them, they need to coordinate a strategy, articulating their thinking explicitly. A simple example of this type of task is shown in Figure 2.

Both methods require two triangle values, and students need tonegotiate with their collaborator. It is possible for both students to request information (e.g., two angles) that still renders the problem unsolvable.

MathCHOPS provides design templates for innovative item types designed to s tretch the student problem-solving experience beyond plug-and-chug or other low-level tactics. In many cases, it is possible to adapt standard textbook items to create new collaborative versions that use these templates. In effect, the student must use cognitive processes that are given short shrift in traditional end-of-chapt er problems (Harper et al., 2006). They may not have all the information needed to solve a problem, irrelevant information may need to be filtered, or there may be multiple solutions. To be able to solve the problem examples shown above, students are dependent on one another. This interdependence is widely acknowledged as a necessary component of productive collaboration and is a key feature of CHOPS. In addition to information request and jigsaw item types, CHOPS allows for more forms of interdependence in how students can arrive collec tively at a valid solution. One mechanism is through problems with multiple answer inputs, each provided by one student in a pair, and multiple possible answers. Another is the use of both local constraints (satisfied for each student’s individual submission) and global constraints (which factor in answers from both students). Pilot study Our pilot study using MathCHOPS took place in a Shanghai high school for about 12 we eks in the fall of 2020. With the support of the principal, one of the authors ran an enrichment class using the platform. Enrichment classes in Chinese high schools are electives on topics ranging from robots to basketball or film studies. The title of our elective was “Educational Technology, Online Learning and Collaborative Platform Design.” A 45-minute session was held weekly with twenty-four 10th grade students (ages 15-16, 20 m ales and 4 females). Parental consent and student assent forms were distributed and collected in accordanc e with a protocol approved by an internal review board (IRB). The course was promoted as an experience to  not only explore the sector of educational technology but also to participate in the user research and design of an application under development. It was organized into two parts. The first part introduced students to the landscap e of educational technology applications and collaborative games. For homework, students tried different online applications and presented their own user perspectives in class. They described, for example, whether they enjoyed the technology and whether it would be useful for learning. In the second part, students used the MathCHOPS platform, first solving pre-written math items. After some time with the system, students were invited to design their own collaborative items to promote higher-order problem solving and collaboration. In total, students used MathCHOPS in the course of five sessions for about 4 hours during the semester. The student-focused research questions during this experimental pilot were a s follows: (RQ1) Would attitudes towards (a) mathematics problem solving and (b) value of collaboration change because of the experience with the online collaborative problem solving system? (RQ2) Would higher order problem solving abilities CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 55 improve after practicing collaborative exercises using MathCHOPS? Resea rch instruments included two questionnaires measuring, separately, student attitudes toward collaboration and math ematics problem solving. These were distributed to the 24 students in the targeted class at the be ginning and the end of the semester. In addition, math tests with content designed to elicit higher order problem solving were administered pre-and postcourse (different forms). The pretests and posttests were administere d to both the target class and to students in another class. In this quasi-experimental design, the target class was seen as experiencing the intervention of using and developing content for MathCHOPS, while the other class functioned as a control group. The collaboration attitudes questionnaire contained 25 original Likert-scale items, written to address costs/difficulty of collaboration (8 items) or benefits/enjoyment of collaboration (17 items) or both (2 items). Among these, fourteen items were phrased positively and eleven negatively (i.e., reverse-scored). For example, the statement “I prefer to work alone” addresses benefits/enjoyment of collaboration in a negative phrasing. “Collaboration with another person means less work for me” asks about cost/difficulty in a positive phrasing. The second instrument was a Mandarin translation of the Mathematics Attitudes a nd Perceptions Survey (MAPS; Code et al., 2016), which assesses the degree to which students assert e xpert-like thinking about math behavior and performance. Although the MAPS instrument has specific subscales (e.g., pers istence, confidence, interest), we examine only the total expertise index here. We developed an original item pool of questions assessing higher order thinking in mathematics. Two distinct test forms were derived from this pool, resulting in Form A and Form B. These forms were subsequently used as pre-and post-tests, with a counter balanced design to compensate for variable difficulties of the two forms. Each test form contained one questi on from each of four categories/types drawn from Thompson’s (2008) definition of higher order thinking (see right side of  Table 1): discovering patterns, interpreting information, understanding concepts, and thinking critically. All items were word problems, scored with partial credit between zero and five points. One post-mortem group interview was conducted with four student participants at the end of the course. The four participants were selected based on the range of outcomes on the  collaboration attitudes questionnaire. The students with the largest increase and largest decrease in overall scores were included and two students were selected, at random, from among those whose attitudes remained approximately constant. The interview prompted students to speak openly about any notable experiences using MathCHOPS in the class (i.e., “Did any part of the course, good or bad, stand out in your memory?”), whether the content met their expectations, and whether their attitudes towards collaboration changed (“Can you describe any changes in your feelings about collaboration?”).

## Findings

A total of 24 students in the intervention group and 40 students in the comparison group participated in this study. However, due to the concurrent COVID-19 pandemic, a number of students wer e absent for either pre-or postadministration of attitude and ability instruments. Sample sizes were thus reduced in the subsequent analyses. Research Question 1 concerned changes in (a) attitudes towards collaborati on and (b) expert-like attitudes towards mathematics resulting from the semester-long experienc e with the MathCHOPS platform. Thirteen students with both pre-and post-course measures from the target class were initially retained. Two items were excluded from the collaborative attitudes questionnaire becaus e of poor functioning. This was determined based on low correlations of the item with the sum over remaining items (using a minimum threshold of 0.2). Examination of a pre-post scatter plot showed a moderate posit ive correlation of 0.58 and one outlier student with an extremely high pre-score (3.7σ above mean) and very low post-score (1.6σ below mean). During the post-course interview, when asked about the class, this student said, “I don’t have any thoughts. I don't really care about the class, and I just did what you told me to do because you are the boss.” While it is possible that this student’s responses represent an authentic shift in attitudes, we suspected that it was more evidence of a non-conscientious response and thus omitted the outlier. A paired-samples t-test comparing pre (M = 3.58, SD = 0.35) and post (M = 3.67, SD = 0.36) scores for the remaining 12 students showed a  non-significant difference; t(11) = -0.95, p = 0.36. Since the collaboration instrument contained separate groups of items for c osts and benefits, we examined whether the direction of change was the same for each sub-category mean as for the grand mean. In fact, the mean of “cost questions” decreased slightly from 3.31 to 3.15 (t(11) = 0.93, p = 0.37) while the mean of “benefit questions” increased from 3.66 to 3.79 (t(11) = -1.13, p = 0.28). None of these differences are significant, however, in part because the usable sample of 12 was so sma ll. We also did not find a statistically significant difference in the measure of expert-like thinking about mathe matics from the beginning of the class (M = 93.5, SD = 14.1) compared to the end (M = 92.1, SD = 14.2); t(11) = 0.31, p = 0.77. On this measure, four original items were excluded for having item-rest correlations lower than 0.2, and the outlier student from the collaboration instrument was again excluded.

Research Question 2 concerned improvement on individual higher order problem solving after working collaboratively using MathCHOPS. For this analysis, we compared pre-to-pos t changes from a control class to CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 56 those of the target class. Scores on test forms A and B were separate ly rescaled to z-scores using only pre-test scores to compensate for the effect of form difficulty. Again, several stu dents, in both classrooms, missed either the pre-or post-test administration because of COVID-19 effects. We pres ent two analyses of the data. The simpler approach is to include only those students who took both pre-and post-tests and compare the gain between the control (N = 31) and target (N = 9) classroom students. The average scaled score in the control group increased from 0.23 to 0.30, a gain of 0.07, while the average in the target group increased from-0.33 to 0.08, for a gain of 0.40 (discrepancy due to rounding). However, this gain difference of 0.33 standard deviations  is not statistically significant; t(11) = 0.62, p = 0.27. Using all available data from any student who took either the pre-or post-test in the control or target class, the sample sizes increase. However, w e can no longer use paired comparisons. We alternatively use a difference-in-differences linear model to estimate  the coefficient of interaction between condition and post-test. The gain difference between the control class and the target class was estimated to be 0.19 with standard error of 0.42 (p = 0.65). While both approaches hint at a positive effect of MathCHOPS exposure, neither is sufficiently powered to distinguish that effect from chance. During the post-mortem interview with four selected students, two of them expressed interest in the class and in using MathCHOPS, while two seemed disengaged and distracted. The student with the largest gain in collaboration attitudes said, “I really enjoyed the class because it was easy, fun, and relaxing. Every time I think that this class is going to be the last class on Friday, I don’t feel tired all day.” He said he also recommended the class to other students. A spontaneous conversation between two of the interviewees, who were friends, addressed the effect of enforced collaboration on students who are otherwise timid. The  gregarious student pointed to his friend and said, “he lacks communication in daily life, so he needs more interac tions with people.” His friend acknowledged some truth to this, but with qualification, “I agree with what you said, a nd I would like to have more collaboration except while doing mathematics problems.”

## Limitations and directions for future research

In this work, we set out to explore whether a platform for collaborative probl em solving, structured only by task requirements, could provide a learning environment for developing student higher-or der thinking skills in mathematics. The main idea behind the two task types we described, jigsaw a nd information-request tasks, is to discourage standard plug-and-chug tactics and encourage meaning-making through negotiate d understanding. Asking individual students to solve problems without given information, or with deli berately unnecessary information, may, at first anyway, provide too much strain on novices. Learning to practice such problems with a partner allows for that burden to be shared. Since information is distributed a nd procedures under-defined, the collaborative problems in MathCHOPS add both positive interdependence and enough richnes s to justify the transactional costs of collaboration. When representations vary between group members, or multiple pathways exist, these exercises address conceptual and not merely procedural understanding. This pilot study reported here had several limitations, the most obvious being the small effective samples since so many students missed either pre-or post-tests due to COVID-19 r estrictions. Although the differences were not statistically significant, students who used MathCHOPS did have a greater gain on higher order thinking tests than students from the control class. It is worth noting that the control class had significantly higher pre-test scores, and the gain difference almost closed the gap between the two c lasses. Experimental studies with larger sample sizes are still warranted to better evaluate potential learning gains. Although the exercises they practiced were of a more sophisticated type than is usually encountered, there was no evidence that students in the target class became more expert-like in their attitudes towards math problem solving. The MAPS assessment may have been a poor choice, given that most of the factors—such as growth mindset, confidence, interest, persistence, and answers—were not likely to change because of (even positive) collaborative higher-or der problem solving experience. The factors of sense-making and real-world relevance, which might be susceptible to change, comprise less than one quarter of the instrument.

Our collaboration instrument was itself experimental. We included both “cost” a nd “benefit” questions because we wanted to recognize that these might offset each other in attitudes towards collaboration, generally, but that they could also be two different dimensions. That is, one could feel neutra l towards collaboration since both benefits and costs are high (or low), or one could feel positively about benefits while acknowledging the high costs. The sample size in our study was nowhere near large enough to discriminate between a unidimensional and a multidimensional factor model. Since we intended to add these items together, the cost items were always scored “oppositely” to the benefit items. We note only that the target students did sli ghtly increase the ir assessment of the benefits of collaboration (positive gain) from the beginning of the course to the end while, at the same time, increasing their assessment of the challenges/costs of collaboration (negati ve change given scoring design). A better instrument might focus more on attitudes towards mathematical sense-making in collaboration. CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 57 MathCHOPS is designed to encourage metacognition and knowledge co-construction when stude nts have asymmetric access to information. However, since the MathCHOPS queue system is first-in-first-out (FIFO) in the pairing phase, students who sat next to each other in class were frequently paired with each other, and some of them took advantage of face-to-face communication instead of using the chat box. Indeed, during the interview, one student revealed, “I would find a collaborator in advance every time, and we would connect at the same time, so it takes less time to complete.” This represented an implementation fidelity problem that could have mitigated the benefit of MathCHOPS. It could be avoided in a formal experiment with tighter classroom management. Notwithstanding small samples, imperfect instruments, and implementation fidelity issues, we found the MathCHOPS platform itself to be stable and reassuringly usable. For futur e experimentation, including shorterduration studies, we have developed several sample problems, demonstrating the jigsaw and information-request task types with extremely simple content such as finding sums. We have also developed a small number of items for physics, a domain with significant conceptual challenges for novice learners. Although the purpose of the technology is for students to support each other, we recognize that a more comparable experimental control would be to practice similar higher-order items in an individual condition. Information-request items can be readily adapted to one-player use by giving the individual student more information requests. Thus, future experiments, with larger samples, could potentially discriminate between individual and collaborati ve practice on specific higher-order practice problems. MathCHOPS makes it easy for instructor s or researchers to generate transaction logs, which include time-stamped chat, answer attempt, and navigation events for both members in each session. These can be used for analytic studies to identify behaviors of success ful collaboration, which may be defined either through concurrent performance as a group or group-to-individual transfer.
