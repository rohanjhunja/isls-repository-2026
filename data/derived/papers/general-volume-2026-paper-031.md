# Piano Lab Pro: An Exposure Based Mixed Reality Application to Mitigate Music Performance Anxiety

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Piano Lab Pro (PLP) is an immersive experience for the Apple Vision Pro headset, designed to help piano students practice coping mechanisms for dealing with musical performance anxiety. To accomplish this, PLP allows students to perform a musical piece using a real piano, while having the Mixed Reality (MR) headset simulate a large performance space both visually and acoustically. During their performance, they experience dimmer house light, brighter stage light, and simulated audience presenting distracting behavior. PLP is designed in collaboration with faculty members from the school of music and addresses a practical need to help music students identify their anxiety triggers and practice effective coping mechanisms.

Music performance anxiety (MPA) is a pervasive and well-documented challenge that musicians face both in professional and in instructional settings. While prevalence estimates vary, research shows that a substantial proportion of student musicians experience significant anxiety before or during performances (Barros et al., 2022). MPA can manifest cognitively as intrusive thoughts and self-criticism, physiologically as heightened arousal or muscle tension, and behaviorally as avoidance or diminished expressiveness (Matei & Ginsborg, 2017). For many musicians, these experiences are not exceptions, but a part of artistic practice and development. We are developing the Piano Lab Pro (PLP) pedagogical toolkit that includes a Mixed Reality (MR) environment where music students practice playing the piano while experiencing MPA triggers. Integral to this innovation are student-facing and facilitator-facing resources that provide strategies to identify and to manage MPA, and to encourage reflecting on the experience to plan for improved anxiety management. Here, we discuss the design process of the PLP MR app, explain the interactive demo session structure, and outline our next steps. Mixed reality to mitigate MPA MPA triggers are multifaceted. Evaluative pressure from juries and audience members, unpredictable distractions such as environmental noise or technical malfunctions, and heightened self-awareness during public performance can intensify anxiety (Twitchell et al., 2025). Despite the prevalence of MPA, opportunities for students to practice managing MPA triggers in authentic settings remain limited. Access to recital spaces is constrained by scheduling, staffing, and cost, while live performances involve high psychological stakes that discourage experimentation and reflection. Music educators face persistent challenges in providing low-risk, repeatable contexts where students can safely confront performance stress and develop coping strategies through experience (Blair et al., 2022). Extended Reality (XR) technologies, including Mixed Reality (MR), offer promising solutions to these challenges by recreating realistic performance environments in a controlled and supportive space. Immersive rehearsal enables repeated exposure to stress-inducing stimuli, such as audience presence, lighting, or subtle distraction, without the social or professional risks associated with live evaluation. Empirical research demonstrates that XR exposure training can reduce music performance anxiety compared to traditional relaxation can prime students to manage anxiety more effectively and allocate greater cognitive resources to task performance (Hawes & Arya, 2021). These findings suggest that simulated practice environments, such as MR, can foster emotional regulation and cognitive readiness, supporting the goals of performance pedagogy. The Piano Lab Pro (PLP) pedagogical toolkit The PLP Pedagogical Toolkit development is a collaboration between the Center for Academic Innovation and two faculty members from the School of Music, Theatre and Dance at the University of Michigan. The PLP Toolkit consists of the three components that together form a pedagogically coherent toolkit: ● Student Preparation Guide: This website is a self-directed resource that explains a range of MPA triggers, includes a self-diagnostic questionnaire for students to identify their triggers, and curates annotated resources to address MPA triggers.

● Facilitator Guide: The PLP MR app is a facilitated activity with a facilitator present to set up the PLP app on Apple Vision Pro headset, guide the students through their practice session, and debrief after the activity is completed. The facilitator guide prepares the facilitator to lead an effective PLP MR session. ● PLP MR Application: PLP uses Apple Vision Pro to immerse piano students in high-fidelity simulations of two music performance venues at the University of Michigan, offering realistic practice in highpressure performance settings (Figure 1). PLP provides exposure to performance stress, paired with external scaffolding to support the development of stress-management strategies, helping students build confidence, reduce anxiety, strengthen their stage presence, and support more effective learning. PLP MR App Design Process We adopted a design thinking approach (Dam, 2025) to develop and test the PLP. Empathizes and define A combination of semi-structured interviews with Piano students, site observations, and faculty consultations informed our design. Piano students represented diverse proficiency levels and technical skill which helped us glean a variety of preparation routines, internal experiences during performance, environmental triggers, and coping strategies in depth. For example, we noticed the importance of physical setup routines, the role of environmental distractions, and the need for pre-performance grounding strategies. We developed two personas:

(1) students majoring in Piano who experience performance evaluation as core curriculum requirements; and (2)

intermediate level musicians seeking confidence and stability despite inconsistent practice habits or moderate anxiety. To develop learner journeys, we finalized the personas’ goals in a Design Jam. Ideate We synthesized Design Jam outputs into a series of design and development discussions to develop a learner journey and ideate the structure of the activity flow. The outcome of the ideation phase was the information architecture that outlined students’ activity flow as they launch the PLP MR app and go through the performance: ● Onboarding Steps: after launching the PLP App, the student goes through o Welcome and introduction to the app o Setup the piano, sheet music, test audio, and confirms that set up is done correctly ● Venue Selection and Setup: using the self-diagnostic questionnaire, the student selects a venue ● Default Play: with no audience, no distractions, and no change in the lighting, the student can o Practice freely and become familiar with the venue. o Start a formal session via Start Performance ● Performance: the student can access Performance to o Return to Default Play using a Stop button o Cue Start Performance: house lights dim, stage lights increase, audience claps to signal Start o Play End-to-End with random audio and visual distractions o End Performance by selecting Stop and trigger audience applause ● Summary: the student reaches a Summary Page that shows the duration of the practice, distractions experienced, and an encouraging message. The student can then return to Default Play. Prototyping tools and processes We created minimal prototypes to test core interactions, gather feedback, and revise learner journey. In an iterative Build-Measure-Learn approach, we identified tasks that required user testing and task that could be simplified. Integral to prototyping were interaction design and MR environment construction processes. Interaction and experience design. While using the PLP app, students need to align the physical piano and the virtual piano. We translated the prototype into spatial layouts using ShapesXR and conducted an early assessment of the spatial clarity and onboarding usability of the PLP MR environment. Rapid spatial prototyping with ShapesXR enabled us to determine the placement of windows and instructions as students experience them inside a headset. For example, in Figure 2 a virtual box is overlayed on the physical piano keys and a virtual cutout is overlayed on the physical sheet music holder. The student receives information on how to align the virtual box and cutout with the physical piano. ShapesXR functioned different headsets, including the Meta Quest 3, giving us a broader pool of usability testers as it reduced constraints for testers who wore glasses. Figure 2 A special Layout of a PLP Viewpoint in ShapesXR MR Environment Modeling Development occurred in parallel with interaction design. Media experts and an environment artist documented two university music venues using photogrammetry, enabling the team to digitally reconstruct each space and preserve its atmosphere for immersive use in the experience. This reconstruction relied on aligning thousands of overlapping photos and performing spatial calibration to generate a high-resolution 3D mesh from the captures. The resulting high-poly scan was then optimized into a low-poly model, with high-resolution mesh detail and lighting baked into textures to preserve essential visual fidelity while enabling efficient real-time rendering on the Apple Vision Pro (Figure 3). Throughout this workflow, the environment artist and XR developer collaborated to pinpoint optimization opportunities and to calibrate the tradeoffs between visual richness and computational cost, ensuring smooth, stable performance. Figure 3 The optimized file of the Britton Hall reconstruction.

Usability testing and the revised activity flow We conducted two rounds of usability testing: (1) We focused on testing the ShapesXR prototype to understand window and instruction placement; (2) We examined the impact of environmental realism with the Britton Recital Hall 3D modeled environment, lighting control, and audience members. We revised the activity flow as follows. O nboarding and venue selection We re-ordered setup steps to first focus on high-level system setup. In this process, the student:

1. Sets up the “world position” and aligns the headset spatial orientation with their current position,

especially the distance to the floor.

2. Connects the audio component routing the music from the piano to headset’s speakers.

3. Starts mixing and aligning the keys and sheet music of the virtual piano with the physical piano.

4. Selects a venue.

1. The student automatically enters a pre-performance, or Default Play, state with the house lights up and

the crowd chatting. The student becomes acquainted with the space and mentally prepares to practice.

2. When ready, the student triggers the Performance Mode directly from the Default Play.

3. The house lights dim, the crowd quiets, and the stage lights feel brighter to isolate the student in a

sudden “all eyes on me” moment. Based on our interviews, research, and usability testing, being the spotlight is a significant performance anxiety trigger. This is a new feature in our latest prototype. Summary At the time of writing this paper, the Summary step of the activity flow is in development.

1. The student indicates that they are done with a performance.

2. The audience clap and cheer.

3. The student leaves the venue. A “Summary” page displays the duration of performance, distractions

encountered, and an encouraging statement. Students receive a copy of the summary to reflect on their performance over multiple sessions and to evaluate the impact of the experience to reduce MPA. Next steps The current version of the prototype provides more scaffolded instructions to foster students’ agency to progress through the steps. We are now refining the written instructions, editing looping videos to demonstrate actions in the headset, and building in user interface feedback.

Structure of the session We will structure our interactive demo session in two parts: ● PLP app showcase and demo: A facilitator and a student will showcase a ten-minute practice session with the PLP pedagogical toolkit. Participants will observe student’s headset view. We will highlight unique design features and innovative solutions that we developed during the project. ● Interactive discussion: We will lead a discussion with participants to examine their experiences form practice or research regarding the longevity of pedagogically useful immersive applications, such as the PLP, as MR technology constantly evolves. We will also share design features that we scaled back due to implementation limitations.
