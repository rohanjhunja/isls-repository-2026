Symposium introduction Fostering productive, connected learning across students’ school and community contexts is an essential concern for contemporary education research and practice (Darling-Hammond et al., 2019).  Grounded in the authors’ recent research on framing in education, the proposed structured poster session will explore the ways frames and framing can foster connections between classrooms and communities across diverse cultural and institutional contexts.

Most basically, frames are a kind of interpretive heuristic that answers the question about the current context: “What is it that’s going on here?” (Goffman, 1974). Because frames and framing shape how individuals think and act in any given situation, many researchers have studied their role in a variety of social sciences disciplines, including socio-linguistics (Tannen, 1993) and psychology (Hammer et al., 2005). In the learning sciences, frames are argued to be especially consequential because they activate particular sets of conceptual resources (e.g., knowledge, beliefs, values, and orientations) that shape what learners notice and remember, what roles they see themselves and others as having, and how they apply their learning generatively in new conte xts (Engle, 2006; Greeno, 2009; Hammer et al., 2005).

The ways teachers frame their practices impact what they deem important in their classes, who they see as more or less capable, and how they determine pedagogical moves they should make (Babichenko et al., 2021). The ways students frame their learning impacts what they attend to, if they feel heard and seen as contributors, and the knowledge and dispositions they carry with them between school and other areas of their lives (Engle et al., 2011; Freedman et al., 2025).  The act of invoking a particular frame or set of frames (i.e., framing) is a largely automatic and tacit process (Hammer et al., 2005). As frames are repeatedly activated they tend to crystallize and become the dominant or default ways in which learners interpret and apply their knowledge over time (Hammer et al., 2005; Louie, 2017). Traditional framings of what classroom learning looks like and how it should unfold (i.e., transmissionist views of learning and learners) tend to predominate in ways that work against ambitious teaching goals and practices (Hammer et al., 2005). However, frames can also shift dynamically moment-tomoment as teachers and students shape and navigate the discourses and activities of the class together (Louie, 2020). For analytic purposes, frames and framing can therefore provide a pathway for capturing the interplay between dominant narratives, cultural perspectives, and individual-level knowledge and experience. In terms of practice, intentional framing decisions can  be employed to promote productive interplay that pr omotes learning. For these reasons, understanding how frames can and do work in learning contexts has the potential to enhance both educational research and practice.

Despite broad consensus that frames and framing play a central role in social interaction, including learning settings, there is some lack of clarity and coherence around what frames are and how they work. Conceptually, frames are often equated with various cognitive constructs such as schema, scripts, or mental models (Tannen & Wallat, 1987). In contrast, others define them as largely artifacts of The Culture (e.g., as epistemological orientations or ideologies) (Greeno, 2009; Philip, 2011). Similarly, some researchers focus more 2492 on descriptive accounts of frames and their effects (e.g., Horn, 2007) while others seek to understand how frames can be shifted to achieve specific goals (e.g., Hand et al., 2013; Philip, 2011). Given both the pervasiveness and malleability of frames in teaching and learning, there is a strong imperative to better understand both how they function and how they can be shifted in ways that optimize learning. Some key questions include: What do we mean by frames and framing, and do our definitions cohere? What are the specific aspects or dimensions of frames and framing that are especially consequential for learning and do these vary depending on context (e.g., learning groups, settings, objectives)? Moreover, recent years have seen framing researchers increasingly expand intonew frontiers, particularly with respect to promoting more socially and intellectually ambitious learning across settings. Our goal is therefore to generate discussion around fundamental questions as well as to examine the various innovations around the use of frames and framing to advance new visions of effective teaching and learning. In this vein, the proposed symposium brings together a group of researchers whose work represents a range of theoretical perspectives (cognitive, socio-cultural, and critical), methodological approaches (conceptual, empirical, and review studies), designs (university and practice-based teacher learning; classroom and out-ofschool learning programs), and learning objectives (described below). The common thread among these contributions is that they integrate framing theory with asset-based frameworks for ambitious pedagogies, and they are all concerned with creating connected learning experiences across settings. As such, we believe this symposium will provide a rich opportunity to advance productive conversations around the potential of frames and framing research to advance the conference theme of fostering partnerships across learning ecologies. The first two posters explore the cognitive mechanisms involved in framing for shifting teachers’ noticing from deficit-to-strength-based orientations (Poster 1 —empirical) and for adaptive teaching expertise (Poster 2 —conceptual). The next two posters broaden existing theories of framing to advance equity-related outcomes, including activating pre-service teachers’ cultural assets in t heir coursework and student teaching placements (Poster 3 —empirical) and situating K-12 students’ science learning in social  justice-related community action (Poster 4— conceptual). Most of the posters include discussion of expansive framing (Engle et al., 2012), an appropriate focus, given its position as a foundational situative construct in the literature on learning and knowledge transfer. The final poster (Poster 5—literature review) reports on a comprehensive review of existing research on expansive framing in the context of both teacher and student learning. The review explores potential benefits and challenges of employing expansive framing to promote agency, equity, and generative learning and sets an agenda for further research.

During the 90-minute structured poster session, authors will first provide a short introduction to their presentations, followed by a breakout session where authors will discuss their poster contributions with the audience, and will conclude with a whole-group, facilitated discussion of questions posed by attendees. Poster 1: Epistemic forms as mechanisms for frame shifting from deficits to strengths in teacher noticing Thorsten Scheiner This investigation examines how epistemic forms facilitate systematic frame transformation in teacher professional learning contexts. Our research advances framing theory by demonstrating how structured analytical tools operate as mediating artifacts for r econstructing interpretive frameworks, specifically addressing the challenge of shifting from deficit-based to strength-based orientations in professional noticing practices. This investigation aligns with the conference theme of “partnering with purpose” by revealing how framing mechanisms can bridge research insights and practical applications, supporting teachers in developing more equitable interpretive frameworks that recognize diverse student capabilities and cultural resources. Expanding traditional framing models through dynamic transformation mechanisms Our investigation addresses a fundamental gap in framing theory by examining the specific mechanisms through which interpretive frameworks can be systematically transformed. Drawing on Goffman’s foundational conceptualization of frames as cognitive organizing principles (1974), we extend framing theory by demonstrating that interpretive frameworks operate as dynamic, learnable processes. This reconceptualization significantly expands beyond traditional models by investigating how interpretive frameworks can be reconstructed rather than treating them as relatively stable characteristics of professional practice. Collins and Ferguson’s (1993) concept of epistemic forms —representational structures that organize how practitioners analyze and reason about domain-specific phenomena—provides the theoretical foundation for 2493 understanding how representational tools serve as mechanisms for cognitive transformation. Epistemic forms function not merely as analytical instruments but as dynamic mediating artifacts that actively participate in frame reconstruction. The focal epistemic form in this study—a structured coding scheme operationalizing deficit-based, strength-based, and uncommitted frame orientations across attending, interpreting, and responding dimensions— revealed three critical mechanisms operating synergistically to support fundamental frame transformation. Three synergistic mechanisms of frame transformation Frame explication and disruption involves rendering tacit interpretive processes visible and amenable to systematic examination. Initially, deficit-based orientations were strongly evident across all dimensions of professional practice, with 30.3% of attending instances, 50.0% of interpreting instances, and 47.1% of responding instances demonstrating deficit-based framing. This prevalence illustrated how problematic frames often operate below conscious awareness, functioning as default interpretive schemas that shape professional observation without explicit recognition. For example, teachers using the epistemic form coded their observations of student work according to specific categories: deficit-based codes captured instances where they focused on what students couldn't do (e.g., “Student failed to use proper mathematical notation”), while strength-based codes captured recognition of student capabilities (e.g., “Student demonstrated creative problem-solving by using a visual representation”). The epistemic form’s systematic structure created opportunities for participants to encounter concrete evidence of their interpretive orientations, generating what they described as cognitive dissonance that motivated exploration of alternative frameworks.

Alternative frame construction represents the second mechanism, providing structured scaffolds for implementing strength-based approaches to professional observation and interpretation. Rather than merely identifying problematic orientations, the epistemic  form offered detailed guidance and concrete examples for recognizing student mathematical assets, capabilities, and reasoning processes. This constructive function proved essential for supporting systematic frame reconstruction, enabling participants to develop sophisticated engagement with student thinking processes.

Progressive frame integration constitutes the third mechanism, supporting systematic embedding of new interpretive practices into participants’ professional repertoires through repeated application across multiple contexts. The systematic nature of observed transformations demonstrated fundamental reconstruction of interpretive frameworks, with strength-based orientations increasing dramatically from 24.2% to 58.1% in attending, 23.7% to 62.8% in interpreting, and 38.2% to 76.7% in responding. Most significantly, strongly evidenced deficit-based attending was completely eliminated, suggesting deep cognitive transformation rather than additive skill acquisition.

## Theoretical implications and future directions

By unpacking the sophisticated mechanisms through which frames can be reconstructed, we provide framing researchers with a refined analytical lens for understanding how interpretive frameworks operate in professional cognition and how they can be systematically transformed through appropriately designed interventions. Our commitments, extending understanding of how framing mechanisms operate in professional learning contexts. Future research should explore how these framing mechanisms operate across diverse professional learning contexts, investigate the scalability of epistemic forms for supporting organizational frame transformation, and develop collaborative approaches for designing epistemic forms in partnership with teachers and communities. This investigation provides both theoretical insights and methodological guidance for advancing framing theory while supporting development of more equitable professional interpretive frameworks. Poster 2: Why generalizing isn’t always best for adaptive expertise: The importance of both instantiating and generalizing in framing of teaching interactions Marguerite E. Walsh, Christiand. Schunn A robust line of research in the learning sciences has centered on the role of frames and framing in learning and expertise. One prominent area explores how framing learning contexts expansively can increase the likelihood of knowledge transfer across time  and settings (Engle, 2006). Central to this argument is the idea of making connections between learning material and varying contexts for its relevance—i.e., generalizing beyond any particular application of a learned concept or skill (Engle et al., 2011). While abstraction is certainly important for supporting learning and transfer, this research has tended to overlook the role that more concrete knowledge 2494 representations can play in activating relevant knowledge resources (Walsh & Schunn, 202 6). Moreover, expansive framing by itself does not provide for how to activate resources that are most appropriate given a learners’ specific problem solving and learning needs (Engle et al., 2012). In this poster, we describe a conceptual framework for employing expansive framing (Engle et al., 2012) and strategic framing (Walsh & Schunn, 2026) in the context of supporting teachers to develop adaptive expertise. Adaptive teaching expertise is considered essential for facilitating ambitious, “student centered” instructional approaches that center student thinking at the heart of classroom activity (Resnick et al., 2015). Adaptive expertise requires integrated knowledge of situation-specific skills and broader conceptual understandings (Bransford et al.,

2005) as well as flexibility and efficiency in problem-solving, including the ability to identify finer-grain nuances

that signal when key assumptions or expectations are violated (Feltovich et al., 1997). Expansive framing supports developing adaptive teaching expertise when a teacher ’s learning interactions are framed in ways that activate many relevant prior (for transfer in) and current (for transfer out) knowledge resources. Strategic framing, on the other hand, supports developing adaptive teaching expertise when learning interactions are framed in ways that are especially useful for resolving a particular, pressing problem and advancing a specific learning agenda (Walsh & Schunn, 2026).

Our core argument is that, in order to build these adaptive expert knowledge and reasoning skills, teachers need to engage in a learning process that involves: 1) instantiating  broader pedagogical principles and goals in terms of pedagogical “specifics” (teaching moves, interactions, and lesson details); and 2) generalizing from pedagogical specifics to more general abstractions (teaching principles, patterns, and issues) that inform their purpose and relevant applications (Lefstein et al., 2020). We specifically highlight and explore applications of instantiating and generalizing for activating relevant knowledge resources across settings (i.e., expansive framing) and supporting efficient and flexible action in light of specific problem solving and learning needs (i.e., strategic framing) in a practice-based teacher learning context (i.e., guided reflection around classroom artifacts). Our focus in this presentation is two-fold. First, we problematize aspects of expansive framing that tend over-emphasize generalization at the expense of instantiation. Second, we enumerate the theoretical benefits and tradeoffs of instantiating and generalizing in expansive and strategic framing, and suggest these have important implications for planning and facilitating effective teacher learning conversations around their practice. Tripp Harris Educational institutions must foster learning transfer across culturally different contexts. To do so, educators must position learners to leverage prior knowledge, skills, and experiences they bring from the diverse social and cultural contexts they navigate outside of disciplinary learning settings. Framing disciplinary learning expansively opens pathways for helping learners transfer in knowledge and experiences from their diverse backgrounds and simultaneously prepares them to transfer their disciplinary learning to diverse cultural contexts (Engle et al., 2012). This project explored foundations for an asset-based approach for learning transfer built on theoretical synergies between expansive framing and a well-known asset-based perspective in Funds of Knowledge. This study investigated the following research question: 1) How might expansive framing open pathways for learners to engage with their Funds of Knowledge? learners draw connections between a disciplinary setting and other people, topics, times, and places beyond that setting—surfaced opportunities for participants to engage with their Funds of Knowledge. This was most salient when intercontextual links prompted them to draw on hometown and family-based experiences. These findings extend expansive framing towards explicitly asset-based ends while highlighting ways instructors can position culturally-based knowledge and experiences as valuable for disciplinary learning.

## Theoretical framework

Drawing from a situated perspective, expansive framing creates intercontextuality (Bloome, 2005) by supporting learners to bridge in-school learning with out-of-school social and cultural practices and experiences (i.e., their Funds of Knowledge) —a foundation of many asset-based pedagogies. Expansive framing also holds that positioning learners as knowledgeable authors rather than passive consumers of disciplinary concepts supports transfer (Engle et al., 2012).  In these ways, an instructional approach that integrates expansive framing and Funds of Knowledge could also function to disrupt deficit-based models that uphold culturally narrow views of participation in school (e.g., Freedman et al., 2025; Harris, 2024; Steinberg et al., 2025). The current study was part of a larger study to explore how theoretical synergies between expansive framing and Funds of Knowledge naturally emerge in practice. Pre-service teacher education served as the disciplinary context of focus given its goal of supporting learning transfer between culturally different contexts (i.e., higher education and elementary and secondary schools). 291 pre-service teachers from five different higher education institutions completed an integrative survey asking about their experiences with expansive framing and Funds of Knowledge in their teacher education coursework. 10 survey respondents also participated in a semi-structured interview. While expansive framing and Funds of Knowledge were not explicit design features of their teacher education experiences, the survey and interview protocols were designed to capture their engagement with features of the frameworks as they experienced them naturally.  For Phase 1 of this study, we used exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to explore latent relationships between survey items corresponding to expansive framing and those intended to capture experiences with Funds of Knowledge. Factor loading patterns showed no instances in which items 2497 associated with the two frameworks loaded meaningfully onto the same factor (i.e., there were no latent factors appearing to capture features of both frameworks). However, correlations between factors showed evidence of moderate relationships (r=.43) for those related to expansive framing’s intercontextuality principle and participants’ family-based experiences and out-of-school interests, hobbies, and skills. For Study Phase 2, the focus of this presentation, we conducted a thematic analysis of data from the semi-structured interviews (Braun & Clarke, 2006) to help contextualize the quantitative findings from Phase 1. During the interviews, participants discussed instances in their teacher education coursework and student teaching placements where they: a) experienced intercontextuality  by making connections to other people, times, places, and topics; b) felt positioned as authors by being empowered to voice and implement new ideas, and c) engaged with their Funds of Knowledge by drawing from socially and culturally-based experiences. Phase 2 findings elucidated multiple experiences representative of theoretical synergies between expansive framing and Funds of Knowledge. Several of these aligned with the moderate factor correlations uncovered in Phase 1—participants often discussed draw ing on their family-based experiences at home and other socially and culturally-based experiences when asked questions related to intercontextuality. In one illustrative example, an interviewee spoke at length about how their family’s socioeconomic circum stances informed their topic of choice for a class presentation. In this case, the opportunity to make connections to other people, places, and times (i.e., their parents, home, and upbringing) while completing the class project opened pathways to draw on their Funds of Knowledge (i.e., family and home-based experiences related to socioeconomic circumstances). Likewise, their Funds of Knowledge gave them a foundation for framing their engagement with the class project more expansively by offering a range of  other people, places, and times with which to draw connections to their disciplinary learning context.

These findings reinforce the notion that expansive framing is compatible with asset-based perspectives generally and Funds of Knowledge specifically. They specifically suggest that experiencing intercontextuality can lead to engagement with home and community-based knowledge and experiences while taking part in coursework and other disciplinary learning experiences. We conclude that expansive framing therefore can support learners’ engagement with their Funds of Knowledge in school when these Funds are recognized as relevant to and valuable for disciplinary learning. Conversely, this study also illustrated ways in which learners’ Funds of Knowledge can provide a foundation for experiencing intercontextuality and ultimately framing learning opportunities more expansively. Finally, while not demonstrated empirically in this study, we contend that the authorship feature of expansive framing has key synergies with asset-based views and is therefore a worthy topic for future investigation. Taken together, these findings deepen our understanding of how expansive framing and asset-based models might serve complementary functions in practice. This poster addresses these challenges by proposing an equity-oriented enhancement to expansive framing that incorporates the three tenets of Calabrese Barton and Tan’s framework for rightful presence  in science (2022): (1) “the right to reauthor rights”; (2) “making justice/injustice visible”; (3) “amplifying the sociopolitical” (p. 54). 2498 Expansive framing for rightful presence  in science leverages students' personal, community, and disciplinary expertise to democratize science participation. It views science as inherently sociopolitical and extends students’ science learning and contribution beyond traditional classrooms to places, people, products, and times that students identify as relevant to their lives and the lives of their communities. This proposal focuses on expansively framing time for rightful presence, while acknowledging that the variety of ways learning contexts can be expansively framed are multiple and intertwined, as are the three tenets of rightful presence. The conceptualization of framing time in Engle’s original model (2006) emphasizes the past and future while perhaps leaving unacknowledged the ways learners, as they are now, can consequentially contribute to social change. The definition of rightful prese nce emphasizes the importance of learner contribution in the now: students are legitimate members of a community because of who they are, not who they should be,  and their agentive contributions now broaden, or transform, the possibilities for what the fut ure can be (Calabrese Barton and Tan, 2019). Expansive framing for rightful presence communicates to learners that they are contributors to their learning community from the moment they enter the classroom. Expansive framing aims to send the message to students that their learning is “building on the past in the hopes of contributing to the future” (Engle, 2006, p. 454). Through a rightful presence lens, “building on the past” means not only building on dominant science histories or individual learners’ prior experiences but also building on the wisdom and practices of the communities, cultures, and landscapes in which students and their schools are embedded, whether nascent or centuries-old. In addition to imbuing students with “hopes for contributing to the future,” expansive framing for rightful presence invites students to see ways they can contribute to social change across scales of time –from the present, to the very near future, to more distant futures. In so doing, expansive framing for rightful prese nce broadens the possibilities for students’ learning and action. However, there is another challenge to this broadening of possibilities that framing disciplinary engagement as “building on the past” does not sufficiently address. Many formal science learning contexts and the professional fields they represent have historically oppressed learners from nondominant groups (Agarwal & Sengupta-Irving, 2019; Bang et al., 2012). In order for those learners to be fully present in ongoing disciplinary conversations, injustices must be made visible, many traditional disciplinary structures must be dismantled, and a process of rebuilding must occur. When time is expansively framed for rightful presence, teachers and students can grapple together with sociopolitical influences on and in science. This grappling itself can contribute to transformation of ongoing disciplinary conversations now and for the future. Freedman, Bianca Schamberger, Grant Chartrand, Tripp Harris This systematic review synthesizes 67 peer-reviewed publications examining the theory and application of expansive framing, a situative framework for promoting generative learning that transfers readily and widely (elaborated above). To examine how expansive framing has been taken up, tested, and extended, we conducted a qualitative synthesis guided by established review criteria. This presents a more complete review complementing the more focused reviews of expansive framing research in Freedman et al. (20 25, in equity-oriented research) and Walsh and Schunn (2025, in teacher learning).

A custom Python script identified over 2,700 Google Scholar entries referencing expansive framing or its precursor, productive disciplinary engagement (Engle & Conant, 2002). Dual coding by two pairs of authors concluded that 67 were accessible peer-reviewed publications that “engaged substantively” with expansive framing. These studies were coded for educational domain, educational level, and study type  (empirical study, extension, naturalistic uses, encouragement, or other studies). Of the 67 publications, 26 were coded as empirical studies, and were further coded by research design (comparison group, other support, or extension) and for evidence of one or more of the five theoretical explanations of expansive framing and generative learning delineated in Engle et al. (2012): (1) metacognitive engagement, (2) anticipation of future relevance, (3) recognition of prior knowledge, (4) epistemic authorship, and (5) generalization of authorial practice; they were also coded for other value outcomes. Consistent with the ICLS 2026 theme, we further determined which ones were described as or included elements of research-practice partnerships. We focused on the 26 empirical studies, searching for evidence that might convince skeptics of Engle’s five claims. However, only ten of these studies included comparison groups to show the effects of expansively framed learning vs. other approaches, and just two of them featured randomized assignment. Engle et al. (2011) provided the clearest experimental evidence, showing that students in expansively framed tutoring sessions demonstrated significantly stronger transfer. While this supported the first, second, and fourth explanations, we questioned the generalizability of their “bounded framing” comparison condition. Michelsen’s (2021) ethnography was the only empirical study that explicitly searched for evidence of all five explanations, finding evidence of the first four. We concluded that twelve studies generated valid evidence of one or more explanations, with only one study providing evidence of the fifth explanation. In ten of the studies, we found that they provided valid evidence of other valued outcomes, such as engagement and identity work. We concluded that these studies provided promising evidence that expansive framing can support generative learning. But we also admit that these based learning, which typically employ what Hickey et al. (2020) call “expert framing.” Further empirical research is clearly called for, ideally with expert framing as a comparison condition and bounded framing as  a control. Nineteen of the remaining 41 studies extended the expansive framing design principles, mostly via design-based research. These studies extended Engle et al.’s (2012) design principles in the context of equity (4) STEM instruction (3), online learning (5), teacher education (4), and other areas (3). These extensions show that expansive framing offers theory and extensible design principles for fostering meaningful and generative learning across diverse settings An additional ten studies were coded as naturalistic uses of expansive framing as an analytic lens to examine how learners and teachers organically connect ideas across contexts. Examining classrooms, museums, and workplaces, these studies showed that expansive framing can emerge naturally when participants draw on personal experience to make learning meaningful.

Eight of the publications enthusiastically encouraged expansive framing, demonstrating its growing influence across disciplines and encouraged its application. These works appeared in writing center pedagogy, literacy instruction, teacher education, and science communication, often highlighting how connecting learning to personal relevance, audience, and future application could foster deeper engagement and transfer. Reconsidering all 67 publications in light of the ICLS 2026 theme confirmed that none were explicitly described as research-practice partnerships. However, aspects of such partnerships were present in 15 publications. These included the four studies of teacher education summarized in Dohn (2021), the four design2500 based studies of online learning following from Hickey et al. (2020), and the four cross-disciplinary studies of biology and physics following from Crouch et al. (2018). They also include the theoretical essay on equity from Hand et al. (2013), the design-based studies of environmental science in Tierney et al. (2020), and the cross-disciplinary studies of chemistry and physics in Patchen and Smithenry (2013). Finally, despite the obvious and growing enthusiasm for expansive framing, our review also uncovered implementation challenges. One strand of four studies from Norway (starting with Silseth & Erstadt, 2018, coded as “other studies”) showed that educators struggled to sustain intercontextual connections in curricular structures bounded by traditional disciplinary norms.

Consistent with the vision of Engle and colleagues (2012), this review affirms expansive framing as a robust and flexible model for cultivating agency, equity, and intellectual continuity in learning. In a period of profound educational change, it offers a  research-informed approach for designing learning environments that honor learners’ histories while empowering them to imagine and author their futures.
