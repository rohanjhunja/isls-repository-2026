# Causal Reasoning Through Drawing: Explore How Students Organise Their Thoughts in Space

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study aims to explore how drawing supports students' causal reasoning when the conceptual complexity of distant attentional frames is present. Five pairs of students (ages 11-13) participated in 90-minute drawing sessions, where they attempted to link causes and effects across different attentional frames, specifically reasoning about how local food waste may be linked to the decline of polar bears in a remote region. We identified four linking strategies students came up with: gap narrowing, finding p ossible channels, identifying mediating steps, and identifying underlying players. In addition, through a multimodal analysis of video recordings, we explored students' actions and interactions with their drawings in specific episodes when they came up with these strategies to propose causal links across spatial gaps. These findings suggest that drawing serves important interactional functions extending beyond its representational one, which in these cases supported sophisticated causal thinking that resembles scientific reasoning strategies.

Reasoning about causes and effects and what links them (causal thinking) is recognized as a key skill in both educational research (e.g., Haskel-Ittah et al., 2025; Grotzer & Tutwiler, 2014) and curriculum standards (e.g., Lead States, 2013). However, this is particularly challenging when there are temporal or spatial gaps between causes and effects (Grotzer & Tutwiler, 2014). For instance, when attempting to explain how local actions, such as food waste in populated areas, contribute to greenhouse gas emissions, which in turn may affect remote polar regions, students face a fundamental cognitive challenge: the cause and effect reside in different attentional frames. Grotzer and Solis (2015) introduced the concept of "action at an attentional distance" to describe instances where spatially discontinuous causes and effects exist not merely in different physical locations, but in different attentional spaces—situa tions where perceiving both cause and effect simultaneously is not possible within ordinary experience. In everyday life, causes and effects are typically encountered in close proximity, reinforcing expectations of spatial contiguity (Grotzer & Tutwiler, 2014), and students tend to favor local explanations rather thandistant factors (Solis & Grotzer, 2019). Yet developing climate and environmental literacy requires reasoning across large spatial and temporal scales to consider candidate causal links that spandifferent attentional frames (Grotzer & Solis, 2023). Research has highlighted the benefits of drawing as a tool for dealing with complexity. As a cognitive tool, drawing brings information to one's attention, helps keep it in mind, and serves as a platform for inference, imagination, and the simulation of processes and relationships (Andrade et al., 2022; Bobek & Tversky, 2016; Sheredos & Bechtel, 2017). Yet less attention has been given to what happens while students draw—the  actions they perform on and around their drawings, the spatial arrangements they create, and the ways they interact with the evolving drawing as part of their ongoing reasoning (Wilmes & Siry, 2019). The drawing process unfolds across time and involves a range of actions and interactions, e.g., embodied gestures, material manipulations, spatial organization of drawing surfaces, which call for analytical tools capable of attending to these dimensions. A multimodal perspective on interaction (Norris, 2004) offers such a lens, foregrounding the embodied, spatially distributed, and temporally unfolding actions that constitute the drawing situation. The current study explores the proposal that drawing serves as a platform for inferring and thinking about causes and effects, involving an attentional distance. It assumes that drawing serves key interactional functions beyond its representational ones, a perspective that has received less attention in the learning sciences. This study addresses the question: What reasoning strategies emerge when students attempt to link causes and effects across different attentional frames, and in what ways do actions and interactions around the drawing situation contribute to the emergence of these strategies?

## Participants and context

We closely observed five pairs of students (four boy pairs and one girl pair) co-creating drawings as they attempted to explain the potential causal links between food waste in populated regions and the decline of polar bear populations. The participants are 11 to 13 years old (5th to 7th grade). Each pair participates in a session lasting 1217 approximately 90 minutes. The session focused on prompting students' causal reasoning when various features of complexity are present: 1) Identifying potential effects for a cause within their attentional frame; we asked: "What effects might a large pile of food waste cause, in general, to us and the world?". 2) Identifying potential causes for an effect within their attentional frame; we asked: "What might be the causes of the decline in polar bear populations?". 3) Linking a cause and an effect that reside in distant attentional frames; we asked: "How might food waste be connected to the decline in the polar bear population?". A4 sheets were intentionally provided to constrain students' drawing space rather than large chart paper. Students were told they can take a new sheet whenever they decide. When constrained by the sheet's space, students may need additional space. The sessions were led by the researchers who introduced each task and invited students to perform it. While students drew and talked, interventions were kept to a minimum. Typically when students signalled completion, a researcher approached to ask them to describe and clarify their drawings, and when needed, prompted further elaboration or challenged partial ideas using open-ended questions.

The data consists of video recordings of the pairs' interactions during drawing activities. The analysis followed a multimodal microanalytical approach (Norris, 2004), focused on drawing, with participants' actions and interactions at its center. The video data were closely examined through an iterative process involving three phases. The first phase focused on students' causal reasoning. The second phase focused on the drawing process and interactions with the drawing. The third phase unpacked "interesting" moments of students' reasoning about causality embedded in their actions performed on the drawing. In the first phase, we conducted preliminary exploration to identify and select instances of causal reasoning and examine whether they considered causes and effects that were not only local. This refers to causes or effects that span across space and time, as outlined in Grotzer and Solis's (2015, 2019) Action at Attentional Distance framework. Second, we examined the reasoning strategies students employed to link a cause and effect across spatial gaps. This analysis used an iterative process combining top-down and bottom-up approaches (Chi et al., 1997), guided by the Epistemic Games framework of Haskel-Ittah and colleagues (2026). We identified four strategies: Gap Narrowing: bringing the cause and effect into the same attentional space. Finding Possible Channels: identifying channels by which something can be transmitted from cause to effect. Identifying Mediating Steps: recognizing intermediary steps between cause and effect. Identifying Underlying Players: recognizing key players linking cause-to-effect and describing their actions. In the second phase, for each instance selected, we marked and described students' actions on the drawing, such as gestures, spatial arrangements of "physical" spaces, inscriptions, etc. Finally, key "interesting" moments were selected. After generating multimodal transcriptions of those excerpts (Norris, 2004), we described how students' interactions with their drawings facilitate the emergence of those reasoning strategies. Students create different“ physical” spaces in their drawings when a causal link is beyond their attentional frame Once students completed their drawings illustrating the effects of food waste, they were asked to draw polar bears in their habitat and to identify potential causes for their decline. All groups took a fresh sheet; some did this spontaneously, while others sought confirmation: "We do not have enough space, can we take another sheet?". In these cases, they were told, "Whatever you decide." This study explored the reasoning strategies that emerged when students attempted to link causes and effects across different attentional frames, and the ways in which actions and interactions around the drawing situation contributed to the emergence of these strategies. Our findings reveal that the drawing situation, involving physical arrangement of sheets, gestures performed over and between them, material manipulations, was not merely a backdrop to students' reasoning but actively contributed to it. Students and researchers created and used the spatial organisation of different drawing spaces, often combining a heterogeneous mix of gestures and pieces of paper, to bring into attention a cause and an effect that seemed disconnected and to organise reasoning across spatially distributed elements. Within this process, students came up with various linking strategies: narrowing the gap by bringing cause and effect into the same attentional space, proposing possible transmission channels (such as the sea or air) by which something can be transmitted across spatial gaps, identifying mediating steps in a causal chain, and recognising key underlying players. Notably, these strategies emerged in coordination with actions performed on the drawings: sliding sheets together, opening spaces between them to insert new sheets, creating virtual channels through gestures over multiple drawing spaces. The interplay between the physical and the conceptual was central: the spatial gap between sheets became a resource for reasoning about the causal gap between phenomena. We acknowledge that this is a small exploratory study and that it does not allow for fully unpacking the role of drawing in mediating the reasoning strategies students employed. The researcher's actions (positioning sheets, gesturing across gaps, prompting elaboration) were part of the drawing situation, and disentangling their specific contribution from the students' own reasoning remains a task for further research. However, our findings point to several identifiable ways in which the actions and interactions around the drawing situation contributed to the emergence of causal reasoning across attentional gaps, including the creation of multiple drawing spaces and the virtual integration of mechanisms through gestures that linked cause and effect across those spaces. This study offers implications for educational research at the intersection of causalmechanistic reasoning and drawing. If we want to propose drawing as a tool to support thinking when considerable complexity is involved, we need to attend not only to what students draw but to what happens while they draw, i.e., the actions, interactions, and material arrangements that constitute the drawing situation. This study is a contribution to this effort.
