# From Resigned to Resolute: Understanding How an Emergent Writer Repositions Himself in a Flexible Assessment Context

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The norms of constrained testing environments often limit student agency. Using interaction analysis, I illustrate a counternarrative of a pre-K student who challenges assessment norms in order to reposition himself as a writer. By studying his decisions on a micro-level, I outline how he negotiates new positioning and how he accepts semiotic mediators as invitations to shift from protocol in order to accurately display his abilities.

## Writer Repositions Himself in a Flexible Assessment Context

Erica Lee Payne, Vanderbilt University, erica.payne@vanderbilt.edu Abstract: The norms of constrained testing  environments often limit student agency.  Using interaction analysis, I illustrate a counternarrative of a pre-K student who challenges assessment norms in order to reposition himself as a writer. By studying his decisions on a micro-level, I outline how he negotiates new positioning and how he accepts semiotic mediators as invitations to shift from protocol in order to accurately display his abilities. Educators and students navigate heavily politicized tensions around testing and assessment in U.S. public schools. Due to concerns around  academic performance,  mandated benchmark standardized testing is commonplace in schools as a measure of accountability, infiltrating this type of testing even  in early childhood contexts (Paulsell et al., 2006). Unfortunately, standardized testing presents many challenges for teachers and students, among these, failing to accurately portray children’s abilities (Yoon, 2015). As a consequence, when assessment norms limit agency, they also limit the possibility of comprehensively knowing what children know. Contending with policies around uniform assessments, many educators insist that the purpose of assessments should be to help us have a sense of what students are able to do. In emergent literacy development, children learn while they repeatedly test out and revise their hypotheses (Sulzby & Teale, 1991). Therefore, it is paramount that educators advocate for student agency in order to better understand student capabilities  (Rowe, 2008). Authentic assessments, which are embedded in the familiar and meaningful contexts of student routines, serve as an alternative way to understand  the capabilities of young learners (Bagnato & Ho, 2006). This type of openness in the assessment environment (Rowe & Wilson, 2015) gives flexibility that allows students the agency to accurately portray their ability  in a meaningful and developmentally appropriate activit ies (Bjorklund, 2022). By leveraging Interaction Analysis (IA) (Jordan & Henderson, 1995), I illustrate the transformation of one student, who contests assessment norms, uses semiotic means to renegotiate the task, then repositions himself in a manner that represents his true capabilities as a writer. My analysis addresses these questions: (a) How does this emergent writer negotiate positioning in an authentic assessment context?  and (b) How do mediators play a role in constraining or promoting student agency in this context? The case comes from a year-long project with pre-K students at a culturally and linguistically diverse public early learning center in the United States. Data in the form of video, fieldnotes, and student writing artifacts  were collected. In the larger project, our team collaborated with teachers to promote meaningful writing practices. This study, Write Start! Assessments (Rowe & Wilson, 201 5) were facilitated by our research team. This assessment protocol included name-writing and caption-writing for a printed photo. The focal case for this analysis was Diego (pseudonym), a multilingual preschooler with an aptitude for creativity and confidence in letter-sounds. His case represents a familiar pattern of defeat that often occurs during assessments, one that is generalizable to other student experiences. I examined his case using a sociocultural perspective (Lave & Wenger, 1991), paying close attention to the situative context that allow ed Diego to successfully display his capabilities. This lens afforded a unique vantage point into the  ways that one student took up agency through positioning (Davies & Harre, 1990) within the constraints and provisions of semiotic mediation (Holland et al., 1998) during a writing assessment. Once this case was chosen, I used a “whole-to-part” inductive viewing process  (Erickson, 2004) and watched the video clips multiple times before further narrowing. I gathered o bservations and feedback using a protocol of IA, as proposed by Jordan and Henderson (1995). Through insights from multiple collaborative viewings, I chose to focus on video clips that afforded narrative power (Derry et al., 2010) and that best illustrated how student agency transformed the ultimate outcome of an authentic assessment event. A pattern that arose was in regard to Diego ’s various contestations, instances where he countered the assessor, as well as the traditional assessment protocol. These contestations  foreground what Diego saw as worthy of prioritizing (DeLiema et al.,

2025) and that became the central focal point for deeper analysis. Diego’s verbal and nonverbal acts of

repositioning were examined using multimodal transcripts, as well as  still frames that demonstrate d body positioning during key points in the episodes (Norris, 2004). 2632 I will highlight the findings by describing three episodes that narrate Diego’s trajectory of assessment. In the first episode, Diego begins his  May Write Start! Assessment giddy and excited. “I’m gonna draw my caption down there, just like Arnold.” He begins his name-writing endeavor flawlessly. Shortly after, when  Diego notices a mistake, his body deflates with realization of having a permanent error on his page. His contestation begins as he slouches over his paper. With no words, he begins to scribble rigorously over his work, covering the entire bottom of the page with green marker. Despite my coaxing, he continues to scribble. Confined by the parameters of what he saw as assessment norms, it seemed too late to fix his error. His agency was used to resign from the assessment task altogether, frustrated with a marked-up page that did not reflect his skills as a writer. In the next episode, Diego accepts a second chance that is quickly stifled again by the permanency of a marker mistake. When he starts to write “painting,” he  makes a mistake with th e “p” that is not apparent to  me (the assessor). Immediately, he starts scribbling over this mistake. When I contest his scribbles, his eyes glance up, confused. “I didn’t do it…”  he gazes down, continuing to color over his mistake. Still confused about the mistake, I say “You did a perfect ‘P’!” Diego looks at my mouth, speechless. He uses his marker to gesture over his mistake, redrawing the “P” as well as the spot where he accidentally added a little tail that made it look more like an “R.” In his gesture, he exaggerates the tail, hanging his head in frustration and scribbling again over the work. Once more, the norms of assessment have positioned him as a “mistake maker” and despite my encouragement to continue, Diego is defeated by the seeming irreversibility of this positioning. The final episode introduces  deviations from protocol. First, a pencil  symbolizes a catalyst for new positioning opportunities, and enables Diego to write his caption  without fear of permanency.  He smiles with pride after completing his sentence, but his gaze drifts back to his earlier mistake.  Offering a pencil highlighted the flexibility of Write Start!, breaking norms in order to  support the ultimate efficacy of student work. Diego  is fueled by the flexibility of this particular assessment. However, despite his satisfaction with his pencil-written caption, he is discontent with his previous error. This time, his contestation is twofold. While I praise his finished work, Diego scopes out his next move. Instead of restricting himself to the constraints of assessment protocol, he asserts agency by requesting a new tool, a tool not typically used for assessment. Not only this, but he agentively takes ownership over his work, this time both with his posture and his words. “Where’s the scissors that you got? I’m gonna cut this.” He proceeds to competently cut the scribbled mistake from the bottom of his photo. Diego’s newfound assertiveness reveal ed a transformation in his assumed positions and sense of agency (Holland et al., 1998), made possible by changes in semiotic means and flexibility in the assessment context. Had the narrative ended at Episode 1, evidence of Diego ’s performance would be disguised under dark scribbles and we would know very little of the writing that Diego  was capable of producing. Flexibility in the assessment context afforded a development in Diego ’s positioning that allowed a frustrating failed attempt to culminate in a dignified final product. Furthermore, the evolving stationery mediated Diego ’s composing process, first constraining, then empowering.
