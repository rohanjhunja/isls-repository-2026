# Putting the Data in MoDa: Integrating Agent-Based Modeling, Quantitative Data Analysis, and Teacher Responsivity to Investigate Complex Phenomena

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This demo introduces key enhancements to MoDa: A free, open-source web based system designed to support students in making sense of complex systems. MoDa is a domain-specific, block-based computational modeling and analysis tool that allows students to build models side-by-side to real-world data. Our latest enhancements include integrating quantitative data in the system using the Common Online Data Analysis Platform and Activity Player to support structured data-rich investigations, allowing students to use a wider variety of real-world data sources to refine their model toward reproducing important features of system behavior both qualitatively, by reproducing important visual and dynamic behaviors, and quantitatively by allowing closer comparison of patterns, relationships, and variability.

A central goal of science education is for students to interpret complex patterns in the world and reason about their potential causes. Sense-making of scientific phenomena and proposing solutions to problems often begins with finding patterns in data and attending to variability in those patterns. Students' lives are filled with such patterns (e.g., The water seemed warmer at my beach this summer-is it getting warmer everywhere?) and sources of variability (e.g., My cousins, who live across the country, aren’t noticing higher water levels-why might that be?). Once initial trends emerge, students can propose causal mechanisms to explain the observed patterns. Typically, modeling activities at the K-12 level engage students in making observations of a real-world phenomenon, constructing models that seek to explain or predict system behavior, and then validating and refining those models based on comparisons to the real-world phenomenon. Most often, such comparisons are qualitative—the simulation is compared to visual and behavioral characteristics of the real-world phenomenon (Schwarz et al., 2009), with less attention to its mathematical and probabilistic aspects. However, there is growing evidence that attending to quantitative characteristics candeepen student engagement. Analysis of statistical features of real-world data can introduce students to variability and error that can then be incorporated into models (Lehrer & Schauble, 2004). Similarly, prompting students to reason about quantitative details of simulation output, such as random noise, supports them in connecting micro and macro level behaviors of a system (Wilkerson & Wilensky, 2015). We extend this work to explore comparing simulated data to real-world data collected in situ, particularly large-scale public data streams. MoDa is a tool to support integrating computational modeling and multiple forms of data analysis, in ways that are responsive to teachers’ and students’ needs. As a domain-specific, block-based modeling toolkit with embedded support to compare students’ constructed models to real-world observations side-by-side (Wagh et al., 2022; Fuhrmann et al., under review) our latest design of MoDa adds support for quantitative and statistical Common Online Data Analysis Platform (CODAP), a web-based data analysis tool designed for middle and high school students, and widely used in schools (Finzer & Damelin, 2014). Because integrating this variety of modalities and evidence sources requires careful coordination, we are designing MoDa with a variety of highly adaptable features so that units can be adjusted in response to students’ and teachers’ immediate classroom needs. Below, we describe three aspects of this new design iteration: (1) A user interface for creating domain-specific agent-based blocks; (2) Exporting data from student models into CODAP for comparison with real-world data; and, (3) Leveraging Activity Player to support modular development and respond to teacher and student needs.

## Quantitative analysis of models

Data explorations support student reasoning about scientific relationships, patterns, and variability (Rivet & Ingber, 2017). Students engage in such explorations when they construct, analyze and use data. Because “data” can take many forms including simulation output, student-generated measurements, and public data (Lee & W ilkerson, 2018), it is crucial for students to understand how different “data-texts” can work together to explore scientific phenomena and the different functions they serve toward building models (Duschl et al., 2021). It is also critical for students to attend to variability in the data so they can reason about patterns and associated levels of certainty in a phenomenon (Lehrer & Romberg, 1996; Lehrer & Schauble, 2004). Figure 1 features a block library focused on modeling air quality, along with the resulting simulated data and real air quality data. MoDa is inspired by work on agent-based modeling (ABM) in education, and NetLogo in particular. ABM supports reasoning about causal mechanisms and probabilistic factors at the individual level, and simulates the behavior of elements in a complex system at different levels of analysis using simple probabilistic rules (de Jong et al., 2013; Wilensky & Resnick, 1999). By creating and studying these models, students can examine how population-level patterns emerge from individual-level processes (Wilensky & Reisman, 2006), supporting reasoning about mechanisms (Dickes et al., 2016; Hsiao et al., 2019) and probability (Abrahamson et al., 2006). Data explorations and ABM are complementary practices. Both data explorations and ABM are rich scientific activities that involve multiple practices highlighted in science education reforms (e.g., NGSS Lead States, 2013). Uniting these practices through a coherent technology and curriculum has the potential to support students in reasoning about patterns in complex phenomena and their underlying causes in new ways. Emphasizing the probabilistic nature of systems, and the inexact science of extracting meaningful patterns from “messy” data, also aligns with important emerging ideas in computing education (what Tedre et al., 2021 call “CT 2.0”). Though our focus is on developing scientific models from data rather than machine learning, the emphasis on deriving meaning from data, parallel processing, and assessing the “correctness” of a model as evaluating its “accuracy at modeling some selected features of a phenomenon” are foregrounded in an approach that brings together quantitative data analysis and agent-based modeling. However, integrating these perspectives and levels of analysis is not straightforward for students and requires careful design and scaffolding (Wilkerson & Wilensky, 2015). That is what we aim to do through this work.

Responsivity to teachers’ pedagogies and student ideas Introducing multiple sources of data across multiple scientific practices can be pedagogically challenging. To support teachers and students to meaningfully engage in this complex work, we aim to be attentive and adaptive to teachers’ needs and students’ ideas as they engage in this ambitious work. To do this, we draw on the notion of responsivity in technology design: Being responsive (Robertson et al., 2015) to students’ ideas through the design of a computational environment.

In prior work, we have considered how responsivity could be built into computational environments by designing for relatively rapid changes to the environment to reflect students’ ideas (Wilkerson et al., 2015). We identified multiple forms of responsivity ranging from renaming, adding, or removing existing blocks to better align with student ideas to extending out an existing blocks to enable students to pursue new investigations beyond the intended design to modifying the definition of a particular block, allowing for “finer tuning” of students’ models. We believe that attending to responsivity in this current project is especially important. When working w ith real-world data, these types of responsivity can allow students to tap into the everyday language they may use to describe patterns, explore the quantitative dynamics of systems, and model more subtle differences between otherwise qualitatively similar patterns. Moreover, because integrating quantitative data analysis and ABM is pedagogically challenging work, enabling teachers to modify the computational environment to tailor to their students’ ideas can help them better support their students. Goals and technology design To combine the scientific practices of quantitative data exploration and ABM into a flexible platform, we are integrating data explorations in CODAP (Common Online Data Analysis Platform) with ABM in MoDa into a curriculum wrapper called Activity Player (The Concord Consortium, N.D.). The latest design of MoDa runs on a lightweight agent-based simulation engine called Atomic Agents (McNeill, 2022/2025). It can export simulated data directly into CODAP using Activity Player provides a platform to house MoDa and CODAP components, enable data transfer between the two so that simulated data can be directly compared with real-world sources, and provides curricular support infrastructure surrounding these technology components. User interface for educators to create domain-specific agent-based blocks An underlying vision for the latest iteration of MoDa is to democratize access to creating and modifying block libraries to support agent-based modeling investigations in classrooms. Our conjecture is that this can enable educators and educational designers (including teachers) to support modeling investigations that are responsive to students’ ideas in ways that go beyond the research team’s intent. To support this, we have been developing a visual interface to create and adapt domain-specific blocks (Figure 2) to design a block library for a particular unit. The interface is intended to support multiple levels of responsivity so that a user without much prior coding experience can modify names of entities, variables, and behaviors in the library, or remove blocks as needed, while users with more coding expertise could also modify the code for an existing block or generate a new block. Figure 2 The Interface for Block Creation (Under Development) Besides supporting block creation, we are envisioning additional ways to make MoDa responsive to teacher and student needs. For instance, earlier implementations of MoDa enacted block unpacking: Students being able to inspect, and modify the definition of a particular domain-specific block (Eloy et al., 2025). We envision making it possible for educators to select which blocks to make “unpackable” based on which mechanisms they would like their students to explore. For instance, depending on whether a teacher wants to focus on mechanisms for ozone production and/or dissipation, they can select which specific blocks to unpack so students cando a deeper dive into those specific scientific ideas (See Figure 1). Simulating and Contextualizing quantitative data using ABMs and CODAP To enable students to compare quantitative data from their ABMs with real world data, we are building an interface to export simulated quantitative data generated by students’ ABM directly to CODAP for further exploration. This function builds on work that has embedded and exported data from simulations directly into CODAP (Finzer, 2014), further coupling such exports with real-world comparative datasets. To allow for flexible u se of these functions across contexts, students will be able to export (as relevant) one or more of the following kinds of data from their ABM: (1) Population data such as the number of a type of entity (e.g., “particles” in Figure 1); (2) Data about the environment in the form of “patch” variables; and (3) Sensors embedded in the model (e.g., a probe collecting data) or global reporter variables relevant to an activity. A central goal of the project is to support students to compare patterns in quantitative data from the real world and their ABMs for sense-making of mechanisms resulting in those patterns. In contrast to simpler systems, doing this in complex systems often involves connecting aggregate quantitative patterns to microscopic mechanisms that are described at a different unit of analysis-that of the agent. Hence, it is vital that each aggregate dataset exported from an ABM to CODAP be associated with the corresponding agent-level mechanisms that produced it. Our hope is that this can help students validate which agent-level mechanisms resulted in relevant aggregate trends that best match the real-world data. We are working towards providing this agent-level context to a dataset about the aggregate system in CODAP in two ways: (1) Enabling students to record and export visual data in the form of a video-recording of an agent-based simulation run; and, (2) Enabling students to “tag” their model code to the dataset in some way. Collectively, these can provide a context for students to compare quantitative trends in data to examine underlying agent-level mechanisms. Modular design/integration of technology components To enable responsivity to teachers’ pedagogical needs and students’ ideas, we will leverage the built-in modularity of Activity Player through both the design of the technology and curriculum. Classrooms across the United States are facing a growing challenge as recent national assessments show substantial declines in middle-school mathematics performance (NAEP, 2024). These learning gaps are especially visible in algebra—an essential gateway subject that is often taught in ways students experience as abstract and disconnected from meaningful contexts (Dougherty et al., 2015; Common Core State Standards Initiative, n.d.; Gates, 2021). When instruction lacks relevance and opportunities for exploration, students have fewer chances to develop the deep reasoning skills needed for future learning. Productive Failure (PF) addresses this challenge by engaging students in solving complex problems before formal instruction, prompting them to draw on prior knowledge, test ideas, and learn through mistakes (Kapur, 2008, 2012, 2015). Research shows this process can strengthen higher-order thinking, resilience, and conceptual understanding (Schwartz & Martin, 2004; Sinha & Kapur, 2021; Young et al., 2024). However, PF is difficult to implement because designing high-quality, open-ended, context-rich problems that balance challenge and accessibility requires substantial time, expertise, and creativity (Holmes et al., 2019; Darabi et al., 2018). We developed ProductiveMath to address this issue. As a generative-AI–powered application, it supports teachers in PF’s preparation phase by helping generate algebra problems aligned with productive struggle principles (Holmes, 2019; Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019; Seo et al., 2024). Importantly, ProductiveMath is designed to extend—not replace—teachers’ capacity by preserving teacher agency to review, edit, and adapt tasks for diverse learners, making PF more practical for everyday classroom use. Development overview The development of ProductiveMath unfolded across five studies in a design-based research approach. Each study contributes a distinct layer of insight into how generative AI can support teachers in creating high-quality PF math problems. These studies, guided by design-based research principles, gradually refined both the pedagogical framework and the practical design of the platform.

Study 1 established what counts as a high-quality PF problem. A systematic review identified five core dimensions—prior knowledge activation, sweet-spot calibration, affective engagement, multiple representations/solution paths, and open-endedness—which were formalized into a 1–3 scoring rubric for each dimension (Kapur, 2008, 2012; Sinha & Kapur, 2021). The team then used the rubric to guide prompt engineering for GPT-4o and to rate 30 AI-generated problems; human raters found high quality overall (M = 13.6, SD = 1.94), strongest in prior knowledge activation and affective engagement. Study 2 tested generalizability across grade levels and standards. Using the same prompt structure, GPT4o generated 60 problems across seven Common Core domains. Human ratings remained high (M = 13.59, SD = 1.28), again strongest in prior knowledge activation and affective engagement, with moderate variation in openendedness and multiple representations. Study 3 examined teacher perceptions of problems and the early ProductiveMath prototype via surveys and hour-long interviews with seven middle-school teachers. AI-generated tasks averaged 17.19 (SD = 2.98) out o f 25, comparable to human-generated tasks (17.43, SD = 3.55) and higher than simple problems (13.29, SD = 4.15). Teachers praised engagement and productive challenge, but noted long text, occasional vagueness, demanding reading levels, and some standards misalignment; participatory design feedback led to requests for clearer formatting, simpler language, better visuals, and scaffolding, informing prompt and UI revisions grounded in HCI heuristics (Nielsen, 1994; Norman, 2013)--more details in the next section. Study 4 evaluated the revised platform in five classrooms during full PF cycles (introduction, problemsolving, consolidation). Observations showed strong student engagement, collaboration, and resilience; student surveys (modified Intrinsic Motivation Inventory; Ryan, 1982) were generally positive, especially for effort, enjoyment, and perceived value. Teachers’ PSSUQ ratings (Gonçalves da Silva e Souza & Canedo, 2018) indicated high usability: system quality (M = 5.07), information quality (M = 4.83), interface quality (M = 4.93), though consolidation facilitation varied.

Study 5 (ongoing) expands to 20 classrooms from 2nd to 12th grade to assess scalability, learning outcomes across settings, and large-scale logs of teacher and student interactions, supporting continued iterative improvement.

Design specifications ProductiveMath (Rahimi et al., 2025) is designed around HCI (Human-computer interaction) principles (Park & Seda, 2018) and usability heuristics (Nielsen, 1994) to facilitate an intuitive and practical experience (Kheder, 2023). Nielsen’s 10 usability heuristics includes: (1) Visibility and system status emphasizes the importance of keeping users informed about what the system is doing; (2) The match between the system and the real world relies on using familiar language and concepts; (3) User control and freedom supports the user’s ability to reverse or exit unintended actions; (4) Consistency and standards emphasise familiar conventions across the interface to reduce confusion; (5) Error prevention encourages designers to anticipate and eliminate error-prone conditions before they occur; (6) Recognition rather than recall focuses on minimizing the user’s cognitive load by keeping key information visible; (7) Flexibility and efficiency of use supports both novice and expert users by providing guided steps for beginners and offering shortcuts, customization, or advanced features for experienced users; (8) Aesthetic and minimalist design focuses on avoiding unnecessary information or decorative elements and prioritizing essential elements; (9) Help users recognize, diagnose, and recover from errors highlights presenting error messages that clearly identify the problem and suggest constructive solutions; and (10) Help and documentation points to providing support when users need guidance. Each design decision we made for ProductiveMath represents one or more of these heuristics. Figure 1 illustrates ProductiveMath’s problemgeneration process. The frontend provides the user-facing interface where teachers enter problem data including grade level, difficulty, targeted concept, prior knowledge, theme, and interests, review AI-generated problems, adjust prompts for sweet-spot calibration, and finalize the problem by editing texts and choosing AI generated images. The backend contains the workflow logic that connects each stage of PF problem creation: it receives data from the frontend, formats it into prompts, routes them to GPT-4o, runs the difficulty engine, manages the iterative editing loop, and integrates results from the preprocessor including problem generation, difficulty assessment, image prompt, and support packages. The database stores all finalized outputs, including problem text, images, support packages, problem data, and difficulty scores, so the system can retrieve, update, and save PF problems in the library. The platform provides the infrastructure that hosts the system and enables coordination among components: the frontend collects teacher input, the backend processes it through prompt-preprocessors and GPT4o, the difficulty engine ensures appropriate difficulty level for the problem, and the database preserves finalized problems, allowing the entire workflow to operate as an integrated PF problem creation system. As indicated in Figure 3, the teacher is in the loop throughout the process to make sure the PF problem is suitable for their students. Intended Audience ProductiveMath is designed primarily for grades 1 through 12 mathematics teachers who want to implement PF in their classrooms. The platform can support teachers who are already familiar with PF as well as those who are trying it for the first time and need more support in designing PF problems. ProductiveMath addresses everyday classroom demands—limited planning hours, varied students’ readiness levels, pressures to meet standards, and the desire to make math meaningful rather than procedural. Rather than expecting teachers to write PF tasks entirely on their own, ProductiveMath functions as a guided design partner. Designing a PF problem usually requires teachers to juggle many things at once: difficulty level, prior knowledge, standards, story context, and likely solution paths. ProductiveMath turns this workflow into a clear sequence of guided choices. Teachers decide on grade level, domain, concepts, and themes, then the AI generates a draft PF problem, and teachers read and adjust it so that the concepts, numbers, pictures, and language are correct for their students. This workflow mirrors the mental steps teachers should take when designing a PF problem but breaks that process into guided, manageable choices instead of forcing them to construct everything at once. Every step maintains teacher agency. Content can still be edited, shortened, reworded, or regenerated with one click, so teachers keep creative control without the time burden starting from scratch. ProductiveMath supports teachers beyond problem creation by attaching a support package to each task, including likely student solution paths, common misconceptions, and facilitation tips. Teachers can also export problems as classroom-ready PDF worksheets, aligning with existing routines. In addition, the Problems Library offers a shared repository of PF-aligned tasks filterable by grade, domain, and difficulty. Teachers can browse their own or colleagues’ problems, react to them, and adapt them rather than starting from scratch—reducing planning time and building a reusable bank of story-based PF tasks. Overall, these features are designed to lower cognitive load while preserving teacher agency: teachers retain instructional ownership while gaining practical resources to support productive struggle with clarity, engagement, and relevance.

We will also include a small set of pre-loaded examples (including at least one “strong” PF task and one that needs revision) to illustrate how teacher review and editing are central to the process. To close the experience, we will invite participants to complete a brief QR-code feedback form (30–60 seconds) and then hold a short Q&A focused on usability, classroom fit, and implementation considerations across different instructional contexts.
