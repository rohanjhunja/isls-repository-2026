# TRAIL: A Human–AI Teaming Research Platform

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
TRAIL (Team Research and AI Integration Lab) is a research platform for studying human–AI teamwork and collaborative learning in postsecondary contexts. The system enables multi-condition experiments where human participants work together on a collaborative task with or without an AI teammate. AI teammate behaviors are configured through a personality interface grounded in the Big Five framework. TRAIL provides real-time team chat, automated team formation, integrated survey workflows, experiment chaining for longitudinal designs, and multi-level analytics. Its dual-memory architecture maintains coherent AI behavior across extended interactions, while rich datasets capture communication, trust, and team dynamics. This demo showcases how TRAIL functions as both a classroom and research tool, enabling controlled studies of collaboration, persona alignment, and group processes.

TRAIL (Team Research and AI Integration Lab) is an experimental platform designed to support randomized controlled trials and structured classroom studies of human–AI collaboration built upon the framework proposed in Samadi et al. (2024). The platform enables researchers to manipulate key aspects of an AI teammate’s persona, including personality traits, backstory, communication style, domain expertise, and education level, allowing investigators to study how different configurations of human-like attributes influence collaborative processes. TRAIL provides a controlled environment where small teams work together on a collaborative task with or without an AI teammate, making it possible to examine how persona design shapes trust, coordination, and communication. A detailed account of results from our pilot study conducted on TRAIL, including analyses of socio-cognitive dynamics and comparative discourse patterns between human-only and human–AI teams, is presented in Choi et al. (2025).

AI technologies in education have traditionally been positioned as tools that automate feedback, provide explanations, or help students generate and refine ideas. However, recent research suggests that large language models are beginning to operate not only as tools but also as collaborators who participate in and shape group learning processes. Atchley et al. argue that tools like ChatGPT can serve as secondary collaborators by offering feedback, generating alternative perspectives, and supporting more productive student interactions during teamwork (Atchley et al., 2024). They further contend that learners must be prepared to collaborate with nonhuman agents whose reasoning and communication patterns differ from their own, noting that higher education will increasingly require collaboration skills that extend beyond human partners. This shift from AI as a tool to AI as a collaborator highlights the growing need for platforms that allow researchers to systematically study how AI partners influence trust, communication, and team dynamics. The importance of persona in LLM-based agents has been underscored in recent work across human–AI interaction and agent modeling. Research shows that many AI systems still struggle to express human-like individuality, often producing generic or uniform patterns of communication that limit their appeal and effectiveness in social contexts. For example, Kruijssen and Emmons argue that AI agents frequently display bland and generic expressiveness and that personality expression is a key prerequisite for creating agents that feel more natural and engaging in human-facing applications. They further show that LLMs can express deterministic and consistent personalities when grounded in structured psychological frameworks such as the Big Five, supporting the development of more individualized and trustworthy AI teammates (Kruijssen & Emmons, 2025). The Big Five model, also known as the Five Factor Model, characterizes personality along five dimensions: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism (John & Srivastava, 1999). TRAIL uses this framework to parameterize AI teammate personas, allowing researchers to systematically vary personality traits and study their effects on team interaction. In related work, Samadi and Nixon (2026) examine how well LLM-based agents maintain assigned Big Five personality profiles during collaborative tasks, finding that alignment varies across traits and interaction contexts. Across related research, persona grounding has been shown to influence the fidelity and stability of LLMbased agents. Park et al. demonstrate that generative agents behave more coherently when their underlying persona is constructed from rich, structured information rather than minimal demographic cues (Park et al., 2024). Their o r short persona descriptions in predicting human attitudes and behaviors. These results highlight that shallow persona conditioning can lead to weaker or stereotyped behavior, while richer persona scaffolds yield more accurate and stable identity modeling.

Together, these findings motivate the need for platforms that allow precise manipulation of persona elements in experimental settings. TRAIL addresses this gap by integrating personality-driven AI teammates into real-time collaborative environments, with interfaces that allow researchers to configure personality traits, backstory, linguistic style, domain expertise, and educational background. By pairing these configurations with controlled assignment across experimental conditions, TRAIL enables systematic investigation of how persona alignment, personality expression, and communication style shape human–AI collaboration. The platform has been deployed in both laboratory and undergraduate classroom settings, supporting research on trust formation, interpersonal dynamics, participation equity, and team performance. Design goals and instructional needs Learning sciences motivations TRAIL addresses several challenges in studying hybrid collaboration. It allows researchers to compare humanonly and hybrid teams across controlled experimental conditions, manipulate personality-driven AI behaviors, and capture structured data linking interaction patterns to perceived trust, inclusion, and effectiveness. The platform also supports instructors who want to embed human–AI teamwork into their courses. Learning engineering perspective TRAIL is grounded in learning engineering principles that emphasize the use of multi-source, authentic data for research and continuous improvement. Each session generates timestamped chat logs, participant identifiers, and pre and post survey responses that capture communication quality, trust, belonging, and demographic information. The AI’s long-term memory summaries provide interpretable traces of how the AI interprets team dynamics. TRAIL computes behavioral and linguistic analytics using frameworks such as the Team Communication Toolkit (Hu, 2025) and Group Communication Analysis (Dowell et al., 2019). These tools identify sociocognitive roles, conversational dynamics, and participation patterns that reveal how teams coordinate and how AI personas shape team behavior. The platform supports both randomized experiments and longitudinal studies.

System architecture TRAIL is built as a lightweight, web-based platform that supports real-time human–AI collaboration, randomized assignment, and multi-condition experimental design. The system consists of three integrated components: (1) an AI persona configuration interface, (2) a real-time collaboration environment, and (3) an analytics and data export module. These components are connected through a message-processing pipeline that batches incoming messages, integrates short-term and long-term memory, and generates personality-aligned AI responses. At a high level, all participant messages sent through the chat interface pass through a batching and memory layer that integrates short-term conversational context with periodic long-term summaries. This design allows the AI teammate to maintain coherence over extended interactions without relying on full transcript replay. When appropriate, the system generates a personality-aligned response based on the configured persona, the current conversation state, and the team’s progress on the collaborative task. Notably, the AI does not respond to every message. Before generating a response, the system evaluates whether participation is appropriate based on contribution potential, conversational flow, and team dynamics, ensuring the AI does not disrupt natural humanto-human interaction. Responses are returned instantly through WebSocket communication, supporting fluid interaction across human-only and human–AI teams.

TRAIL’s backend stores complete chat histories, reflective AI memories, researcher-defined conditions, and survey data, enabling controlled studies with precise experimental assignment. Researchers can export raw or derived data for external analysis or use the built-in analytics dashboard to compare team participation, communication patterns, and condition-level outcomes. By combining a configurable AI agent, a real-time interaction environment, and structured data outputs, the architecture supports both classroom deployment and rigorous human–AI teaming research.

TRAIL also supports experiment chaining for longitudinal study designs. Researchers can link multiple experiments into ordered sequences where team compositions, group assignments, and participant nicknames persist across sessions. Each experiment in a chain can use different AI configurations, enabling within-subjects designs such as a baseline session without AI followed by sessions with varying AI personas. This capability s upports research on how sustained exposure to different AI teammates shapes trust, communication norms, and role distribution over time.

This setup enables investigation of human–AI collaboration dynamics in realistic team settings. Researchers can examine how students negotiate meaning, coordinate actions, and adapt communication strategies in the presence of different AI personalities. Studying trust and interpersonal communication in hybrid teams TRAIL allows controlled manipulation of AI persona characteristics that are difficult to replicate with human confederates. Teams interact with AI partners that vary in warmth, assertiveness, initiative, or communication style. This supports research on trust formation, psychological safety, and adaptation to AI behaviors. The dualmemory system enhances interpretability by allowing the AI to reference earlier interactions and articulate reasoning processes.

Investigating personality-driven effects on coordination and role distribution TRAIL’s persona and personality configuration tools allow researchers to examine how different AI teammate profiles influence coordination, leadership emergence, and role negotiation. A dominant persona may guide planning, while an affiliative persona may encourage consensus-building. TRAIL’s behavioral and linguistic data support analysis of how AI personalities influence team cohesion, communication flow, and sociocognitive role distribution.

Demo format and interaction plan This interactive demo will guide attendees through the core components of TRAIL and allow them to experience the platform from both the researcher and participant perspectives. The session is structured around three short segments that mirror the lifecycle of a TRAIL study: configuring an AI teammate, participating in a live collaborative activity, and exploring automatically generated analytics. Segment 1. Researcher view: Configuring an AI teammate In the first segment, the demonstrator will introduce TRAIL’s researcher interface and show how AI teammates are created and customized. Attendees will see how personality traits, backstory, communication style, domain expertise, and education level can be configured to create expressive and human-like personas. The facilitator will walk through the process of setting up an experimental condition, including team size, timing, and random assignment to human-only or human–AI groups. This mirrors the setup process used in actual TRAIL research studies and highlights how the platform supports controlled experimentation.

## S egment 2. Participant view: Live human–AI team interaction

Next, attendees will participate in or observe a short collaborative activity inside TRAIL’s real-time chat environment. Two parallel teams will be shown: one human-only and one working with a configured AI teammate. This side-by-side view allows attendees to compare how AI personas influence team interaction, initiative, and communication flow. The facilitator will briefly expose TRAIL’s dual-memory system by displaying how the AI generates and updates its reflective long-term memories, offering an interpretable look into how the AI tracks the team’s progress and dynamics.

Segment 3. Researcher view: Analytics and data capabilities The final segment focuses on how TRAIL captures and organizes data for research and instruction. Using a prerun dataset, the demonstrator will showcase TRAIL’s multi-level analytics: experiment-level summaries, condition comparisons, team-level measures, and individual participation indicators. Attendees will see how researchers can export chat logs, survey responses, and derived metrics for analysis with external frameworks such as the Team Communication Toolkit or Group Communication Analysis. This segment highlights how TRAIL functions as a research infrastructure for studying trust, communication, and socio-cognitive dynamics in human–AI teams.

## Closing and discussion

The demo will conclude with an open discussion of use cases in classroom settings and research contexts. Attendees will be invited to explore potential collaborations, share ideas for integrating TRAIL into their own studies, and ask questions about implementation or experimental design. Technical architecture TRAIL uses a microservices architecture with a Django backend, Flask–SocketIO chat server, PostgreSQL, Redis caching, and a Next.js frontend. Components are containerized with Docker for scalability and cloud deployment. More information about the platform is available at https://the-language-and-learning-analyticslab.github.io/TRAIL-landing-page/. TRAIL offers a flexible and extensible environment for studying human–AI collaboration, providing capabilities that candirectly benefit the learning sciences community. First, TRAIL supports controlled experimentation with AI teammates at a level of precision that is difficult to achieve in classroom settings or commercial platforms. By allowing researchers to manipulate persona attributes, team configurations, and experimental conditions, TRAIL creates opportunities to investigate questions about trust, communication, and socio-cognitive dynamics in mixed human–AI teams. These affordances can help the community generate new empirical insights into how AI systems shape collaborative learning processes and how design choices influence learner experience. Second, TRAIL lowers the barrier for conducting large-scale, multi-condition studies by automating random assignment, data collection, and workflow management. This makes it feasible for researchers to run replicable studies across institutions, contributing to the growing need for robust, cross-context evidence in human–AI interaction research. The platform’s structured data outputs, including chat logs, AI reflective memories, and derived communication metrics, also provide rich resources for methodological innovation in discourse analysis, multimodal learning analytics, and team interaction modeling. Third, TRAIL has the potential to support instructional innovation. As AI becomes more embedded in collaborative work, educators need tools to help students practice communication, coordination, and critical thinking in settings that include AI partners. TRAIL provides a safe, configurable environment for giving students these experiences while allowing instructors to study how learners engage with AI personas and how team dynamics evolve when non-human agents are present.

Looking ahead, future development will focus on expanding TRAIL’s analytics capabilities, integrating additional modalities such as audio or physiological signals, and enabling more advanced forms of adaptive AI behavior. We also aim to cultivate a community of researchers and instructors who can share experimental templates, persona configurations, and datasets. By supporting collaborative development and data sharing, TRAIL can serve as a research infrastructure that advances collective understanding of human–AI teaming and contributes tonew methodological and pedagogical directions in the learning sciences. Learning Black-GPT: Demonstrating the Creative Workflows of Black Artists Using Generative AI Tools Kalonji Nzinga, University of Colorado Boulder, kalonji.nzinga@colorado.edu Abstract: This demonstration presents an investigation of the creative workflows Black artists use when producing multimodal art with generative AI systems. Through screen-captured thinkaloud protocols with three artists, we highlight routines that reveal culturally grounded practices rooted in hip-hop, Black Arts Movement, and broader African-American artistic lineages. The project argues for pluralistic AI design—an approach that recognizes multiple cultural repertoires and aesthetic traditions as foundational to AI system development and use. The session features edited workflow recordings and a live audience-guided demo that together illustrate how Black cultural knowledge, sociotechnical workflows, and ethical considerations shape AI-supported creative production and offer valuable insights for AI literacy learning environments that are culturally sustaining.

AI tools are not neutral; they are designed through particular ethical and epistemological frameworks. As OpenAI CEO Sam Altman (2025) noted in an interview with Tucker Carlson, OpenAI’s goal is not to make ChatGPT act like a human, but rather to reflect “the collective of all humanity.” He argued that it is important that AI thinks pluralistically, and does not just represent one set of parochial values. At the same time, the ChatGPT model spec is a publicly shared document outlining the rules for behavior for Open AI models, and it is far from the value neutral goal Altman is hoping for. The rules and values in the model spec reflect the priorities and biases of its authors. This critique has longstanding precedent in the work of researchers such as Ruha Benjamin (2019, 2024), who point out algorithmic biases within AI systems.

The field has understandably focused on algorithmic bias, but the most promising new directions in learning sciences are not only critical—they are also imaginative. Tanksley (2024) advocates for “critical race algorithmic literacies” as tools to reimagine algorithms for liberatory purposes. Lizáraga (2023) likewise documents how communities of color enact “cyborg sociopolitical reconfigurations” through creative engagement with emerging technologies. We also draw on Saidiyah Hartman’s (2008) concept of critical fabulation, which invites us to imaginatively fill in the silences and erasures in archives. Generative AI becomes a tool to produce visual, sonic, and textual counterhistories that "unforget" the past and imagine the yet unborn. This shows the importance of investigating how pluralism is or is not encoded into AI systems and how various diverse communities engage with those systems in a multiplicity of ways. Users prefer different functionalities based on their cultural contexts. Specifically, AI artists engage with functionality based on the cultural histories of artmaking they emerge from. For example, African-American creatives might desire algorithms and routines informed by the aesthetics and ethos of African-American art history, including the Harlem Renaissance, the Black Arts Movement, and hip-hop. By studying AI creativity within Black expressive traditions, this project contributes to a pluralistic understanding of AI—one that recognizes how cultural contexts shape the ways people think, learn, and innovate with intelligent technologies. By examining the workflows of Black AI creatives, we deepen understanding of how communities innovate within, adapt, and reshape sociotechnical infrastructures.

## Conceptual framework

Generative AI has particular potential to revolutionize arts and creative education (Zhou & Lee, 2024). Artists who use these systems engage in forms of problem-solving, pattern recognition, and reflective abstraction that parallel higher-order thinking skills central to human cognition. These artists thus embody habits of mind that are of keen interest to cognitive scientists and educators. However, previous studies of AI artistry have largely overlooked how cultural traditions—with their unique ways of making meaning, improvising, and reinterpreting—shape these human–AI interactions.

This study addresses that gap by focusing on Black AI creatives, who draw from African and African American lineages of expressive innovation. Prominent educational researchers focused on minoritized learners have long looked to Black artivists as exemplars of the intellectual dispositions schools should nurture. Carol Lee’s (2004, 2008) theory of cultural modeling draws from Toni Morrison’s literary genius to design pedagogy that leverages the deep interpretive practices embedded in Black linguistic and expressive traditions. Similarly, scholars of hip-hop pedagogy (e.g., Alim, 2011; Love, 2019; Nzinga, 2023) have turned to hip-hop artists and t heir cultural practices as models for culturally sustaining curricula. These approaches share the belief that Black cultural production is not only artistically rich, but intellectually generative—that the cognitive habits required to make resonant, socially conscious art are themselves pedagogical gold. Black AI creatives represent a new generation of artists whose habits of mind act as informative mental models for what should be nurtured in educational settings. This study therefore draws upon methods within the learning sciences of studying expertise (Chi et al., 2014) in which researchers try to develop a cognitive map of experts within a certain domain, in order to inform how this particular skill should be cultivated in novices. The work of Black AI creatives exemplifies what comedian Karlous Miller (2025) called Black-GPT— a movement of artists and technologists using generative AI to extend the archive of the Black radical imagination. The participants in this study will be prominent artists within the Black-GPT milieu, including a few that our team has previously collaborated with:

• Jidenna, a Grammy award winning music performer who has produced Oga City, a generative AI film

series drawing from his Nigerian-American heritage, using tools such as Kling, Veo, RunwayAI, and Higgsfield.

• Jelie, a multi-genre music producer integrating AI tools like Playbeat 4 as a generative drum sequencer,

exploring how machine improvisation intersects with hip-hop production.

• Kalonji Nzinga, the project’s principal investigator, who has used Midjourney, OpenArt, and Veo to

produce video works reimagining African and African-American mythologies (such as the resurrection of Osiris) as symbolic music videos for his hip-hop compositions. These artists illustrate how generative AI can be used to remix cultural archives, visualize ancestral narratives, and prototype new aesthetic grammars. Their creative processes reveal cognitive patterns of improvisation, call-and-response prompting, and mythopoetic reasoning—habits that are simultaneously artistic, intellectual, and educational.

We conducted screen-captured cognitive think-aloud sessions with three Black artists as they produced multimodal art using text, image, video, and audio-based generative AI systems. Artists narrated their decisions while referencing historical works, iterating prompts, adjusting parameters, and combining tools into multimodal production pipelines. Our methodological focus was on the workflow itself as a sociotechnical unit of analysis— capturing how cultural knowledge, technical skill, and improvisation interact across tools and modalities. ## Implications for learning sciences

This work contributes to understanding culturally sustaining AI literacy. By foregrounding Black creative workflows, we illustrate how AI systems can become generative spaces for metacognition, creative inquiry, and cultural modeling. The findings demonstrate the importance of grounding AI learning environments in pluralistic design principles and community cultural wealth. These findings elucidate design principles that inform the building of 1) generative AI tools 2) the creative workflows that incorporate these tools and 3) the learning environments that cultivate AI literacy.
