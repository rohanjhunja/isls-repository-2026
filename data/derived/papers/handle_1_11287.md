# Knowledge and Agency: The Reciprocal Development of Interdisciplinary Teaching Practice

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores the reciprocal relationship between the development of interdisciplinary teaching knowledge and Teachers' Professional Agency (TPA) in an interdisciplinary pedagogical context. Through a case study at an Israeli high school, we examine how teachers expand their knowledge and adapt their teaching practices to navigate uncertainty, foster collaboration, and innovate within interdisciplinary settings. Our findings suggest that the development of interdisciplinary knowledge activates TPA, creating a dynamic cycle where each element reinforces the other. These insights highlight how interdisciplinary contexts can support both teacher agency and knowledge development in education.

## Introduction

In today’s rapidly evolving educational landscape, interdisciplinary pedagogy has become a powerful approach for fostering deeper learning and student engagement. As educators strive to prepare learners for the complexities of the 21st century, integrating knowledge across disciplines has become increasingly vital. This shift challenges conventional teaching practices and calls for reevaluating teachers’ professional agency. Teachers’ Professional Agency (TPA) refers to educa tors’ capacity to make intentional choices, engage in decision-making, and bring about meaningful change in their professional environments. While the literature has emphasized how various contextual factors shape TPA (e.g., Cong-Lem, 2021; King & Nomikou, 2018), most studies have focused on traditional, subject-specific settings. As a result, relatively little i s known about how teachers enact agency in interdisciplinary environm ents—contexts that often in volve unfamiliar content, new collaborative dynamics, and innovative pedagogical demands. This study addresses th is gap by investigating how TPA manifests in interdisc iplinary teaching. Specifically, we ask: (1) How do teachers exercise professional agency in interdisciplinary teaching contexts? and

(2) What is the relationship between teachers’ development of integrative teaching knowledge and their expression

of professional agency?

We examine these questions through a case study of an Israeli public high school implementing an interdisciplinary pedagogy across three subject clusters over two academic yea rs (2018–2020). Using qualitative methods, we explore how teachers navigated this initiative and how their knowledge and agency evolved through the process.

Building on our previous work conceptualizing Knowledge for Integrative Teaching (KIT), we offer new insights into how KIT and TPA interact in interdisciplinary contexts. While prior research has acknowledged that teacher knowledge and agency are related c onstructs (Eteläpelto et al., 2013), our study unco vers the specific mechanisms that link them in practice. We show that  interdisciplinary teaching fosters a distinctive, reciprocal relationship between KIT and TPA through three inte rrelated processes:  Navigating uncertainty, building collaborative agency, and fostering an Innovative Mindset. Teachers need to create new pedagogical approaches that simultaneously drive knowledge expansion and the enactment of agency. This reciprocal dynamic creates a self-reinforcing cycle: expanded knowledge enables meani ngful agency, and the exercise of agency further deepens and refines interdisciplinary knowledge. Our findin gs have important implications for educational policy, tea cher preparation, and professional development. The y suggest that knowledge and agency should be treated as inte rconnected constructs and explored within specific pedagogical contexts. Considering the growing emphasis on interdisciplinarity in educ ation systems worldwide, understanding how to support teachers in such environments is both timely and essential.

## Literature review

We rely on  three bodies of literature: Teachers’  Professional Agency, interdisciplinary teaching, and teacher knowledge.

Teachers’ professional agency (TPA) TPA refers to teachers’ capacity to make intentiona l decisions and enact meaningful change within thei r professional contexts (Biesta & Tedder, 2007; Toom et al.., 2015). It includes both individual and collective dimensions (Eteläpelto  et al., 2013) and is shaped by institutional struct ures, professional cultures, and opportunities for collaboration (Cong-Lem, 2021; King & Nomikou, 2018). While supportive environments foster agency, restrictive systems may constrain it.

Interdisciplinary teaching Interdisciplinary teaching integrates knowledge and  methods from multiple disciplines to deepen unders tanding and tackle complex issues (Tarrant & Thiele, 2017).  It aligns with the growing emphasis on 21st-century skills, such as critical thinking, collaboration, and probl em-solving (Lenoir & Hasni, 2016). It also  increases student engagement by connecting learning to real-world contexts (Boix Mansilla, 2016). However, its implementation is often challenged by rigid timetables, subject-based assessments, and structural silos (Termaat, 2023). These challenges demand adaptability and innovation, maki ng interdisciplinary teaching a fertile ground for the development of teacher agency.

Knowledge for interdisciplinary teaching (KIT) Interdisciplinary pedagogy requires a rethinking of  teacher knowledge frameworks. While Pedagogical Co ntent Knowledge (PCK) (Shulman, 1987) has shaped understa ndings of expertise within disciplines, it offers l imited guidance for cross-disciplinary teaching. To address this, we proposed the Knowledge for I ntegrative Teaching (KIT) framework (Resnick & Ben David Kolikant, 2022), which includes (1) Expanded Content Knowledge (ECK): Beyond subject-specific expertise, (2) Expanded Pedagogical Knowle dge (EPK): Strategies for teaching integrated content, and (3) Expanded Connectedness Knowledge (ECNK): The ability to identify and build crossdisciplinary connections.

## Research context and data collection

This study was conducted at an Israeli public high school implementing an interdisciplinary pedagogy i nitiative across three subject clusters: Humanities (Literatu re, History, Language Arts), Sciences (Biology, Che mistry, Physics), and Social Studies (Civics, History, Geog raphy). The school supported the initiative with de dicated weekly planning time and flexible learning spaces.

Data were collected over two academic years (2018–2020) and included: 38 non-participant observations of weekly curriculum devel opment meetings (13 Social Studies, 16 Science, 9 Humanities), each 45–90 minutes long, three end-of-semester reflection meetings, semi-structured interviews with teachers and administrators,  and documentation such as lesson plans, instructional ma terials, and student work samples. All meetings were audio-recorded with detailed field notes. This paper focu ses on six teachers —two from each cluster—representing a range of experience levels (4–23 years).

## Data Analysis

We conducted a thematic qualitative analysis, movin g from data organization to interpretation (Creswel l, 2013). Audio recordings of team meetings, reflection sessi ons, and interviews were fully transcribed.  Both researchers independently read all the data to gain a holistic understanding and identify initial patterns. We then applied an iterative open-coding process. Early codes captured aspects of teachers’ experiences, including decision-making, adaptation, and collaboration. Through constant com parison, these were grouped into broader categories. For example, codes like “adapting content,” “fragile knowledg e,” and “working through uncertainty” were clustere d under the theme Navigating Uncertainty.

To strengthen analytical depth, we focused on how knowledge development and agency appeared in what teachers said and how they interacted—how they posed questions, reframed problems, or negotiated meaning with colleagues. This micro-level lens helped surface instances where teachers’  epistemic agency was expressed through collaborative engagement and adaptive thinking. After initial coding, we synthesized themes across data sources. This process yielded three overarchin g themes at the intersection of KIT and TPA: Navigating Uncertainty, Building Collaborative Agency, and Fostering an Innovative Mindset. Each theme was defined by the conditions that sup ported agency and how agency shaped interdisciplinary knowledge.

Both authors coded the data independently to ensure  rigor before discussing discrepancies and aligning interpretations. We applied triangulation by confirming that themes were evident across different clusters, sources, and data types (Kern, 2018; Smith, 2018).  Quotations were selected based on three criteria: (1) clarity in illustrating the theme, (2) representation of diver se clusters, and (3) explicit linkage between knowl edge development and agency. Each quote is identified by  cluster (Science [SC], Social Studies [SSC], or Hu manities [HC]) and data source (Team Meeting [TM] or End-of-Semester Reflection [ESR]).

## Findings

Our analysis shows that Knowledge for Integrative T eaching (KIT) both enabled and shaped Teachers’ Professional Agency (TPA). Interdisciplinary teaching pushed teachers beyond traditional boundaries, prompting them to make decisions, collaborate, and innovate. This process activated their agency through three k ey mechanisms: navigating uncertainty, building collaborative agency, and fostering an innovative mindset. Navigating uncertainty Developing KIT required teachers to operate in unce rtain, evolving teaching contexts. Without predefin ed curricula, they had to make informed decisions abou t content integration and balance disciplinary dept h with interdisciplinary coherence. One teacher n oted, “You have to be more flexible about your content… s kipping a certain topic was not the end of the world” (HC, ESR, Teacher 6). This flexibility fostered ownership. One teacher said, “We are all the CEOs of this thing; everyone here is responsible for the final product” (SSC, ESR, Teacher 3). Teachers grew more comfortable taking risks and  accepting failure as part of the process: “You need to be prepared that sometimes things will not work, and i t is all part of the learning process”  (SC, ESR, Teacher 1). Navigating changing cluster structures and team dyn amics pushed teachers to actively shape what and ho w they taught rather than implement existing materials. These conditions cultivated agency through purposeful decisionmaking and pedagogical adaptation. Building collaborative agency Interdisciplinary teaching fosters a distinct form of collaborative agency. Teachers needed to co-construct understanding across disciplinary boundaries, which required continuous communication and joint planning. One teacher shared, “There is a need for coordination, sharing, and pla nning. It is daily and very demanding. Communication is key” (HC, ESR, Teacher 4). Collaboration was not limited to logistical alignment but extended to epistemic negotiation. Teachers engaged with eac h other’s disciplinary frameworks to co-design instructional materials: “We redesigned the assignment based on this exchang e” (SC, TM, Teacher 2). This exchange of expertise shaped both content and pedagogy.  The shift to shared practice was transformative. On e teacher reflected, “The interdisciplinary teacher is, first of all, a teacher who knows how to work in a team… in your class, it is j ust you and the class, and this is a very big chang e” (HC, ESR, Teacher 6). Another emphasized mutual growth: “You need to give yourself to the task and also to help your colleague grow” (HC, ESR, Teacher 5). This ongoing collaboration became both a structural  necessity and a source of professional learning, w ith teachers’ collective agency enriching and expanding their KIT. Fostering an innovative mindset The intersection of KIT and agency enabled teachers  to experiment and innovate. Without linear curricu lar models, teachers had to design new approaches for integrating content. One teacher explained, “Normally when I develop lessons, it is a linear process… but there is no linear progression within the cluster” (SC, ESR, Teacher 2). The assessment  was a significant site of innovation. Traditional methods did not cap ture interdisciplinary learning, prompting teachers to develop new tools that assessed both disciplinary mastery and integrative thinking. In this way, teachers redefined what counted as success and expanded thei r pedagogical repertoires. Designing new teaching strategies became an agentic act. Teac hers exercised creativity and professional judgment  in ways that traditional, discipline-specific settings rarely demanded. Through this pro cess, they expanded their KIT and enacted agency simultaneously.

## Discussion

This study explores the reciprocal relationship between Knowledge for Integrative Teaching (KIT) and Teachers' Professional Agency (TPA), revealing how these cons tructs are deeply intertwined in the context of interdisciplinary pedagogy. Our findings show that the expanded knowledge demands of KIT compel teachers to navigate uncertainty, collaborate across disciplina ry boundaries, and develop innovative teaching prac tices. In turn, these agentic actions contribute to the devel opment and refinement of KIT, forming a dynamic, se lfreinforcing cycle where knowledge and agency continuously inform and enhance one another. These insights extend existing theoretical framewor ks in several important ways. First, they challenge static conceptions of teacher knowledge by positioning knowledge as both a precursor to and a product of agentic action. Second, the study highlights the significan ce of interdisciplinary teaching as a context that fosters what might be termed a boundary-crossing collaboration. Unlike conventional collaboration within discipli nes, this form of collaboration requires teachers tonegotiat e epistemological differences while co-constructing new pedagogical approaches.

This study also contributes to the literature on TPA by emphasizing interdisciplinary pedagogy as a key contextual factor shaping agency. While previous research has identified a range of influences on TPA—including beliefs, emotions, and institutional support (e.g., Vähäsantanen, 2015; Pyhältö et al., 2015; Imants & Vander Wal, 2020)—our findings suggest that the interdisciplinary con text itself plays a catalytic role in enabling agen cy. In our case, administrative support and the structural  affordances o f the cluster-based model granted teachers the autonomy to shape their own pedagogical models. Thi s autonomy created space for intentional action and professional growth.

Moreover, the study demonstrates that TPA is not a fixed attribute but a dynamic quality that evolves through the development and application of KIT. As teachers adjust curricula, design new assessment me thods, and refine their pedagogical practices to mee t interdisciplinary demands, they actively enact and expand their agency. The reciprocal relationship observed here s uggests that agency and knowledge are not merely co-occurring but mutually generative.

These findings carry important practical implicatio ns. First, they underscore the need for teacher education and professional development programs to treat knowledge and agency as interconnected constr ucts. Programs that focus solely on content or pedagogica l knowledge may fall short unless they also attend to cultivating teachers’ capacity for intentional action and adaptive decision-making. Preparing teachers to thrive in interdisciplinary contexts requires a dual emphasis: equipping them with expanded forms of knowledge a nd fostering the agency necessary to apply it in complex, uncertain environments. Second, the findings highlight the pivotal role of school leaders in creating environments that suppor t both autonomy and collaboration. By providing dedic ated time and space for co-developing interdisciplinary curricula and experimenting with new pedagogical ap proaches, leaders can establish the condition s for professional agency to flourish. Additionally, reth inking traditional subject boundaries and fostering  cultures of shared expertise are essential for sustaining interdisciplinary innovation. While this study offers valuable insights, it is li mited by its focus on a single school context. Futu re research should explore the KIT –TPA relationship across various educational settings, including age groups, disciplines, and cultural contexts. Longitudinal st udies could further illuminate how this relationshi p develops over time and how it impacts both teacher learning and student outcomes.

In conclusion, this study contributes to a deeper u nderstanding of the reciprocal relationship between Knowledge for Integrative Teaching (KIT) and Teache rs' Professional Agency (TPA). By recognizing and nurturing this dynamic interaction, educational lea ders and policymakers can support teacher growth, f oster pedagogical innovation, and better prepare educator s to meet the challenges of interdisciplinary teach ing in the 21st century.
