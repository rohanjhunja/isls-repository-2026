# Knowledge Network for Innovations in Learning and Teaching (KNILT): Teacher Learning Through Knowledge Creation

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This research engages teachers as knowledge builders who create minicourses on the Knowledge Network for Innovations in Learning and Teaching (KNILT; https://knilt.arcc.albany.edu/) to support peer teacher learning around authentic issues of educational practice. A content analysis of 42 teacher-designed minicourses examined the types of knowledge addressed and the teaching methods employed to support teacher learning. The findings show that the minicourses devote substantial effort to helping teachers deepen their knowledge of teaching, student learning, curriculum content, and technology, with rich co-occurrence among knowledge themes and learning activities. KNILT operationalizes a learning-as-creating model for professional development, positioning teachers as knowledge builders who advance both their own and their peers’ pedagogical expertise through iterative minicourse design and refinement.

To prepare students for a rapidly changing world, education needs to develop new classroom practices that engage students in creative knowledge work as a collaborative community (Chinn & Duncan, 2021; Paavola et al., 2004). To develop such practices, teach ers themselves must have opportunities to engage in creative knowledge work with colleagues so that they can embrace an identity as professional knowledge builders (Chan, 2011; Teo, 2023; Zhang et al., 2011; Vokatis & Zhang, 2016). Unfortunately, many current teacher professional development programs prioritize content delivery by external experts, which tends to limit teachers’ creative input and has shown limited impact on instructional practice and professional growth (Ball & Cohen, 1999; Borko, 2004; Darling-Hammond & Richardson, 2009; Wilson & Berne, 1999; Yoon et al. 2007). The field thus needs to explore new ways to create participatory teacher learning environments that encourage teachers’ agency for addressing authentic challenges and working on innovative learning designs drawing upon personal experiences and distributed expertise (Günal-Aggül et al., 2024).

The research project reported in this paper aims to create an open and participatory environment that engages teachers as pedagogical knowledge builders. Teachers create knowledge artifacts for peer teacher learning, focusing on authentic issues in educati onal practice. Building on research on professional knowledge-building communities, our design supports teachers in co-designing innovative practices and pursuing continual improvement through data-informed reflection (Chan, 2011; Ma & Scardamalia, 2022; Popp & Goldman, 2016; Teo, 2023; Zhang et al., 2011). As guiding principles, our design foregrounds teacher agency for addressing authentic problems /needs through knowledge-building efforts. They share their knowledge and insights through designing public knowledge artifacts for peer teacher learning. The  knowledge artifacts are structured as minicourses, each with  3-5 learning units moving from pedagogical principles (know-what, knowwhy) to practice (know-how in classroom context), using design as a ladder for principle-based practical problemsolving in education (Bereiter, 2014). A participatory, open-access environment is design ed to support teacher contribution and peer learning: Knowledge Network for Innovations in Learning and Teaching (KNILT, https://knilt.arcc.albany.edu) KNILT is a long-term design-based research project implemented  in an online teacher education program (MS-CDIT) at the University at Albany. The teacher lear ners participating in KNILT carry diverse expertise as in-service teachers, college instructors, technologists and designers, librarians, and other professionals. Over 700 minicourses have been created to support  teacher learning in high-need areas related to pedagogical innovation, curriculum change, new assessments, and technologies.   Initial designs of the minicourses were reviewed by a small group of peer teachers for feedback. After completion, the minicourses are shared on KNILT and continually used for peer teacher learning in the MS-CDIT program. The minicourses are further made freely available to educators from all around the world. In the context of the KNILT, the current study intends to investigate how teachers build public knowledge in the form of minicourses for peer teacher learning.  Our research questions ask: What kinds of teacher knowledge is addressed in their minicourses, using what teacher learning activities? Related to principle-based 429 practical problem-solving among teachers, our analysis looks at how the minicourses connect  pedagogical principles with classroom practice through developing principled practical knowledge (PPK): coherent principlebased accounts that offer practical guidance for classroom teaching (Bereiter, 2014).

## Method

Dataset From KNILT’s more than 700 minicourses, we drew a purposive sample (Creswell & Poth, 2018) of 42 minicourses created in the five most recent semesters. Selection criteria focused on K –12 contexts and ensured coverage of multiple learning areas, including S TEM, literacy and language, social-emotional learning, new technologies, and special education.

## Data analysis

The main data source was the unit pages of the minicourses, supported by the design process documents created by the minicourse authors. We conducted systematic content analysis at the learning unit/module level. Drawing on trathe related literature (Bereiter, 2014; Darling-Hammond et al., 2024; Mishra & Koehler, 2006), the coding scheme addressed three dimensions: (1) areas of teacher knowledge—seven level 1 categories (Knowledge of Learning, Learner, Teaching, Teacher, Content Knowledge, Technology Knowledge, and Context/World Knowledge) with 168 level 2 subcategories; (2) types of teacher learning ac tivities—f our level 1 categories (Sources, Engagement, Reflection, Assessment) with 19 subcodes; and (3) theory-practice connections (principled knowledge, practical knowledge, and integrated PPK).

During the codebook development,  two coders first independently coded 15 minicourses through open coding, followed by three iterations of discussion and consolidation. Formal intercoder reliability on 10 minicourses yielded Cohen's κ = 0.82 for knowledge (range 0.76–0.89) and κ = 0.85 for activities (range 0.79–0.91), indicating substantial-to-near-perfect agreement (Landis & Koch, 1977). Ambiguous segments in the remaining corpus (n = 23, 2.6%) were resolved through coder discussion. Co-occurrence matrices  and PPK distribution visualizations  were generated in MAXQDA (VERBI Software,  2026) to examine how  different types of knowledge and activities are integrated in each minicourse. Theory-practice connections across minicourses Principle knowledge (guiding theories and principles) appears in 100% of minicourses, practical knowledge in 93.3%, and PPK in 80%. Most minicourses follow a principle → practical → PPK sequence (86.7%), moving from principle-based knowledge to practical strategies in contexts to creating principle-based designs for practical problem solving. For instance, the minicourse Utilizing Graphic Organizers  illustrates the deepening process: Unit 1 builds principled knowledge (what a graphic organizer is and why it works), Unit 2 addresses practical knowledge (how to create one with digital tools), and Unit 3 culminates in PPK —applying graphic organizers across grade levels in authentic clas srooms, with the explanatory accounts presented in Units 1-2 guiding the design work in Unit 3.

To advance designs of teacher learning through knowledge creation, this study examined 42 minicourses created by teachers on KNILT for peer teacher learning.  In line with KNILT’s guiding principles, the findings show that the teacher authors were able to create public-facing minicourses to address a wide range of knowledge needs among teachers (Figure 1), such as those  highlighted in the literature (Bereiter, 2014; Darling-Hammond et al., 2024; Gavrilas & Kotsis, 2026). The minicourses help teachers deepen their knowledge of teaching, learning, disciplinary/interdisciplinary contents, and new technologies  as related to their work with different student populations. A less addressed area of knowledge is to better understand the local and global contexts of teaching. Since the minicourse  designers are themselves teacher learners and practition ers, their field-based experience helps them to understand their peers’ needs and create accessible knowledge resources and learning activities. A rich set of teacher learning activities are involved to help peer teachers learn with the minicourses (Figure 2). The co-occurrence analysis reveals how the different activities are used in combination. The read-watch-make rhythm provides a learning flow in which teachers digest new knowledge through close reading, analyze lesson examples and classroom cases, and then work on authentic design to improve their classroom practices. In response to the persistent chasm between theory and practice in teacher education, the teacher-designed minicourses demonstrate productive patterns for integrating pedagogical principles with classroom practice. The flow of learning units in most of the minicourses allows teacher learners to bui ld on principle-based knowledge to learn new practical strategies in specific contexts and eventually create principle-based designs they may adopt to improve their future teaching. Teacher designers with graduate-level education training seem well-positioned to synthesize  different layers of knowledge and create peer learning  materials that support PPK  development, which  is essential to adaptive teaching and continual improvement (Bereiter, 2014). As limitations, the data analysis  only provides a  general view of the content and activity features in  a small sample of the KNILT  minicourses. Our ongoing analysis will further expand the analysis and  examine teachers’ professional design and knowledge-building efforts that lead to the minicourse creation. Future upgrading of the KNILT environment will also integrate tools to better support teachers’ instructional design, including using generative AI as design partners to analyze authentic needs, synthesize the field’s best knowledge and practices, and create engaging learning activities that support teachers’ pedagogical sensemaking and design.
