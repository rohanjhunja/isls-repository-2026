# Learning Lab Methodology for Expansive Learning and Inclusive Education During the Covid-19 Pandemic in Brazil

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The global policies of inclusive education often ignored local knowledge and cultural-historical ecologies. As a result, the top-down policies become either irrelevant or oppressive. This study presents a formative intervention study, Learning Lab, conducted in Brazil to design a new system at specialized school for blind and visually impaired students. Fourteen practitioners engaged in nine meetings with the final goal of producing a new system of inclusive education for students with multiple disabilities during the COVID-19 Pandemic. We will present the expansive learning actions, which educators took as a conduit for critical dialogue, collective agency and expansive learning for designing the future of their school.

Objectives The global movement of inclusive education “as a global travelling policy and practice” (Singal, 2016, p. 7) has often ignored local social-historical-spatial contexts that leads to practices that do not hold or hav e unintended results. A seemingly unifying set of international recommendations to ensure inclusive education for children with disabilities will take different flavors mediated by culture in specific contexts in Global South (Artiles & Dyson, 2005). In Brazil, for instance, cultural and historical as pects of education (e.g., quality of public school system and political hegemony of specialized school organizations) shaped an initial full inclusive education agenda to a different direction (Mendes, 2019). Though inclusive education legislation asserted students with disabilities the right to access age-appropriate regular education c lassrooms, it also established a continuum of provi sions for those students whose intense and continuous support as well as significant curricular adaptations was beyond what other schools can provide.

Specialized schools for the blind and visually impaired in Brazil Following a national inclusive education policy, the number of blind students with additional disabilities gradually increased in Brazilian specialized schools for the blind and visually impaired (Mendes, 2019). Interna lly, the specialized schools for the blind and visually impaired discontinued a self-contained program for blind students with additional disabilities (e.g., autism, intellectual disabilities, and cerebral palsy) to place them in the regular classroom having access to general curriculum conte nt alongside peers with visual impairments. On the other hand, re-structuring of the public regular school system also meant the placement of students with disabilities in the regular classrooms with specialized educational  services support. However, in the hierarchy of dis abilities, inclusion of students with significant support needs is met with resistance. Though schooling of students with significant disabilities, historically segregated within segregation, is an uncharted terrain for both specialized and regular schools, they are typically sent to specialized schools taking for granted their collective self-efficacy for addressing the emotional, social, and academic needs of students with significant disabilities (Mendes, 2019). The re-str ucturing of both specialized schools and the genera l schools prompted by an inclusive education agenda, placed t he specialized schools in a double bind. On one hand, specialized schools historically were envisioned to  develop the academic potential of students with vi sual impairments. On the other hand, the entrance of stu dents with significant disabilities as residues of an inclusive education agenda. These “competing alternatives” ta ke teachers into a need state in which practitioner s are “unable to determine the direction of their efforts” (Engeström, 2015, p.133). The need state “manifested in threats and conflict situations” (Engeström, p.154) within spaces struggling to redefine their practices, repr esent opportunities for potential expansive learning.

Learning Lab: A formative intervention Informed Cultural Historical Activity Theory (CHAT), Learning Lab, a formative intervention model, aim s to empower local practitioners facing daily contradict ions in their practice and facilitate expansive lea rning. Expansive learning stems from the articulation, identification., and resolution of challenges or contradictions in an activity system (e.g., the specialized school). For instance, educating blind students with additional disabilities means adaptations that go beyond Braille, tactile g raphics, contrast, or large print books to encompas s modifications in content or assessment which disrupt the normative center and trigger contradictions b etween a previous historical activity and an emerging new form of activity and practice. To our knowledge this is the first formative intervention for collective systemic design in a specialized school. Furthermore, though using the first generation of Vygotskian CHAT is more prevalent in Brazil, only a ICLS2022 Proceedings 1441 © ISLS few studies has ventured into the third or the fourth generation of CHAT which takes activity systems and social movement as the unit of analysis (Cenci et al., 2020).

## Theoretical framework

CHAT provides analytical tools to examine and intervene in activity systems (e.g., hospitals, schools, factories, and social movements) facing historically accumulated contradictions (Bal, 2017)). In a formative inte rvention, participants actively follow a cycle of change asce nding from the abstract to the concrete. When accom plished, this movement supports an understanding of challenges not as a mystical form but as a conceptual one articulated in both social relations of the here and now as wel l as in the larger cultural-historical context (Ily enkov, 2009). Daily disturbances, dilemmas, or conflicts experienced by practitioners in the local specialized institutions should be analyzed in relation to the evolving activity system of the school which in turn is examined across  the larger national and international discourses of special education and inclusion. As individuals progressively understand how the elements of a phenomenon are constituted by the whole, consciousness about reality expands embodied into expansive learning actions towards a transform ation process that has the potential to re-mediate practice through innovative pedagogical solutions (Bal, 2017).

Activity systems Expansive learning stems from the articulation, ide ntification, and resolution of contradictions in an  activity system. Across cycles, the subjects within a community of practice and, according to their expertise and related tools, simultaneously shape and are shaped by the o bject – the element that gives meaning and directio n to the activity. Multivoicedness the source of conflict of  motives around the object, give rise to the intern al contradictions, “the driving force of change and development in activity systems” (Engeström, 2015). Contradictions Contradictions are part of every component of object-oriented activity system evolving historically in the context of global capitalism. In the current neoliberal era, the primary contradiction in the activity system of a school, for instance, is the competing motives between accountability (exchange value) and meaningful pedagogy (use value). In this case, contradictions harnessed by dialogue and collective work become a driver for innovation. Contradictions are only visible through their discursive manifestations. Engeström and Sannino (2011) identified four types of discursive manifestations of contradictions: Dilemmas, conflicts, moral conflicts, and double binds. Double binds, the highest level of consciousness and, thus the most aggravated contradiction, demands a collective call for change. Usually, the result is a “third wa y” in which practitioners generate “novel mediating  models, concepts and patterns of activity that go beyond and transcend the available opposing forces or options, pushing the system into a new phase of development” (Engeström & Sannino, 2011, p. 371). This third way depends on multivoicedness embodied in the manifestations of contradictions or level of consciousness. Learning Lab: A formative intervention model for education systems Learning Lab is a formative intervention that takes expansive learning theory and respective methodological cycle of expansive learning with a commitment to give voice to historically underrepresented groups in schools’ knowledge-production and decision-making activities such as Black, Indigenous, People of Color (BIPOC) and low-income families. Expansive learning is defined as the formation of a new, expanded object and an expanded activity system organized around the new object (Engeström, 2015). Learning Lab differs from other activity theoretical formative intervention approaches) for its commitment to participatory social justice (Bal, 2017). Contributing to the newly emerging fourth generation CHAT, Learning Lab builds heterogenous coalitions and deals with complex societal problems or runaway objects such as racial disparities in educational opportunities and outcomes or inclusion. Drawing from critical education and radical pedagogies, Learning Lab engages school stakeholders in collective decision-making that address disparities in educational outcomes and opportunities experienced by BIPOC students and families. A full cycle of change in Learning Labs encompasses the following expansive learning actions: (1) Forming Learning, (2) Questioning “Here and Now”,

(3) Analyzing, (4) Modeling, (5) Examining, (6) Planning for Implementation, (7) Reflecting on the Leaning

Lab Process and Outcomes, and (8) Actual Implementation.

## Methods

The goal of this case study is to examine how practitioners design a model of inclusive practice through expansive learning actions to respond to their local challeng es. Learning Lab took place during the COVID-19 out break which made a Virtual Learning Lab as a natural choice.

ICLS2022 Proceedings 1442 © ISLS Setting The setting of the study is a specialized school for the blind and visually impaired students facing organizational changes (e.g., student profile, teaching methods). Regarded as a landmark in the history of special ed ucation in Brazil, the specialized school was influenced by th e different trends in the field: segregation, integ ration, and inclusion (Jannuzzi, 2017). After studying for six years at the Institut des Jeunes Aveugles a Paris, Álvares de Azevedo pursuit the idea of building in Rio de Janeiro an institute like its Parisian counterpart. The institute for students with visual impairment was developed under the influence of the Jesuit model of education in which the objective was to separate students from the world. In a pedagogical universe, students under constant surveillance had access to an elementary school with religion, music, and crafts for manual work (Januzzi, 2017). In the late 80’s, the identification of some blind students with additional disabilities, initiated th e activities of an Alternative Educational Program (A EP), opened in 1991. This separate self-contained p rogram catered for the need of blind students with additional disabilities with activities based on a functional perspective. In 2016, following the national inclusive education policy guidelines (Brasil, 2008) the new administration closed the self-contained program and the blind students with additional disabilities entered the regular classrooms with access to grade-level general curriculum. The schoo l took the role of an ‘inclusive’ specialized schoo l and following the inclusive education initiative (Brasil, 2008) in which special education was no longer a place but a service for blind students with additional disabilities within the age-appropriate regular classes.

## Participants

Learning Lab consisted of 14 practitioners: eight teachers from the different school levels (e.g., early childhood, elementary school, and middle school), three specia l educators, two administrators and a former staff from the early intervention department.

## Data collection

Data was generated through video recordings of nine  monthly meetings, two agenda meetings and one subcommittee meeting. Artifact analysis included hi storical and institutional documents from the speci alized school (e.g., audio-recorded past community meetings and institutional videos), field notes and collective map of the activity system. All transcribed data was transferred into Nvivo 12™, a qualitative data analysis software.

## Data analysis

To understand how Learning Lab facilitated practiti oners’ expansive learning, the study utilized two complementary methods of analysis. The first method  offers a method for the identification and analysi s of discursive manifestations of contradictions (Engeström & Sannino, 2011). The second method included analysis of expansive learning actions (Engeström, 2015).

## Findings

Participants completed the cycle of change and desi gned a new model of inclusive practice. The followi ng expansive learning actions were identified: The action Forming Learning Lab occurred nine times in the first two meetings, questioning actions occurred 21 times so far, and analyzing twelve times. Questioning school’s existing inclusion support system and questioning school’s practices emerged as subcategories of questioning action. The analysis of the discursive manifestations of contra dictions indicated dilemmas are the most frequent d iscursive manifestation with 33 occurrences, followed by conf licts that emerged 20 times, nine critical conflict s and two double binds. In the initial sessions dilemmas were linked to discussion topics related to the pandemic outbreak. On the one hand participants were excited about the collaborative project and possible outcome, on the other hand they expressed concerns with the current events and explained they would participate “as much as possible.” Other dilemmas were tightly related to the inclusi ve practices in the school and in the classroom. Learning Lab members acknowledged the importance of access to the curriculum for students with additional disabilities. They also communicated uncertainties about the feasibility of an individualized instruction in the collective environment of the classroom. Margarida, former head of the early intervention department, highlighted the challenges of instruction in the classroom contrasting with her experience in the early intervention department. She compared early intervention as a one-on-one “craft work,” while described learning and teaching in the classroom as challenge for the teacher that needs to involve everyone the process. Conflicts were related to diverging views between the access of a functional or academic curriculum. Disagreement with the position of the institution in relation to blind students with multiple disabilities. Pedro, the history teacher, was very straightforward and criti cized the laissez-faire position of the institution  which led to some teachers going above and beyond to include students with additional disabilities while others did nothing. ICLS2022 Proceedings 1443 © ISLS According to Pedro, families with students with additional disabilities demand very little from the school because the institution itself allows teachers to be less a ccountable for students with additional disabilities than students with visual disabilities: “There is a tacit hierarchy and that is accepted by the school.” Critical conflicts were highly emotional and relat ed to the current pandemic. Practitioners felt guilty or had inner doubts about the best ways to support students with additional disabilities in the online mode of instruction. Isabor, Language Arts teacher, admitted that because of the pandemic she did not know most of the students with additional disabilities; some came to the first class and then disappeared: “I punish myself for not trying to learn what happened to those students. I thought to myself ‘Oh, they gave up.’ This was my mistake; I should not have done this.” Finally, double binds as unequally unacceptable alternatives in the activity system emerged in the tension about the need of new staff such as paraprofessionals supposedly prepared to mediate appropriate services for students with multiple disabilities.

## Discussion

Through Learning Lab, participants designed a new model of inclusive practice as they articulated contradictions and collectively found the means to resolve them. I nitial analysis of discursive manifestations of contradictions and expansive learning actions reinforce the non-li nearity of the cycle of change. The cycle of change  offers a blueprint to guide facilitators and members towards  expansive learning actions. However, it does not e ntail linearity in the process. For instance, analyzing the historical-genetic occurred in the second meeting during the questioning of the ‘here and now.’ This shows the i mportance of the historical development of the acti vity to understand the challenges of the present moment. Th e personal sense that gives meaning and direction t o the activity seems to be paramount for the involvement of practitioners in the collaborative process. Thou gh many were uncertain about the future and had many other additional responsibilities, they participated acti vely and engaged in serious reflective conversations. Learni ng Lab formative intervention accomplished its prom ise bringing practitioners together in a virtual space where they were able to discuss the new identity of the school, the inclusion of a new group of students in the spe cialized school and concrete ways to respond to the  changes and challenges in their practice.
