# Entities Detonating Potentials: Interuniversity Design Workshop TiUD as an Exploratory Lab for Actants of the Futures

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The Interuniversity Design Workshop is an exploratory laboratory that promotes the study of design activism, sustainable design, and transition design. The seventh edition of the workshop aimed to visualize the organizations, institutions, or companies that must be created to enable Mexico City to reach its potential for social well-being. The object of this educational experience is to enable design students to discover new ways to promote social activation through design as a professional activity. A group of 60 undergraduate design students from five high education institutions (HEI,’s) analyzed their own contexts, emphasi zing the identification of potential sources of wellbeing; later, they were given the task to design interactions, including giving shape to the agents that would make them possible. Their projects revealed three key findings: (a) that students are able to identify potentials through contextual observation; (b) that the creation of new actors can potentially activate new realities; and (c) the proposed entities hint potential future roles of designers in Latin American contexts. This study contributes to the field of design, looking towards a fairer, more pluralistic and diverse future.

The Interuniversity Design Workshop or TiUD (Taller Inter Universitario de Diseño) is the result of a collective initiative to nudge social activation in design schools in Mexico City, searching for a better community, that has been running since 2017. The  collective is mainly formed by researchers, lecturers, students and activists from five design schools in public and private higher education institutions; but the network extends also to representatives of collectives, companies and organizations that be lieve in the collaborative spirit of design for a positive transformation. Following Lafuente (2007), our aim is to prototype collective responses to current challenges, united by what connects us, not divided by what sets us apart. TiUD proposes to use the design process and methods as a tool for change and transformation, starting with recogni zing ourselves as active members of our community and creating a solid guild as a platform for Mexicandesigners. We trust our students to become models of collaboration and agents of change towards the wellbeing of their social and natural context. Once a year, our students go through what we call Circuits of the Common (Alvarez-Icaza et al., 2021), a collaborative learning tool that we designed to assist them in the process of finding where they can meet in terms of priorities. This workshop and its methodology allow them to identify what they have in common: exposing their hopes and challenges, and also their concerns. In a country as unequal as Mexico, and a city as large as Mexico City, the teams are formed by what at first glance could seem to be very different individuals, but who surely have something in common, whether it is the advancement of a cause or to fight a particular injustice. TiUD is an opportunity for committed people to understand the potential impact that design proposals oriented to civic participation can have. Potentials vs problems Traditional design approaches have focused on identifying and solving specific problems, following a process of research and rapid idea generation known as “design thinking”. This method involves addressing a “problem” by creating hypotheses that are tested and constantly iterating to improve and find solutions (Wizinsky, 2022). However, this approach can be limited to looking for immediate solutions, without considering the latent opportunities that may arise from looking at the context as a whole. Following Montaña (2022), who bases his work on that of Capra (1996),  the Inter-University Design Workshop considers that it is important to focus not only on problems, but also on the potentials of design situations. When thinking of students as agents, this change 226 might be triggered when imagining a better future, and for this they are encouraged to explore the potentials that are manifested in their city.

The concept of potential refers to a latent agency or property inherent in a context that unfolds over time. Unlike a problem, which requires immediate intervention, potential is something that may or may not manifest itself at any given time (Easterling, 2021). This implies that designers should be attentive to trends and predispositions that emerge from interactions in the context, rather than focusing exclusively on creating solutions to predefined problems. In this way, design can play a key role in creating new realities, reorganizing human relationships and proposing spaces for action and experience. By adopting a prospective approach, the Inter-University Design Workshop proposes to visualize and detonate potentials for well-being rather than simply solving problems that might be just a symptom of something else, not visible at the current time and space. In contrast, observing potentials  requires a relational evolution of each observed situation, where ideas are understood as pragmatic structures that interact with other ideas and actors in a given context (Burgos, 2016). Thus, in a complex world governed by multidimensionality, it is possible to create positive-sum solutions that benefit all parties involved (Jiang, 2021), clearing the path towards a sustainable and desirable future.

In this sense, applying a co-design perspective, a s a process and mindset for planning based on collaboration (Mattelmäki & Sleeswijk Visser, 2011), prepares students to integrate visions form each other and from other stakeholders. In the workshop, students with singular life experiences but shared concerns, form teams that are seen as incipient communities; but they also identify a real-life consolidated community (preferably one to which at least one of them already belongs) to work with and within. Additionally, they are provided access to a network of  experts and the whole teaching team, who provide additional  visions throughout the process, and encourage the students to always observe the collectives and entities that might be impacted or affected by their decisions or intentions.

Social activation and agency During the 2024 edition, the TIUD community identified key areas on which to focus when creating interventions and participating in social activation within their communities. Throughout this process, students discovered and transferred relevant information that enabled them to take a different approach to social activation. This process begins with self-recognition of the role that each individual can play in the positive transformation of our shared context, with the aim of building long-lasting bonds as a collective agency. Drawing on Winterhouse's Social Design Pathways (2013), we have developed a new reinterpretation to addressing complex social issues. In our Pathways for Social Activation, we encourage participants to reflect on their engagemant in transformative projects  within their communities. Our goal is to shift the focus from developing a project for its launch as an individual endeavour, which is the standard curriculum in design schools, to a common-goal approach. We propose teaching and transferring methodological tools to encourage students to evolve along a diagonal pathway. Our aim is for participants to progress from individuals with limited experience and engagement to collective action with transformative agency. This is not currently taught in any undergraduate design curricula at our institutions and is not widely accepted in master's programmes in the Latin American context. In this sense, we believe that TiUD is a valuable addition to the Mexicandesign education landscape. 227 Figure 1 TiUD’s Educational Pathway for Social Activation

## TiUD method

For the 2024 edition, the workshop was divided into eight phases that included individual and team activities, conducted either in presence or asynchronously; activities led by each docent in their universities; and activities where the whole TiUD community (students and docents) participated.

1. The course started with a bootcamp; a two-day workshop conducted at El Eco Museum, that

was dedicated to setting up the workshop and integrating the students into a community by: a. Introducing them to the theoretical background; mainly, design for sustainability (Ceschin & Gaziulusoy, 2019), transition design (Kossof & Irwin, 2021), and design activism (Fuad-Luke, 2013). b. Assembling random interuniversity teams for a quick immersive rally into the surrounding neighbourhoods (San Rafael and Santa María la Rivera), looking for potentials. c. Sharing collected potentials. d. Clustering potentials to find the main topics of the course.

2. The students were presented with T he Pulpo (octopus in English) or systemic map, a tool

developed by TiUD docent team to help them analyze their topics by making them think of factors such as actors, inhibitors, magnifiers, related problems, causes, and manifestations of potentials. This tool also enabled them to explore potentials in a systemic manner, by identifying relationships between potentials and their context, and among other potentials mentioned by other groups.

3. Having reached that general level of understanding around different topics and their potentials,

the students regrouped into interuniversity teams, this time depending on their own preferences.

4. Next, the teams mapped the historic evolution of their topics, which included identifying causes

and/or milestones in the process of getting to where we are at now.

5. Then, the students were introduced to the concept of organizational entities and were challenged

to identify core components of an entity that would enable them to practice design in a different manner, while helping a designated community to reach its potential.

6. The following step was the creation of four different futures scenarios, by selecting the two

dimensions of STEEPLE analysis that they considered most relevant for their projects. These scenarios were later validated by members of their chosen communities,  with the aim of generating a final desirable and probable future scenario. 228

7. The student teams were then asked to go back and, having their desirable future in hand, revise

the core components of their organizational  entity. The teams were given the challenge of generating unique entities with no overlapping functions;  thus, they needed tonegotiate or harmonize with one another.

8. After the negotiation, they were finally able to design their unique organizational  entities

dedicated to co-designing with specific communities with the aim of helping them to reach their potential and making their preferable future come true. Groups and actants We have challenged our students over the last seven years to discover different ways to collaborate within their communities as promoters of social activation. In this sense for our seventh edition, we have explored and identified potentials to build and promote wellbeing through the design of the int eractions between actors and actants that would make them possible. Latour (1997) describes that associations, institutions, organizations, states and nations, which are actants-to non-human or non-individual entities, receive their agency from others. According to his Actor-Network Theory (ANT) an actant’s activity is granted by others, so it will not have a special motivation until it is provided for by someone. We expect students participating in this workshop to uncover new kinds of associations, institutions, or organizations with the capacity to turn the identified potentials into an effective opportunity.

King (2024) who considers Latour a “great sociologist” wrote an article after his death in 2022, where, through a careful review of many of his documents, concludes that “[…] the contradictions at the heart of Latour’s work are evident. Latour consistently denied the existence of the social group and the collective. Rejecting sociological reification, he tried to eliminate social groups and collective actors from his work.” (p. 290). From our perspective, and this is how we have transferred it to our students, we  take it as a fact that we, humans, assign agency to our associations, organizations, and institutions; that our purpose as a group, with our sum of agencies, is to address our intentions and the creation of new forms of reaching a collective intention. Although despite our best efforts, a collective intention will never be reached if the collective does not build trust first.  According to Cipolla (2017), who recovers Ventura’s concept of Broken City (1994), there are certain initiatives that candevelop new networks across a fragmented social fabric. These initiatives can promote the creation of different dynamics in which the agency transferred to an actant can follow the collective interest in the search for the common well-being. In this sense, collective interest emerges from trust —the belief that others’ interests may intersect with one’s own. Such alignment enables the co-creation of solutions grounded in present possibilities  (Wibeck et al, 2022)  while also identifying the conditions required to expand those possibilities in the future. This is precisely the kind of process we aim to provoke in our students. Potentials as tools for agency During the workshop process, students must  first identify “potentials for wellbeing” and then iterate their definition through insight and manifestation of these potentials across the city. In the context of Montañana’s work, "potencial" often refers to the inherent capabilities or opportunities within a community or a project that can be harnessed to drive innovation and positive social change. It is the ability of a design or social intervention to activate latent resources and capacities that, when appropriately engaged, can lead to transformative outcomes (Montañana, 2022), gradually arriving at actionable concepts that involve intimate connections to contexts, actors and actants and the relational connections that result. Encouraging reflection and iterative development in the redefinition process helps students refine their ideas and strengthen their sense of agency, connecting the actionable verbs that turn a theme or problem into a potential involving their participation and stake in the adaptive and collaborative potential at hand.

From this process, the identification of potentials must be carried out collectively, enabling students to challenge their individual intentions and perceptions. From this perspective, the emergence of new forms of collective agency arises through the critical confrontation of individual beliefs in the pursuit of shared well-being. |Students often realize that their initial visions were partial, and that solutions are not the result of simple responses but rather of a complex analysis of diverse intentions. This process involves negotiating differences, reaching compromises, reaching agreements, and advancing through incremental stages of improvement that can eventually lead to a more desirable future.

Co-design as path for social activation During the TIUD process, engaging students in collaborative learning and co-design methods can foster teamwork and create shared languages. On the one hand, students were nudged to identifying alternative ways in which design can be practiced; on the other, they aimed for creating possible and actionable groups that can later be 229 imagined as new design entities; that is the organizations, institutions or companies that must be created to detonate potentials for wellbeing. Bringing together a group of 60 pre-graduate design students from five different public and private  institutions for a semester-long participatory process can highlight the languages and tools essential for envisioning possible and sustainable futures (Holing, 2020). This process also emphasizes the design activism / social activation experienced through the workshops that can eventually be replicated into the “actionable entities” they will design.

Ecologies for intervention In light of the Transition Design aspect of the lab, the final process involves creating a complex system where these actionable entities will cohabitate, collaborate and pollinate each other. During this part of the project, students advocate and harmonize these entities, providing the possibility of specialized channels which promote the division of potentials for wellbeing and collaboration between them (Gil & Vilches, 2003). It is important to highlight that students are required to interconnect their solutions as a preliminary exercise in negotiating the trade-offs they may encounter when working in diverse groups to develop real-world initiatives, programs, or public policies in their future professional practice.

The students’ findings allowed us to confirm that the TiUD method guided the creation of paths to discover opportunities for intervention towards social activation that designers as professionals can take over. From the result of each stage in the methodology, design students have discovered new possibilities to promote social activation as a professional activity.

During the construction of the mapping of potentials in the city, the students had to write down their half-day Rally in a 5 km radius. The aim was to put them in a common space so they could hear and understand how the issues of the city were something that people deal with in different ways. After the Rally, the students made a synthesis of the information in the logs that resulted in 8 categories of potentials: identity, diversity, inclusion, resistance, social fabric, support networks, decent housing and common space.  These cate gories emerged from a participatory process using a systemic mapping tool designed within the TiUD 2020 Edition, El Pulpo (The Octopus). To assemble the categories, we used an affinity diagram technique. Students in groups clustered their insights and the clusters were revised plenary ses sion. These potentials were transferred into El Pulpo (Figure 2), which is onwards called a Map of Potentials. Figure 2 An Example of Potential: “A City that Cares and Promotes Integration  of Students form other Cities” This analysis required students to articulate their observations in a systematic manner, enabling them to define the themes to be developed and to identify the people  with whom they would collaborate. Although the eight categories are not exhaustive in capturing the full range of a city’s potential, naming them helps frame the scope of the edition and facilitates the construction of an ecology of interventions in subsequent stages. Furthermore, we have observed that the use of a tangible tool, such as the M ap of  Potentials, supports the development of students’ systemic thinking. It enables them to infer interrelations between their intended goals, and the potential impacts these intentions may have across other dimensions and elements of the city. 230 To continue working with the systemic analysis,  students needed to understand the present and design for the future. After assembling the Map of Potentials,  the students built a timeline  from the pas t to that very moment to understand the historical evolution and detect the milestones that shaped the present that they observed in their journey  up to this moment. The work was done first with newspaper cut-outs, looking for  drivers of change, a task that enabled them to articulate the immediate past. Once this step was completed, they could start searching for clues in  the web to observe the more distant past. From this process, students identified key milestones in the evolution of different situations, including the emergence of institutions and outcomes shaped by past decisions. These decisions, although not always optimal in hindsight, were made under conditions where decision-makers could not fully anticipate the consequences, given the limited information available and the priorities that guided their actions at the time. Figure 3 is the result of the collected data and findings made during the process by all teams. The complete map includes in corresponding levels: Potentials – what can be done; Related Problems  – the affected dimensions; Actors  – positive and negative; Consequences – the cost of not reaching the potential; Magnifiers – Facilitators for reaching the potential; Inhibitors – what makes things worse; and Manifestations – tangible and concrete evidence of the potential reached. After this stage, students developed a clearer understanding of the approach they wished to pursue — sometimes aiming to address or rectify past shortcomings—as well as the specific topic they intended to develop. Building on this clarity, they were able to construct a manifesto by formulating and consolidating a set of statements that articulated both their goals and the strategies through which they aimed to achieve them. The projects presented in the final event addressed diverse potentials, expressed in the form of manifestos, for instance: Women who are university students in Mexico City have the potential to promote academic diversity, and strengthen equity, to ensure equal opportunities, and to build a more just and equitable society. Their manifesto is: “ We are a group of design students, committed to supporting women who are university students to achieve their potential to promote academic diversity, to strengthen equity, to ensure equal opportunities, and to build a more just and equitable society.” Once the manifesto is made, the students take a political stand with regards to a real situation to aspire to become agents of change. The next step was to move on to field work, this with the purpose of co-creating future scenarios through an approach of discussing the dreams and desires of the community where the potential is embedded. To assemble a proposal to reach a potential,  the students visualize the intersection of the two dimensions they render most relevant, which allow s them to build a 2x2 matrix, producing 4 possible outcomes in a future set in 60 years in the future. The final outcomes consisted of projects aligned with the e ight categories of potentials that emerged through the Rally, encompassing a diverse range of proposals focused on social activation through collaboratively developed initiatives. Some students leveraged these projects to apply to international design competitions, while others pursued them as academic exercises. Notably, one student team, whose project addressing student housing was invited to present at the weekly open sessions held by the  Government of Mexico City, where citizens are given a space to pitch initiatives directly to city authorities. 231 During the TiUD’s 7th edition, our students transformed the observed context into a potential ecosystem, addressing eight different forms of social interactions within communities in the city that they share. Addressing potentials instead of problems fosters hypotheses (Wizinsky, 2022) of the professional activity that designers in Latin American contexts c an take over using design methodologies to develop the inherent capacities or opportunities within a community to drive innovation and positive social change (Montañana, 2022). TiUD as an activation cell of potentials ha s influenced, in this sense, over 400 students in seven years, which potentially validates us as a force for change with group agency. Our students unveiled nine timelines that connect present potentials with the milestones of the past that caused the evolution of “how things are” in eight categories. This visioning to the past fosters the connection with the future in terms of possibilities and the purpose of each group in a manifesto. Proposing and creating solutions in the search for common wellbeing (Jiang, 2021), leads the path to building a sustainable and desirable future. In this sense, students become advocates of such potentials in collaborative construction (Moreno, 2022) of their contextual reality in the present and for the future. If each participant of this workshop embraces the idea that by recombining the social, the environmental and the economic spheres, there can be different development paths in their mind,  then we can probably shift the way design as a productive activity is developed, shifting to a responsible and productive system.

We intend to provoke in our students as many questions as possible regarding how they will develop their professional activity while searching for the wellbeing of their own communities. Following Cipolla’s (2017) narrative, a Broken City (Ventura, 1994) can be repaired through the creation of new networks across the divisions of the social fabric. Throughout  eight categories of potentials, the participants of this edition of  TiUD interconnected their knowledge, sensibilities and perspectives, to ensure the existence of a more just and equitable society. We aim for a new generation of designers that mark their position as contributors of sociotechnical change in their career’s development as agents of change.

The Interuniversity Design Workshop (TiUD) exemplifies how design students can become active agents of social transformation by shifting focus from problem-solving to potential activation. Through collaborative exploration of their local urban contexts (or city), the students identified and mapped potentials for well-being. The systemic approach encouraged them to envision future scenarios and design actionable entities that could combine and address these potentials into ecologies for intervention. By integrating complex thinking, design activism, and co-creation, the TiUD workshop enabled students to develop a deep understanding of their roles as designers in Latin American contexts. This exploration provided tools for recognizing the evolving socia l dynamics and their capacity to influence them actively. As participants co-created visions of present, past and future scenarios, they constructed a breeding ground for agency and collaboration. Ultimately, TiUD's methodology and process during its 7th e dition explored the concept of design education towards proximity and change, aiming to cultivate a generation of professionals who will prioritize sustainable, active, and transformative practices. Through this workshop, students learn that design can be a powerful catalyst of actionable entities, not just in products and services, but in the core of human relationships and their capacity to design them.

The limitations of this study refer to the sample type, as although it includes participants from different institutions, it includes a limited number of students overall. A wider number of participants from other institutions in other cities might be helpful to have a proper measurement of the impact in the students’ mindset. Additionally, setting a quantitative instrument for the same purpose can be helpful to systematize  the results and comparing them to different approaches in design education. This is part of the future work our group is aiming for in the following years. This work is a contribution to the exploration of the impact that design as a discipline and an active research process can have in approaching contemporary issues and crafting solutio ns by identifying potentials even in highly complex and problematic situations. Educators, researchers and policy makers have applied our findings and methodologies for knowledge creation,  and they have described our participatory approach as a needed tool for addressing global challenges in local contexts.
