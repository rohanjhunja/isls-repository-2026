# Designing for “Layered” Sensemaking About Environmental Injustice: The “Guided Tour” Activity

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Issues of spatial and environmental (in)justice are fundamentally “layered,” comprised of heterogeneous sets of experiences, materialities, and histories. To investigate these issues in the classroom, then, teachers and students must develop multidisciplinary strategies for sensemaking across these layers: practices we call “layering”. This paper describes an inquiry activity – the “Guided Tour” – developed for K-12 teacher professional development settings and classrooms using a framework that identifies four such layers, including data from lived experiences, local histories, social sciences, and natural sciences (Barton et al., In Press). We ask: what forms of reasoning across these layers emerged as teacher participants (N=8) crafted their “Tours” focused on local environmental justice issues, and how else did participants engage in “layering” beyond them? We found frequent instances of layering in participants’ work, as well as opportunities for redesign to better support emergent forms.

## Background and theoretical framework

Studies of place in K-12 science education often separate “natural scienc e” concepts – investigations of water (Grades K-5), natural disasters (6-8), or chemicals (9-12), for example – from other forms of place-based inquiry (Gruenewald, 2003a, 2003b). The se include disciplinary lenses and practices inform ed by local histories, social sciences, and lived experiences. Dissociating these  approaches from one another risks isolating “scien tific” phenomena and sensemaking practices from their political, cultural, and embodied realities for students, teachers, and their communities (see Davis & Schaeffer, 2019). For issues of environmental justice especially, t hese realities are fundamentally “layered,” comprised of heterogeneous sets of experiences, materialities, and histories. Explicitly attending to this layeredness (Barton et al., in press) is an underexplored area in the development of justice-centered science pedagogies (Mutegi, 2011; Morales-Doyle, 2017): that is, creating opportunities for critical multidisciplinary sensemaking across these disciplinary areas and their widely varied forms of inquiry. This paper describes the design and implementation of a place-based inquiry activity called the “Guided Tour” designed to engage the layered nature of envi ronmental injustice. The Guided Tour invites participants to weave together several layers of disciplinary inquiry into a multidimensional portrait of p lace. Participants place several key locations (or “tour stops”) on a physic al map along with forms of evidence and media they use to address a focal question: in our case, “What are the impacts of extreme precipitation in [your local area shown on the map]?” As a pedagogical framework, the Guided T our is structured around four informational and nar rative layers outlined in Barton et al. (in press): a.) lived experiences, b.) histories of place, c.) social sciences, and d.) natural sciences. In our experiences as K-12 teachers and teacher educators, these layers (wh ile non-exclusive) have been especially generative for teachers’ and s tudents’ sensemaking about social and environmental issues. The activity itself draws from Hall & colleagues’ (2020) “Digital Spatial Story Lines” in its artifact-based storytelling structure. We address the following qu estions: 1.) In what ways did the activity design support participants’ critical reasoning across these four layers as they created their tours? 2.) In what oth er ways did participants engage in “layering” practices that went beyond these four initial layers? As a construct, “layering” – participants’ simultaneous use of more than one of these layers above (a-d) in the course of reasoning, argumentation, or narra tive – is rarely attended to in research on inquiry activi ties based in spatial frameworks (Mitchell  & Elwood, 2012, Gordon et al., 2016; Rubel et al., 2016). Designing for layering practices, as well as studying their appea rance in the course of sensemaking activity, is par ticularly important because they may enable precisely the kinds of interdisciplinary, justice-oriented reasoning that science educators increasingly seek to design for (see, e.g., Morales-Doyle, 2019; Morales-Doyle et al., 2019).  This is especially critical because such layering is not supported by the current Next Generation Science Standards (Clark, 2024; Morales-Doyle et al., 2019). This particular study is significant both for the d esign of the activity and the underlying concepts we address: that is, the relationship between critical forms of interdisciplinary reasoning and an activity which may support their emergence in local, lived, and relatable contexts.

## Context, participants, data sources and analysis

The “Guided Tour” was implemented on day 4 of a 5-day summer professional learning institute around “Precipitation Extremes and Climate Justice” for K-12 teachers in the San Francisco Bay Area. Throughout the institute, teachers engaged with a wide variety of social justice-focused (e.g. examining case studies of mutual aid in the face flooding causes by racist infrastructur e negligence), socio-scientific (e.g. engaging in humanistic data protocols), and science focused (e.g. using hot and cold water to model climate impacts on precipitation) activities and considered a daily “justice lens” (see Reigh et. al, 2025). While the institute and the activity were both focused on the nature and impacts of extreme precipitation in urban areas, the Guided Tour was designed to be adaptable to a wide range of topics and classroom contexts. The 8 teachers whose work during the activity we describe here taught science or multiple subjects at a range of grade levels (K, 3, 6-8, 10-12) at different schools and held a range of racial (Latine, Asian, Black, white), gender, and other identities. Seeking to understand participants’ sensemaking and  possible “layering” practices in greater detail, w e studied the Guided Tour artifacts that two small groups created, as well as video recordings of their presentations to each other. We deductively coded participants’ reasoning, argumentation, or narrative expressed through these presentations for evidence of engagement with the four disciplinary and informational layers described above: a.) lived experiences, b.) histories of place, c.) soci al sciences, and d.) natural sciences (See Table 1). Participants’ turns of talk were typically treated as the coding unit: the following statement, for example, was coded as showing evidence from Histories and Natural Sciences: “Redl ined districts [Histories of Place ] are clearly the ones that have experienced the most pollution burden [Natural Sciences ] to this day, right, to draw those historical parallels”. Authors coded independently, and resolv ed discrepancies in code assignment and turn of tal k unit by consensus; one unresolvable discrepancy was deferred to Barton’s coding. For example, in the case of urban air po llution: highways routed through marginalized commu nities carry legacies of discriminatory building and zoning p olicies as well as generations of unequal health bu rdens from breathing unhealthy air. To begin the “Guided Tour” activity at our summer institute focused on e xtreme precipitation, we introduced three guiding questions to participants: 1.) How could an extreme precipitation event impact students and school communities in this area ? 2.) How are these impacts related to the built en vironment and histories of marginalization? 3.) How and why m ight marginalized communities be especially vulnerable to the impacts of extreme precipitation events? These questions built on previous themes and inquiries th roughout the institute. Participants set out to address them  using a range of resources, including digital maps  and datasets (e.g. land cover databases, city hazard planning documents) as well as physical materials (e.g. historical redlining maps, demographic data and maps, information on racially exclusionary civic policies and health inequities). With these resources, participant groups created five explanatory “panels” pinned to five locations on the map covering the area where they taught. These locations represe nted the “stops” on their tour, which they’d subseq uently present to another group. Panels (see Figure 1 for an example shown to participants) were created on large pieces of poster paper, using physical pieces of maps and text as well as information from other resources. G roups had roughly 1 hour to create their tours, and 10-15 minutes to present.

This suggests that participants may have substantial previous experiences framing their lived experiences and/or historical contexts in layered ways: experiences they leveraged during this activity. For example, one teacher described h ow some neighborhoods are neglected by the city, they also shared how they’d previously described those experiences in a locally-made documentary. Another teacher gave the followin g description of one of their tour stops, which was coded as leveraging lived experiences, histories of  place, and natural science perspectives: “We chose  the [local to the elementary school] community in West Oakland [...] There’s a lot of trucks that are like, going back and forth to the port and stuff, and we talked about how a lot of our students have asthma and, um, just a lot of [truck exhaust] being put out into the air”. The immediate spatial proximity of these factors (the port, the trucks, students’ asthma) makes their layeredness a constant presence, one that this teacher confronts on a daily basis. A second hypothesis regarding this finding is that participants may have weighted the importance of evidence from these different layers in different w ays. The “social valences” of the data (Fiore-Gartland & Neff,

2015) may have impacted how and whether participant s’ felt they had to layer or corroborate them with other

forms. Certainly, that  Natural Science perspectives having the lowest “Lay ering” ratio may be because participants saw them as least in need of corrobora tion: a longstanding epistemological privilege affo rded to Western, Natural Science-based perspectives (Medin & Bang, 2014). Another di mension to this pattern is that participants’ layering practices differed between t he two groups: one group, in which three of four pa rticipants were identifiable as white and all were secondary STEM teachers, did not reference lived experiences at all. This made us wonder: how do participants’ positionalitie s impact the kinds of layering practices they engag e in this context, including their (dis)comfort sharing particular forms of knowledge as evidence? Participants repeatedly engaged several other “layers” that we didn’t initially code for Participants engaged several forms of evidence and reasoning (“layers”) that we hadn’t initially included in our coding scheme. These included: 1.) interrogating ci ties’ civic responsibilities to their residents, 2.) exploring solutions and 3.) reasoning based on imagined personal experiences. One participant, for  example, raised the following near the end of their group’s presentation: “The part we didn’t get to is like, now looking at all this, so what kinds of things need to be done?” Designing fu ture versions of the activity to address that quest ion might better encourage participants to describe the environmentally-just cities and communities they imagine. Adjusting our coding scheme to account for these other forms of “layering” will help us develop an  increasingly nuanced understanding of the modes of “layering” that participants employ to achieve different analytical ends.

## Discussion, Conclusion, and Limitations

These findings illustrate the complexity and multidimensional nature of layered forms of reasoning that emerged from a spatial inquiry activity about local issues of environmental (in)justice. They suggest the need  for further research to determine how par ticipants themselves view or approach these differe nt modes of reasoning, as well as different valences or weights they ascribe to different layers. They also suggest several further implications for activity design, including having activity particip ants track the different layers of evidence they engage as they go. This, along with a more explicit focus on engag ing lived experiences, might encourage participants to reflect metacognitively about how they balance these multip le ways of knowing (an explicit focus of the summer institute) as well as how they might design versions of this activity to fit in their own K-12 classrooms.
