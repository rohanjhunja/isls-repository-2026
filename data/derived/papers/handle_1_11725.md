# Reimagining Community Futures: Supporting Science Teachers and Students as Climate Justice Action Researchers and Change Agents

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To explore the interrelated issues of racial and climate justice, this study examines how engaging teachers and students in climate justice action research (CJAR), a novel approach to community-based science at the intersection of history, race, place, and community health, supports learning, identity construction, and opportunities for reimagining sustainable futures. Using teacher/student interviews, surveys, and classroom observations this study examines: 1) How participation in a year-long professional learning experience shaped science teachers’ knowledge and practice as they designed/implemented CJAR units in their classrooms, and 2) How engaging in CJAR practices (e.g., science, mapping, arts-based storytelling) shaped student learning, identity construction, and the futures they imagined for themselves and their communities. Findings show that teachers were empowered as designers of climate justice experiences that fostered a shared sense of humanity, and that engaging in CJAR practices supported student identity construction, critical sense making, and provided opportunities to reimagine just community futures.

## Introduction

This research critically engages teachers and stude nts in climate justice at the intersection of history, race, place, and community health. Heat is a critical point of inquiry and action, as heat-related deaths have become the number one climate-related killer (Jones, 2023).  Further, p resent day urban heat islands map directly onto historically redlined areas (Visintainer, 2023 b). Given the reality they were handed, young people  express anxiety over the climate crisis (Schiffman, 2022). The CJAR project empowers youth as leaders of community-based action to create change.

Objective To explore the interrelated issues of racial justic e and climate justice, this research ex plores how engaging teachers and students in climate justice action res earch (CJAR), a novel approach to community-based science in the classroom, supports learning, identity const ruction, and creates opportunities for community re imagining and sustainable future remaking. Specifically, this study examines how  participation in a year-long professional learning experience and design of a CJAR curricular unit for implementa tion in their classroom, shape d science teachers’ goals, knowledge of critical and youth-centered pedagogies, and expertise in guiding stude nts through the components of action research in the science cl assroom. This study is guided by  following research questions:

• How did participation in the CJAR fellowship shape science teachers’ knowledge and practice as they

designed and implemented CJAR units in their classrooms?

• How did engaging in CJAR practices (e.g., science, mapping, and arts-based storytelling) shape student

learning, sense making, and identity construction in science? Findings show that engaging in a climate justice ac tion research unit shaped identity construction in science for high school students, including their ideas about l ocal issue of urban and extreme heat, community upl ift and action, and future career options.

## Theoretical framework

I draw  on sociocultural theories of learning, and identity  (Nasir, 2012; Wenger, 1998). Race and racial groupings are conceptualized as socially constructe d, historically embedded categories of power and pr ivilege (Omi & Winant, 1994). Science learning is view as  a cultural process (Nasir et al., 2006)  and identities are constructed through interaction between individuals and the social world (Holland et al., 1998)/ This research builds on  justice-centered science  approaches (e.g., Bang et a., 2017; Bouillion & Gomez, 2001; Brown, 2021; Morales-Doyle & Frausto, 2021; Visintainer, 2017). This research utilizes CJAR, a novel approach that combines elements of community-based science (Morales-Doyle & Frausto, 202; Visintainer, 2023) and youth participatory action r esearch (Cammarota & Fine, 2010), as a way to foster student learning and action.

## Study context

This research took place at a  high school in Central California that serves a maj ority Latina/o (48%) and white (47%) population. The specific context for this study was an Advanced  Placement Environmental Science classroom comprised of 11th and 12th grade students.

## Participants

The focal teacher for this study, Mr. Woodson,  is a white male. He was part of the first yearly  cohort of 10 CJAR teacher fellows  (project is ongoing)  that participated in a year-long professional learning experience consisting of a Summer Institute  (SI) and Inquiry Cycle during the school year. Students in one period of Mr. Woodson’s AP Environmental Science classroom  comprised of high school juniors (grade 11) and sen iors (grade 12) participated in the study. All students in the class had the opportunity to participate in the res earch. Twenty-six of 28  students in the class, participated in the study. T he participants identified racially as Latinx (38%), white (35%), Asian (15%) and mixed race (12%).  All participant names have been changed to pseudonyms to protect their anonymity.

Data sources and analysis Surveys for teachers and students  contained multiple choice, Likert scale, and open-ended questions. Teachers (n=10) completed pre and post Summer Institute (SI) survey s. Students (n=26) completed pre and post  CJAR unit survey s. Survey analysis. Likert scale items were scored from 1-5 with 5 representing the most positive response and 1 representing the most negative respo nse and averaged and explored across group s. Interviews. Semi-structured, conducted one-on-one with the first or second author.  P rotocols were designed from an interpretivist perspective using descriptive and st ructural questions (Spradley, 1979). Interview Analysis. Teacher and student i nterviews were transcribed, read in full, coded inductively using an open coding process (Miles & Huberman, 1994).

## Findings

Teachers felt empowered to engage history, race  and place in science class because the science and mapp ing evidence of urban heat clearly illustrates undeniab le connections between income, race and access to/d enial of trees and greenspace (e.g. wealthier and whiter areas tend to have more trees). As described by Mr. Wo odson: “I think that was a big takeaway for me, to bring in m ore social justice issues related to climate…that c rossdiscipline approach, which is really valuable.”  Engaging in CJAR practices  and creating a rts-based science stories created opportunities for students to reimagine themselves in relation to science and healthy community futures.

Based on their experience with CJAR, students repor ted that they learned science can be used to address issues of social justice, that their voices  can be heard by local politicians, and they were e mpowered when connecting topics from history class (e.g., re dlining) to scientific issues in their community. I n addition to creating an arts-based product, Mr. Woodson had students write lette rs to local politicians. Many students didn’t think they would hear back, and when some did, they  were shocked.  In addition, f inding show the CJAR unit supported critical thinking and sense making studen ts are already doing in ways that cultivates justic e-centered place remaking. Engaging in the unit provided an op portunity for students to build on the sense making  they already do at the intersection of race, place, and power, through evidence-based reasoning. Mr. Woodson described how CJAR created a space for shared humanity: “Seeing the connection between the science of heat and cities and realizin g that it is an active issue, if not in their perso nal life, in the lives of people in their communities. So really per sonalizing and humanizing the science.” This type o f community relevant education is essential for reimagining sustainable and healthy communities.

## Conclusions and implications

Findings illustrate that the CJAR approach empowered teachers as climat e justice advocates and designers of experiences that fostered a shared sense of humanity. In addition, f indings show that engaging in CJAR practices shape d student learning, identity construction, critical sense making, and  provided opportunities to reimagine more just and sustainable community futures.
