# Towards Developing Scalable Assessments of Higher-Order Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To improve and broaden participation in science, technology, engineering and math (STEM) requires assessments of learning that are created within a compassionate learner-centered philosophy with heightened attention to ethics and bias. This poster proposes a path towards scalable, learner-mediated assessment instruments of higher-order cognitive abilities that would be convenient to implement at large scales or in any university class.

## Motivation

It is increasingly more valuable for students to learn higher-order skills such as creativity and critical thinking than to learn factual or procedural knowledge (OECD, 2018), especially as artificial intelligence gains sophistication. Yet many assessments of learning and teaching in STEM (sc ience, technology, engineering, and math), especially at large scales, tend to emphasize and reward lower-level factual a nd procedural learning (e.g., Momsen et al., 2013). Furthermore, many historical and current ways of assess ing knowledge and abilities disadvantage marginalized groups (e.g., Kincheloe et al., 1997; Clark, 2013). Learner-centered instructional approaches help students develop higher-order skills in STEM (e.g., Etkina & Planinsic, 2015; Handelsman et al.,

2006) and especially help underrepresented students (e.g., Theobald et al., 2020). Yet faculty are slow to adopt

learner-centered approaches (e.g., Henderson & Dancy, 2007). Most universities prim arily assess teaching through student evaluations, which are biased against minoritized faculty and ma y incentivize authoritative approaches (e.g., Kierstead et al., 1998; Deslauriers et al., 2019). To transform  educational institutions into equitable systems will require rethinking how we perform and use assessments, both for provid ing students with credentials and for judging the effectiveness of instruction. Proposal and invitation We need new ways to assess higher-order transferable learning of compl ex skills at large scales to find valid approaches that are easy for all instructors to implement, inherently helpf ul to both students and instructors as formative assessments, and learner-centered through integrating student fe edback in defining assessment categories (Sealfon, 2022). At large scales or in large university classes, it can become prohibitively costly to manually evaluate open-ended student learning artifacts in ways that encourage creative and divergent thinking. Short-answer reflection assignments such as exit tickets or one-minute  papers (Stead, 2005) require minimal time for students to complete, offer useful feedback to the instr uctor throughout the semester, can be implemented in any class or subject, and can be analyzed to assess student  learning. The quality of questions students ask correlates with higher-order learning (Harper et al., 2003; Chin & Osborne, 2008), and students’ reflections can assess the sophistication of students’ epistemological belie fs and metacognition (May & Etkina, 2002). If we could combine natural language processing and peer assessment to  gauge the quality of student responses to short-answer reflection questions quickly and easily, providing feedbac k to both the students and instructors without requiring significant instructor time, this could offer an effective assessment of higher-order learning at scale. If these short-answer reflection questions were asked regularly throughout each course in conjunction with timely feedback, not only would student learning improve, we could also as sess the growth of higher-order abilities over time. Such an assessment tool, if applied at large scales across faculties and departments to evaluate instructional approaches, could also help probe and reward learner-centered pedagogies. To develop equitable assessments, we need instruments that include explicit r ecognition of the uncertainties and biases in their results. Bias in human assessments of human learning is well recognized (e.g., Burgess & Greaves, 2013). Any automated classification of learning artifacts would encode the bias of the humans that create it (e.g., Hajian et al., 2016). Using peer assessment (e.g., Pare  & Joordens, 2008; Kay et al., 2020) combined with machine learning offers a promising direction to both uncover biases and address them. An AI can look for patterns in how a large number of diverse students and instructors evaluate work based on a rubric, and, through clustering, perhaps uncover multiple valid classifications and diversity in how pe ople interpret and express higher-order thinking. Such an assessment could evolve to include more  diverse ways of knowing and expressing learning, including multiple representations (Rosengrant et al., 2007) beyond the written word (Jones & Okun, 2001).

Eventually, we could imagine expanding this combination of peer assessment and ma chine learning to develop alternative credentialing mechanisms. Imagine that all students could pr oduce a portfolio of work CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 387 assessed by a group of peers and experts, and present those collective as sessments as evidence of their learning and qualifications for job opportunities. We could leverage technologies such as blockchain to keep track of these collective assessments for each learner, potentially allowing for more democratic and less centralized academic credentialing.

I invite discussions and collaborations to address this important issue of how we can assess higher-order learning equitably at scale, to work across multiple relevant fields, and to em power learners from diverse backgrounds to learn and to contribute to what it means to learn STEM.
