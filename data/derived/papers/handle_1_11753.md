# Engagement in Computational Thinking: The Role of Mother, Father with Daughters in After-school Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Parent-child interactions are pivotal for nurturing Computational Thinking (CT) in children, yet existing research often centers on parents with coding or STEM backgrounds interacting with one child, overlooking non-STEM parents, unique parental contributions, and sibling dynamics. This case study bridges these gaps by exploring how a mother and father without STEM backgrounds interact with their two daughters during an after-school CT program. Adopting Family System Theory and micro-analysis of video-recorded interactions, this study identified eleven shared and unique parental roles. The father uniquely acted as active observer and playful companion, while the mother displayed mediator and conflict navigator, maintainer of focus, and negotiator of autonomy. Viewing family as an interconnected system, these parental roles influenced children’s engagement and were shaped by individual and sibling characteristics. The findings affirm the importance of diverse family dynamics in creating supportive environments for CT education, with implications for parents, educators, and program designers.

## Introduction

Informal STEAM education can play a pivotal role in  fostering creativity, critical thinking, and probl em-solving skills by engaging learners in hands-on, real-world applications that complement formal education  (Banks et al., 2007; Yao & Mohr-Schroeder, 2019). After-school activities that involve parents provide valu able opportunities to observe family interactions and understand how they impact learning (Rosenberg et al., 2014), especially in the context of emerging educational priorities like com putational thinking (CT). Computational thinking, a fundamental skill in the digital age, involves syst ematic analysis and problem-solving across disciplines (Wing, 2006, 2011). Increasingly introduced to young child ren through manipulative tangible toys, CT can en hance problem-solving abilities, foster creativity and democratic participation, and support children’s social, emotional, and cognitive development (Bers, 2018; Clements & Gullo, 1984; Yu et al., 2020). Existing literature underscores parents’ diverse roles—such as teachers, collaborators, learners —in supporting children’s technological fluency and CT development in informal learning contexts (B arron et al., 2009; Ehsan et al., 2023; Hiniker et al., 2018). However, many of these studies primarily center on  parents with coding or STEM backgrounds interacting  with one child (Barron et al., 2009, 2019; Govind et al., 2020; Hiniker et al., 2018; Yu et al., 2020), overlooking nonSTEM parents, the unique contributions of different parents, and the dynamics introduced by sibling interactions within family learning environments such as after-school programs. Additionally, frameworks like pare ntal mediation theory (Yu et al., 2020) predominantly em phasize adult-child interactions without fully exploring the broader family system, including sibling relationships. Therefore, there is a need to examine how different parentsibling interactions within the family system influence children’s engagement in CT informal learning. To answer this research question, I conducted a case study in an informal setting with parents without STEM backgrounds, exploring how father-daughters and mother-daughters interactions within the family system shape parental roles and influence young children’s engagement in CT informal learning. An after-school CT colearning program provided a valuable environment for observing these dynamics, as it fac ilitated the joint participation of parents and siblings. Using Family  Systems Theory (Bowen, 1978) as a framework, I performed micro-analyses (Graue & Walsh, 1998) of selected video recordings to examine interactions. The findings inform strategies that combine both parent’s involvement to create a nurturing environment that fosters CT skills  in a family. This research underscores the need for designing CT  programs that incorporate family dynamics and provide adaptive resources for parents in informal learning settings.

## Literature review and theoretical framework

Research in early childhood education (ECE) has shown that children as young as four to six years old candevelop CT skills by building and programming simple roboti cs projects (Bers, 2010). Engaging with tangible ro botic manipulatives enables children to develop fine motor skills and hand-eye coordination while potentially fostering collaboration and teamwork (Bers et al., 2014). Fur thermore, such CT learning opportunities benefit young children by cultivating their interest in computing, lowering p articipation barriers, and supporting their social, emotional, and cognitive development (Bers, 2018; Clements & Gullo, 1984; Yu et al., 2020). These benefits are further amplified when learning is situated within family contexts. The substantial time young children spend with parents during early childhood presents a unique op portunity to influence their CT education, emphasiz ing parental critical roles in early learning, play, and technology use (Lavigne et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2020). Parental roles in supporting informal CT learning Studies have identified various parental roles that support technological fluency and engagement in CT education. Barron et al.’s (2009) ethnographic study of middle school students and pa rents was among the first to identify parental roles in technology-based learning environments,  including general roles like  teacher, collaborator, learning broker, resource provider, and other roles like non-technical consultant (offering advice on non-technical issues like project planning or time management), employer (employing children for technical services), and learner (learning technical skills from children). Sadka and Zuckerman (2017) expanded this work by explorin g parental roles in parent-child (age 8-12) interactions in co-making activities. They  identified a spectrum of parental roles along two dimensions: peer-parents, who prioritize successfully and efficiently completing the activity regardless of leadership, and mentor-parents, who emphasize supporting children’s learning process. This framework highlights the adaptable and fluid nature of parental roles in informal learning settings. Subsequent research has shown that parental roles i n informal CT learning with young children follow similar patterns. Through an observational lab study examin ing how 15 parents engaged with children aged 4 –6 play with tablet apps and analog toys, Hiniker et al. (2018) identified four parental roles: bystanders (maintaining a physical presence without active engagement), spe ctators (actively watching without direct involveme nt), coaches (guiding and supporting children with sugge stions and encouragement), and teammates (participa ting equally in the activity).  Yu et al. (2020) further explored the experiences of 18 parents with childre n ages 3-9 using coding kits at home  through interviews, finding that p arents adopted roles as spectators, dominators, executors (children direct parents to perform tasks), scaffolders (parents suggest a child to try different ways of playing, or ask questions to scaffold the play), and enforcers (parents mediate conflicts between siblings, remind them to take turns, and enforce time limits). They also indicated that  parents adjust their support based on children’s evolving skill levels and familiarity wi th the coding kits.  Ehsan et al. (2023) extended this line of research by examining interactions among 14 families with children aged 5-7 engaged in a computer-based coding game. They found that parents initially provided di rective support but gradually shifted to more passive roles, allowing for increased child autonomy as children gained con fidence and underscored a dynamic, responsive strategy where parents adapted their involvement to their children’s growing familiarity with CT tasks. Together, these studies illustrate parental roles evolve as children develop CT competencies. There is no consensus on the terminology or definit ions of parental roles in children’s CT education, as different studies use varied terms that often overlap or intersect in meaning. Despite this lack of standardization, common themes about how these roles influence young children’s CT learn ing and engagement  have emerged. Roque and her colleagues ’ work on Family Creative Learning (2016, 2021) indicates that when parents act as collaborators or learners, both their and their children’s engagement and learning significantly improve. Similarly, facilitation and co-learning are identified as particularly effective s trategies for fostering engagement during complex tasks such as debugging and algorithm devel opment (Ohland et al., 2019).  Govind et al. (2020) further identified parental roles like novice, assistant, teacher, coach, playmate, and pl anner can support CT learning during joint coding activities. Relkin et al. (2020) observed that parents who employed modeling, scaffolding, and collaborative problem-solving significantly shaped their children’s robot ics experiences by helping them stay focused, manage frustration, and encourage teamwork, thus enhancing their CT abilities. In contrast, Ohland et al. (2019) note that more authoritative roles, such as supervising and directing, were less effective in promoting higher-order CT engagement than facilitation and co-learning. Beals and Bers (2006) emphasized that children might learn less when parents dominate the learning  process without adjusting instruction to the child ’s comprehension level. Moreover, parental disengageme nt or a passive observer role was found to be minim ally beneficial or even detrimental to children’s CT learning (Govind et al., 2020; Ohland et al., 2019). Family dynamics on CT learning: Parental and sibling influences Parental roles in supporting children’s CT learning are also shaped by various family dynamics, including parents’ prior experience with coding, gender, and sibling relationships. Many studies focus on tech-savvy families, where parents often have coding or STEM backgrounds, enabling them to take on active roles as collaborators, mentors, or employers in their children’s computing activities (e.g., Barron et al., 2009, 2019; Govind et al., 2020; Hiniker et al., 2018). In contrast, parents without technical expertise often face challenges in defining their roles and identifying appropriate learning opportunities for their children (DiSalvo et al., 2014; Roque et al., 2016). Studies have shown that these parents  tend to adopt more passive roles, acting as reviewers or no vices while allowing their children to lead computing activities (Govind et al., 2020; Roque et al., 2016). To address this gap, our study offers a CT co-learning after-school program designed for families without STEM b ackgrounds and provid es instructor support, positioning parents as learners and enabling them to develop their roles alongside their children throughout the CT learning process.

Research indicates that parental gender also shapes parent-child interactions in CT learning. Relkin et al.

(2020) found that mothers participating in a KIBO robot day activity were more likely to describe the experience

as collaborative, whereas fathers tended to perceiv e it as child-directed. Similarly, Barron et al. (2019) observed that mothers are generally more proactive in facilitating learning opportunities. However, Cai and Wong’s (2023) systematic review highlights a gap in understanding the distinct roles of fathers versus mothers in CT education and their impact on child participation and outcomes. Additionally, existing studies rarely explore how parent s within the same family uniquely contribute to their  child’s CT learning.  These parental differences, alongside sibling dynamics, offer valuable insights into designing family-based CT learning environments that leverage the complementary roles of mothers and fathers. For instance, Yu et al. (2020) introduced the concept of the parental enforcer role—mediating sibling conflicts and enforcing time limi ts—but its influence on CT learning remains underexplored. Investigating these dynamics could i nform the creation of collaborative and inclusive l earning environments that effectively support children’s engagement and development in CT education. To bridge these gaps, the current study applies Family System s Theory (FST) to explore how father-daughters and mother-daughters interactions within the family system shape parenta l roles and influence young children’s engagement in CT informal learning.  FST views the family as a social system composed of interconnected individuals whose interactions influence one another and the system as a whole (Broderick, 1993, p.49). This perspective provides a lens to examine the dyn amic relationships within families and their  influence on children’s learning experiences. Key concepts such as differentiation—the capacity of individuals to maintain their autonomy while staying emotionally connected —and triangling— the process of the predictable patterned moves of emotional forces between any three people (Bowen, 1978)—illustrate how family members mutually shape behaviors and development. Furthermore, equifinality, a concept rooted in general systems theory and applied within family systems theory,  suggests that families can achi eve similar outcomes through different pathways (Cicchetti & Rogosch, 1996). This framework acknowledges the complexity and interconnectedness of family relationships, offering a richer understandi ng of how family dynamics enhance or impede  children’s engagement in CT learning.

## Methods

This case study drew on a design-based research Learn CT Tog ether aimed at empowering parents and young children to learn CT using toy kits. Conducted in 2023, the five-week, nine-session CT summer camp took place at a local non-profit urban center in the Northeastern United Stat es. Families engaged with Thames & Kosmos Coding and Robotics (TKCR) toys  (see Fig. 1), selected for their tangible coding cards and stor y-based CT concepts like sequence, loops and conditionals, which provided accessible scaffolding for diverse  learners. The TKCR kits enable families to create personalized characters, explore engaging stories illustrating CT challenges, and use coding cards to program robots collaboratively on assembled map cards. In this study, “coding” refers to assembling arrow cards to guide robot movement.  Each session typically begins with an instructor-led introduction of a new concept, followed by a playfu l embodied task or simple coding challenge to reinf orce understanding. It then progresses to a more complex coding challenge and concludes with a reflective discussion. I wore different hats throughout the research  as a researcher, instructor, curriculum designer, and ob server, preparing materials, facilitating activities, introducing story-based challenges, and supporting parents as needed. Due to scheduling conflicts, the two families attended the sessions sequentially, with a typical overlap of about half an hour. Data collection included video recordings, pre-and post-interviews with primarily involved parents, and reflective field notes.  This study focuses on one bi-racial family (n=4) without a STEM or coding background: M ary (a White mother and social worker), David (a Wh ite father working in a museum), and their adopted Afri can Americandaughters, Kay (age 5) and May (age 6). The videos from the first three sessions were purposely selected to allow for an in-depth analysis of unique family dynamics and parental interaction styles.  In Session 1, both sisters collaborated to build a robot with Mary, providing insights into sibling interactions and Mary’s engagement style. In Session 2, due to scheduling conflicts, David led the session, offering an opportunity to o bserve his approach to guiding the children through  coding games and challenges through learning sequencing. In Session 3, Mary returned to explore loops with the children, allowing a comparison of parental interaction style s across tasks. The analyzed data includes a total of 3.7 hours of video recordings, along with a transcript of the parent-child conversations. For data analysis, a micro-analysis of video recordings (Graue & Walsh, 1998) was employed, focusing on father-daughters, mother-daughters interactions, and individual child characteristics within these interactions, to examine their engagement in CT learning sessions. The audio was transcribed using TurboScript and verified by the author to ensure accuracy.  I employed grounded theory strategies (Charmaz, 2006) to uncov er processes contributing to parents’ role development, focusing on their initiative actions, interactions with their daughters, children’s responses, and individual child characteristics during these interactions. The previously identified roles were referenced but renamed or redefined to better align with the interaction patterns observed in our study. All three video recordings were reviewed for an overview of interactions. Descriptive coding in Atlas.ti, captured 226  observable interaction features, including both parent-initiated and child-initiated key verbal responses, actions, and indicators of children’s engagement (e.g., M: clarify objectives; May: “it feels too long”). These codes were grouped and thematically analyzed to identify recurring patterns, with role s consolidated into themes comparing father-and mother-initiative roles (see Table 1). Smagorinsky’s (2008) “collaborative coding” approach was used  to ensure relia bility, with preliminary analyses reviewed and finalized through another teammate’s consensus.

## Findings and discussions

This study identified eleven roles parents assume while interacting with their children using CT coding kits. Seven roles are shared between Mary and David: instructor, collaborative facilitator, evaluator, t ask manager and material organizer, encourager providing emotional support, encourager promoting independence and risk-taking, and reflective practitioner. David exhibited two unique roles: active observer  and playful companion, while Mary displayed three unique roles: mediator and conflict navigator, maintainer of focus, and negotiator of autonomy, highlighting each parent’s unique contributions t o their children’s CT learning experiences.  In the following sections, I examine these shared and distinct roles, contextual izing findings within existing literature and micro-interactions, while exploring how individual charac teristics both influence and are influenced by parental roles through the lens of FST.

Shared roles Parents predominantly act as instructors, blending the roles of coach (Hiniker et al., 2018) and scaffolder (Yu et al., 2020) while also emphasizing their learning process. A key strategy within this role is re-voicing. Unlike typical re-voicing strategies that involve repeating, paraphra sing, or expanding on students’ utterances, this approach also includes reiterating instructor instr uctions, reinforcing CT concepts, and reflecting on  children’s coding progress—both correct and incorrect. This method supports and enhances children’s learning by adapting to their developmental needs for repetition, assisting them in reflecting on their coding processes and encouraging parents to reflect on their own understanding. As collaborative facilitators, parents take on playmate and part of enforcer roles, from collaborating with their children on co ding tasks to fostering teamwork between siblings. Furthermore, parents serve as task managers and material organizers, akin to logistic supporters (Yu et al., 2020) or resource providers (Barron et al., 2009), by ass igning tasks and supplying necessary resources, esp ecially during activities like map card  assembly. The nuanced meaning of these latter roles is shaped  by sibling characteristics, as parents often adjust their strategies based on sibling interactions—encouraging collaboration or balancing attention to ensure equitable participation.

Unlike previous literature, which often integrates evaluation into parental scaffolding or guiding roles, our study identifies the evaluator role as distinct. This role allows parents to assess their children ’s progress and decide whether, or to what degree, to intervene or give them space to explore independently. The coding process with TKCR toy kit particularly supports this role by allowing parents to visualize their child’s coding process. Parents observe as children sequentially place the start card, select and assemble arrow cards, use the red card as the end, position the robot to scan the codes, place the robot on the map cards, and press the start button to make it move. The time gap between coding and the robot’s movements offers parents a clear opportunity for evaluative observation, enhancing their ability to decide on appropriate timing and levels of intervention. While previous literature often describes changes i n parental roles as part of the interaction process (Ehsan et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2020), our findings highlight reflective practitioner as a distinct role in which parents actively reflect on and continuously adjust their b ehavior. Both parents displayed this capacity for r eflection and adaptation throughout the sessions. For instance, at the start of session 1, the mother sought guidance on her role, asking the instructor,  “So how much intervention should I have? Should I ta lk? Should I just stay quiet? ” (S1), indicating uncertainty about which roles to take and showing her awareness of how her involvement might impact her children’s learning. In session 3, when she realized she had mistakenly led May in the wrong direction, causing an error, she quickly corrected herself and apologized (S3). Similarly, at the start of session 2, David initially led the children to code correctly but later reflected on his role by asking, “ I’m allowed to help them, right? They don’t have to be able to do it independently?” (S2). This reflection prompted him to adjust his approach; he then decided to give his children more learning space by  standing back and observing what they could accomp lish individually without direct guidance or instructions. These instances demonstrate that parents are not always sure about which roles they should assume and that both consciously adjusted their involvement based on the ir reflections, children’s responses, and instructor insights, with their roles evolving throughout the program.  Their adaptability and self-awareness allowed them to effectively support their children’s learni ng by providing appropriate guidance or fostering independence.

Roles that emerged in our study but are rarely discussed in existing literature are two distinct encourager roles: offering emotional support and promoting independence  and risk-taking. These functions are often embedded within roles like spectators or coaches (Govind et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2020). Here, we emphasize these encourager roles as essential for fostering children’s engagement in learning and supporting their social-emotional development. Specifically, both parents take roles like encouragers with e motional support  by offering encouragement and praise, celebrating successes, and providing comfort during challenges, which is key  to keeping children engaged. In some instances, when May struggled with coding a nd expressed frustration, her parents’ encouragement and validation of her effort s helped her regulate her emotions and persist in c ompleting the coding tasks.

In comparison,  the role encourager of independence and risk-taking varies between parents slightly. David tends to be more proactive in promoting independence, directly encouraging their children with statements like, “Just do your best, do the best you can, then we’ll test it (S2),” and, “It’s okay to make mistakes. When we test it, we figure out what doesn’t work, and we fi x it (S2).” In contrast, Mary encourages independence more passively, often responding to the child’s frustrat ions or when prompted by the instructo r. These differing approaches further influence the interactions between parents and their daughter s: when coding with David, the daughters take more initiative and remain actively engaged, while with Mary, they tend to be more passive  or defend their autonomy. This observation sets the stage for the next session of each parent’s distinct roles. Distinct roles The father often assumes the role of an active observer, similar to the spectator role, but with a stronger emphasis on stepping back to give the child space to explore  independently. For instance, in session 2, he stood up and simply watched as his two children engaged in codin g activities  after he reflected  and adjusted his guiding strategy. He intervened only when assistance was necessary —when Kay failed multiple times and did not know which code needed to be changed —thereby fostering an environment that promoted auto nomy and self-directed learning. This selective support allows the children to build problem-solving skills independently. In addition, the father acts as a playful companion. He uses humor and positive body language—such as smiling, high-fiving, and laughing—to create a relaxed and enjoyable atmosphere. This approach helps alleviate the stress of completing the challenges successfully and makes the learning process more engaging for th e children. For example, he smiled at children’s failed try without further comment and used laughter to releas e any tension and frustration caused by it, promoting “pleasant frustration ” (Sadka & Zuckerman, 2017). This corresponds to his previous comments, “It doesn’t have to be perfect. Just leave it like that. ” and about mistakes,  which reframed errors as natural parts of the learning process, encouraging resilience and a growth mindset. Under this relaxed atmosphere, the children did not show much frustration after failures; instead, they immediate ly engaged in debugging and were eager to try again. These roles are less observed in mother-daughter interactions, as Mary often remains more involved in the children’s coding process and shows a stronger eagerness to ensure the challenge is completed. Additionally, siblings tend to interact differently with Mary and David, a distinction explored further in the following paragraphs.

The mother frequently takes on the role of a mediator and conflict navigator, similar to the enforcer role (Yu et al., 2020), managing disagreements or misund erstandings among the children. She facilitates communication and compromise, ensuring that both ch ildren’s perspectives are considered and fostering a cooperative environment. For example, when the children disagreed about which robot to make, she proposed a solution that accommodated both preferences with a reason, “ Since we have limited time today, let ’s do the penguin robot today and save the sandwich robot for a future session.  (S1)” This mediation helped the children resolve conflicts amicably and continue working toget her effectively. The mother also serves as a maintainer of focus, actively working to keep the children engaged wit h the tasks and re-engaging them when they become distracted. This likely reflects her expectation for the childr en’s continuous engagement in CT learning. In contrast, this role is less evident with the father, as the siblings generally remained engaged during his session, and he allowed them to disengage and re-engage at their own pace. While the mother’s extended time with the children may have exposed her to more disengagement, necessitating her efforts to maintain their focus. The mother also acts as a negotiator of autonomy, a s her involvement can occasionally create tension with the children’s desire for independence. She ma y inadvertently interfere with their sense of contr ol by not providing sufficient space for independent exploration or an appropriate level of scaffolding, prompting resistance as they assert greater ownership over their learning. For instance, in session 3, the mother attempted to guide her children’s coding choices multiple times, resulting  in refusals—May declined her help four times and responded with, “No, no, no” or “stop, stop, stop” (May, S3), and Kay once. This reflects an awakening of autonomy in the older child, May, who is beginning to assert more c ontrol over her learning experiences. It also suggests that as children grow older, they may have different expect ations of parental scaffolding —what one child perceives as helpful guidance, another may view as unnecessary i nterference. In addition, Kay’s refusal of her mother’s help after observing May’s similar refusals exemplifies how si bling behavior can impact individual responses with in the family system. This reciprocal influence aligns  with FST’s concept of interconnected roles, where one sibling’s behavior sets a precedent for the other’s interactions (Kerig, 2019). After May declined her help several times, the mother redirected her focus to Kay, offering direct instructions, “So we need two more straight cards,” and pointing at the provided guidebook due to the complexity of the task. However, this approach caused Kay to disengage and become unwilling to finish the task, as it didn’t align with her preferred learning styl e and limited her agency. This scenario reflects that excessive parental invo lvement can hinder children’s learning by limiting their independence (Beals & Bers, 2006), highlighting the balance between guidance and autonomy. These distinct parental roles highlight different s trategies for supporting children’s CT learning. Th e father’s approach as an active observer and playful  companion fosters independence and reduces pressur e, encouraging confidence and problem-solving skills through humor and positive reinforce ment. This reflects the FST concept of differentiation (Broderick, 1993), highlighting maintaining autonomy while staying emotionally connected. In contrast, the mother’s role as a mediator and fo cus maintainer creates an organized, collaborative environment, ensuring children work together and st ay on task —though her well-intentioned efforts may have overlooked the children’s growing desire for autono my and their unique learning preferences.  This dynamic can be explained by triangling (Bowen, 1978); the interactions between the mother and her two daug hters form a triangle where emotional dynamics influence each individual’s behavior and development. Recognizing when to step back and allow the childre n to take the lead can help balance necessary guidance with promoting autonomy, and enhancing development of differentiation within the family system. The principle of equifinality (Cicchetti & Rogosch, 1996) suggests that both the father’s and mother’s strategies can effectively support children’s CT learning despite their differences. Understanding these distinct roles can inform strategies for involving parents in CT education. In this family context, e ncouraging David to maintain a supportive yet autonomous learning space and guidin g Mary to balance involvement with opportunities for independence may enhance parental participation. Combining the strengths of both approaches enables parents to build a supportive environment that cultivates CT skills and nurtures independent, confident learners. Individual’s influence Our analysis of individual characteristics (not shown due to page limitations) shows that while May and Kay share similarities in playfulness and independence, they differ significantly in their learning styles and r esponses to challenges. Kay adopts an experimental, embodied ap proach to learning, using physical movements and mo dels during coding activities. She usually works independently on straightforward tasks but ac tively seeks support when faced with more complex ones. One year older than Kay, May exhibits an active, exploratory learning style characterized by increasing independence and select ive help-seeking. She prefers tackling challenging tasks independently and only seeks assistance when necessary. By incorporating their individual characteristics i nto parent-daughter interactions, we can see how individuals influence the family system overall. For example, both parents provide more scaffolding to  Kay than May, acknowledging that Kay, being younger and often needing more guidance, benefits from additional support. May, demonstrating greater independence and asserti veness, receives less direct intervention, allowing  her autonomy in her learning. In addition, the parents employ different comforting strategies when dealing with each child. With Kay, who may become frustrated with com plex tasks, parents often offer gentle encouragement and step-by-step guidance to alleviate her frustration. With Ma y, recognizing her persistence and resilience, they affirm her efforts and allow her space to tackle challenge s independently. This tailored approach highlights how each child’s needs and characteristics shape the dynamics of parental interaction and influence the family system (Shanahan & Sobolewski, 2003). By adapting their support to meet each child’s individual needs, parents not only facilitate their children’s engagement in CT learni ng but also contribute to the overall functioning o f the family system.

## Conclusion and implication

In conclusion, our study identified eleven parental  roles in supporting children’s CT learning with co ding kits. These roles, which include both shared and unique c ontributions from the mother and father within one family, highlight the complex dynamics of family interactions and their impact on children’s engagement in CT learning. By understanding these roles through the lens of Fa mily Systems Theory, this study emphasizes the importance of tailoring parental involvement strategies that c onsider family dy namics and children’s unique characteristics to effectively nurture independent, confident learners. Applying FST reveals how family dynamics intricately influence children’s engagement in CT learning. First, the individual characteristics of siblings K ay and May significantly shape how parents interact  with each child. Second, interactions between one child and p arents can influence another child’s engagement, as one sibling’s behavior sets a precedent for the other’s  interactions (Kerig, 2019).  Third, the distinct roles the father and mother take affect the siblings’ engagement in CT learning and the ir reciprocal interaction with each parent. For example,  the father’s role as an active observer and playful  companion creates a relaxed environment, allowing children to engage naturally without needi ng frequent re-engagement. Conversely, the mother actively re-engages the children  when they become distracted, possibly reflecting he r expectations for sustained engagement or concerns about balancing the attentio n of both children.  These differing parental approaches illustrate how individual roles impact the family’s overall functioning (Broderick, 1993).

Our findings highlight the importance of understanding family dynamics in informal educational settings. For parents, recognizing each child’s unique needs and characteristics can guide more effective and personalized support strategies that balance guidance with independence. Educators and program designers can leverage these insights to develop resources and training that equip parents with adaptive facilitation strat egies, promoting children’s collaborative and autonomous learning. Furthermore, incorporating structured collaborative activities that account for sibling dynamics can foster positive relationships,  enhance engagement, and enrich the overall learning experience.  Future studies should explore interventions to opti mize family roles and interactions, focusing on the long-term impacts of parental and sibling dynamics  on children’s CT learning and engagement. Expanding research to diverse family structures and cultural contexts would provide deeper insights into how FST applies across varied settings, enhancing the design of inclusive CT programs. At the same time, researchers and educators should be cautious not to essentialize or privilege  any particular family structure or reinforce heteronormative or gendered assumptions and instead  consider the diversity and complexity of family ro les and identities in both analysis and program design.
