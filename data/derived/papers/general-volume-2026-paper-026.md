# CLUE: A Learning-Goal Oriented AI-Enhanced STEM Inquiry System

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Integrating AI into STEM classrooms risks shortcutting the productive struggle that makes inquiry-based learning effective. This session introduces CLUE, a collaborative platform that addresses this risk through rubric-centered AI personalization: rather thandeploying generative AI as an open-ended chat interface, CLUE uses curriculum-tuned categorization rubrics to make student problem-solving strategies visible to both learners and teachers. In this way, CLUE’s rubric-centered approach operationalizes sensemaking for any particular curriculum. We conjecture that this design supports teacher noticing, promotes metacognitive reflection, and sustains productive struggle by shifting AI feedback away from answer-giving and toward strategy awareness and peer comparison. We will demonstrate several classroom-tested STEM curricula paired with these AI scaffolds, and attendees will participate in a live collaborative experience to see how CLUE's rubric-based approach makes AI-generated enrichment transparent and purposeful. The session will explore the design opportunities and challenges behind this methodology, invite discussion about alternative approaches, and share directions for future empirical evaluation. Attendees will leave with practical strategies for integrating AI into STEM classes and access to CLUE tools for use in their own classrooms.

Keywords: Artificial Intelligence in Education, Collaborative Learning, STEM Inquiry Tool demonstration description Intended audience and duration This half hour tutorial/demonstration is for teachers of grades 6-12 STEM classrooms wishing to incorporate collaborative learning, interactive STEM inscriptional tools (student-controlled graphs, tables, programs, equations, drawings, etc.), and an AI system that fosters productive metacognitive discussion into their classrooms. This demo will invite teachers to reflect on a system based on the premise that AI should be used to ask the student to reflect on their own answer rather than provide one. Theme and goals We will introduce and demonstrate a purpose-built AI-enabled system called the Collaborative Learning User Environment (CLUE) (Bondaryk & Dorsey, 2021). This system allows students to create open-ended digital work with live-linked tools for STEM problem solving including graphs, data tables, programming tools, sensor tools, sketches, and others (Figure 1, right). Students can borrow and expand upon each other's work in real time, and teachers can observe the latest and historical versions of students’documents. In particular, we will explore CLUE AI features that associate students’ work with a categorization rubric that can be tuned to support a variety of curricula (Figure 1, below). We will discuss how centering views of work according to the rubric provides a novel technique for introducing AI assistance into a STEM course. From a theoretical standpoint, this work will be some of the first to use AI to personalize feedback about the students’ learning journey rather than focus on problem scoring and sequencing. The ability to identify and track problemsolving strategies across multiple problems and learning goals allows teachers and students to observe how reasoning evolves over time. We will discuss how CLUE AI affordances encourage reflection on key curriculum ideas. ## Theoretical background and relevance to field and conference

Collaborative, inquiry-oriented curricula are increasingly used across STEM disciplines, especially in science education (Crippen & Archambault, 2012). These curricula are ideal for generating interest and facility in technical subjects and boosting achievement (Roehrig et al., 2021). In contrast with more traditional math and science teaching, these curricula afford students an opportunity to learn STEM in an environment that more authentically mirrors the practices of practitioners and STEM-literate laypeople (Chinn & Duncan, 2021) Further, collaborative inquiry has been shown to work well for low-performing learners and underrepresented students in STEM disciplines, particularly when combined with peer-to-peer learning that distributes intellectual authority to learners and disrupts status and power hierarchies (Harris et al., 2024). Well-aligned curricula that emphasize a descriptive domain-specific language and progress against outcomes produce more motivation and retention across the subject area (Levine et al., 2008; Winkelmes et al., 2016). Despite the advantage of this type of curriculum and the productive struggle embodied by inquiry, such courses can be challenging to teach because they require instructors to notice and navigate student thinking with multiple representations and align those noticings with overarching course practices productively in real time (Bolyard et al., 2023; Dreher & Kuntze, 2015). For other teachers, attending to student thinking is a critical part of their planning and reflection (Lampert, 2001; Stein et al., 2008) but they require robust tools to help them summarize efficiently across large numbers of students. Still other teachers face significant challenges when facilitating discussions that build on student thinking (e.g., Leinhard & Steele, 2005). Supporting these inquiry classrooms full of open-ended descriptive STEM work would seem an obvious application for new large language model (LLM) AI systems. Unfortunately, open-ended AI analysis of student generated content is fraught because of the potential for hallucination, bias, and decline in student critical thinking (Ahmad et al., 2023) exposed by open-ended AI chat interactions. A more structured AI approach is required that enhances teachers’ noticing and surfaces moments of productive self-reflection for students. The AI approach taken in CLUE is to try to mimic the best practices of teachers in inquiry classes, supporting both teachers and students to apply the benefits of metacognitive analysis more robustly in their day to day work. For each curriculum developed in the CLUE platform, we work collaboratively with teachers to establish a categorization rubric that specifically allows AI to expose student strategies in problem solving. This AI approach aligns with the needs of the teachers in scaffolding, supports students in self-analysis, and surfaces the larger goals of the curriculum. It also offers a repeatable and influential pathway to codesign, rather than requiring teachers to speculate on ‘including AI in their classrooms’ which is often too vague or unfamiliar to be productive. Instead w e ask our teacher collaborators to discuss how they evaluate student progress, and what metrics we should look for in student work to identify trajectories of reasoning. Alongside the strengths of using inquiry to learn, authentic engagement with science involves learning to inquire, individually and as a science community (Chinn & Duncan, 2021; Ford, 2008; Pedaste et al., 2015). Students need to metacognitively develop the practices of the field and understand the nature of the STEM domain(s) they are working with so that they will understand how STEM works and how they can inquire beyond the classroom walls (Veenman, 2012). Accordingly, CLUE’s strategy categorization rubrics can be used as a guide to suggest productive directions to students with or without teacher intervention, focusing on expanding strategy use rather than assessing or marking student correctness. CLUE introduces natural options and interfaces for inclusion of the AI categorization that support deep reasoning without revealing answers or over-directing inquiry. The CLUE platform uses a combination of real-time sharing of interactive notational tools (Edson et al.,

2023) and AI interventions and summaries that ask students to describe and evaluate what they have learned, what

strategies they have employed, and to compare the work of their classmates to observe the variety of approaches that produce comparable results. These results are returned to both students and teachers, allowing them to find better patterns of review and collaboration that support this style of problem-based learning. We conjecture that ready access to reliable and class-wide rubric-driven categorization will shift classroom discourse toward strategy comparison by making visible the variety of approaches students take, which in turn supports deep thinking about content and the STEM inquiry process, rather than rote memorization of solution techniques. We already have some early evidence that this approach will be productive. For example, in middle grades mathematics classrooms using the Connected Mathematics 4 curriculum (Phillips et al. 2025), we focus on students’ proportional reasoning and use a machine learning–supported rubric to analyze their approaches, representations, and strategies rather than correct answers (Edson et al., under review). In this design, AI functions as an analytic layer that identifies and tracks evidence of student reasoning across problems, making patterns of strategy use visible to teachers and students in real time. By structuring feedback around strategy categorization and teacher interpretation instead of open-ended chat responses, this approach offers a principled alternative to answer-oriented AI while directly supporting metacognitive reflection and teacher noticing in inquiry classrooms. The math classrooms engaging in this form of collective summary discussion using CLUE’s dashboard views (Figure 1) are able productively use this motivation of class discourse and productive struggle and even reuse and manipulate each other’s techniques for greater understanding. Preliminary informal observations from other classroom deployments of CLUE across other research curricula are consistent with this expectation, though we present them here as motivation for future studies rather than as confirmatory evidence. Outline of planned demonstration Participants will create logins in the online CLUE system, and will be briefly introduced to the broad features of the system. They will be taken through several lessons in biology, algebra, and engineering design, experiencing first hand the collaborative and AI features of the system. Feedback will be solicited from the audience on the approach employed to incorporate inquiry AI, group work, project-based learning, and interactive tools for exploring dynamic multiple representations.

We will further discuss how AI categorization is technically produced by ChatGPT 4.5 models, including both in the moment and summary information keyed from text and image analysis of student work and logs of sequences of student actions. Participant feedback from the demo will be gathered both on the usability and accessibility of the platform, and ideas about what kinds of rubrics would be effective in different domains and grade levels.

We will provide references to the existing CLUE curricula available through the Concord Consortium’s free learning management system, and instruct attendees on how they can create their own custom CLUE curriculum if they desire.

## Facilities, equipment, participants

Each participant who wishes to participate in the live collaborative demo will require a laptop with a modern version of the Chrome Browser and internet connectivity. If attendees do not wish to participate in the collaborative demonstration they are still welcome to watch the demo and join the discussion. Tools demo coordinators Ms. Bondaryk is the lead designer of the CLUE system, and an investigator on 8 CLUE-related NSF grants. Ms. Bondaryk has introduced new technologies to educational research and school publishing, including the first Web Calculus text, Pearson’s Mastering and MyLabs product lines, and Schaum’s Interactive Outline Series. She was a defining force behind Mathcad software and the educational version Studyworks. She is widely published on t echnology in classrooms, and on collaborative and AI technologies in STEM teaching and learning. The present demonstration brings together a variety of hands-on teacher training experiences Ms. Bondaryk has conducted over the last 6 years.

Dr. Ido Davidesco is an assistant professor and the director of the Lab-to-Classroom Research Group at Boston College’s Lynch School of Education. His research is positioned at the intersection of cognitive psychology, neuroscience, and educational technology. He conducts Electroencephalography (EEG) and eye-tracking research in the laboratory and in real-world classrooms to understand fluctuations in attention and engagement over time and their relation to learning. He is also interested in using neuroscience methods to understand how students engage with educational technology.

Dr. Na’ama Y. Av-Shalom is a postdoctoral scholar in the Lab-to-Classroom Research Group at Boston College’s Lynch School of Education. Her work centers on students’ epistemic thinking and discourse in inquiry-oriented STEM contexts. She is also interested in teachers’ learning and implementation of integrated-STEM curricula. Dr. Alden J. Edson is a research associate professor in the Program for Mathematics Education (PRIME) and the Connected Mathematics Project (CMP) at Michigan State University. AJ is interested in the enactment of problem-based, inquiry-oriented mathematics curriculum by students and their teacher in a digital world and the affordances of innovative mathematics curriculum materials as a context for teacher learning.
