# Learning in Your Own Direction: Examining Relationships Between Non-Sequential User Choice and Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study sought to address an aspect of learner control known as non-sequential user choice. Leveraging open driver education resources, this research explored how learners’ chosen learning sequences and the time they spend with each learning resource relate to performance on an assessment of learning. The study examined learning outcomes for subjects with free choice over learning sequence based on time spent and chosen sequence. Analyses yielded a significant regression model and raise the possibility that time spent and chosen order may be able to predict performance.

## Introduction

The open learning movement has grown significantly in recent years as a result of work in both the pub lic and private sectors.  Inherent in the open learning movement is the notion of choice-based learning: that is, that learners can and should be given substantial control over their learning processes.  Unfortunately, significant gaps remain in the research literature examining the effects of  giving learners extensive control, particularly wi th regard to learners’ control over the sequence of the learning process (Koseoglu & Bozkurt, 2018).

## Background

Non-sequential user choice is defined as the ability for individual learners to use a set of learning materials in a non-linear fashion (Chou, 2003).  Existing research has found mixed effects of this type of choice.  For instance, Taub et al. (2020) found that a moderate amount of choice led to larger learning gains than high-and low-choice conditions, while Nebel et al. (2016) found motivat ional benefits but not improved learning gains. The  present research seeks to further explore this issue, with a focus on the effect of users’ specific choices and pacing.

## Methods

Materials and data sources The open learning materials used for this study wer e developed to target specific gaps in drivers’ kno wledge of advanced car safety systems as identified by McDona ld et al. (2016), and leverage the mental model-ori ented design framework from Beggiato & Krems (2013).  They are divided into three main sections focused on Adaptive Cruise Control (ACC): Understand, Challenge, and Play.

## Participants & Study Procedures

42 college undergraduates were recruited via mass email and tasked with learning about ACC.  Only individuals who were familiar with conventional cruise control but unfamiliar with ACC were included in the present study. Participants were required to use all three learnin g resources, but were able to choose the order in w hich they interacted with them, as well as their pacing.  The order in which the resources were listed on the ch oice screen was counterbalanced.  After choosing a resource, pa rticipants could interact with it for as long as th ey wished. Following engagement with all three resources, participants’ knowledge of ACC was assessed using a pos t-test assessment developed by Valentine et al. (2018) for this set of learning materials. Analysis Post-test scores were calculated for each subject, and those scores were used as the outcome variable in a multiple linear regression analysis (alpha =.05).  The regression analysis involved four predictor variables: chosen order, time on the Understand resource, time using the Challenge resource, and time spent with the Play resource.

Participants spent far more time using the Play (M = 635.02s) and Challenge (M = 467.02s) resources than the Understand resource (M = 106.74s). Regression analysis The multiple linear regression analysis found a significant regression equation (F(4,37) = 3.930, p <.05) with an R2 of 0.298, indicating that order and timing variables predict a significant proportion of the variation in post-test score in this sample.  Participants’ predicted post-test score is equal to 8.745 +.002a -.002b +.023c +.446d, where Challenge time is coded as a, Play time as b, Understand time as c, and chosen order as d.  Although the overall model was significant, Understand time was the only individually significant predictor of post-test score.

## Non-significance of chosen order

The present research sought to examine the relationships among chosen learning sequence, time spent on learning, and performance on an assessment of learning.  The results indicate that timing and chosen sequence can be used to predict post-test score, but time spent on the Understand resource was the only individually significant predictor. Surprisingly, chosen learning sequence was not an individually significant predictor of post-test scores. One possible explanation is the homogeneity of learning sequences chosen by study subjects.  As a result of this homogeneity, it becomes much more difficult  to detect any significant predictive value of chos en order. The descriptive statistics point to the possibility that choice homogeneity obscured an effect, with l earners who chose Order 1 (U,C,P) scoring lower overall on the post-test assessment.  This suggests that Order 1 c ould represent a poor choice of sequence and that participants may be performing worse on the post-test as a result.

## Potential significance & future research

These findings present an intriguing extension upon previous research by Bamberger and Tal (2005) and Taub et al. (2020).  Namely, that previous work suggests th at the provision of choice matters in learners’ performance, while the present research indicates that the specific choices learners make in those situations may not be crucial. Although chosen order was not found to be an individually significant predictor of post-test performance, descriptive statistics suggest that its influence m ay have been obscured by the homogeneity of partici pants’ sequential choices.  Studies with a larger sample size or forced sequencing may be better equipped to investigate the effects of specific learning sequences, as well  other types of choice, varied levels of choice gui dance, and different learning contexts/materials.
