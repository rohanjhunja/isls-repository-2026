# “A Makerspace is a Lousy Place to Do School In”: Studying Student-Driven Sociocultural Shifts in a Classroom Makerspace

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper investigates longitudinal shifts in sociocultural activity in a K-8 classroom makerspace in the United States. Using cultural historical activity theory (CHAT) as a theoretical framework and practitioner reflections over three years as a primary data source, a school-based practitioner researcher and university-based researcher identify key shifts in activity and tensions that emerged when making was both enabled and constrained by school. Findings illustrate the central role that students’ acts of self-determination can have on shifting a classroom makerspace activity system, particularly when a teacher is attuned and receptive to those acts of self-determination. Key tensions emerged related to teacher as central node for facilitating activity, even as the system shifted with student-directed activity.

The emergence of makerspaces over the past decade and a half offer s opportunities to study designs for and processes of learning within and beyond school s (Blikstein et al., 2023; Halverson & Peppler, 2018).  While making and makerspaces are often positioned as alternatives to school, “uncritical adoption” of narrow visions for making (Vossoughi et al., 2016) risks replicating some of the same shortcomings schooling in the United States (e.g., Becker, 1972; Rogoff et al., 2001). Researchers have characterized tensions that emerge when making practices and models are imported into schools (e.g., Gravel and Puckett, 2023; Stornaiuolo & Nichols, 2020). In their review of research on school-based makerspaces, Rouse et al. (2022) called for attention to teaching practices that support the development of maker communities of practice. Rouse et al. noted that school-based makerspace studies with younger children tended to resemble classrooms with centers rather than the more open design of out-of-school makerspaces with wider access to tools. In this paper, we —a school-based practitioner researcher (teacher) and university-based researcher (professor)—study sociocultural shifts in a K-8 classroom makerspace in the United States over three years. In this study, the teacher began his tenure in the classroom makerspace designing class-wide projects organized around teacher-selected practices and tools. Over time, students proposed projects and used tools of their choice, in and outside of class time. We investigate these questions: (1) What were the catalysts and conditions for shifts in activity in a classroom makerspace over three years ? (2) What tensions contributed to and emerged from shifts in overlapping classroom makerspace activity systems? Schools, makerspaces, and school-based makerspaces There is no shortage of arguments about the shortcomings of schools in the United States, from Dewey (1902) and Baldwin (1963) to Papert (1980) and Ladson-Billings (2006). In playfully detailing how “schools are a lousy place to learn anything in,” Becker (1972) c ritiqued the standardization and simplification of curriculum, hierarchical roles of teachers and students in terms of who know things and who decides what to do and when, institutional focus on proving school’s efficacy through narrow measures of learning disconnected from the world beyond school, and uses of grades not as feedback but as labels and social markers. Rogoff et al. (2001) similarly enumerated taken-for-granted assumptions about schools and offered alternative models based on studies of children learning alongside adults. Researchers have argued that the primary sociocultural activity in schools is often about learning to do school  and less about engaging in disciplinary pursuits or connecting to the world beyond school (e.g., Lemke, 1990). For students of color and Indigenous students in the U.S., school as an institution has had harmful effects  when shaped by settler colonialism and anti-b lack racism (Ewing, 2025). In detailing these critiques of schools, we acknowledge that some critiques, particularly of public schools in the U.S., are rooted in visions for an undemocratic and inequitable society; we reject those bad faith critiques. As a way forward, we embrace the too-often-unrealized possibilities of schools demonstrated by the fact that communities often fight for their public schools and their associated democratic promise (Ewing, 2018; Nuamah, 2022). Centering goals of building equitable and just social relations (Vossoughi et al. 2023), communities can create spaces for meaningful learning in schools. Educators and researchers have provided accounts of what these practices and spaces can look like (e.g., Givens, 2021; Lee, 2001; Lytle et al., 2009; Mirra & Garcia, 2023; Paris & Alim, 2017). In the learning sciences some scholars actively avoid school-based studies. Yet research on learning outside of school stems, in part, from the field’s commitments to leveraging innovative ways of 340 supporting learning to transform schools in addition to reimagining altogether new learning configurations (Hoadley, 2018; Sawyer, 2022). In this paper, we draw upon both the honest critiques of schools as well as the hopes for designing spaces for learning “within, against, and beyond” (Lytle et al., 2009) schools as institutions, seeing schools as tangles of sociocultural activity (Engeström, 2022) with desirable and undesirable features. Although they did not necessarily begin as educational reforms or design experiments, makerspaces have emerged as counterexample and context for reimagining what learning environments can look like in and beyond schools (Blikstein et al., 2023; Halverson & Peppler, 2018).  A makerspace is just one of among several names for the physical locations (e.g., garages, schools, Boys and Girls Clubs, libraries) with a range of names (e.g., FabLabs, workshop) where participants carry out making activities (Calabrese Barton et al., 2017; Mersand, 2020; Peppler et al., 2015). Making has been described with combinations of terms like collaborative, creative, constructionist, democratic, interest-driven, technology-infused, and world-making activity carried out by hobbyists and hackers, by youth and adults, by novices and knowledgeable peers, by communities of practice (Halverson & Sheridan, 2014; Peppler et al., 2016; Seitamaa-Hakkarainen et al., 2025). Makerspaces as models, as varied as they may be, provide comparative cases for challenging myths, assumptions, and actions that undergird and reinscribe school as institution and schooling as sociocultural activity, particularly in the U.S. When makerspaces are in  schools, they provide opportunities to study and design for learning in ways that candisrupt some of the more undesirable dimensions of schooling. In a study of school-based makerspaces, Stornaiuolo & Nichols (2020) identified six tensions at the intersection of school pressures and makerspace possibilities: (1) who has access to inquiry-and interest-driven spaces in a school; (2) how school and distric t infrastructures enable and constrain makerspace activity; (3) when is standardization of assignments and assessment desirable; (4) how is failure experienced by student in schools themselves labeled as failing; (5) how is collaboration enabled when class schedules and course grades are involved; and (6) how do logistical concerns of staffing and scheduling get solved when schools are already understaffed. They offered these tensions as areas for further research and design attention. In somewhat of a cont rast, in their own review of makerspace studies, Rouse et al. (2022) raised concerns that many studies of school-based makerspaces did not bear out key tensions that researchers have raised about the mismatch between making and schooling. As an example, they articulated that school-based makerspaces for younger students often operated more like classroom centers than open access workshops. In the same review, authors reported that few of the studies directly or deeply employed equityoriented views of making and called for more attention to the practices of experienced makerspace teachers, echoing some of the calls previously issued by Vossoughi et al. (2016). More research is needed, then, on schoolbased makerspaces to address these and other tensions researchers have raised, particularly around the ways that makerspaces can both disrupt and reinscribe inequitable and undesirable dimensions of schooling. Shifts in sociocultural activity in makerspaces To examine the complex activity that not just occurs in a school-based makerspace but constitutes it, researchers can employ a wider unit of analysis beyond studying what individuals know and cando. Cultural-historical activity theory, (or CHAT, Engeström, 2014; 2022; Mersand, 2020) provides a framework for accounting for shifts in the object  of activity (i.e., overall purpose of the activity in a system) mediated by tools, rules, and division of labor. Recent iterations of CHAT acknowledge that multiple subjects shaping activity systems may or may not have shared objects (Figure 1). CHAT is particularly helpful in considering how a system or community itself learns and how the system or community affords learning opportunities for participants (Engeström, 2014).

In this paper, we are interested in the intersecting phenomena that arise when a teacher (subject) and students (subjects) engage in the development of activity systems —and shifts (and tensions) in activity that emerge  place over time. Shifts can in volve changes in the mediational tools available for participants in the classroom, the division of labor, the subject-subject relationships, and the social rules governing activity system. Bringing makerspaces into schools requires teachers, as those with the most social power in their classrooms, to be open to certain kinds of shifts in their practice. Gravel and Puckett (2023), in framing the work of teachers as occurring across multiple systems of reform, described shifts as the social and material “distances” (skilled, physical, disciplinary, and structural) that teachers must travel to facilitate learning in a school-based makerspace. CHAT makes clear that multiple participants in an activity system can shape the system through their goal-oriented actions. Researchers have leveraged the possibilities of CHAT’s frame of “expansive learning” to characterize instances of and opportunities for learning through making (e.g., DiGiacomo & Gutiérrez, 2015). Youth choice, agency, and self-determination in making Bringing makerspaces into schools invites changes  to both material and social infrastructure. Learning sciences literature on making often addresses these material and social shifts in relation to student agency. Recognizing agency might involve embracing “more symmetrical relations among teachers and learn ers” (DiGiacomo & 341 Gutiérrez, 2015, p. 5); listening to and learning from “productive deviations” even within a defined maker curriculum (Hilppö & Stevens, 2023); and/or young people enacting their “rightful presence” as they bring their full selves to making (Calabrese Bart on & Tan, 2019). As Goeke and DeLiema (2024) noted, teachers may espouse multi-voiced visions of and for student agency, as our field does. In their study on an after-school tinkering program, Davis, Vossoughi, and Smith (2020) argued for attuning to both individual and collective acts of self-determination in the forms of “contestations” (resistance and refusal) and “moves to elsewhere” (acts of claiming, artistic expression, and/or reimagining). Rooted in social histories, children’s acts of self-determination contribute to consequential shifts in sociocultural activity in a given making and tinkering setting.

In this paper we leverage CHAT and literature on agency to offer  a n analysis of teacher (subject) reflections and action at an interpretive level above the microinteractional while focusing on a classroom makerspace activity system. In these reflections, we also get glimpses of the objects of other subjects (students) as they further change the activity system. In studying the activity system through reflections of the teacher at a longitudinal scale, we identify shifts and generative tensions. At the level of the activity system, tensions are keys to understanding how learning happens by participants in a system and how the system itself learns or  expands. We present this study as a practitioner inquiry, drawing from Lytle et al.’s (2009) example of teachers reflecting together in inquiry communities about their work going “within, against, and beyond” the constraints of their schools, district, and the broader institution of schooling. Ballenger (2025; 2004) has characterized teaching as a form of ongoing inquiry, describing practitioner inquiry moves such as “stopping time” with audio recordings and attending to “puzzling moments” for further reflection, often in an inquiry community with other practitioners. While self-study and practitioner inquiry have traditions in education research (e.g., Cochran-Smith & Lytle, 2015; Lampert & Ball, 1998) and even have examples in the learning sciences (e.g., Lee, 2001), there has been emerging interest in rethinking the roles of practitioner partners, researchers, and learning scientists in recent ISLS conference themes and papers (e.g., Davis, Hooper, et al., 2020). We offer this paper and its practitioner inquiry participatory design research (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Hoadley, 2018).

## Context and researchers

Author 1 (Mr. Smith) teaches K-8 students in a public school in a major city in the Northeastern United States. The school is situated in an historically African American neighborhood near the city’s center. In recent decades, the neighborhood’s racial and economic demographics shifted (i.e., wealthier and whiter residents moving in) and housing prices increased given the neighborhood’s proximity to government, technology, medical, and university jobs, as well as tax p olicies enabling private housing development. Community support for a school-based makerspace began in 2014 with grant funds and donations for digital fabrication tools (3D printer, CNC router), construction tools (hammers, drills, saws), digital recording  tools (recording booth, sound mixer), and robotics kits under the leadership of an engineering teacher. Since joining the school five years ago, he expanded available tools and materials: crafting (sewing, weaving, wearable circuits, found materials), art and design (light painting, graphic design, cardboard saw), digital media (cameras, green screen, lighting, stop motion), computer science (Scratch, code.org, micro:bit), and writing (storytelling, civic argument, journalism, historical research). Mr. Smith taught secondary science and social studies before leaving the classroom for policy fellowship and graduate school opportunities. Upon returning to classroom teaching and when designing for learning in his classroom makerspace, Mr. Smith drew upo n interdisciplinary making and tinkering project ideas (e.g., Davis, Vossoughi, & Smith, 2020; Hilppö & Steven, 2023) as well as discipline-specific curriculum materials that he would modify by weaving in civic (Smith et al., under review), computing, engineering, and storymaking tools and practices. For instance, he described pairing a science storylines unit (Reiser et al., 2021) with a light painting tinkering activity (Vossoughi et al., 2021). Drawing upon his research experience on spaces that were not his own, as a practitioner researcher Mr. Smith redesigned his curriculum to provide what he believed were opportunities to support curriculum coherence from students’ perspectives about what they were doing and why (Zivic et al.,

2018) alongside more expansive visions of self-determination (Davis, Vossoughi, & Smith, 2020).

The makerspace in this study is Mr. Smith’s classroom, not a shared school space for multiple instructors or courses. Students visit the classroom makerspace once or twice a week for 45 minutes. The school’s schedule prioritizes uninterrupted 90 to 120-minute literacy and math blocks, as part of a district-wide focus on state standardized test scores. Science and social studies receive less attention, sometimes 45 or less minutes every other day in early grades. Classes like digital literacy (Mr. Smith’s class) and gym provide disciplinary instruction to all students but also function as coverage blocks for other teachers to have planning and collaborative professional learning time. There are no collaborative planning or learning blocks scheduled for Mr. Smith. 342 Author 2 (Colin) met Mr. Smith when they were both graduate students. At Mr. Smith’s invitation, Colin has been visiting the makerspace classroom as a professor and with undergraduate students for the past three years. Developing a partnership over time, Colin has  been a partner in curricular and pedagogical discussions. Colin has invited Mr. Smith to share examples of practice in courses he teaches and inquiry groups he convenes. Data sources and analysis The primary data sources for this paper are transcriptions of reflective voice memos recorded by Mr. Smith, from winter 2022 through summer 2025. Mr. Smith began audio recording reflections on classroom practice to make sense of his work in a classroom mak erspace using autoethnographic methods (Chang, 2016) and practitioner inquiry (Ballenger, 2025). A year later he invited Colin to read some of his transcribed reflections. For a first round of analysis, we (both authors) read through transcriptions of the reflections and developed initial thematic codes using discourse and ethnographic analysis approaches (Erickson, 2004). We did this separately but with weekly check-ins, discussing emergent themes. We developed analytic memos as part of a second round of analysis, further refining thematic codes in conversation with research literature. And he would go into the tool closet, get a saw, and say, “Hey, can we use this?” He got a screwdriver, paint scraper, large pair of scissors, rubber mallet. With Mr. Smith’s help, Nate designed four walls for his model using an online application connected to the Carvey CNC router. Catalysts and conditions for change in the activity system We identified five contributors to the shifts in the classroom makerspace activity system toward student-directed projects. These catalysts and conditions included: (1) visibility of tools and materials around the room; (2) students seeing and hearing what  other students were doing; (3) the impacts of the various competitions, with students expanding time to work during school and in after school clubs; (4) a change in the school schedule, which initially felt like a crisis but offered an opportunity; and (5) Mr. Smith’s openness to students’ acts of self-determination. Visible tools and materials Tool and materials placement were a key condition for shifting the activity system. When students could see tools, they asked about using them. Early on, Mr. Smith put materials in clear bins and open bins on shelves around the classroom, many of which he inherited from the previous teacher. Even less visible tools gained visibility due to students’ curiosity and self-determined acts. In Mr. Smith’s reflection on Nate’s wooden house, Nate accessed tools in a closet with an opaque, closed, and unlocked  door. By April 2025, if not before, some students saw supply closets as places from which they could acquire tools for their own uses and goals, with some negotiation with Mr. Smith. Visibility of tools shifted over time in other ways. Mr. Smith continued designing projects for students to support them in using new tools, materials, and practices that they could then take up and use for their 344 own projects and purposes later. For instance, a group of third grade students drew upon their experiences with a second-grade shadow puppet project that Mr. Smith led each year; they created a sequel when they could choose their own project. Even later, Mr. Smith set materials out (e.g., sand, light table, webcam, and stand for sand stop motion) and invited exploration for students who might be in between project ideas or who wanted to take a break from a project. Mr. Smith reflected on playing with materials as a worthy endeavor in and of itself. (“[I] also think there's a lot of value in giving them free exploration with materials, right?” 6/2/24 reflection). Witnessing peer activity Similar to tool visibility, as students saw  peers using tools and completing projects (e.g., witnessing a previous class putting away projects as one class was entering), they asked to do the same. We refer to this as the snowball phenomenon. Mr. Smith posted projects to a class YouTube page (with guardian permission), which allowed students to see what other classes were doing or to show as examples the following school year. These videos spurred more asks for tools and materials (e.g., recording booth and microphone, 3D printer, cardboard saw). Additionally, as students witnessed peers  working on non-class projects (often for the competitions mentioned below), they asked to engage in similar activity during class. Finally, after the principal asked Mr. Smith to begin holding an enrichment project period for students with gifted IEPs, students without gifted IEPs  asked if they could work on projects during the enrichment period. They had witnessed peers leaving class on Friday mornings, bound for the makerspace. Mr. Smith opened the enrichment period (which was scheduled during homeroom time) to any student who asked and had the permission of their homeroom teacher. We highlight this move as potentially disrupting an  often inequitable dimension of schooling: more privileged students (i.e., white, higher income) are more likely to be identified as “gifted” and therefore have access to enriching curricular experiences (Howard, 2018). Mr. Smith noted in his reflections that the racial makeup of students participating in the enrichment period shifted when he made the space available to students who did not have formal documentat ion calling for “enrichment,” a key part of the story of further expanding the possibilities for making in the makerspace. The move also contributed to a shift in the space from just a classroom to a drop-in space. Competitions and after school clubs Our review of the data also revealed the impact of afterschool competitions (robotics team, science fair, media and design competition, journalism club) on the shifting rules, division of labor, and object of classroom activity (February 2023 in Figure 2).  Mr. Smith began to relax his insistence on completing work for his class before moving on to self-selected projects. He even encouraged students to focus on robotics, science fair, design, or journalism projects over time. During the first week of school  in 2024, multiple students approached Mr. Smith about preparing for a media and design competition held in spring 2025. The previous year they had placed second and wanted to start preparing earlier for the next event. Mr. Smith justified this shift in his reflections by describing competitions as opportunities for students to create artifacts for audiences outside of the school  and also recognized that some students were claiming practices as their own (Davis, Vossoughi, & Smith, 2020). However, there is m ore complexity to unpack. For instance, we recognize that introducing competition into making can bring added pressure (Hennessy Elliott, 2020) and power-laden assumptions about how students can learn from mistakes with a deadline approaching or with a focus on winning (Hennessy Elliott & Ma, 2025). Given the data, we are unable to say much more about the impact of competitions beyond characterizing how Mr. Smith provided project work time during class (replacing planned lessons) to prepare students for competitions, and how students sought time (during and outside of their scheduled class time with Mr. Smith) to work on these projects. Change in school schedule and introduction of “Genius Hour” A change in the school schedule in August 2023 also was a key catalyst in shifting the activity in the makerspace away from teacher-directed projects to student-selected ones. When a new principal took over leadership of the school, classes began rotating which specialist (art, gym, digital literacy, music) they would see twice a week, on Mondays and Fridays. This meant that Mr. Smith might see one class in a grade level once a week and another same grade level class two times. Mr. Smith anticipated difficulties in keeping same-grade-level classes working at the same pace, a concern that is common is schools addressing the same standards and curriculum with students. However, this change allowed Mr. Smith to imagine what differences in timing could mean for his classes while also maintaining commitments to ensuring students are introduced to tools, practices, and content in his curriculum scope and sequence. Mr. Smith created a “Genius Hour” (McNair, 2022) for the classes he saw twice. One day a week, students would work on projects of Mr. Smith’s choosing; the other day, students would propose and pursue their own projects, individually, in pairs, or in small groups. The introduction of Genius Hour dramatically shifted student expectations for what activity sho uld look like in the makerspace. As Mr. Smith reflected a few months later (1/23/24): "[W]hen I announce we aren't doing Genius Hour [to third and fourth 345 grades], and we're doing a project of mine for the day, there's not a lot of enthusiasm in the beginning. I think they, in some ways, value that freedom.” An initial scheduling crisis gave way to a consequential system shift. Openness to students’ acts of self-determination Once Genius Hour was institutionalized in the makerspace, Mr. Smith noted an increase in the number of students requesting time in the makerspace outside of their scheduled time in his class. The example of Nate’s wooden house is just one of quite a few instances where students sought to work on Genius Hour projects throughout the school day, and Mr. Smith had to develop new systems for managing who would be in the makerspace and when. There are significant social phenomena undergirding the shift in activity system that Mr. Smith and his students embarked on over the course of the 2023 school year in the creation of a Genius Hour: students perceived power in deciding what the class is going to do (division of labor); Mr. Smith’s moves to enact changes based on students’ acts of self-determination; Genius Hour signified and solidified a different kind of classroom activity—and in turn activity structure—than what they might do instead (new rules, division of labor, access to tools, and object of activity); students saw ownership in their ability to use the tools in the room to make their project as they imagine it (student agency over the activity system itself). These shifts were enabled by Mr. Smith’s openness to students’ acts of self-determination, including instances noted as early as September 2022 (Figure 2). Tension: Teacher as central node Generative tensions arose as the classroom makerspace activity structure shifted away from something more identifiable as “doing school” to something more in line with “making” in a makerspace, where students individually and in groups chose what to work on, for how long, and when it was completed. Tensions included

(1) where the spatial and temporal boundaries of the classroom ended and the makerspace began, (2) moves by

teacher in his position as a “central node ” (early codes: bottleneck), and (3) questions about productivity looks like and when a project is finished (early codes: productivity). Due to space, we explore the second tension below. Ms. Smith often positioned and repositioned himself as a “central node” of activity. When imagining a classroom makerspace as being constituted by multiple activity systems—with teacher-and student-subjects with their own objects that may or may not overl ap—there are instances in this makerspace when activity was both enabled and constrained by a teacher acting to achieve his goals. Specifically, Mr. Smith sometimes held up or slowed down student-driven activity across the following categories: (1) (re)focusing his own and/or student attention on a scheduled class and/or teacher-planned class project and (2) providing some kind of feedback or assistance, including solving a technical problem to enable student engagement with a specific process or tool. While the “teacher as central node” tension is connected to a design feature of most schools—a “single teacher in a classroom with 20-30 students” (Rogoff et al., 2001)—the specifics of how this tension emerged and how Mr. Smith and students shifted activity in response provide further insight into the design of a classroom makerspace. (Re)focusing attention on class projects When Mr. Smith (re)focused his attention on an assigned class, he was responding to school-level rules and divisions of labor (e.g., designations as a teacher of record, assigning grades, district curriculum goals). However, he sometimes bent those rules, supporting middle school students with a range of tasks outside of their assigned class time while younger students were keyboarding or completing class  projects that did not demand all his attention. For instance, he helped students with a logo design project and gave feedback on stop motion techniques for a documentary project, both of which were for external competitions. On other occasions, like with Nate, he would refer to his responsibility to a class to put off supporting on a student’s independent project. We saw this throughout the data, long before Genius Hour, and long after Genius Hour was established. Mr. Smith also (re)focused student attention on a class project because he continued to believe  that he should introduce students to specific tools, materials, and practices as part of a more formal curriculum. Even with the introduction of Genius Hour and projects for competitions, Mr. Smith continued to articulate a need for a balance between spiraling curriculum ideas (e.g., students play with mixing RGB light in second grade during a yearlong focus on light and electricity; in fourth grade, students learn to code RGB colors with binary code) so that students have exposure to tools, materials, and practices that they can build on for their own expanded purposes later during Genius Hour. As we shared earlier in the data, Mr. Smith said that students began to communicate their initial disinterest in a class project because they felt it kept them from Genius Hour. For Mr. Smith, teacher-led projects would better enable Genius Hour because students would have experiences with tools, materials, and practices that they could draw on for their own aims. Providing just-in-time supports and new tensions There were other instances when Mr. Smith asked a student to wait to work on an independent project or come back at another time because he needed to acquire materials, repair a tool, or learn more about a process or practice 346 to be able to offer support. In related cases, he communicated that he wanted to assist to ensure safety. This concern for safety and needing to decide on an appropriate tool impacted Nate’s work with this wooden house. Mr. Smith also asked students to slow down or wait with a student-selected project so that he could provide them with feedback. In some  instances, students requested feedback or support. In both types of instances, Mr. Smith enabled and constrained (at least temporarily) the work for a student or group. While these instances might be read as doing school  (i.e., teacher evaluating student), feedback is not undesirable in makerspace settings and, indeed, is often an essential part of learning in any setting (e.g., DiGiacomo & Gutiérrez, 2015; formal feedback cycles in Calabrese Barton et al., 2017). The tension that arose was primarily due to an expanding number of student-directed projects, and Mr. Smith increasingly became a bottleneck for providing feedback. Mr. Smith attempted to shift the division of labor so that he was not the bottleneck for some student-directed projects by inviting in community partners to provide assistance. However, these efforts brought new tensions, particularly around the time it took to build and maintain partnerships (community), ensure partners had clearances (rules), and coach partners on pedagogical practices like just-in-time supports (division of labor). He also enlisted the help of students who demonstrated expertise with tools, materials, and practices. This kind of assistance is typical in making and non-school learning configurations (e.g., Rogoff et al., 2001). New tensions emerged with this strategy. For instance, when Mr. Smith noticed that a student had expertise with sound systems and recording, he asked the student to assistant him and other students with operating a sound board. The student, in an act of refusal and pursuing a personal interest  (Davis, Vossoughi, & Smith, 2020) said he would prefer not to help and went back to a project that had nothing to do with recording. In other cases, an older peer might have offered to help younger students but would have missed a scheduled class to assist in the makerspace, which required Mr. Smith to navigate school rules and coordinate with colleagues (division of labor). Schools can be powerful spaces of possibility, and making in school can offer meaningful opportunities for learning, participation, and self-determination amidst institutional shortcomings. Vossoughi et al. (2016) challenged researchers and practitioners to attend more carefully to visions for making, facilitation, and sociopolitical values of making, as well as to historical educational injustices. The preceding analysis, with the help of CHAT as a theoretical frame, begins to unpack each of those dimensions of making, particularly when the larger system is organized around standardized tests and curricula. For example, when students who were not part of the gifted program requested to work on projects in the makerspace during the morning enrichment period, Mr. Smith and students challenged the settled, inequitable  notion that children deemed “ gifted” should have special access to making and a makerspace. The shifts in the makerspace indicate that not only are teachers multi-level actors integrating a makerspace into a school (Gravel & Puckett, 2023), but students can be co-producers of a makerspace particularly when a teacher recognizes and takes seriously students’ acts of self-determination (Davis, Vossoughi, & Smith, 2020). More work is needed to unpack how particular children experienced the makerspace, and how those experiences intersected with schooling, Ms. Smith’s facilitation, and co-constructed values. Even as Mr. Smith was attuned and open to acts of student self-determination and as the makerspace shifted toward student-driven activity, generative tensions emerged, including teacher acting as a central node. This tension opened up deeper considerations  for Mr. Smith about just-in-time and targeted supports that could enable self-directed and self-determined student making. The “distances” Mr. Smith and his students traveled (Gravel & Puckett, 2023) include overcoming internalized institutional pressures to have students working on the same thing all the time, to disciplinary pressures about what counts as something worth learning—and doing. Moves to resolve tensions gave way tonew tensions, revealing complexities that did not fit neatly into categories of making or doing school (i.e., inviting community partners meant helping them learn to support students and attending to possible cultural mismatches; asking students to be experts of a tool was sometimes refused). As practitioners and researchers contin ue to design for making and learning in schools, they must contend with the complex ways that making and schooling emerge with each design decision. Principled partnerships with practitioners and practitioner inquiry methods provide fruitful paths for making tensions visible and finding ways forward, for rethinking a design and practice, and for clarifying commitments.
