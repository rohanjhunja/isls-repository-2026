# Broadening the Boundaries of the Learning Sciences: Reclaiming the Territory of Latin American Contributions to Educational Research

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
From Mapuche palikantun rhythms in Chile to Uruguayan rural futures, from WhatsApp organizing at the Mexico-US border to Brazilian constructionist reinterpretations— this symposium weaves together studies reimagining the Learning Sciences through Latin American epistemologies. We gather not to import theories from distant academies, but to honor knowledge systems flowering in our territories: mathematical wisdom in Mapuche weaving, learning that unfolds at the Tijuana-Sandiego border, resistance enacted through digital practices. Our work traces how communities transform constraints into creativity, how intergenerational memory becomes a pedagogical method, how the periphery generates its own centers of gravity. Through empirical studies spanning Colombia, Ecuador, and beyond, we demonstrate the generative potential of putting Learning Sciences theories in dialogue with Latin American frameworks —where constructionism converses with conscientização, ethnomathematics meets epistemic footprints, and speculation becomes decolonial praxis. Together, we offer distinctive interpretative lenses that expand the field by centering community knowledge and lived experiences situated in Latin America.

Keywords: Latin America, community knowledge, south epistemologies, intergenerational learning Symposium overview The Learning Sciences stands at a critical juncture in its development as a field. While it has evolved as an interdiscipline committed to epistemological pluralism and methodological diversity, significant gaps remain in whose voices, perspectives, and scholarship shape its core understandings of learning and knowing. Tsovaltzi et al. (2022) emphasized that the Learning Sciences must deepen connections with justice-oriented theories and methodologies from communities historically marginalized in the field, particularly minoritized communities and Global South perspectives. They argued that ongoing dynamics of US-centrism and Western normativity mean the field has important work to do to expand and enter dialogue with researchers across the globe and build collective socio-epistemic awareness based on respecting heterogeneity. Latin America represents a particularly striking example of these gaps. Coelho et al. (2022) documented that despite Brazil’s profound contributions to education research—as the homeland of Paulo Freire, Ubiratan 2330 D’Ambrosio, and Terezinha Nunes, scholars who developed influential frameworks including Critical Pedagogy and Ethnomathematics —Brazilian participation in the Learning Sciences remains relatively minimal. Paper submissions and conference attendance from Brazilian scholars are low, and the country does not offer graduate programs in the field. This pattern extends across Latin America more broadly, representing a significant loss for the international Learning Sciences community.

Sengupta et al. (2022) demonstrated that major reviews of Learning Sciences programs have revealed notable omissions stemming from Western lenses, settler-colonialism, imperialism, and white gaze that have shaped the field. They argued that for the Learning Sciences to remain viable as an interdiscipline, the field must ensure porousness in boundary spaces where heterogeneous disciplinary practices confront each other and engage in genuine conversation. This porousness can only be understood and maintained through observing movement and welcoming new perspectives that challenge taken-for-granted assumptions. Takeuchi et al. (2022) further argued that transforming the Learning Sciences requires critically examining the social reality created within the field and undertaking its transformation as a historical task. This work demands centering relational ontology and epistemology, seeking common threads across voices while paying attention to unmistakable differences in perspectives.

This symposium builds directly on initiatives like the Brazilian Affiliate Group of ISLS, formed to expand representation while accelerating the establishment of Latin American Learning Sciences communities (Coelho et al., 2022). As they articulated, the commitment must be to not merely import predominantly European/North American Learning Sciences frames but to make learning theorists and empiricists from Latin America visible to the international community. Their work has exposed the enormity of making the Learning Sciences truly and systemically international, beyond tokenization, mere representation, and the colonial makings of academia.

Designs for the pluriverse: Ontological, epistemic, and pedagogical dialogues rooted in colombian rural communities Tatiana Becerra, Jose Sandoval-Llanos This poster explores pluriversal frameworks for teaching and learning through the lens of Arturo Escobar’s ontological design and his vision of “a world where many worlds fit” (Escobar, 2018). Drawing on Latin American intellectual traditions, it places Escobar in dialogue with Paulo Freire’s critical pedagogy (Freire, 2005, 2021), Orlando Fals Borda’s participatory action research (Fals Borda, 2015), and Walter Mignolo’s decolonial thought (Mignolo, 1994, 2010). Together, these thinkers offer a compelling critique of modernity and propose educational practices rooted in relationality, dialogue, and epistemic disobedience,  resonating with the increasing relevance of these perspectives in the learning sciences scholarship (Warren et al., 2020). Escobar's notion of the pluriverse challenges universalist assumptions of Western modernity, opening a dialogue on ontological pluralism, intercultural learning, and community-based knowledge production (SandovalLlanos et al., 2025). Embracing a pluriversal perspective in our educational practices (Perry, 2024) requires interrogating a Western tradition rooted in the idea of a one-world view, rethinking the very foundations of what it means to be literate, the ways in which we build knowledge, and whose epistemologies are valued and amplified in the educational process (Becerra-Posada, 2025b; Sandoval-Llanos et al., 2025). In this poster, we will discuss how Escobar’s pluriversal framework has reshaped our conceptualization of literacies, which have informed our research methodology and pedagogical work.

2331 In our work with Colombian rural communities, this perspective has materialized in ways that we didn’t anticipate. For instance, recognizing the practices of peasant farmers, such as plant weaving, crop harvesting, and cattle raising, requires decoding specific signs to interact with and make meaning of the world around them (Becerra-Posada, 2025a, 2025b). These socio-cultural practices, passed on intergenerationally within communities, are also literacies that we center on in our work on language curricula  (Becerra-Posada, 2025a; Becerra-Posada & Ehret, 2024). On the other hand, our exploration of the impact of financialization (Sandoval-Llanos, 2024) in these communities has shown how, through decisions like what and when they plant, small farmers resist a nd challenge the oppressive practices of traditional formal and informal financial systems, demonstrating levels of understanding that, even if considered “incorrect” or “wrong” by mainstream financial literacy approaches, respond to their context and interests. These principles of relationality, communal dialogue, and epistemic disobedience have inspired us to challenge logocentrism and instead embrace the embodied experience of learning and constructing knowledge through walking (Marin, 2020) and land-storying methods (Meixi et al., 2022), on the one hand. Additionally, these principles have led us to adapt our methods to participants’ everyday practices and tools, such as WhatsApp, the online app. This attunement has shaped the design of a dialogue-based learning experience inspired by social design-based experiments (Gutiérrez et al., 2020) and participatory design methods (Booker & Goldman, 2016; Fals-Borda, 2001). Together, our methodologies aim to honor the voices and knowledges of marginalized rural communities with whom and for whom we are doing this work. By engaging with these thinkers, the presentation seeks to inspire research methodologies and pedagogical practices that are dialogical, participatory, and grounded in the lived experiences of marginalized communities. Ultimately, it calls for an educational shift toward ontological openness and epistemic justice, where learning becomes a space for co-creating pluriversal futures. Artifacts in interdisciplinary Co-design: Integrating computing into social studies in k-12 brazilian education Adelmo Eloy, Nathan Rabinovitch, Walter Akio Goya In recent years, Brazil has advanced the integration of computing into K –12 education, driven by national computing standards for all stages of schooling (Brasil, 2022). As elsewhere, motivations span workforce preparation and the development of interdisciplinary skills such as computational thinking (Blikstein & Moghadam, 2019). In Brazil, constructionist perspectives have been particularly influential in debates about why and how to learn computing. While originally articulated in the Global North (e.g., Papert & Harel, 1991), constructionism has been continuously reinterpreted in Brazilian contexts through the work of scholars such as José Valente, who contrasted constructionist and instructionist paradigms (Valente, 1993) and later elaborated their implications for computing education (Valente, 2019). We situate our work within this horizon, aiming to integrate computing concepts and practices into social studies. Following research that shows the promise of grounding computing in meaningful disciplinary contexts (Wilkerson et al., 2020), we partnered with middle-school history and geography teachers (Years 7–9) in a threeyear co-design effort to develop interdisciplinary curricular units (Eloy et al., 2025). Rather than reporting classroom outcomes, this paper presents a design analysis of the units and accompanying materials (lesson plans, teacher guides, activity sheets, prototypes, and co-design notes), examining how the units specify artifacts constructed by students with different computational media (e.g., block-based programming, microcontroller sensing, conversational interfaces, and linked knowledge graphs) to anchor integration. Our analysis focuses on each artifact’s intended affordances: the disciplinary ideas it foregrounds, the computing practices it is designed to organize (e.g., representation, iteration, data modeling, debugging), and how its medium enables learners to reorganize and express ideas. We describe four curricular cases: (1) dynamic visualizations that invite students to propose alternative regionalizations of the Americas beyond the standard north–central–south division; (2) a local weather station for collectin g humidity and temperature data and comparing local readings to reference sources; (3) branching timelines that decenter Eurocentric periodization by juxtaposing African, Asian, and American histories; and (4) graph-based representations of 19th-century Regency-era revolts that support comparative analysis across causes, leaders, and outcomes. Across cases, the units articulate plausible pathways by which student-constructed artifacts can connect social studies reasoning with computing practices through the representational affordances of their respective media. We discuss implications for interdisciplinary computing design: aligning artifact forms with disciplinary aims; leveraging media that invite revision and comparison; and supporting teacher appropriation and adaptation (e.g., shifting content focus while retaining the artifact form). Future work will examine enactments to trace how students and teachers take up these artifacts in practice and to refine the professional-development model that 2332 sustains their integration. More broadly, we argue that learning theories such as constructionism—contextualized through the work of local educators and researchers —can productively inform the design of pedagogical interventions across diverse settings.

Immersion in cultural practice as a path to mathematical literacy: An ethnomathematical-epistemological perspective from mapuche knowledge Luis Llancapan, Eugenio Chandía, Anahí Huencho, Patricia Fuentes Acevedo While some mathematical concepts in the Chilean elementary curriculum share epistemic roots with Mapuche ways of knowing, some concepts differ, and others are not addressed by the curriculum but are key to the Mapuche cosmovision (Huencho, 2015). Designing effective learning experiences requires taking into account the implications of students constantly navigating multiple epistemologies (Bang & Medin, 2010). With a particular focus on mathematics education, ethnomatematics explores the multiple ways in wh ich different cultural groups mathematize their realities, characterizing the use of mathematical ideas in their lives and the implications for their learning experiences (D’Ambrosio, 2001; Rosa et al., 2016). This poster portrays community-engaged research conducted in Araucanía and Biobío regions, in the south of Chile. We examine how mathematical knowledge can be constructed when learning is not solely mediated exogenously by the school, but occurs through immersion in Mapuche cultural practices. Using field notes and semi-structured interviews with kimche/papay (wise men and women), we develop an ethnomathematical and epistemological analysis that intersects three core areas of school math (probability/statis tics, algebra, and geometry) with a coding system of “epistemic markers” (procedures, parameter-result relationships, orientation/measurement).

Our findings articulate the origin of acquisition with description features, and cultural mathematical practices such as palikantun (similar to the Spanish game chueca and considered the predecessor of the global sport field hockey), gillatun (Mapuche prayer ceremony that seeks to ask for, give thanks for, or maintain the balance and well-being of the community), ruka (traditional housing construction of the Mapuche people in Chile), weaving, agriculture, traditional measurement, and ritual music. This art iculation shows that endogenous, intergenerational learning mediated by Mapuzugun (Mapuche language) and ritual generates richer and more technical descriptions for mathematical knowledge: procedural rules, local units (brazadas, wif), functional relationships (e.g., tension for contraction in weaving), and geometric structures (circularity/cardinal orientation in gillatun and ruka). For example: in a witral (loom) session to weave a trarilonco (headband), an elder instructs a child—speaking Mapuzugun—to se t the warp to “epu brazadas küla küme wif” (roughly two arm-spans with well-set passes). She explains a functional relation: “Tüfachi wif müten, tüfachi kütxan”—the tighter the wif (weft beat), the greater the contraction —so they overestimate the initial length by one küpa (a hand-span) to reach the desired final size after tightening. They follow a procedural rule for the motif—“küla urdin, kiñe wif kolor” (every three warp threads, one colored pass)—and count aloud “kiñe, epu, küla, meli…” while tracking repeats. Finally, they orient the loom toward puel (east), echoing the circular and cardinal layout seen in the gillatun and the doorway of the ruka, thus embedding geometric structure (circularity and axial symmetry) into the craft routine. We propose the Modelo de Huella Epistémica (Epistemic Footprint Model), which links the origin of acquisition (family/lof, ritual, language) with sociomaterial mechanisms (body, artifacts, norms), epistemic marks, and the three core areas of school mathematics. The model includes design principles for classroom practice (generative situations, co-teaching with kimche/papay, language as a conceptual tool, observable parameters, prognosis –action–review cycle) and assessment instruments (epistemic mark rubrics  and decision portfolios). We argue that teaching anchored in cultural practice not only legitimizes local knowledge but also enhances school formalization by stabilizing rules, relationships, and structures, offering a robust and measurable intercultural instructional path.

Teaching seasons in equatorial contexts: Curricular challenges and the role of students’ lived experiences in their conceptual ecologies Cassia Fernandez, Tatiana Laganá, Claudia Godinho Peria, Tatiana Hochgreb-Haegele Understanding how students' everyday experiences influence their conceptual explanations of scientific phenomena is essential for developing effective teaching strategies that connect intuitive reasoning with formal scientific knowledge. In this work, we i nvestigate how sixth-grade students living near the equator in Brazil develop explanations for the seasons.

2333 In this geographical context, learners experience unique climatic conditions that challenge traditional instructional materials. In mid-and high-latitude regions, seasonal transitions are marked by variations in temperature and day length, which can be intuitively connected to the canonical explanation linking solar radiation patterns to Earth's axial tilt. However, students in this study live in low-latitude regions where temperatures remain consistently high year-round, and seasons are primarily distinguished by precipitation patterns ("winter is rainy, summer is dry"). Thus, they do not directly associate seasons with temperature, but rather with precipitation. This presents a challenge for science educators: traditional curricula and instructional mater ials in Brazil are often designed by authors from mid latitudes who disregard how people in low latitudes experience seasons, featuring images and descriptions that reinforce the idea that seasonal changes are universal and related to temperature and day length variations.

To address this challenge, we developed a ten-lesson curricular unit aligned with the Brazilian National Standards (BNCC) as part of the IDEIA Project (Hochgreb-Haegele et al., 2023). The project emphasizes curriculum design and professional development for teachers, focusing on engagement with scientific practices through learning activities that draw upon students' lived experiences (Freire, 2014). In the unit, students contrasted their own experiences with textbook images to generate driving questions, analyzed data from cities at different latitudes, and engaged with physical models to modify solar system parameters and build explanations for the phenomenon of the seasons.

We conducted interviews with eleven students before and after unit implementation and analyzed written assessments from two classrooms to examine students’ conceptions of the seasons. The interviews incorporated adaptive probing techniques to challenge students' reasoning and reveal real-time conceptual shifts (Sherin, Krakowski, & Lee, 2012), requiring them to make drawings to explain their reasoning. Their explanations were analyzed using thematic coding to characterize their conceptual ecology (diSessa, 2002) across six dimensions (including Earth's movement, axial tilt, solar incidence, and latitude). seasons were initially explained through meteorological aspects such as clouds, most learners recognized the role of Earth's axial tilt and revolution arou nd the Sun in post-interviews. However, interestingly, they struggled to connect different “pieces of knowledge” (diSessa, 1993) into complete explanations, depicting specific concepts without necessarily combining them into a full, coherent conceptual framework. These findings reinforce the notion that students may hold complex conceptual profiles, often resulting in "hybrid explanations" that blend scientific and intuitive reasoning (Mortimer, 1995).

Additionally, asking students to create drawings proved problematic, as they had explored physical models during the unit and struggled to translate their reasoning into 2D representations. Moreover, teachers' approaches often emphasized memorizing definitions after experimentation moments, which may be connected to the fact that the lesson plans had limited space for adapting instruction to incorporate students' emerging conceptions.

Our study underscores the need for culturally responsive curriculum design that actively considers learners' lived experiences, highlighting the need for materials and professional development programs that encourage teachers to adapt instruction based on learners' local experiences. This is particularly important in contexts with non-hegemonic ways of living that rely on large-scale materials not suited for their realities. En (la) Línea Se Aprende Todo: Transfronterizx learning and resistance along border asymmetries Isaac Félix With over 90,000 crossings each day, the Tijuana –Sandiego border is the most transited border zone in the Western Hemisphere (U.S. General Services Administration, 2025). Heightened policing and border enforcement policies enacted by the U.S. government over the past two decades (Nevins, 2001; Ramírez, 2009) have created arduous and prolonged wait times at U.S. Land Ports of Entry (LPOEs), ranging from one to two hours, sometimes extending up to four to six hours at a time. For transfronterizx commuters —in dividuals who live in México and routinely cross into the U.S. for work, school, or other quotidian activities (Ojeda, 2009; Zentella, 2013) —these extended waits are not merely logistical challenges; they constitute a persistent, embodied constraint shaping the rhythms, routines, and temporal organization of their daily lives. Scholars studying commuters in the Tijuana– Sandiego region argue that these waits function as racialized forms of psychological and spatiotemporal punishment, generating uncertainty, fatigue, and heightened exposure to policing and surveillance (Avalos, 2022; Castañeda Pérez, 2022).

2334 This poster leverages two interrelated theories to reconceptualize routine border-crossing practices as forms of resistance. First, Latin American theories of border asymmetry (Bustamante, 1989; Velasco Ortiz & Contreras, 2014; Vila, 2001) illuminate how relationships in the México– U.S. border region are structured by unequal power dynamics, foregrounding the ways asymmetries mediate human interaction. Second, Maria Lugones’ (2003) conceptualization of resistance highlights how commuters’ mundane practices can be understood as agentic strategies enacted in response to structural constraints. Together, these frameworks allow us to examine not only how everyday practices constitute subtle acts of resistance, but also how they emerge in response to larger asymmetries of power shaping life in the border region. Building on this conceptual grounding, the poster draws on a larger study that used digital ethnography and content analysis of over 400 multimodal posts from a publicly available Facebook group. These digital ecologies allow commuters to share real-time updates—including text, images, videos, and emojis —about border conditions, wait times, and optimal routes. Anchored in Learning as Movement (Gutiérrez, 2020), this work foregrounds how learning emerges through movement across socio-spatial and sociocultural dimensions, showing how commuters mobilize social, spatial, and digital resources to navigate the border efficiently. In doing so, the study illustrates how learning, human ingenuity (McDermott & Raley, 2011), and subtle, everyday acts of resistance unfold across transfronterizx ecologies.

Ultimately, by shifting analytic attention to how individual, collective, and distributed practices emerge in everyday border-crossings, the poster highlights how people learn through movement and mobilize social, spatial, and digital resources to navigate  asymmetrical power relations structuring the México –U.S. border. In doing so, it foregrounds acts of everyday resistance and offers conceptual and methodological insights for understanding learning, human ingenuity, and the subtle strategies transfronterizx commuters employ to navigate sociocultural, political, and spatial constraints—both at the border and beyond. ## Uruguayan rural context

Ezequiel Alemán TRAMA (Trayectorias Modulares Abiertas) is conceived both as a pre-college program and conceptual framework for reimagining education from the margins (Massey, 2005). Emerging in Uruguay’s rural territories, it offers an infrastructure for weaving together  learning, imagination, and community life. At its simplest, TRAMA connects youth from small towns to modular routes in creative industries such as game design, filmmaking, animation, and digital storytelling. Yet more fundamentally, TRAMA serves as a spac e for speculative inquiry into what education could mean when it begins from the periphery rather than the center. When education is not designed to reproduce established systems of college access but to generate new, uncharted pathways. It is, therefore, a practice of ontological design in Arturo Escobar’s (2017) sense: an attempt to create the conditions for multiple worlds (possible futures) to coexist and for rural youth to act as designers of their own futures.

The conceptual roots of TRAMA lie in Speculative Youth Participatory Action Research (SPY-PAR) (Martinez & Aleman, 2025), an approach that emerges from participatory action research as decolonial praxis (Fals Borda, 2005; Mignolo, 2010; Cammarota & Fine, 2008) extended this lineage by situating inquiry within imaginative practices as decolonial acts. It invites youth not only to research the world as it is, but also to prototype the worlds that could be, thus engaging in a process of co-creation of social possibility (Mirra, 2025). SPY-PAR departs from four interdependent principles: a) organic origins, b) crafting alternative scenarios, c) rememorative words, and d) nourished body and spirit. These four principles situate research as a form of collective w orldmaking, insisting that youth are not only interpreters of social problems but cartographers of new possibilities. Speculation, in this view, is not a theoretical exercise, but a relational practice through which communities imagine and rehearse new fo rms of coexistence (Mirra, 2025). Within TRAMA, these principles translate into learning design: programs arise from community initiatives already in motion, learning environments center imagination and affect, and inquiry unfolds through artifacts that materialize alternative scenarios in the forms of games, stories, maps, or performances that tap into collective memories of the past to make the future tangible in the present.

Speculative YPAR emerges in conversation with a broader Latin American intellectual tradition that conceives learning as inseparable from liberation and world-making. Paulo Freire’s (2005) pedagogy of conscientização offers the dialogical ground on which TRAMA stands: the act of learning is understood as a process of unveiling and transforming reality. Freire’s call that the oppressed “name the world” resonates here with the speculative imperative to imagine it otherwise. In our work with 160 youth from 23 different rural provinces in Uruguay, youth engage in imaginative practices (designing games, writing poetry and stories, 2335 creating conceptual art, or writing code) as tools for thinking collectively about their territories and communities. These artifacts operate as a form of escrevivência (Evaristo, 2020), understanding that youth forms of production are not just media where youth express about themselves and their challenges, but become an evocation of the past, of those who came before. As rural communities are increasingly abandoned by youth who believe their futures can only be built elsewhere, TRAMA invites creative prod uctions that use storytelling to counter oppressive narratives of time and space. Through these practices, young people engage in (re)mapping their territories by recalling other forms of discourse and memory. In this sense, youth-generated artifacts becom e rememorative words (Goeman, 2013), culturally significant elements that anchor shared narratives and serve as seeds for speculative imagination.

The recognition that the future must be designed through forms of coexistence grounded in reciprocity is not just a call for rural youth to envision new futures for themselves, but also a call to rethink what counts as learning. It proposes that to educate  is to speculate; to generate conditions where other worlds can breathe. TRAMA embodies a philosophy of learning as world-making, where imagination can be both method and right. Maker women STEM: Advancing gender equity in stem in latin america through making and critical pedagogy Juan Torralba, Edgar Omar López Caudana Over the past decade, makerspaces in various forms have gained popularity around the world as spaces that foster and promote collaborative and interdisciplinary learning, extending the reach of the Maker Movement into socially and culturally diverse learning contexts (Schad & Jones, 2020). By design and functionality, the adoption of these spaces in the Global North have contributed to the proliferation of its theoretical roots such as experiential learning (Dewey, 1938), cognitive constructivism (Piaget, 1970), social constructivism (Vygotsky, 1978), constructionism (Papert, 1991), and situated learning in communities of practice (Lave & Wanger, 1991). The adoption of makerspaces, and making as a way of learning, have not been unproblematic. Godhe et al. (2019) raise several concerns regarding the integration of makerspaces into formal learning environments, citing the tensions between the open-ended nature of maker education and the often highly structured mandated curriculum in formal education. Whereas maker education centers around the development of maker and 21st century skills through the construction of artifacts, the mandated curriculum centers around the acquisition of academic content knowledge measured by academic performance on standardized asses sments. Similarly, Calabrese-Barton and Tan (2020) warn that the adoption of makerspaces in education must also consider the inherent power dynamics of the maker culture, so as to avoid perpetuating the oppressive practices of a White-male dominated field that often determines what making is, how it is evaluated, and by whom. These tensions are not exclusive to the integration of makerspaces into formal schooling; they are also exacerbated when makerspaces are replicated in countries in the Global South and learning is understood and valued through the dominant theoretical lens of the Global North.

In our interinstitutional collaborative work, funded by an Erasmus Capacity Building grant, three European and six Latin American institutions set forth the Maker Women STEM initiative, coordinated by Tecnológico de Monterrey (MX) and supported by Universi taet Potsdam (DE), Ita-Suomen Yliopisto (FI), Fundación TEC para la Innovación y el Imprendimiento (ES), Fundacion Universitaria CEIPA (CO), Universidad de la Guajira (CO), Universidad Bolivariana del Ecuador (EC), Coordinacion General del Distrito de Educ acion, Salud, Ciencia, Tecnologia e Innovacion (MX), and Universidad de Desarollo Intelectual (MX). The Maker Women STEM initiative aims to: (1) develop an integrated learning and mentorship ecosystem to train at least 500 women as maker-STEM mentors in Mexico, Colombia, and Ecuador; (2) build and strengthen global makerbased environments through hybrid (in-person and virtual) learning and open educational resources; (3) embed continuous assessment and feedback mechanisms for local adaptation, sustainabili ty, and reflective practice; and

(4) institutionalize Maker Women STEM as a formal research and collaboration network across Latin America

and Europe. These initiatives aim to create scalable, adaptable ecosystems that promote lifelong learning, digital inclusion, and social transformation.

At the core of this work is a guiding theoretical framework that integrates the pillars of maker education (Dewey, 1938; Piaget, 1970; Lave & Wenger, 1991; Papert, 1991; Vygotsky, 1978) with critical pedagogy (Freire,

2000) and funds of knowledge (Gonzalez  et al., 2005). Through this integrated lens, we developed a training

model that is grounded in the local needs, opportunities, and limitations of the populations we aim to serve. This model consists of an intensive training program designed to train core mentors from each of the three Latin American countries. Upon completion of the program, the core mentors will receive a micro-credential from the host institution (Tecnológico de Monterrey) to replicate the training program with at least 165 female mentors at 2336 their own institutions in Colombia (Universidad de la Guajira), Ecuador (Universidad Bolivariana), and Mexico (Universidad de Desarrollo Intelectual). To ensure the success of the replicated programs, these institutions will receive maker tools, technology, and instructional resources funded through the Erasmus grant, as well as continuous support from the collaborative interinstitutional network.
