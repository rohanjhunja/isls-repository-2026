# Learning from the Experts: Exploring Human–AI Feedback through Expert-AI

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This demo introduces Expert-AI, a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) system designed to emulate aspects of human experts’ reasoning and feedback. Drawing on experts’ writings, lectures, and interviews, the system provides dialogic, context-sensitive feedback grounded in expert perspectives, allowing experts to review and refine responses over time. We report preliminary findings from a pilot study in a business idea creation course at a private university in Japan, where students used both Expert-AI and general-purpose generative AI systems (e.g., ChatGPT). The findings suggest complementary roles: students used Expert-AI for conceptual framing and strategic evaluation, and general-purpose generative AI for elaboration and implementation planning. These findings suggest learners’ interpretations of AI feedback may vary depending on how it is presented. Accordingly, the demo presents the text version of Expert-AI alongside early multimodal prototypes including voice and android embodiments to examine how interaction modality shapes perceived credibility, empathy, trust, and engagement in expert-like AI feedback.

Research on expertise has long shown that experts differ from novices not only in how much they know but also in how that knowledge is organized, accessed, and applied (Chi et al., 1981; Ericsson et al., 1993). Hatano and Inagaki (1986) distinguish adaptive experts—individuals who flexibly devise new procedures and restructure their knowledge when confronted with unfamiliar situations—from routine experts, who perform well in familiar contexts but struggle when task demands change. Adaptive experts understand why strategies work, under what conditions they apply, and when new methods must be invented (Schwartz, Bransford, & Sears, 2005). This suggests that expertise involves continual refinement through reflective and flexible thinking rather than mere automatization of skills.

Yet opportunities for learners to observe and internalize such expert thinking processes remain limited. Expert reasoning and tacit decision-making are often invisible in educational contexts. Frameworks such as Cognitive Apprenticeship (Collins et al., 1989) attempt to make expert reasoning visible through modeling and guided reflection, but these depend on sustained expert presence and are difficult to scale. Recent advances in generative AI raise the possibility of extending access to expert-like reasoning without requiring experts to be physically present. Motivated by this potential, we explore whether RetrievalAugmented Generation (RAG; Lewis et al., 2020) can surface tacit knowledge, reasoning patterns, and decision processes that characterize adaptive expertise. Building on this idea, we developed Expert-AI, a system designed to reconstruct expert cognition using experts’ writings, lectures, and interviews. Rather than serving as a simple information source, Expert-AI simulates aspects of learning from an expert by offering personalized, contextsensitive feedback grounded in authentic expertise. This demo investigates how learners perceive and interpret such feedback across different modalities.

## Related work

Tacit knowledge in expert performance Expert performance draws on structured domain knowledge and context-sensitive reasoning (Chi et al., 1981; Ericsson et al., 1993). Tacit knowledge (Polanyi, 1966) and reflective practice (Schön, 1983) highlight that much of expert cognition is experiential, embodied, and difficult to articulate. Expertise involves adaptive problem solving and refined judgment—skills that are challenging to observe directly. Expert-AI seeks to externalize these implicit cognitive patterns and make them accessible to learners. Because such processes rarely surface in authentic expert–novice interactions, many learners lack opportunities to observe how experts reason through uncertainty, reframe problems, or evaluate competing options. Building on these perspectives, we conceptualize “knowledge” not only as propositional content but also as situated reasoning practices, tacit judgments, and ways of participating in expert discourse. Accordingly, Expert-AI is not merely a tool for knowledge transmission, but a medium through which learners can engage with and appropriate expert-like ways of thinking. Learning, in this context, is understood as a transformation in how learners frame problems, generate questions, and reflect on their ideas, rather than simply acquiring correct answers. Evidence of such learning may be observed in the emergence of more generative questions, deeper reflective dialogue, and shifts in how learners interpret and extend feedback. How learners access expert thinking Learning with experts requires participation in expert discourse rather than merely receiving information (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Cognitive Apprenticeship (Collins et al., 1989) aims to make expert reasoning visible through modeling, coaching, and guided reflection. Yet opportunities for direct interaction with experts remain limited due to scarcity of time and availability. Prior technological approaches—such as intelligent tutoring systems and rule-based expert models—have attempted to simulate expert reasoning but often capture only explicit procedures, failing to represent tacit, experience-based aspects of expertise. This gap has motivated interest in systems capable of representing both explicit and tacit expert insights through interactive dialogue. Feedback plays a central role in learning by helping learners understand the gap between current performance and desired goals, although its effectiveness varies across contexts (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). Its impact depends heavily on how learners interpret feedback and the extent to which they trust its source (Carless & Boud, 2018). Recent studies on generative AI highlight both opportunities and challenges in producing feedback that is perceived as credible and empathetic (Lee & Moore, 2024). Building on these insights, ExpertAI investigates how learners perceive and engage with expert-like AI feedback, particularly across different interaction modalities.

System design for expert-like feedback Expert-AI is a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) system (Lewis et al., 2020) designed to emulate the reasoning and communication style of human experts across diverse domains. It is intended for learning contexts with no single correct answer—such as open-ended problem solving and creative decision-making—where learners can benefit from access to diverse expert perspectives and reflective feedback. The system combines retrieval from a curated corpus of expert materials with generative synthesis, producing individualized feedback grounded in authentic expertise. Users interact with the system by selecting a topic or submitting a free-text question or idea through a web-based interface (see Figure 1). Upon submission, the system retrieves semantically relevant passages from a curated corpus of expert materials—including books, unpublished notes, and original interviews—from experts selected based on recognized professional experience. Many of these materials are not publicly available online, and the system synthesizes responses that reflect the expert’s reasoning style. Figure 1 Input Interface of Expert-AI Users can select a topic or submit a free-text question or idea. Once the query is processed, Expert-AI provides text-based feedback that integrates theoretical reasoning with the expert’s practical perspective, enabling learners to reflect on both the logic and the stance underlying the feedback (see Figure 2). Learners can continue the interaction through follow-up questions in a chat format, allowing them to probe deeper into the expert’s reasoning process. This dialogic structure supports sustained engagement and scaffolds learners’ inquiry.

The system supports both Japanese and English inputs and outputs. While the expert corpus primarily consists of Japanese-language materials, retrieval is conducted across languages using semantic similarity, and responses are generated in the user’s input language through LLM-based translation and synthesis. Although cross-lingual interaction is supported, potential differences in retrieval accuracy across languages remain an area for future investigation. A distinctive feature of Expert-AI is its collaborative update mechanism: experts themselves can review and refine the AI’s responses or add new reflections, turning the system into a living archive that evolves with their ongoing insights.

## Pilot study and preliminary findings

This pilot study examined how Expert-AI and general-purpose generative AI (e.g., ChatGPT) function differently within a business idea creation course at a private university in Japan. The course is an undergraduate–graduate integrated lecture, in which a total of 19 students (12 undergraduate and 7 graduate students) received course credit. Participants were provided access to both Expert-AI and ChatGPT and were asked to reflect on their use of these systems in their idea generation and evaluation process. multiple reports, students described using Expert-AI during the early stages of ideation, particularly when identifying core concepts, framing problems, and evaluating the strategic direction of their ideas. In contrast, ChatGPT was more frequently used in later stages to elaborate business models, organize information, and develop implementation plans. Qualitative analysis further suggested differences in how learners perceived the feedback provided by each system. Students tended to perceive Expert-AI as offering more structured, decisive, and insight-driven responses. For instance, one student noted that “ChatGPT provided extensive information, but the conclusion was often buried,” whereas “Expert-AI offered clearer judgments and more actionable insights.” In particular, Expert-AI responses were valued for introducing perspectives that extended beyond students’ initial ideas, such as phased implementation strategies or concrete approaches to differentiation. By contrast, ChatGPT w as recognized for its breadth of information and its ability to support detailed elaboration, although its feedback was sometimes perceived as generic or reiterative of already-identified challenges. These preliminary findings suggest that Expert-AI and generative AI serve complementary epistemic functions in learning. An effective pedagogical approach is therefore to sequence their use: first leveraging ExpertAI to shape high-level concepts and strategic framing, and then using generative AI to expand, structure, and operationalize those ideas. This staged integration may support both originality and feasibility in student outputs. For future educational design, these findings highlight the importance of orchestrating multiple AI systems based on their distinct cognitive roles, enabling learners to engage in both conceptual exploration and practical elaboration in a more structured manner.

## Implications and future directions for multimodal learning interaction

Building on the pilot findings, an important limitation lies in the current interaction modality. In the pilot study, interactions with Expert-AI and generative AI were conducted exclusively through a text-based web chat interface. While this enabled a clear comparison of their epistemic roles, it does not account for how different modalities may influence learners’ perceptions and experiences. The pilot results also indicated that learners’ perceptions of clarity, usefulness, and actionability—factors closely related to perceived credibility and trust—differed between the two systems. This suggests that such perceptions are shaped not only by the content of feedback but also by how that feedback is represented and delivered, highlighting the importance of interaction design in AI-mediated learning. More broadly, these findings point to the potential of Expert-AI as a form of distributed mentorship, in which expert cognition is translated into a reflective, dialogic format that learners can interact with. By enabling access to representations of authentic expert reasoning, Expert-AI may preserve the depth of expert thinking while enhancing scalability and accessibility. At the same time, this raises important theoretical questions in the learning sciences regarding how trust, credibility, and perceived empathy are constructed in human–AI interaction. These considerations motivate the need to examine the role of modality more explicitly. Multimodal representations—such as voice, video, or embodied agents (e.g., androids)—may significantly influence how learners interpret feedback, relate to the system, and sustain engagement. To explore these questions, the demo session is designed as an experiential extension of the pilot study, allowing participants to compare different modalities of Expert-AI interaction and reflect on their impact on learning. Demo session description This interactive demo session is designed as an experiential extension of the pilot study, enabling participants to explore how different interaction modalities influence their perception of Expert-AI. The session consists of the following components:

• Introduction and Concept Overview: Participants are introduced to the design of Expert-AI as a RAG-based system that reconstructs expert cognition using curated writings and interviews from Japanese experts across domains such as academia, art, and business. The session situates Expert-AI within the broader goal of supporting reflective, expert-like feedback in learning contexts.

• Interactive Demonstration (Text-Based Interaction): Participants first engage with the web-based chat

interface used in the pilot study. They submit short idea proposals or questions and observe how ExpertAI retrieves relevant materials and generates structured feedback. This serves as a baseline experience aligned with the pilot findings.

• Multimodal Prototype Experience: Participants are then introduced to alternative modalities of Expert-AI interaction, including voice-based systems and an embodied android prototype. These experiences allow participants to directly compare how the same underlying expert reasoning is perceived differently when presented through text, voice, or physical embodiment, highlighting the role of modality in shaping interaction.

• Comparative Reflection and Discussion: The session concludes with a facilitated discussion on how

multimodal Expert-AI systems could support distributed mentorship in educational settings, particularly in relation to trust, credibility, and learner engagement. The discussion also addresses how future research can systematically investigate the role of modality in shaping human–AI learning interaction. Through this experience, participants will gain insight into how the representation of expert knowledge— whether through text, voice, or embodied agents—affects learners’ cognitive and affective responses, and how multimodal AI systems may transform the design of reflective and dialogic learning environments.
