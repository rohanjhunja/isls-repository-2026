# Designing Comic Cons for Scientific Literacy and Sustained Engagement

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Preparing scientifically literate individuals who can use science in their day-to-day lives is an important mission for science educators. Research and interventions in service of this often emphasize practical applications in formal settings. However, we argue that informal, adult-focused science events at comic cons offer potential as a venue for cultivating scientific literacy. Promoting scientific literacy in these contexts requires balancing the need for designs that support focused learning goals without detracting from attendees’ personal enjoyment, impeding science identity development, or snuffing out emerging interest in science. Here we present a design conjecture aimed at balancing these competing concerns. Cultivating scientific literacy at adult science entertainment events Public trust in scientists is declining, and many individuals perceive science as something distant, inaccessible, or “not for them” (Kennedy & Tyson, 2023). Cultivating competent outsiders — scientifically literate individuals who can “recognize the moments when science has some bearing on their needs and interests and to interact with sources of scientific expertise in ways that help them achieve their own goals”, is a critical mission for science educators (Feinstein, 2011, p.180). Traditionally, research concerning competent outsiders has been framed with a focus on practical use, such as decision-making surrounding socioscientific issues (e.g., Feinstein & Baram-Tsabari, 2024). Although valuable, we argue that helping learners recognize science as a part of recreational, fantasy, or leisure contexts offers a valuable opportunity for enacting the vision of a competent outsider in science literacy. We present a design conjecture (e.g. Sandoval, 2014) for resources which support comic con attendees in seeking out and evaluating scientific knowledge after they leave comic cons. Specifically, this conjecture targets three outcomes derived from Feinstein’s competent outsider vision: identifying ways that scientific knowledge can enrich one’s life; pursuing scientific knowledge with intention; and aptly vetting new sources and claims. Scientific literacy and adult science entertainment events Informal science learning opportunities which target adult learners in social or recreational contexts are known as adult science entertainment events (i.e., ASEEs; West & Feinstein, 2025). A growing body of researchers have begun to focus on bringing science to everyday spaces and/or bringing everyday spaces to science (e.g., Rosin et al., 2023; Stofer et al., 2019). A specific component of these everyday spaces are those where adults can explore interests and enjoy science, such as science cafes (Childers et al., 2022), collaborations between scientists and breweries (Barrett et al., 2024), or science at art festivals (Rosin et al., 2023). ASEEs offer adults rich, fun social experiences that center science, prioritizing identity and interest development wi th developing scientific knowledge, vocabulary, or practice being obtained incidentally. This framing stands in contrast with other adult-focused informal science learning events such as citizen science bioblitzes or science policy forums which prioritize providing learners with opportunities to engage with science as scientists (West & Feinstein, 2025). Rather than nudging learners towards becoming “marginal insiders” (Feinstein, 2011) by having them engage with science in ways that replicate professional practice (e.g., citizen science), ASEEs offer a venue for participants to engage with science in ways that are directly aligned with their own interest for no reason other than to enrich their own experience (West & Feinstein, 2025). As a result, we see AS EEs as aligning with the spirit of the competent outsider vision of scientific literacy articulated by Feinstein (2011) which centers itself around recognizing instances where scientific ideas can add value to one’s life. Comic convention events as a genre of ASEEs Comic conventions (i.e., cons) are large, multiday leisure events in which fans gather to celebrate popular media, including comics, movies, video and board games, fantasy, sci-fi, and anime. During the event, fans can attend expert or celebrity-led panel discussions about their favorite media, buy merchandise or visit booths on the convention floor, cosplay (i.e., dress up in costume), and attend meet-and-greet events with celebrities (Woo et al., 2020). Fandoms, communities composed of fans of specific media, franchises, or games, are integral to comic 1247 cons. Fans focus on specific objects (e.g. Marvel comics and collectables, Magic the Gathering cards) and are united by “love, devotion, and appreciation” of those objects (Bird, 2002, p. 33). Popular culture fan identity is centered on enthusiasm, appreciation, and social interaction (Vinney et al., 2019). In the learning sciences, fandom has been explored in relation to online discussions of historical fiction (Matthews, 2016). Despite some work on popular culture in the learning sciences, relatively little is known about the intersections between popular culture, fandom identity, and science learning. In recent years, comic cons have begun to feature events which draw on fan ide ntities to cultivate interest in science (Childers et al., 2023). For example, Lundgren and colleagues (2024) found that comic con visitors’ interest and curiosity about science topics were piqued when science topics were paired with scientists dressing up in costume and roleplaying characters from popular culture (“cosplayed”). As an ASEE, the primary focus of this event was cultivating science interest by contextualizing science in an intrinsically meaningful space, however, participants may also learn s cientific ideas and vocabulary through the event. Exploring what science learning occurs during these interest and identity-focused ASEE events and how that learning can be supported presents an opportunity for future learning sciences research. ASEE/Competent outsider tensions and design constraints In their recently advanced research agenda for the ASEE field, West and Feinstein (West & Feinstein, 2025; West, 2022) identify a need for a more concrete articulation of program goals, and design-based research studying how design and implementation decisions shape the impact of these events. Accordingly, we advance a design conjecture for leveraging these events to cultivate competent outsiders (Figure 1). Despite ASEEs’ axiological alignment with the competent outsider vision, two sources of tension aris e. One of these tensions is the fleeting nature of participants’ engagement with science through these events. Previous research into cultivating competent outsiders has focused on environments where sustained engagement is possible (Osborne & Allchin, 2024). Because ASEEs occur infrequently (i.e. once a year) and for a brief time (i.e. a few days), cultivating the skills and dispositions of a competent outsider at ASEEs presents a challenge. A second tension is the need to preserve the recreational experience while helping learners develop concrete, evidence-based strategies for evaluating unfamiliar information. ASEEs’ power lies in their ability to blend science and recreation; compromising this through overly structured experiences which approximate classroom learning run counter to the design features which make them so successful. Leveraging ASEEs as a context for developing competent outsiders means finding ways to extend the learning experience, encouraging sustained engagement with scientific ideas and offering tools for interested learners once they leave the initial event. This must occur without compromising the design features which make these events enjoyable to participants. Lundgren and colleagues (2024, 2025) showed that comic cons can support individuals in seeing ways that science is personally meaningful-an important characteristic of a competent outsider. The activity structure of these events create spaces for participant interactions where fans and cosplaying scientists interact with one another. These interactions cen ter around overlaps between scientific knowledge and fan knowledge, layering ideas from both knowledge funds into an understanding of a science concept (e.g., evolution) which bleeds into both the natural and fictional worlds. Throughout these interactions, scientists and fans also come to recognize one another as members of a shared community which blends norms and values from both scientific and fan cultures. This knowledge layering and mutual recognition help position science as a personally meaningful body of knowledge for fans, and help fans see themselves as science people (Lundgren et al., 2025; Gutwill, 2018). Discursive practices, including comic con norms and values, such as embodying a character through costume and acting, and applying knowledge of a fandom have been studied to understand the mutual recognition that can occur at comic cons (Lundgren et al., 2024). In previous work, Lundgren et al. (2024) found that cosplaying scientists used narrative structure and effective framing to encourage comic con visitors’ interactions with science topics inherent to their particular fandom. For instance, when cosplaying as a Jedi knight from the Star Wars franchise, cosplaying scientists used planets from Star Wars, like Tatooine, to describe geologic processes that occur on Earth and Mars, showing comic con visitors samples of rock with fossilized ripple marks. By telling a story that incorporated the fandom (i.e., knowledge of Tatooine as a desert planet with large dust storms) and science (i.e., scientists can extrapolate ancient environmental conditions from fossilized ripple marks), the scientist and the comic con visitor’s knowledge of science and fandom were combined and celebrated, showcasing the ways science resonates with the fans’ personal interests and values. Although we see this outcome as a valuable end in itself, recognizing the value of science is not sufficient for operating as a competent outsider alone. Competent outsiders must also continually pursue scientific knowledge, and skillfully appraise the quality of new information (Feinstein, 2011). To date, these two features of a competent outsider have not yet been designed for in comic con contexts. Accordingly, we advance design recommendations to support “Scientific Collecting”: a mediating process which draws on the discursive practices endemic to these events and specially designed tools to support these two outcomes (see red highlights in Figure 1). Supporting scientific collecting as a mediating process Collecting is a central practice for many fandoms and is featured prominently at comic cons through specialized events and vendor exhibitions. Fandoms often collect real-world artifacts (e.g. comics, figurines, playing cards, autographs; Geraghty, 2014). H owever, in some cases, collecting is a central part of the fictional world. For example, in Pokemon, the activity of both real-world players and fictional characters is centered around the goal of catching and training as many species of Pokemon as they can. We argue that framing the sustained pursuit of science ideas as “Scientific Collecting” aligns science learning goals with comic con norms and values. We believe in doing so, this will promote sustained engagement with science ideas beyond the ASEE. To support this mediating process, we propose two tools which ASEE participants can take with them beyond the event: Snowball Materials and Appraisal Materials. Snowball materials Snowball Materials are materials designed to support participants extending their engagement with scientific ideas that were foregrounded in the ASEE event. These materials allow participants to “snowball” interest outwards from the fandom world and into their daily lives, incorporating “proximal” examples embedded in the fantasy world as well as “distal” examples which are concrete examples in the natural world. For example, a comic con booth crewed by cosplaying scientists might focus on ecological princi ples reflected in Pokemon. First, at the proximal end, participants will chat with cosplaying scientists who are cosplaying as Pokemon professors. During the interaction, cosplaying scientists and/or participants might describe interesting aspects about Pokemon and elements of the game, allowing connections between scientists and participants. For instance, a cosplaying scientist might pretend/share that they are a Pokemon trainer who mostly catches Combee. Combee, a bug-type Pokemon, collects Honey, an item in the world of Pokemon, for the queen of their hive. The scientist or participant might then share that this is similar to the real world (the distal example), except that bees need to first collect pollen to make honey. After the interaction, a scienti st will give a “natural history collectors’ card” modeled off Pokemon collectors’ card with information about a real-life bee species common to the area of the comic con. The card also contains resources that highlight other pollinator species, ways to attract pollinators to your home, or pollinator-safe pest management strategies. As interest snowballs it expands, collecting and integrating 1249 increasingly distal examples along the way. We provide examples in an online appendix. These materials are brief, but offer “learn more” extensions which take participants to vetted sites which contain additional information intended for the general public (e.g., the CDC, WWF). These resources may also be leveraged as research tools, tracking post-comicon engagement through clicks. These materials are designed in ways that are congruent with the participating fandom (e.g., modeling materials after a “Pokedex” - a digital reference guide within the Pokemon universe; modeling materials as collectibles themselves). Drawing from fans’ propensity to collect, obtaining all snowball materials could be gamified with a collection element, encouraging collecting of materials with a unique pin or badge that coincide with the event. Snowball materials and collecting them are thus designed to scaffold engagement and offer “just in time” information from reputable sources. Appraisal materials Collectables can be highly valuable, and counterfeits are often produced as a result. Keen collectors often must attend to subtle differences in features to discern an authentic collectable from a counterfeit (e.g., printer identification codes; see Howar d, 2025). Similarly, counterfeit scientific knowledge (e.g., misinformation and disinformation) are often found in contexts where knowledge is valuable. Because it is unlikely for a non-expert to possess the depth of knowledge to evaluate the value of a claim by examining the supporting methods and data, discerning high-quality from misleading information as a competent outsider often involves attending to subtle, surface-level indicators of its authenticity and trustworthiness (e.g., author credentials; Fe instein, 2011). We propose framing information literacy scaffolds in this lens, drawing parallels between fandom collecting practices and epistemic vigilance practices (e.g., vetting author trustworthiness; Sperber, 2010). As part of these appraisal materials, we propose the M aker, Intent, Network, Traceability (MINT) test (see https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1B-6FFOPhmhmqi6BQL5BOMX3_jPM05BjN?usp=sharing), a resource which takes common information literacy practices like corroborating claims across experts, and reframes them in the context of collecting (e.g., “If a claim sounds surprising, shocking, unbelievable, or important, treat it like a collectible. Before you add it to your knowledge collection, make sure it is MINT”). The focus on spotting counterfeits aligns the competent outsider vision with fandom norms and values; these are reliable strategies aimed at evaluating surface-level indicators of trustworthiness, rather than attempting to evaluate the quality of an argument itself with limited expertise. Conclusion: Partnering with a purpose ASEEs are informal science events designed to support adult engagement and identification with the scientific community. We argue that by building a design conjecture with cosplaying scientists to help encourage the development of competent outsiders at comic cons, our research aligns with the 2026 conference theme of partnering with purpose. This design conjecture stands to inform the design of innovative ASEE events which meet the goals of partner scientists, science educators, and fans alike. Although ty pically focused on interest and identity development rather than specific learning goals, we argue that with deliberate design choices ASEEs can also be platforms for promoting sustained pursuit of scientific knowledge and the apt evaluation of new information, thus fostering a competent outsider vision of scientific literacy. For comic cons, this involves providing resources which when combined with community norms and values, foster scientific collecting beyond the event. This conjecture also offers conceptual advances for exploring how and why learning can occur within these environments. In Sandoval's (2014) introduction to conjecture mapping, mediating processes are produced when participants interact with specific tools and tasks. Previous research has explored two mediating processes specific to ASEEs: mutual recognition (James, 2020) and knowledge layering (Authors, 2025). In this paper, we introduced and explored the potential for the mediating process of Scientific Collecting, as supported by existi ng discursive practices and innovative tools. We see this mediating process as a critical process which can build on the success of ASEEs, extending their impact by better positioning learners to pursue reliable, trustworthy scientific knowledge. These materials are designed in ways that are congruent with the participating fandom (e.g., modeling materials after a “Pokedex” - a digital reference guide within the Pokemon universe; modeling materials as collectibles themselves). Drawing from fans’ propensity to collect, obtaining all snowball materials could be gamified with a collection element, encouraging collecting of materials with a unique pin or badge that coincide with the event. Snowball materials and collecting them are thus designed to scaffold engagement and offer “just in time” information from reputable sources. Appraisal materials Collectables can be highly valuable, and counterfeits are often produced as a result. Keen collectors often must attend to subtle differences in features to discern an authentic collectable from a counterfeit (e.g., printer identification codes; see  Howar d, 2025). Similarly, counterfeit scientific knowledge (e.g., misinformation and disinformation) are often found in contexts where knowledge is valuable. Because it is unlikely for a non-expert to possess the depth of knowledge to evaluate the value of a claim by examining the supporting methods and data, discerning high-quality from misleading information as a competent outsider often involves attending to subtle, surface-level indicators of its authenticity and trustworthiness (e.g., author credentials; Fe instein, 2011). We propose framing information literacy scaffolds in this lens, drawing parallels between fandom collecting practices and epistemic vigilance practices (e.g., vetting author trustworthiness; Sperber, 2010). As part of these appraisal materials, we propose the M aker, Intent, Network, Traceability (MINT) test (see https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1B-6FFOPhmhmqi6BQL5BOMX3_jPM05BjN?usp=sharing), a resource which takes common information literacy practices like corroborating claims across experts, and reframes them in the context of collecting (e.g., “If a claim sounds surprising, shocking, unbelievable, or important, treat it like a collectible. Before you add it to your knowledge collection, make sure it is MINT”). The focus on spotting counterfeits aligns the competent outsider vision with fandom norms and values; these are reliable strategies aimed at evaluating surface-level indicators of trustworthiness, rather than attempting to evaluate the quality of an argument itself with limited expertise.

ASEEs are informal science events designed to support adult engagement and identification with the scientific community. We argue that by building a design conjecture with cosplaying scientists to help encourage the development of competent outsiders at comic cons, our research aligns with the 2026 conference theme of partnering with purpose. This design conjecture stands to inform the design of innovative ASEE events which meet the goals of partner scientists, science educators, and fans alike. Although ty pically focused on interest and identity development rather than specific learning goals, we argue that with deliberate design choices ASEEs can also be platforms for promoting sustained pursuit of scientific knowledge and the apt evaluation of new information, thus fostering a competent outsider vision of scientific literacy. For comic cons, this involves providing resources which when combined with community norms and values, foster scientific collecting beyond the event. This conjecture also offers conceptual advances for exploring how and why learning can occur within these environments. In Sandoval's (2014) introduction to conjecture mapping, mediating processes are produced when participants interact with specific tools and tasks. Previous research has  explored two mediating processes specific to ASEEs: mutual recognition (James, 2020) and knowledge layering (Authors, 2025). In this paper, we introduced and explored the potential for the mediating process of Scientific Collecting, as supported by existi ng discursive practices and innovative tools. We see this mediating process as a critical process which can build on the success of ASEEs, extending their impact by better positioning learners to pursue reliable, trustworthy scientific knowledge.
