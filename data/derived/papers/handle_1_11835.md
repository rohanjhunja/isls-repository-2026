# Reification of Dialogic Practices Through Collaborative Coding: Teacher Learning During Classroom Video Analysis

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Academically productive talk (APT) moves are strategies that teachers use to extend student responses for further elaboration and reasoning, thereby creating a dialogic classroom. In this study, nine teachers collaboratively coded APT moves in lesson video recordings during two workshops using the Classroom Discourse Analyser, alongside their colleagues and a facilitator. Qualitative analysis of the interactions during the workshops showed that this coding process created opportunities to resolve misunderstandings and solidify knowledge of APT through discussions about the videos. The coding process helped teachers develop their noticing skills and professional vision for dialogic teaching. We found that the workshops increased teachers’ awareness of APT, improved their understanding of classroom discourse, and enhanced their ability to apply APT. Overall, these findings indicate that collaborative coding facilitated the process of knowledge reification, during which teachers co-constructed knowledge from ‘raw’ video recordings to ‘structured’ visualised representations.

## Theoretical background

Dialogic teaching and talk moves Previous scholarship has demystified how the term ‘dialogic teaching’ is used among scholars, with similarities and differences in their approaches to teaching ‘ through, for, and as dialogue’ (Kim & Wilkinson, 2019, p. 70). To promote dialogic practices among teachers, profe ssional development (PD) programmes are designed wi th growing evidence of their effectiveness in altering  teaching and learning practices (Alexander, 2018; Chen & Chan, 2022; Hennessy et al., 2021). One of the comm on foci for professional development on dialogic te aching is teachers’ effective use of classroom talk. A wid ely found structure is Initiation-Response-Evaluation (IRE), which is known as the recitation s cript or triadic structure, as teachers’ feedback t o students’ response is usually an assessment of its correctness (Walsh, 2011). Teachers could consider using talk moves to go beyond evaluation, which are ‘strategic teacher moves designed to open  up the conversation and support student participat ion, explication, and reasoning’ (Michaels & O’Connor, 2012, p.7). Researchers have established various pedagogical theories and frameworks aimed at fostering classroom discussions focusing on either teacher-student or student-student interactions. In this study, we draw on the Academically productive talk (APT) framework which is grounded in sociological, linguistic and anthropological approaches to classroom research (Michaels & O’Conn or, 2015). There are four goals in APT, including (a) helping students share their own thoughts, (b) helping students listen carefully to one another, (c) helping students deepen their reasoning and (d) helping students think with others (Michaels & O’Connor, 2015, see Table 1). Reifying teacher knowledge in videos To help teachers develop professionally, videos are  commonly used artefacts for review and reflection,  which present vivid details and stimulate discussion. Video-based PD afforded reification of teacher knowledge.  The practice of teaching is a complex process that invo lves the whole person, including doing, talking, th inking, emotions, and social relations (Wenger, 1998). Sinc e teaching is highly contextualis ed (e.g., subject-related), representations of practice could not be simply see n as ‘carriers’ of teacher knowledge that could be applied universally (Oliver et al., 2018). Reifications are abstractions from the teacher’s practices, to give form or congeal the experience by producing  objects (Wenger, 1998). The levels of reification o f teacher knowledge, although which are indicative and continuous, could be broad ly categorized as unstructured (e.g., ‘raw’ video c lips of teaching, unedited transcripts of teaching), struct ured (e.g., case study accounts, ‘dashboard’ repres entations of indicators) and codified (e.g., executable learning  designs) (Oliver et al., 2018). During PD, classro om video recordings and their analysis could also be a form of representation of teacher knowledge, either cons tructed by the facilitators and/or the teachers. However, the reification process and its effect on teacher learn ing are rarely investigated.

Collaborative coding of videos Classroom video recordings are authentic representations of teaching, which contain rich information that mirrors the dynamic and complex classroom environment. Deve loping teachers’ ability to notice and professional  vision is one of the goals afforded by video-based PD. Teacher noticing and professional vision requires teacher to 1) identify what is important about a classroom situation; 2) interpret it with p riori knowledge; and 3) evaluate, reason about and predict the result of the situation (Seidel et al., 2011; van Es & Sherin, 2002, 2008). This ability moves beyond the declarative, pedagogical or content knowledge that teachers have, which requires the application of knowledge in a situated context.

To investigate the knowledge that teachers activate  and use in simulated authentic classroom situation, video-based tools have been examined, for example, video analysis (annotation/tagging/commenting) task, which invites teachers to mark special moments through pausing the video, and attach notes on these special occurrences (Chan, 2023; Larison et al., 2024; Walsh, 2019). Vi deo analysis enhances reflection during PD by facil itating teacher noticing (Larison et al., 2024), allowing teachers to revisit the classroom event from different perspectives and seek alternatives (Li & Walsh, 2023).

To code the classroom talk, teachers need to apply skills of noticing or professional vision. Peguera - Carré (2024) sees noticing similar to highlighting,  which stress the teachers ability to identify and recognise important events. Coding is a process after the identification of these events by giving ‘professional significance’ to them, which requires interpretation and argument ation (Peguera-Carré et al., 2024, p. 199). In this study, we do not distinguish noticing from coding, as identif ying is an integral part of interpreting and arguin g the significance an event. And thus, to code, in this s tudy, requires three integrated steps a) to identif y a significant event, which could be compared against certain crit eria or framework; b) to interpret this event i n the context, based on the criteria or framework; and c) to argue  with evidence and elaborate with reasons why this event is significant to teaching and learning. From this per spective, how collaborative coding of video contrib ute to the effectiveness of teacher learning during video-based PD is underexplored. The research question of this study is: How did teachers reify knowledge of APT as a form of dialogic teaching through collaborative analysis of lesson videos?

## Methodology

Design of video-based teacher professional development programme The nine participants of this study were from a larger study which involved 27 English teachers from seven schools in Hong Kong participating in a professional develo pment programme  on dialogic teaching. The programme consisted of five video-and reflection-based monthly cycles to promote dialogic teaching among English teachers, and the participating teacher recorded their lesson video before taking the monthly one-hour workshop. In addition to the five workshops in monthly cycles, there were  two one-hour collaborative video coding workshops, which are the focus of this study. The collaborative video coding workshops happened near the end of the five cycles, one before the fourth workshop and one before the l ast workshop, when all APT moves were introduced to teachers, who had already made attempts using them. For the collaborative coding of videos, the first session used exemplar videos selected by the facilitator (the first author) from the internet, while the second session  used the videos recorded from the participating teachers who attempted to use APT moves in their lessons. Both workshops used the same flow of activities as specified below. When selecting videos clips for collaborative coding, the facilitator focused on the clips that contained rich APT moves. The flow of the one-hour session on collaborative coding videos was as follows:

(1) The facilitator showed the objectives of the sessio n (‘To code APT moves on classroom videos for an

in-depth understanding on their application in authentic contexts’);

(2) The facilitator briefly reviewed the core concepts (names and definitions of eight APT moves) and the

background (e.g., subjects, lesson objectives) of the videos for analysis;

(3) Teachers watched the first video clip together (aro und 3-5 minutes in length). Then, teachers coded the

transcript of the video on Classroom Discourse Anal yser (CDA, see Figure 1 below), with a reference sheet showing the definition of eight APT moves. Te achers individually coded of the video clip using the APT moves, and they could revisit the video clip if needed;

(4) In pairs or groups of three, teachers shared and co mpared their coding of the video clip and discussed

the discrepancies. For those discrepancies that the y could not solve, they could seek help from the facilitator. The facilitator would also intervene if there was a shared problem in the group.

(5) The teachers used the second video clip (around 3-5 minutes in length), and repeated the steps (3) to (4);

(6) The facilitator guided teachers to summarise the key points of the session.

## Data collection

To answer the research question on understanding ho w teachers collaboratively learned when analysing t he videos, the data used for this study focused on the  video recordings of teachers’ collaborative video analysis workshops. For the workshops, teachers only worked with their colleague(s) from the same school, and t he workshop took place individually in the four school s with the first author as the facilitator. The four groups (and their school levels) were: Teacher O and Teacher P (Primary school); Teacher V and Teacher Z (Primary school); Teacher R, Teacher S and Teacher T (Secondary school); Teacher H and Teacher I (Secondary school).

## Data Analysis

Qualitative data analysis is ‘an art and a science’ (Corbin & Strauss, 2008, p. 47) and is ‘the act of giving meaning to data’ (p.64) The data analysis mainly used groun ded theory, with some elements of conversation anal ysis, as the research data were mainly the teacher-facilitator discourse in the workshops. To analyse the data, firs tly, videos recordings were transcribed, after which mic roanalysis on the discourses were conducted. Microa nalysis is a form of ‘open coding used to break data apart and to look fo r varied meanings of a word or phrase ’ (Corbin & Strauss, 2008, p.  46). During and after the open coding phase, the co des would be constantly compared and contrasted to see if a theory is emerging from actu al data. Constantly grouping the theories that are similar conceptually helps with generating the core categor y, which is the major theme of the theory (Corbin &  Strauss, 2008). After open coding, axial coding and selectiv e coding were conducted. Axial coding helped with d efining the causal conditions of the categories and selecti ve coding helped to identify the core cat egory, relating to all other categories. As for theoretical sampling, it means data collection is responsive to the concepts deriving from the data that data collection and analysis would be conducted concurrently (Corbin & Strauss, 2008).

Developing awareness of APT moves Differentiating talk moves to understand their goals.

Excerpt 1 happened when Teacher P and Teacher O just finished coding a video of other teachers. The facilitator noticed that there was discrepancy between Teacher P and Teacher O’s coding, where the teacher was clearly using a revoice move. Teacher P and Teacher Oneed guidance from the facilitator to identify the features of a revoice move. There are two steps in a typical revoice move, a teacher first repeats what a student has ju st said, then invites the same student to verify, which serv es the first goal of APT, ‘to help students share their own thoughts’. Different from revoice, in an  add on move, a teacher invites another student to contribu te instead of focusing on the same student, which serves the fourth goal of APT ‘to help students think with others’. Although both revoice and say more  serves the first goal,  in a say more  move, a teacher does not repeat students’ contribution.

## Discussion

We analysed the video recordings of two collaborative video coding workshops, in which teachers coded the transcripts of the videos using the APT framework.  From the analysis, we found that the workshops helped teachers develop their awareness of the APT moves u sed in the lessons. They were more able to recognis e the features of classroom talk and made plans for future application of APT moves. In this study,  we narrowed teachers’ attention towards APT framework as a guided approach to help develop teacher noticing (van Es & Sherin, 2002, p. 573)  and professional vision (Sherin & van Es, 2009). APT framework served as prompts to scaffold the coding of the videos. Similar as in video tagging or annot ation, prompts help guide teachers to selectively attend t o particular events of student thinking in classroo m videos (Larison et al., 2 024). Through APT framework, teachers were invited to notice the key event in the classroom talk (i.e., use of APT), interpret teachers’ intent ion, and argue its coding with reference to the ove rarching functions of classroom talk.

Videos in this study were used as the technology fo r promoting the articulation of individual and collective reasoning (Mercer and Littleton, 2007) a nd stimulating collaborative meaning-making (Mortimer and Scott, 2003), with the help from the facilitator.  During the process, participants selectively attend ed to how teachers followed up student responses. When there were discrepancies between teachers’ and facilitators’ codes, teachers articulated the reasons why they coded the  turns in a situated context. The collaboration pro cess also supported teacher reasoning to seek consensus among  colleagues and facilitators instead of simply pu rsuing ‘correct’ answers. Compared to a ‘live’ coding sess ion in which participants code while teacher teachi ng, the transcript and video mode of analysis allowed more in-depth discussion especially on those ambiguous case s (Vrikki et al., 2019). With the development of teacher noticing and professional vi sion, teachers understood that classroom talk could be ambiguous and fluid, and bu ilding on this feature of classroom talk, they plan ned to be more explicit with the use of APT moves in order to convey their expectations to students. Collaborative coding workshop served as a tool for knowledge reification. Video recordings that contained rich APT moves were purposeful selected b y the facilitator and they are the artefacts as the representations of practice (Oliver et al., 2018). They could be seen as a ‘raw’ form of teacher knowl edge, that viewers still need to co-construct the teacher knowledge with the clips. Whe n teachers collaboratively code the lesson transcripts with APT moves, these raw videos were transformed from ‘raw’ to ‘structured’ representations of teacher knowledge, in the form of visualisation graphs. APT framework helped to congeal the experiences i n the videos by providing abstraction of these practi ces, and these video clips provided a context for t eachers to understand how APT moves were used.

## Conclusion

Our study revealed the process of teacher learning of APT through collaborative coding of lesson video s with colleagues and the facilitator. The coding process provided opportunities for teachers to engage in di scussion, which helped them develop noticing and professional  vision for dialogic teaching. From the excerpts of  the workshops, teachers demonstrated an increased aware ness of the APT moves, understanding of classroom t alk and skills of applying APT. Taken together, these f indings suggest that collaborative coding supported  the knowledge reification process, which enhance d teachers’ experience in learning to notice and lear ning through videos. Since the tool we used in this study did not afford checking the coding against suggested an swers nor comparing the differences between results, future study could also consider developing an interactive environment that support teachers’ collaborative coding with co mparison, discussion and refle ction on the accuracy or discrepancy between results, which prompted room for learning and space for dialogues.
