# Integrating Local Spaces into Mathematics Learning: An Investigation of Place-Based Mathematics Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To address the persistent and well-documented gap between schools and local communities – a gap that looms particularly large for the supposedly abstract discipline of mathematics – there is a growing need to understand the possibilities and challenges posed by approaches that integrate local spaces into learning. Yet few studies have investigated place-based approaches to mathematics learning. This qualitative case study examines the classroom implementation of two high school geometry place-based learning modules. We find that the messiness of doing mathematics in the real world prompted genuine mathematical problem solving. Findings indicate that students’ sense of place (encompassing both knowledge of place as well as the meanings and attachments that places hold) is a valuable resource for, and is itself altered by, mathematics learning. When sense of place is drawn on and displayed in the classroom, teachers come to see the student as a whole person.

## Introduction

Educational research has seen increasing interest in place-based education (PBE), a pedagogical approach which uses the local community and environment as the con text within which to engage students in learning (Gruenewald, 2003; Gruenewald & Smith, 2014). PBE emphasizes hands-on learning and real-world experiences (Sobel, 2004) while leveraging students' sense of p lace (Lim & Barton, 2006; Semken, 2005) and cultiva ting greater connectivity to local contexts (Gruenewald,  2003; Smith, 2002; Soble, 2004; Orr, 2013). PBE ha s been shown to invest students with a  sense of agency (Rodriguez, 2008) as it positions t hem as producers rather than consumers of knowledge (Smith, 2002). PBE has emerg ed as a promising way to support educational equity (Vander Ark et al., 2020; Gruenewald & Smith, 2008;  Johnson et al., 2020) as it bridges the persistent and well-documented gap between schools and local communities (Dewey, 1902; Leander et al., 2010; McInerney, Smyth & Down, 2011), a gap that looms particularly large for the supposedly more “abstract” discipline of mathematics. Despite their benefits, place-based approaches have largely been limited to scien ce education (Yemini et al., 2023). The studies that do address mathematics PBE are largely confined to examining students’ spatial reasoning, neglecting the human and social aspects of space and place (Rubel & Nicol, 2020).

To address this gap, this article investigates the role of PBE in mathematics. In particular, we exami ne an instantiation of place-based mathematics education that integrates the soc ial and human aspects of space and place. We ask the following research questions:

(1) How did students engage in place-based mathematics learning?

(2) How did  students’ sense of place both serve as a resource for  and shift as a result of  mathematics

learning?

(3) What challenges arose in our place-based approach to mathematics education?

To answer these questions, we examine classroom imp lementations of two geometry project-based learning (PBL) modules in two urban high schools, one of which is designated Title I. In one module, a classroom of students engaged in redesigning local buildings to better fit the needs of their community as they learned about solid geometry. In another module, students created  maps of local spaces using a crude sextant and the ir knowledge of right angle trigonometry. In this cont ext, we investigate the possibili ties and challenges of place-based learning in high school mathematics.

Theoretical frameworks Place-based education in urban mathematics classrooms In what some call the “spatial turn” in educational research (Bright, Manchester, & Allendyke, 2013; Rubel, Lim, Hall-Wieckert, & Sullivan, 2016), scholars are increasingly seeing connection to local spaces as an integral aspect of robust educational approaches. Evidence suggests  that PBE can promote civic engagement whilst ensuri ng a deep understanding of content (Gibbs & Howley, 2000; Smith, 2002; Sobel, 2004). Yet, research on PBE h as largely focused on environmental education and science education. A meta-analysis of PBE literature (Yemini et al., 2023) found that only 4% of PBE articles speci fically considered mathematics. In terms of context, existing PBE literature tends to focus on rural (e.g., Jenni ngs et al., 2005) or indigenous settings (e.g., Moewaka Barnes & McCreanor, 2019). As such, PBE is often discussed at a distance from urban spaces. There are a few notable examples of research that e xamines PBE within mathematics education in the context of urban environments. Most notably, the Algebra Project advocates for beg inning lessons with concrete experiences such as taking classes onto the metro o r taking buses to a local destination (Moses & Cobb, 2002). Other examples include a community mapping project in which high schoolers were engaged in statistics learning (Enyedy & Mukhopadhyay, 2007), a project that engaged high school students in exploring the impact of the New York City lottery on city residents (Rubel, Lim, Hall-Wieckert, & Sullivan, 2016) and a project that addressed the spatial distribution of financial resources across New York City (Rubel, Hall-Wieckert, & Lim, 2017). While not explicitly framed as a PBE project, Ma's (2016) Wal king Scale Geometry project extended geometry learn ing beyond the classroom, tasking students with constru cting large-scaled geometric figures outdoors, repositioning students from above to within geometric figures. Be nally et al. (2022) describe an Inside Geometry, and Out! project that engaged high school students in collaboratively constructing icosahedron models outdoors, indicating the affordances of perspectival transitions from outside to within geometric figures. Space, place, and sense of place This study extends the literature on mathematics PB E to incorporate explicit attention to the social a nd human aspects of place within mathematics place-based learning. While space and place are in some cases used interchangeably, we use the latter term to highligh t the socially constructed nature of place. Tuan (1 977) treats space as “abstract” and “undifferentiated” (1975, p.152),  and place as “a center of meaning constructed by experience” (1975, p.152).

To highlight the socially constructed nature of space,  we draw on the construct of sense of place, encompassing the meanings and attachments that plac es hold for people (Semken & Freeman, 2008). Sense of place consists of both the material setting and what a person brings to it. This might include aesthetic, economic, and familial meanings of a space,  as well as individuals’ emotional attachments to me aningful places. It extends from the cognitive (knowledge of places) to the affective (attachments, attitudes, and preferences toward places). In this study, we take students’ sense of place as both affecting and affected by place-based mathematics learning. On the one hand, students’ sense of place is a valuable resource that students bring to the classroom, and effective and equitable teaching should leverage and draw on students’ funds of knowledge (González et al., 2006). On the other hand, an al tered sense of place is also one of the effects of place-based educational approaches in the mathematics classroom: as students reason mathemati cally within the context of local spaces and places, their perceptions and ideas about those spaces shift over time. Project-based learning and local community This qualitative case study (Yin, 2009) is situated within the context of a larger design-based research study (Anderson & Shattuck, 2012) in which nine high school teachers from across the United States collaborated with researchers to design project-based (Krajcik & Blumenfeld, 2006) curricular units for high school geometry. Two such curricular units were designed to engage students in thinking about,  reimagining, and improving spaces in their local communities as they learn high school g eometry. There is significant overlap between project-based and place-based educational approaches: Smith (2002) identifies five characteristics of place-based pedagogy, the first four of which are also central to project-based pedagogy (Krajcik & Blumenfeld, 2006). These include the identification of a specific problem related to the space, students as creators rather than consumers of knowledge, teachers as facilitators rather than transmitters, student interests and questions as drivers of content, and the erasure of boundaries between the classroom and the communi ty. A key difference between project-based and place-based pedagogy is that the former does not explicit ly address the relationship between the classroom a nd the community. The “real world” experiences emphasized by project-based approaches are not s pecifically local in nature. Our approach to place-based (and project-based) educational approaches grounds student learn ing in the local community, comprising the complex web of peop le, places, relationships, and associated meanings within which a classroom is situated.

A first unit  project-based curricular unit, the Building Project, engages students in redesigning a local building to better fit the needs of the community. As part of this project, students select a building  of interest, survey the community to identify needs for improvem ent, make measurements of the existing building, and redesign the building. Their final products include 3D models and floor plans of the redesigned buildings. Through the project, students develop their mathematical content knowledge of three-dimensional solids, two-dimensional shapes, and relations between the two.

A second unit, the Mapping Project, engages students in mapping a local space using a  homemade sextant (angle-measuring tool). In the process, students select spaces of interest  that warrant mapping, identify landmarks of personal or community significance to include in  their maps, and use angle measures and trigonometric calculations to construct their maps, either digitally or on paper.

## Participants and site

A subset of teachers from the design collaborative volunteered to pilot specific units in their classr ooms. The Building Project was implemented in a geometry classroom within a Title I charter school in the southern United States. The school population was 58% Black and 32% White, with 34% of students receiving free or discounted lunch. Researchers worked closely with the implementing te acher, who formed part of the ongoing design collaborative, as well as with an instructional coa ch, who supported the tea cher in implementing the unit in her classroom. The Mapping Project was implemented in a geometry cla ssroom within a large public high school in a small city in northeastern United States. The school population was 59% White, 14% Hispanic, 14% Black, and 10% Asian. 43% of students are categorized as low-income, with 29.5% of the student body speaking English as a second language. The teacher for this classroom also pa rticipated in the design collaborative and worked w ith researchers to design and refine the  curricular unit. The sites were selected because of the willingness of the teachers and administrators to participate in the s tudy. The participants in this study were the t wo classroom teachers, one instructional coach, and s tudents within each classroom, who did not interact  with researchers outside of the focus group interviews.

## Data collection and analysis

Each module was implemented over the course of 3-4 weeks. Teachers were supported to enact the curri culum through regular conversations with researchers. To gain insight into the implementation of the modules, we conducted pre-and post-interviews with the teachers of each classroom, as well as with the i nstructional coach that worked with one of the teachers over the cours e of the study. To uncover the effects of the curri culum on student learning and engagement, we conducted pre-and post-interviews with two focus groups of 3 students in each of the two classes. A total of 12 students were interviewed  in focus groups, selected by teachers for their propensity to share their thoughts and ideas. Data sources consisted of student work collected from th e two classrooms, as well as audio-video recordings of teacher interviews and student focus group interviews. To analyze student work, we examined student projects to illuminate the ways in which local places were incorporated. We drew on student artifacts to compi le a list of the spaces that students chose to rebu ild (in the case of the Building Project) or map (for the Mappi ng Project) and their reasons for selecting each space. These lists were collaboratively coded to identify patter ns. A second line of analysis examined interview tran scripts using a grounded theory approach (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). Student and teacher statements that referred to local spaces and students’ sense of space were identified and compiled. For each idea, codes were created and assigned before grouping similar concepts into themes. Throu gh constant comparison, the most relevant themes we re brought together to inform our theory of students’ sense of place in place-based mathematics learning.

## Results

Analyses illuminate student engagement in the two c lassrooms, the ways in which students leveraged and displayed their sense of place in their projects, t he ways in which students’ sense of place shifted a s a result of mathematical reasoning and problem solving, and challenges presented by the place-based approach. Students’ place-based mathematics learning First, we found students in both classrooms connected their mathematics learning to a variety of local spaces. Building project For the Building Project, students chose to redesig n a wide variety of local spaces, with a maximum of  two students selecting the same space. Spaces included those within the school (the school gym, cafeteria,  theater), commercial spaces (Nike store, Barne s and Noble, Valero gas station, a store where a family member worked), domestic spaces (student's home, an aunt's home), cultural spaces (arts gallery and studio, symphony hall, public library), and recreational spaces (volleyball club, YMCA, neighbo rhood gazebo). The fact that students did not choose to redesign the most convenient spaces, such  as their classroom or bedroom, indicates that they  were invested in their projects.

We take students’ statements about their reasons fo r selecting each space as additional indication tha t the spaces chosen were meaningful to students. In justifying their choice of space, many students (14/21) referred to their own frequency of use. Some students (5/21)  referred to the fact that their chosen space was f requently used by the community. Many students (14/21) stated that there were  existing issues with the spaces that needed to be addressed.

In addition to students’ investment in the spaces t hey chose, we saw evidence of students putting extra work into their projects. For example, as part of t he project, students were asked to create a rough b lueprint for the building they elected to redesign. A student wh o was redesigning a local store, unsure about where  a door lead, went so far as to call the store to ask about it. The store clerk invited the student to stop by and obtain official blueprints. The instructional coach supporting the implementation of the Building project noted that “ The level of investment is there.” For another student, “the most interesting part” of the project “was just whe n we were designing our own versions of the buildings and imp roving the buildings, I think it just, I think it t ook a lot of a lot of thought and effort to improve these buildings how people requested them to be.” This student found purpose in solving a genuine problem situated within his local community. Mapping project In the Mapping Project, students chose to map either domestic spaces (living rooms, bedroom, kitchen, house) or spaces within their school (classroom, hallway). Students' reasons for selecting each space seemed focused more on convenience than utility. One third of students cited convenience as the reason for their choice of space, while one fifth of students stated that a map of their space might be useful. Students were not only afforded the choice of spaces to map, but also what landmarks to include in their maps. In their selection of landmark, students displayed their values and interests. Three students who all mapped their rooms did so each in his own unique way. While one student chose animal tanks as landmarks to in clude in his map, another mapped different pieces of furnitu re, and a third mapped the posters she had on her w all. The fact that students were able to make these choices seemed to contribute to their sense of agency. In a focus group interview, one student stated that he was able to express himself in the p roject “because you get to choose like your own space,” while another added “and what you're showcasing.” The teacher for the Mapping Project noted that oppo rtunities to connect mathematics to students’ everyday lives led to higher levels of engagement. Marvin stated, Because [students] were talking about something that  they're used to every day, and how the math kind of connected to it – and I was a little skeptical at first that they wou ldn’t want to do that as much – but it seemed like when I gave them those opportuni ties to kind of talk about it and let them see where it goes, I didn't get as man y [disruptions], I didn't have to like redirect them as much.

Marvin noted that when his students were connecting everyday spaces to mathematics, they wer e more likely to stay on task.

In another comment, Marvin attributed increased student engagement to  the openness of the project, in comparison to traditional instruction. As opposed to “just work on a worksheet together,” stu dents “got into the discussions, and it'll last a little bit longer, wh ich was…valuable.” Whatever the reason, students were engaging more deeply in classroom discussions, and these discussions were lasting longer. Students’ sense of place as a resource for mathematics learning We found that students were supported to make conne ctions between the mathematics classroom and their outof-school lives within a larger community. In particul ar, they drew on their sense of place as a resource  for their projects.

And most peo ple use it.” Further, in describing how he obtained  data on how the gazebo is used, he stated, “I was able to g et data because most people come to our neighborhoo d. Like, Andre lives in my neighborhood. And these two have been to my neighborhood, so it was really easy to get data.” Sean identified the ways in which populations flow betwe en the classroom and the gazebo, drawing linkages between the two social spaces. First, we found that students’ sense of place influ enced their selection of spaces in both projects. I n the mapping project, students referred to their feeling s within a space and the amount of time they spend in a space (“I chose my room since it was the space I was most  comfortable and spend most of my time.”) as well a s their ownership of a space (“I wanted to [map] my room because it is my own space”) and the importance of the space in their lives (“I chose my living room because it was an important loc ation to me.”). The teacher noted the connections students made between the mathematics and their everyday sense of place as a surprising and positive development: “I guess the biggest surprise I had was when they…got into some of the reasons more than I thought, which is good, because they were talking about some thing that they're used to every day, and how the m ath kind of connected to it.” Second, students’ values about various features of their spaces were displayed in their selection of landmarks to include in their maps. For example, on e student who mapped his house chose to map “all of  my animal tanks,” indicating that these were importa nt features of the space for him. Another student e xplicitly referred to the relationship between her interests and the landmarks she chose to include in her map, stating “I picked parts of my room that mean a lot to me becau se I like music a lot… so I picked like my –I have a lot of posters and I just picked my favorite posters in my  room.” “I chose to display the furniture and the w alls of the room, as that (to me) is all that is really importa nt…” “I chose the objects that I did because I want ed to pick the most important things in the room like the couch and TV.” These choices, informed by students’ sense o f place, involved elevating certain elements of the space while ignoring others. Effects of place-based mathematics learning on students’ sense of place We found that participation in the two place-based modules assisted students to both attend to the physical features of local spaces and to make novel observations abou t local spaces and their use. At a basic level, students began to attend to the physical features of the spaces around them. For example, when asked to describe their school, in addition to his peers’ comments about the size of t he school, the teachers, the curriculum, and the le arning processes, Ryandescribed the physical space. “I think the building  itself adds a lot to it. Because there's a lot of natural lighting there. It just feels kind of nice in the building. Versus other schools where it feel s kind of dark and drab.” Students’ perceptions of space shifted as a result of making their maps. Many students referred to differences between perceived and measured distances in their everyday spaces. “I didn't realize that the chorus hallway was longer than the band hallway until I measured it.” “I noticed that the distances between certain objects were much different than I expected. I also realized that the directions of objects in my house were much different than I expected…” “I am surprised that it is like this because you would expect the room to be longer.” “I noticed how spread out things were in my living room. It made me realize that the objects were not that close together and that things were a lot further apart than I thought.” Each of these students had their perceptions of local spaces challenged by doing mathematics. Students’ work in the modules also helped them notice new features of everyday spaces. One student stated, “I noticed how almost everything in my living room has a generally rectangular shape.” Another stated, “I noticed that this room has more things that I never noticed before.” Despite using this space every day, the student was supported to gain new insights into the physical features of the space. In addition to shifts in their perceptions of space, students also made novel observations about their use of spaces. Mapping his room made Luis think about how he uses the space and how much time he spends in it. “I like how much space I had in my room. And I realize d like how much time I spent in like, such a, not l ike a, not a huge place, but like not a small place. So I was, I, I didn't know that. I was like, I was like, I was l ike, yeah, I spent time like in my room, you know, it's not a bi g room, but it's not a small room. You know what I' m talking about?” In a few cases, students’ mathematical measurements  contradicted their intuitive sense of place. A student who completed his map stated, “I realized t hat the distances between objects outside of my roo m were much farther than those inside my room.” (This find ing confirms prior research about human’s intuitive sense of place as logarithmic. In situations that discourage counting, humans tend to view distances that are closer to us as larger thandistances that are further away.) Engagement in mathematical measurement and reasoning about reallife local spaces led students to confront these intuitive biases. Challenges to the place-based approach We found that the place-based approach to mathematics learning and teaching posed some challenges. First, there were logistical constraints to engaging in mathemat ics learning within the local community. For exampl e, some students in the Building Project chose to redesign buildings that they ultimately could not access for measurement. Other students found it difficult to find time outside of class to visit their space to make measurements, and some chose sites for which they could not find existing floor plans. Yet, these challenges led to opportunities for stud ents to engage in deep mathematical problem solving. For example, Madison chose the city’s Symphony Hall  as a space to redesign. After an extensive online search for floor plans, she decided to use mathematics to estimate measurements for the space. She explained, “the way I measured mine was like, by the seats. I assumed one seat is like a one to one foot… I would hope that's accurate. But I don't know exactly how big or small the…Symph ony Hall is.” Because of the challenges presented b y the place-based approach, Madison had to leverage mathematical tools and reasoning processes as authentic solutions to a problem of measurement.

This also occurred with Laila, whose challenges wit h measurement led her to engage in complex practices of mathematical modeling, as well as to draw on multiple uses of technology. Laila visited her building and attempted to use her phone’s measuring app t o take measurements. “But that doesn’t get it the f ull accuracy, so that was me rounding up. So that just took away the credibility.” Laila then attempted to use Googl e Maps to obtain a bird’s eye view of the school, “but like… it’s still just hard to get the interior and get the exact floor plan.” Laila also had trouble modeling her building  in terms of known shapes. “My building was like re ally irregular. Like my roof, it was slanted. So I didn' t know how to break that shape down. So I had to ki nd of guess in my model, so I broke it down. But I don't know h ow accurate it is.” To solve these challenges, Lail a engaged in complex practices of modeling in authentic ways.

This highlights a second challenge of perceived tensions between the supposed accuracy or precision of mathematics and the messiness of the real world, wh ich is prone to various forms of measurement error.  As students engaged with the real world, they stru ggled with the inaccuracy of their measurements. Li ke Laila, who felt that her inaccurate measurements “took away th e credibility,” another student, Mason, described t hat “I just feel like… paranoid. I’m just like, oh, is it wrong ?” Students struggled to reconcile these tensions, often finding them to be the most challenging portions of the projects.

## Discussion

In this case study analysis of the implementation of place-based learning modules in two high school geometry classrooms, we found both possibilities and challenges in place-based mathematics education. First, we found that students drew extensively on local space s in their projects, displaying engagement and agency. This confirms prior findings regarding the impact of place-based approaches on student agency (Rodriguez, 2008). In particular, students drew on and displayed their sense of place in their project s, including both their knowledge of the material spaces and the ir social and human conne ction to each place. This affirms prior research about the importance of drawing on students’ funds of knowledge (González et al., 2006). We found that the choices students made in their projects displayed their values and interests and drew on their out-of-school experiences.

As a result of students drawing on and displaying their sense of place, teachers became better acquainted with each student as a whole person. Teachers learned about what was important to their students, like music (for the student that selected posters as landmarks for his Mapping Project), fashion (for the student that  chose the Nike store for his Building Project), friends (for the student that redesigned the neighborhood gazebo  where he spent time with his friends), and sports (for the s tudents that redesigned the YMCA).  Prior research has shown the importance of teachers’ knowledge of students’ out of school lives, especially for marginalized students (Boaler, 2002).

In addition to opportunities for students to draw o n their sense of place in mathematics learning, we found that mathematics learning affected their sens e of place in significant ways. Students made novel observations about spaces they use everyday, either noticing new features or having their intuitions about the size of spaces challenged by their calculations and measurements. In both case s, engaging in mathematics reasoning and problem solving led them tonew understandings of spaces that they use every day, potentially affecting the ways in which they move about in the world.

We found two primary challenges posed by our place-based approach to mathematics learning. First, there were logistical difficulties that teachers and students had to overcome. However, we found that when given adequate supports, teachers did not find the logist ical aspects of the place-based approach taxing. One of the teachers had both in-class and out-of-class support from her instructional coach. The other teacher was an expert teacher with decades  of experience.  In the classroom whose teacher was receiving close support from her instructional coach, these challenges were not ment ioned. That teacher went so far as to  modify the written curriculum to incorporate more place-based elements. During one of the preparatory lesso ns for the Building Design project, the teacher and coach decided to ta sk students with exploring their school building to  identify instances of geometric shapes. They explicitly took  one day out of the curriculum so that, in the coac h’s words, “[students] can make a connection to the building.”  This explicit pedagogical move was made to reinfor ce the place-based component of the module.

A second challenge had to do with apparent tensions  between the precision of school mathematics and the messiness of measurements in the real world. Students struggled to reconcile themselves with the imprecision of their measurements. Yet in this struggle, they developed novel approaches that leveraged ma thematical and technological tools to solve problems.  For example, one student estimated the size of a sy mphony hall by reasoning about the number of seats and the size of  each seat. Another student used Google Maps to obt ain a bird’s eye view of her building. In each case, stud ents engaged in genuine problem solving, leveraging mathematical tools to think about their local communities.

## Conclusion

This study contributes to our understanding of how to narrow the gap between mathematics classrooms and local communities. Specifically, we investigate an approach to place-based mathematics learning with attention to the human and social aspects of place. We find that place-based learning can take place in the supposedly mor e abstract discipline of mathematics. When it does so, students’ sense of place can be le veraged to support mathematical reasoning, and their mathematical reas oning can lead tonew perceptions of and insights a bout everyday spaces. To fully uncover the potential of this approach, more work needs to be done to investigate placebased mathematics teaching and learning. The finding that students’ sense of place is a resource  for mathematics learning  has implications for educational equity. Within the place-based approach, students are supported to bring the ir whole selves to the mathematics classroom. Not only does this have the potential to affect students’ mathematical identities, but also teachers’ perceptions of their students, as they  obtain glimpses into out-of-school aspects of students’ lives. A pedagogical implication of this work is that practi tioners should make conscious efforts to leverage s tudents’ sense of place in mathematics classrooms. In bridging the gap between the classroom and the comm unity, we might also tridge the gap between teacher and student.

While this qualitative case study lacks large-scale generalizability, our results indicate the po ssibilities that approaches to place-based mathematics education can afford when teacher s are provided adequate support. We look to future research to uncover best practice s for mathematics PBE curriculum development and te acher support.
