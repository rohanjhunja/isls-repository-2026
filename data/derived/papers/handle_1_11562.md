# Attending to Teacher Perspective on the Role of Network Visualizations in the Humanities

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To provide students with opportunities to develop data and network literacy in K-12 learning, we aim to understand how to support teachers in their design and implementation of network visualization lessons. This study examines teacher reflections on the enactment of network visualization lessons in middle school humanities classrooms, a relatively nascent field. Teacher adoption of new practices like network visualization requires changes in knowledge and attitudes that are influenced by and have influence on teachers’ implementation of these new strategies. We used Clarke and Hollingsworth’s Interconnected Model of Professional Growth (2002) to identify shifts in teachers’ knowledge and attitudes when implementing these lessons. We found student social and cognitive engagement salient for teachers, impacting their changes in beliefs about network visualizations in the humanities. Findings indicate professional development for network visualization in the humanities should be designed with a focus on student connections and interests as well as curricular alignment.

## Introduction

Data literacy is an essential skill to prepare today’s students f or our increasingly computational and data-filled world. One way to build students’ data literacy skills is through learning experiences that use data visualizations. Rubin (2022) argues visualizations are a way to int eract with large sets of data, with some digital to ols allowing for different data display options and ways to high light patterns in the data. Recently,  network visualizations specifically have emerged as one such powerful devi ce for teaching data literacy skills in a variety o f disciplines and to younger learners  (Zhou, 2024). To better understand the affordances of network vis ualizations in middle school humanities classrooms, it is necessary to understand, how humanities teachers learn about and discern the value of network visualization tools in their teach ing practice, an area that is currently  underexplored. In this study, we worked with two middle school humanities teachers to codesign network visualization activiti es. By focusing on humanities teachers’ enactment of network visualization activities in their teaching, this research aims to uncover potential shifts in their understanding of the pedagogical value of these visualizations for teaching data literacy in the humanities. This holds possible imp lications for designing and enacting learning opportunities integrating network visualization and data literacy  into humanities courses  as well as the development of professional development opportunities for teachers in these areas. This study seeks to answer the question: “How are teachers’ knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs abo ut the role of network visualizations in the humani ties influenced by enacting network visualizations activities in their classrooms?”

## Background

Each day students engage with digital technologies that use their personal data to shape the information they see. Despite efforts to increase student capacity to ana lyze, interpret, and reflect on this deluge of data, students are still entering the world  after their K-12 education lacking the capability to read, unders tand, and interpret data visualizations (Maltese et al., 2015). One way to ensure students are gaining data literacy skills is to integrate data literacy lessons into content area courses in  K-12 learning. While STEM courses seem a logical plac e to incorporate data literacy due to the existing conne ctions with data and statistics in their curriculum, determining successful methods  to incorporate data literacy into STEM content area s is still unclear (Jiang et al., 2022). Additionally, it is important for students to see d ata literacy in non-STEM contexts and see how data can be personal and applicable to other disciplines (Lee e t al., 2021). Humanities classrooms offer a promisi ng opportunity to integrate data literacy (Zhou et al., 2024) yet even less is known about ho w data literacy might be integrated into humanities classrooms.

Some scholars have begun to explore how data litera cy can be integrated into social studies content in the form of analysis of maps, infographics, and tim elines (Shreiner & Guzdial, 2022). However, despite  the opportunities present in social studies classes for  data literacy to be integrated and practiced, soci al studies teachers are rarely teaching students how to read a nd interpret these data sources explicitly (Shreine r & Dykes, 2021). Though work is being done to integrate data science and data literacy into humanities and other disciplines outside of STEM, it is still a developing practice and one that this study aims to contribute to. A subset of data literacy is network visualization,  which has been used in historical research to expl ore connections, collaborations and interactions in historical primary sources (Decuypere, 2020; Gienapp et al., 2021). Networks are aggregate collections of individual relationships between actors in a system. Because networks offer a simultaneous view of the one-on-one relationship in its larger context, this relational structure offers a valuable lens through which to analyze and understand histor y. Network visualizations take this network data and present it in a graph. In network visualizations, the individual actors (nodes or vertices) usually appear as points or circles, and the connections they have (edges) are lines dra wn between the points (Ahnert et al. 2020). In less technical terms, nodes are the subject and object in a sentence, and edges are the verbs that connect those nodes, creating a node-edge pair or dyad. Nodes that have the most interac tions, or degrees, are represented as larger circle s to represent their high connectedness, or high degree-centrality measurement. In representing them this w ay, network visualization tools can also calculate what  is referred to as "gravity" for each node, using a n approach force-directed display to move and rearrange the nodes ba sed on that gravity so that their import and impact  on the network is even more salient to users.

## Theoretical framework

Since there are not currently strong connections between data literacy and the humanities within K-12 education, it is also integral to understand how teachers grow and learn in their practice in this area. Early scholars on teacher professional development provided models of teacher change that showed tea chers having shifts in beliefs after student learning outcomes were improved (Guskey, 19 86). This conceptualization of teacher growth has g one through several iterations, but the conceptual mode l that we use for this study builds on Guskey’s (19 86) model to include the mediating processes of ‘reflection’ and ‘enactment’ and four domains of the teacher’s w orld and practice (Clarke & Hollingsworth, 2002). This model, called the Interconnected Model of Profess ional Growth (IMPG) acknowledges that teacher growth is not linear and that it can take multiple pathways to result in teacher growth. The four domains include: the personal domain, the domain of practice, the domain of consequence, and the external domain (See Figure 3). The personal domain consists of teachers’ beliefs, knowledge, and attitudes whereas the domain of practice details their profes sional experimentation. The domain of consequence i s concerned with salient outcomes for students, and the external domain is where outside sources of information or stimulus occur (Clarke & Hollingsworth, 2002). Thes e domains and the mediating processes of reflection  and enactment help to explain how teachers’ mindsets and practices shifted during this study. Clarke & Hollingsworth

(2002) expand on the change environment that the IMPG is situated within, writing how the school context affects

teacher professional growth through “access to opportunities for professional development; restriction or support for particula r types of participation; encouragement or discoura gement to experiment with new teaching techniques; and, administrative restrictions or sup port in the long-term application of new ideas.”  (p. 962). This is relevant in understanding the different affordan ces that teachers in both English Language Arts (EL A) and Social Studies are given in their standards, curric ulum, and activity design, as well as differences t eachers may experience based on their school and administration. This could differ by factors such as district, school, or content area.

Since implementing data literacy practices was a new practice for both teachers in this study, this model provided a framework for analyzing the changes that  we uncovered in teacher interview data. Similar wo rk has been done previously in professional development th at aimed to support teachers in implementing projec t and problem-based learning and service learning strategies in their  practice (Dunbar & Yadav, 2022). The IMPG model has also been used to understand teacher grow th when implementing computational thinking (Ketelhut et al., 2019). These studies align with our research i nterests as the IMPG model is used to understand if  and how teachers’ beliefs and practices changed with the ad dition of new content and tools in their classrooms  with implications for how to support humanities teachers’ professional learning around network visualizations.

## Methods

The results reported here are part of a larger study in which researchers collaborated with  a rural school district in the southern region of the United States to codesign and implement network visualization lessons using  [name of tool], which is a tool that allows users to input and connect data that is then represented in a network or a table (Craig et al., 2021; Humburg et al., 2021). The two teachers that were recruited to participate in this project were selected based on the content and grade levels they taught and their willingness to participate in the project. Mason taught 8th grade World History and Gabby taught 7th  grade English Language Arts (names are pseudonyms). Mason and Gabby taught in the same middle school and had between two and five years of experience teac hing. Both teachers participated in a one-day co-design session with the project team. The co-design consisted of learning about data literacy and [name of tool]  then co-designing a series of lessons that used [name of to ol] to align to state content standards, connect to studen t interests and identities, and introduce network v isualization literacy skills.

In addition to the codesign sessions, teachers met twice a month with researchers to finalize lessons and develop classroom materials. Gabby and Mason met se parately with members of the research team. These meetings were virtual. The co-design session and follow-up meetings resulted in several lessons that used network visualizations, aligned with content goals, and enc ouraged students to share personal interests. Gabby  designed four multi-day lessons utilizing network visualizations that a ligned with the n ovel she was teaching, The Book Thief. As an example, one network had a few characters and character interests from the novel pre-loaded to model node-edge connections and the network visualization show ing commonalities between characters. Students then added their own name and own interests to the netwo rk and created edges connecting their name to what they were interested in, both added by the students themselves or by a peer  (Figure 1). Mason also designed several multi-day lessons. His lessons centered on Ancient Greece. One network activity he designed and implemented highlighted the policies of voting in Ancient Greec e by first having students propose changes to their  classroom. These proposed changes were then added to the network and students could “vote” on the changes they wante d by adding their own name as a node and then connect ing themselves to the change they wanted to vote fo r. This network allowed students to see the most popular ch anges. Students were then assigned random attribute s which were connected to who could and could not vote in A ncient Greece (male/non-male, citizen/non-citizen, landowner/non-landowner). Mason then filtered the network to show which classroom changes would win if only certain groups could vote (See Figure 2).

Following the lessons, each teacher participated in an in-depth, semi-structured interview. These hourlong interviews were conducted virtually, were reco rded, and are the focus of this paper.  Each interview was carefully transcribed, reviewed, and edited for accuracy. Two researchers used open concept coding at the phrase level (Saldaña, 2016) to develop a list of codes re lated to teacher experiences integrating data liter acy into their classrooms. Initially a list of 45 codes were independent ly identified. Two researchers then met to review a nd codes were combined or renamed to more accurately describe the data, resulting in 15 codes. For instance, codes that referred to different instructional strategies like formative assessment, scaffolding, or technology integration were combined into one code called “teacher moves.”  The initial code “engagement” was separated into t hree codes, “student affective engagement,” “student soc ial engagement,” and “student cognitive engagement with content” to distinguish different kinds of student engagement emerging in the data. A description of e ach code was written and sample quotes were selected for eac h code which was shared with the research team. Two researchers then conducted a second round of coding  of each interview independently after narrowing co des. These two researchers met again to compare codes. Any disagre ements were discussed and resolved to reach 100% agreement. Researchers then collaboratively id entified how these codes aligned with the IMPG mode l categories’ functions (personal domain, external do main, domain of consequence, and domain of practice). For instance, “student cognitive engagement” was aligned with the domain of consequence and “teacher development of data literacy skills” was aligned with the personal domain (see Table 1). ## Results

We found that changes in teacher’s knowledge, beliefs, and attitudes about enacting network visualizations in the humanities were complex and multifaceted. As indicated by the IMPG model, teachers’ knowledge, beliefs, and attitudes (personal domain) are influenced by the enactment of new practices and influenced how they enact new practices (domain of practice). We found that when teachers co-design and enact network visualization lessons in their humanities classrooms using a new technology tool (domain of practice), their reflection on the tool itself (external domain) and the level of student engagement with that tool (domain of consequence) also influenced their beliefs and attitudes about network visualiza tions in the humanities (personal domain). This rec iprocal relationship is represented by the solid (enactment) and dotted (reflection) arrows in Figure 3. In this section, we will first describe ways in which we saw teachers’ knowledge, beliefs, and attitudes about network visualizations (personal domain) shift after enactment of network visualizations in their classes (domain of practice). We will then expand on those shifts  with the additional influences of factors from the domain of consequence such as student engagement and from the external domain suc h as features of the network tool. Finally, we will  describe how differences between Gabby and Mason’s content a reas (change environment) and choices about implementation (domain of practice) may have played  a role in their beliefs about network literacy and  plans for future implementations of network visualization lea rning experiences. For insta nce, Gabby shared “ as I saw the kids having those conversations that I really wanted them to have about the text, through using the program, I was like, ‘Okay, I can see how this is tying together very well. ’” She elaborated that using a network allowed students to see connections among characters more clearly than other traditional ELA instructional practices. While Mason confessed that he hadn’t ever really considered network literacy before this project, once he began using the network visualization tool, he saw the value for his content area. “ As a humanities teacher, I've never really thought about the data side of things… But now I guess my opinion has kind of turn ed into. While Gabby seemed to have new beliefs about the va lue of network literacy in the humanities, her enactment of future network lessons (domain of practice) centered around student interests rather than on content learning. When asked what networks she would recommend to other teachers, Gabby focused more on the student interest side of network visualizations focusing on  the use of networks as a get-to-know-you activity. “ Cause we're always, all of us, are always looking for new stuff to do at the beginning of the year to like, get the kids, you know, talking, get to know them, like an icebreaker.” In contrast, when discussing how he might use net work visualizations in the future (domain of practice), Mason shared several ideas he had for future uses o f network visualization in history classes to support content learning. In addition to indicating that he would repeat the Greek Voting lesson again next year, he shared an idea that when teaching World War I students could create a network visualization of “ the interactions and the events that a soldier went  through.” He also thought that network visualizations help with concepts like theme, chara cter, and timelines. “ I think it's definitely the way to go or if you're studying, you know, chronological. I think i t's a good way to visualize it using the network. ” While both teachers saw value in network visualization lessons, Mason focused on the value for his content and Ga bby focused on how network visualizations can highlight student interests. Relationship among domain of consequence, practice and personal domain To better understand the factors that were a part of the shifts in teachers’ knowledge, beliefs, and attitudes related to the role of network visualizations in the humani ties (personal domain), it is important to consider  the role of the domain of consequence and the external domain. We found that both teachers focused on student engagement (domain of consequence) during their network visual ization lessons. To these teachers, the salient out comes of these lessons were how students engaged both cognitively and socially while building and analyzing the networks. For instance, Gabby observed that students came tonew understandings about the content through their interactions in the network and with the network, “ they [students] were, you know, kinda understanding  some things that maybe they had some difficulty with in the past that I've seen students struggle with unde rstanding.” Gabby also found that the network activities create d high social engagement among her students. By eng aging with the network, students were able to get validat ion from their classmates. Gabby saw that this vali dation was important for students, “They [students] really liked that. Cause they were like,  ‘Okay, people are agreeing with me!’ And so they got that validation.” Gabby also observed how when students completed the network in which students made connections with their own interests,  those of their classmates, and those of the charac ters in the book, they learned more about how their own interests overlapped with others in the classroom: because, surprisingly, some of them don't even know  some of their classmates that well. So they're [students are] like,  “Oh, my goodness! Lik e I had no clue that we had this similar interest. I don't even talk to this person!” So that was cool, seeing them realize that. For Gabby, this realization that even halfway throu gh the school year her students didn’t know much ab out each other and would benefit from finding connection was  an important aspect of using network visualization s. The ability of the network visualization s to create high social and cognitive engagement wi th her students served as salient outcomes of these lessons for Gabby.

Mason also reflected on the high level of social and cognitive engagement of students during the network visualization lessons, however, Mason saw how the n etwork visualizations created cognitive engagement.  He shared that when reviewing content at the end of the unit, a student raised his hand and said “I don't need to study this. I know who got to vote in Athens. ” This indicated to Mason that the lesson was not o nly enjoyable for the students to participate in but that it helped convey the content in a way that helped students retain the knowledge. Mason remarked, “They seemed very confident in the fact that they were gonna do well in that area.” In terms of social engagement, he observed, “ They had to connect. They were connecting themselve s to others. They were forced to see how the other students were thinking.  and they were forced to communicate. ” Mason found this particularly valuable because he felt that communic ation was an important social skill that he had see n students struggle with: I see in kids these days that I've taught for the l ast, you know, 2 and a half years is they don't communicate well with each other. They can communic ate, but they don't communicate well. They communicate using the wrong tones the wrong wa y when they talk to each other. This made them, you know, communicate how a team member should. These positive observations about student social and cognitive engagement during the network visualiza tion lessons (domain of consequence) indicates the posit ive influence enacting network lessons (domain of p ractice) had on these teachers' knowledge, beliefs, and atti tudes about network visualizations in the humanitie s (personal domain).

Relationship among the external domain, domain of practice, and personal domain In addition to the role of the domain of consequenc e, the external domain also plays a role in the extent to which enactment influences the personal domain. In this s tudy, the external domain includes the design of th e network visualization tool. How teachers perceived this tool impacted how teachers reflected  on their enactment of the network visualization lessons. Both teachers in thi s study valued the design of the network visualizat ion tool in that it appealed to students with interest in techn ology and pr ogramming and that the visual nature of the tool supported content learning. Gabby shared “ they really, you know, enjoyed, that aspect of it. I think the programming. I have a lot of people in there who are really interested in digital tools. So I thought that was cool.” Mason also felt that he saw students engage in the lesson who don’t usually engage because they were interested in using digital tools. The visual aspect of the ne twork tool was another feature that the teachers fo cused on as being beneficial for their students. Gabby reflecte d on how networks can highlight the connections in text, “One of the things that I think was beneficial is just kind of presenting my content in a different way than anything I've ever thought about doing. So I do think that the kids really saw the connections better.” Mason also felt this visual aspect was important. He said, “ the fact that they got to see it, you know, from a digital perspective, and see the whole grade level, not just themselves. I think that made it a perfect blend of hands on, and visual.” These features of the network visualization tool (external domain)  that teachers observed as a result of their enactm ent of these lessons (domain of practice) had a positive influen ce on teachers’ knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs (personal domain) about network visualizations in the humanities. The change environment In addition to considering the personal domain, domain of practice, external domain, and domain of consequence, it is also important to recognize that each teacher implemented network visualization lessons in a specific context which also played a role in how teacher beliefs about network literacy shifted. While Mason and Gabby taught in the same school, they taught different subjects and  got different feedback from their administrators a bout their role in the network project (change environment). F or instance, during this study, Mason was observed by his administrators and he received positive feedback about his lesson and his role in this study. He shared, “Everybody loved it and everybody told me that they were happy that I was doing it. They were proud of what we were doing in the classroom.” Gabby did not share whether the administration provided any feedback to her about her lessons. Furthermore, Mason had a great deal of latitude in planning his history lessons and felt comfortable taking longer to teach some of the concepts in his curriculum. Gabby shared that she had to begin each ELA lesson with required daily warm-ups that were unrelated to the network visualizatio ns she co-designed as part of this study and expressed concern about adding in lessons that migh t put her behind the required pacing for her course. These different change environments may relate to Mason b eing more interested in future content-related implementations of network lessons while Gabby seemed less interested in using the network tool for her content teaching.

## Discussion

Using the IMPG model to analyze teachers’ reactions  to implementing humanities lessons that use networ k visualization tools serves to highlight the complex and multifaceted nature of the enactment of a new instructional approach. The idea of teaching network literacy in middle school humanities classes is a novel concept  and incorporating a new digital tool requires the devel opment of technology knowledge along with content a nd pedagogical knowledge (Koehler & Mishra, 2009). Sup porting teachers to take on this level of change re quires attending to teachers' knowledge, beliefs and attit udes (personal domain), how students respond tonet work visualization activities (domain of consequence) and features of the tool itself (external domain). We  found that by seeking to understand the interrelated nature of these factors, we were able to bring to light key aspects of how teachers perceive network visualization activities in the humanities and how to support teachers as they enact and reflect on these learning experiences.

Focus on alignment of network literacy with content standards and the benefit of incorporating student interests Teachers particularly attended to student cognitive  and social engagement during the network visualiza tion lessons and how the tool supported this engagement.  Noting teachers’ focus on the impact of network visualizations on content learning has impacted our professional development work with teachers. We have begun to first demonstrate how a network can convey key c ontent learning and then support teachers in design ing a network activity that supports their particular sub ject area needs. Furthermore, notic ing that teachers were also focused on the social engagement of their students during network visualization lessons, has encourage d us to continue to incorporate student interests as part o f the design of the learning experiences. We have d esigned sample lessons and networks that incorporate student interests as models as we continue to support middle school humanities teachers with enacting network literacy lessons. Attend to individual teacher’s beliefs, attitudes, and context when designing professional learning for new tools and practices The IMPG model provides a framework for noticing in dividual teacher’s perspectives and outlooks (Clark e & Hollingsworth, 2002). As we scale this project from co-design with a small number of teachers to larger implementation with a larger number of teachers, using the IMPG model helped us to notice differences in content areas. While we are interested in network visualiza tions in the humanities, in this study there was a difference between the response from the history teacher and the ELA teacher. When designing resources and materials  for teachers to use when implementing network visualiza tion learning experiences, it may be  important to differentiate history from ELA  and how network visualizations align with content p riorities in these different disciplines. It may also be important to examine whether the trends we saw between these two teachers in different content areas are true across a broader group of te achers or whether these are teacher or school speci fic factors rather than subject-area ones. Furthermore, it is worth future exploration of the importance of implementing the network activities with a variety of student learners. Mason’s experience implementing the network activities with multiple classes seemed related to his interest and willingness to implement more network lessons in the future as he reflected on the salient outcomes of the lessons. Gabby’s shifts in beliefs and in future implement ations may have differed if she had implemented the activities in multiple classes.

## Conclusion

Students need skills in data literacy to navigate t he increasing amounts of data that are presented to  them in complex visual representations in their daily lives. One way to provide students with more experiences  for interpreting and analyzing data is to integrate network visualization activities in K-12 content area classrooms. While previous work has focused on data literacy le ssons in math and science courses, this limits the contexts in which students are exposed to data literacy. Integr ating data and network literacy into the humanities  provides more opportunities for students to master the complex skills needed to understand data in their world and to do so when considering historical and literary concepts. However, we must understand how humanities t eachers perceive the role of data and network literacy in their classrooms to best support this integration.

Using the IMPG framework helped our research team see the importan ce of the multiple and complex processes involved in teacher learning and teacher change that will continue to shape our future resea rch and future professional development with teachers. Recognizing the importance of teacher’s attitudes towards the role of network visualization in supporting their students’ content area learning is central to future implementation of network visualization lessons. Furthermore, the deg ree to which teachers perceive this connection as s upportive may be dependent on the subject area taught. To support continued implementation of network visu alization lessons in middle school humanities classes, a tigh t connection between the student skills acquired in  network visualizations and key conceptual learning in histo ry and ELA must be established. Future research sho uld continue to explore in what ways network visualizat ion supports concepts central to humanities and tea cher perspectives on this relationship.
