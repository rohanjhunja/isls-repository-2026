# Addressing the Data Set Dilemma With Personally Relevant Data Generation and Distributed Labeling in the Classroom

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Educators and learning scientists are now encountering the “data set dilemma” whereby the ongoing massive generation of new data still does not yet yield data sets that are easily used for learning purposes given the varied needs and goals of different teachers and classrooms. We have developed a technology innovation, dubbed “Datamax”, that generates customizable simulated data that can be quickly labeled by students through crowdsourcing. Then, students have a data set to which they are personally connected and were involved in modifying or creating. We report the rationale, precedent in learning sciences and CSCL literature, and interfaces of Datamax along with qualitative excerpts from high school students using it for a college application scenario.

## Introduction

As of late, the learning sciences community has been actively exploring how to integrate the topic of data and its contemporary uses in the sciences, technology, and society in novel learning environm ents (e.g., Polman et al., 2022; Wilkerson & Polman, 2020). With more design and development efforts currently unde rway, a major unaddressed need for researchers and educational designers has recently been identified and vocalized at a public workshop hosted by the National Academy of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2023). The need is for the continual availability and replenishment of usable data sets for use by tea chers and classrooms supportive of various data learning goals. For instance, elementary teachers who want to empha size learning about how variation appears in normally distributed data may have a different set of nee ds from teachers of high school students who are striving to help students develop critical data literacy to better understand their local communities which candiffer still from teachers leading project-based science units that want their students to work with data collected and used in professional science practice. Sometimes more variables are desired, and sometimes fewer, and sometimes different ones than are available. Teachers also exhi bit complex judgments about what makes a data set more authentic and how they expect students will make inquiries about different data sets (Lee & Delaney, 2021). Stated another way, educators and learning environments have various weakly understood data needs, little is known about how to meet those, and sustainable approaches for providing easy acce ss to such data that will meet those needs have yet to be developed. For ease, we will refer to this as the “data set dilemma”. This paper reports on our yearlong effort to develop and launch the first iteration of a web app, currently called “Datamax”, that can help to address aspects of the data set dilemma, particularly in the context of providing data that can be used to explore the process of training machine learning and artificial intelligence systems. While we are currently doing more work to refine this app, the current form em bodies several ideas from the learning sciences and computer-supported collaborative learning communities and can provide one model for addressing the data set dilemma. Specifically, we are drawing from ideas related to participatory simulation and personal relationships to data. Students access a common class code and are given a r andom subset of data cases to label, and then those data are pooled for full class examination. Initial tests from  two schools from a single lesson also offer some guidance for use of the tool and the context in which it can be introduced. Goals and design principles The primary goals of Datamax were to 1) provide students with a persona lly-relevant data set that they could visualize in a two-dimensional vector space where a decision boundary for classification could be defined, 2) help students recognize how the behavior of many users interacting with small s ubsets of data can be aggregated into a larger collection of data for analysis, and 3) offer an object for students  and teachers to talk about that would help them to surface the limits and risks associated with vectorized data representations and classification. A major commitment for our work has been to make a digital resource th at is easy and desirable for teachers to use because it has minimal overhead and addresses an instructiona l need. We were also concerned with ease of use given existing school infrastructures and constraints – such as reliance on Chromebooks, student phones, and limited class and prep time to load and navigate software. Another com mitment in our design work was to continuously ask whether a new technology merited classroom time, i.e., that the simultaneous co-presence and involvement of all in the room could be leveraged and students could be orie nted to the technology quickly. Finally, at the time of development, we knew that the tool should generate per sonally relevant data, but we did not know the exact data context to which the data would refer. With an e ye toward eventual use beyond our immediate contexts and partner teachers, we wanted to provide options for other teachers to create and establish data contexts that would be appropriate for their locations and students given what they knew. Thus, we also present a data context that has worked well for the students in our pilo ts, but the Datamax system is intentionally built so that the data context is completely customizable by any teacher.

## Prior literature and perspectives

Lee & Wilkerson (2018) have advocated for data that are derived from simula tions and microworlds to be considered an important part of the data ecosystem for use in classrooms. Examples would include data generated from repeated trials in an online simulation or virtual laboratory activity or data about the changes in virtual populations of organisms over time in an agent-based model. While these are “algorithmically-generated” data rather than coming from real-world phenomenona (such as seismic activity data or pH levels in water sources), they can serve as key resources for students to engage in data analysis and practices such as argumentation. With simulation in mind and our desire to make things “worthy of classroom tim e”, the simultaneous multi-user nature of the participatory simulation model for design of technology-enhanced learning activities was especially appealing. Participatory simulations are CSCL-influenced activi ty structures that lend themselves to collaborative understandings where students’ individual actions and aggregate phenomena can both be understood

– what some have called “embedded complementarity” (Stroup & Wilensky, 2014). Wearable badges and tags

have been used to help simulate disease spread (Colella, 2000; Klopfer et al. 2005) and computer network structure (Brady et al., 2017). However, participatory simulations do not require wearable s – they have been done effectively with multiple remote access devices (e.g., TI calculators) where students provide input (White, 2006). Another goal of ours was to support a personal relationship to data. Learning scient ists have examined approaches that involve youth looking at data about themselves (Lee et al., 2021), actively collecting data themselves so they are part of the data creation process (Van Wart et al., 2020), and drawing upon other personal connections such as family history or geography (Kahn et al., 2020). To that we are adding the activity of students’ data labeling, which is relevant for seeing how machine learning based artificia l intelligence works. A common form of data labeling would be designating a human-assigned classification label  to a data record. This might involve deciding whether something is a bridge (e.g., reCAPTCHAs) or if a pi cture is of a specific person (e.g., labeling faces in photographs). In the case we describe, it was the determination of whether to  further consider a particular hypothetical student application for college admissions. Datamax Datamax was built in React.js with a serverless backend supported by Google F irebase, that is used for authenticating teachers and students and for storing application data in a NoSQL database (Cloud Firestore). The tool has two modes: student labeling and visualization. When the teacher is preparing for student labeling, they specify parameters f or the features of the data set that students will generate. Figure 1(a) shows a filled-out example of  this interface; in that example, the app will generate college applicants, each of whom has a random number of extrac urriculars (uniformly chosen between 0 and 10), a random GPA, and a random SAT score. After the app generates  the random college applicants, it performs de-duplication so students are presented with substantiall y different cards. We implemented de-duplication so the resulting data set has enough variance for most activities teachers might have in mind. Instead of designing their own context, teachers can also select from a number of pre-designed templates. Once all students have entered the class code for student labeling (simil ar to what students do with Kahoot!), the teacher can activate the system and it will generate several random in stances of data based on the teacher’s specification and distribute them for students to upvote or downvote. T he choice to use upvote and downvote was to emulate familiar systems to students, such as those th at would be found in streaming media services, and to make a general binary classification. A future option could be to customize the labels. Figure 1(b) shows an example of the student interface during this phase of the labeling, whe re students can upvote or downvote college applicants, which they were told would be a decision about whether  that applicant would proceed in the hypothetical scenario given the available information. Once the labeling is over, the system aggregates student responses by majority vote (e.g., if three students saw the same randomly generated college applicant and at least two students voted to accept, that applicant is CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 422 “accepted”) and generates a CSV file with the random attributes as we ll as the majority vote (“yes” or “no”). At this point, the tool enters visualization mode, where teachers can generate a two-dimensional visualization of the data (Figure 1(c)). This was designed to be projected against a whiteboard s o teachers could mark up the image (e.g., draw a decision boundary that an algorithm might use to distinguish accept from reject). Classroom pilots Datamax was tested in a public and private high school in the bay area of California in the United States. Leading up to the activity, students had explored cases of algorithmic bias including w ith an existing self-contained narrative simulation that involved job hiring for a hypothetical company. We solicited a nonymous suggestions from two classes of students for contexts that would be of relevance for yout h like them to investigate for algorithmic bias prior to introducing Datamax. Students were welcome to provide  one or more suggestions or none at all. It turned out that the most frequently suggested context was colle ge applications and how they are evaluated for admissions decisions (44 out of 70 student-offered suggestions). Oth er suggestions, each included sports, grading, course selection, and social media. However, none of these others appeared more than five times in the set of suggestions.

While prominently represented here, we do acknowledge that college applications and admissions is not necessarily the ideal context for all cases as not all students are  planning on pursuing formal postsecondary education. However, it resonated strongly with the youth who were involved in these pilots. In both the public and private schools, we used the college application context, and students first ide ntified factors that were considered for college admissions. While it varied from one period to the next, some combination of grade point average, standardized test scores, extracurricular activities, and advanced coursework were named regularly. Students testing this in the context of one of their high school computing classe s at the end of thir academic year were videorecorded as they used and discussed the visua lization produced in data max. Multiple utterances by students suggested that this context and activity where they looked at and challenged their own labels (upvote or downvote) did succeed in provoking critical reflection on what an automa ted system might decide. Students were able to draw from their own understandings of schooling and could offer hypothetical situations that posed problems.

For example, a student immediately observed that number of advanced classes  or extracurricular activities had limitations because "the school you come from, that matters a  lot for what opportunities you have at that school." During a later recorded discussion, a different student (who we refer to as student 1) suggested that a decision boundary that allowed for an inverse relationship between SAT and GPA (GPA was set to have a maximum of 5.0 to allow for AP classes) would make sense because it if someone had a low GPA but high standardized test score, they would be eligible for consideration. Another student (student 2) raised the following as an objection to relying on that too heavily: Student 2: The issue with [classmate] saying that GPA is a kind of qualifyin g when the SAT score is lower is that we don't really know if what was the school district’s standard of quality. Say 5.0 worth of home ec[onomics] classes or being an office aide…if you have a 5.0 it’s great but that’s not indicative of a very high achieving student and the SAT might not reflect that. Finally, students recognized there were edge cases and uneven distributions for various reasons. One student (student 3) commented about a proposed straight-line decision boundary as b eing too imprecise. There would be cases that cross the boundary in the wrong direction that would be worth considering. These excerpts suggest that given the opportunity to examine and discuss labeled data as rendered in Datamax as part of a whole class discussion, students were able to begi n critical commentary on data and classification.

Next steps Datamax is now becoming one resource in a larger collection that we are  preparing in partnership with a larger number of teachers throughout the United States. Some additional work to be done for Datamax includes creating more default settings for scenarios that teachers can select, although the y will always have the option to create their own. Exploring different distributions of random values also remains as work to be done. In some situations, we would want random numbers to be drawn from a Gaussiandistribution, but in  others, uniform distributions are preferable. Teachers in the pilot also requested that students be able to also answer open-ended questions for formative assessment purposes and to give judgments beyond a simple upvote/downvote. A s more features get added, work also remains to provide support materials in the system to help teachers know how to manipulate the settings as they wish and what to anticipate in discussions that students have about the resulting labeled data sets.
