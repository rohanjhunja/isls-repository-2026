# Online Instructional Coaching During the COVID-19 Pandemic

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
With COVID-19 necessitating a shift to online teaching and professional development, a unique opportunity to study online instructional coaching emerged. With prior evidence that the provision of a STEM curriculum unit and online coaching supported a firstyear science teacher in improving his instructional practices, this study utilized interaction analysis to explore the coaching moves that were implemented by three STEM instructional coaches and identify the online coaching moves that prompted teacher reflection. Findings highlight the distinct coaching styles of the individual coaches and the range of moves that preceded teacher reflection. Implications for online instructional coaching are considered.

## Introduction and rationale

The COVID-19 pandemic caused many aspects of teaching, including professional development (PD) to shift to online modalities. Researchers have called for more studies of online PD programs (e.g., Dede et al., 2009) and have raised concerns about the quality and effectiveness of online PD (Lynch et al., 2019). The present study is grounded in distributed cognition theory, which emp hasizes the social and collective nature of knowled ge construction (Hutchins, 1995; Zhang & Patel, 2006), and recognizes the central role of reflection in instructional coaching (Knight & Cornett, 2009). In our previous study of teaching practices, we found that the provision of a STEM curriculum unit and online coaching supported a first-year science teacher in utilizing research-based best practices for teaching within the COVID context (Wieselmann et al., in review). The present study sought to better understand the nature of virtual coaching that supported this teacher and addressed the research questions:

1. What coaching moves do STEM instructional coaches implement in an online coaching environment?

2. What coaching moves prompt a first-year teacher to reflect on his instructional practices?

## Research methods

We utilized a multiple embedded case study design (Yin, 2014) to explore the phenomenon of online instructional coaching. Each of three instructional coaches represents a case, and turns of speech served as the units of analysis. The coaches, two of whom are the authors of this pa per, worked with a first-year science teacher to su pport his implementation of an integrated STEM unit focused on physics concepts of force and motion in his seventh-grade classroom. All three coaches had prior science teaching experience in a range of contexts. The unit was taught over the course of five class p eriods, each of which was 90 minutes long. Prior to each class period, the coaches and teacher met on Zoom to discuss the day’s lesson plan. The coaches observed the teacher’s implementation of the lesson and again met with the teacher on Zoom following the lesson, utilizing these debrief meetings to promote reflection and co nsider any modifications that needed to be made to the subsequent lessons. All of the planning and debrief meetings were recorded and transcribed for analysis. Utilizing interaction analysis methods described by Jordan and Henderson (1995) and collaborative viewing of the meeting videos, we developed a codebook and coded the data using inductive process coding (Saldaña, 2016) to capture the coaching moves evident in each speech turn. Thi s inductive and collaborative approach to coding he lped reduce bias (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). As disagree ments among coders emerged, we refined our codes and their definitions and ensured that we were calibrated in their use (Wasser & Bresler, 1996). The final  codebook included 24 codes. Once coding was complete, we con ducted code frequency counts for each coach. We als o examined the temporal relationship to determine which coaching moves preceded the teacher’s reflective practice.

## Findings

Our interaction analysis revealed that Coach 1 performed a total of 165 coaching moves, drawing upon her prior knowledge and expertise to provide an affirmation-r ich approach to coaching that focused attention on student learning, revising lesson plans, and prompting reflection. Coach 2 performed a total of 138 coaching moves and used her expertise surrounding the subject content and the state standards to focus on pedagogical kno wledge, how students learn, and sharing her personal teachi ng experiences. Coach 3 performed a total of 69 coa ching moves and employed his expertise surrounding integrated STEM curriculum design and the learning sciences to promote a focus on integrating technology and pedagogical knowledge. Because of the unit of analysis focusing on speech turns, it was possib le for more than one coaching move to be used in th e turn preceding teacher reflection. As expected, reflection was often preceded by a coach prompting this reflection (13 occasions). However, even in the absence of explici t prompting, the teacher reflected on 15 occasions.  In analyzing the coaching moves that preceded these oc casions of reflection that were not explicitly prom pted, several moves were most frequent. In descending ord er of frequency, these coaching moves included affi rming (9 occasions), focusing on pedagogical knowledge (6  occasions), focusing on science content (5 occasio ns), focusing attention on how students learn (4 occasio ns), and developing or revising lesson plans (4 occ asions). Providing the teacher with feedback on his instructional practices only prompted reflection on one occasion.

## Discussion and implications

While there was overlap in the coaching moves used by the three coaches, each coach also adopted a dis tinct coaching style in the online environment. Coach 1, who was the most senior, adopted a coaching approac h that emphasized the positive aspects of the teacher’s pr actices through affirmations. This may have served to make the teacher more comfortable with being observed and open to discussing his teaching practices. Coach 2 drew heavily upon her knowledge of general pedagogical strategies, offering suggestions for everything from classroom management to ensuring equitable participation by u sing a random name generator. Coach 3 had extensive experience with technology integration and was able  to share specific advice related to the hybrid for mat of instruction with both online and in-person learners. Each coach leveraged their position and distinct set of experiences in unique ways in developing the coaching relationships. With reflection being one of the key elements of in structional coaching (Knight & Cornett, 2009), it is critical to consider how teacher reflection was pro mpted and supported in the online coaching setting.  While explicit prompts for reflection were effective in e liciting reflection, the teacher also reflected in the absence of such prompts. Interestingly, the coaching move of affirming the teacher was the most frequent prompt that led to reflection. It is possible that the teacher felt more comfortable critiquing his own instructional practices when he had already been affirmed by the coaches.

This study of instructional coaching was grounded in the notion that knowledge was distributed across the three coaches as well as the teacher. While limited in scope, it provides a practical codebook for use in future studies. It also reveals promising opportunities for online instructional coaching. Given the evidence of improved teaching practices (Wieselmann et al., in review) and the range of coaching moves that prompted teacher reflection, findings from this study can be used to inform instructional coaching decisions.
