# Collaborative Learning with Concept Maps: Experimental Investigation Using Epistemic Network Analysis

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
With the aging global population, lifelong learning is gaining attention. However, research on older adults’ learning remains limited. This study reanalyzed collaborative learning with concept maps data from a previous study by applying the Interactive–Constructive– Active–Passive (ICAP)-based utterance analysis and network analysis to examine older adults’ knowledge construction. By comparing them with young adults, we investigated the differences in learning processes and their effects on learning performance. Utterances were ICAP-classified and analyzed using epistemic network analysis. Young adults enhanced their learning by claiming their idea and reconstructing their understanding from others’ knowledge (Constructive and Interactive). Conversely, older adults mainly engaged in recalling information (Active) and reflecting on a partner’s concept map (Interactive). These findings clarify the mechanisms of older adults’ learning from a theoretical computer-supported collaborative learning perspective and highlight the need for designs of support systems that compensate for Constructive and Active.

As global aging accelerates, lifelong learning, particularly among older adults, is attracting increasing attention. According to a World Health Organization report  (2025), the global population of older adults is progressively growing. In today’s aging societies, learning is essential for extending working life and achieving social self-realization. Regarding older adults’ cognitive characteristics, research has shown that fluid intelligence— knowledge-independent abilities such as critical thinking and processing speed—decline s with age; conversely, crystallized intelligence, which includes knowledge and vocabulary, tends to increase and remains relatively stable even after peaking (Cattell, 1963). Against this backdrop, collaborative learning has the potential to serve as an effective strategy to promote learning without rel ying heavily on facilitators while helping compensate for cognitive decline.

In recent years, research on c omputer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) has sought to identify collaborative learning processes that effectively promote learning and examine the types of scaffolding that foster deeper understanding among learners (Dillenbourg & Fischer, 2007). Group awareness enables learners to access and leverage others’ knowledge as an external resource. Accordingly, it supports the development of mental models (internal representations of situations or concepts), construction of transactive memory (shared awareness of who holds what knowledge within a group), and formation of common ground (Clark, 1996). Therefore, to design CSCL environments that support older adults’ learning, it is essential to capture the collaborative learning processes that emerge among them and clarify how learning occurs within those processes. This study aimed to clarify how learning occurs in older adults during collaborative learning using concept maps. F ew studies have examined the collaborative learning processes through which older adults’ understanding deepens from the perspective of the constructive interaction theory. In our previous study (Shimojo & Hayashi, 2025) examined age-related differences between older and young adults  in collaborative learning using concept maps. The results showed that young adults outperformed older adults in terms of learning performance. However, the previous analysis did not directly examine the relationship between detailed utterancelevel processes and learning performance. Therefore, in the present study, we reanalyzed the same dataset (Shimojo & Hayashi, 2025) by integrating the Interactive–Constructive–Active–Passive (ICAP) framework (Chi & Wylie, 2014) with epistemic network analysis (ENA) to explicitly investigate how collaborative learning processes are associated with performance outcomes. Building on this approach, we combined fine-grained ICAPbased utterance analysis with network-based quantitative modeling to elucidate the knowledge construction processes of older adults during collaborative learning. The research questions were as follows: RQ1: How different is the cognitive engagement of young and older adults during learning? 314 RQ2: How does cognitive engagement affect learning performance?

## Participants and consent

A total of 52 individuals participated in the study: 26 university students majoring in psychology (10 male s, 16 females; M = 19.77 years, SD = 1.90 years) and 26 older adults recruited from the Silver Human Resources Center (8 males, 18 females; M = 68.46 years, SD = 3.80 years). All older adults had completed university-level education, indicating broadly comparable formal educational backgrounds across groups. Although computer experience was not systematically measured, all participants demonstrated sufficient basic skills to op erate the concept-mapping tool. The sample size was determined using G*Power, assuming a two-tailed t-test (d = 0.80, α =.05, power =.80), which indicated 26 participants per group. This study employed a one-factor (cohort: young vs. older adult s) between-participants experimental design. The study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Ritsumeikan University (Kinugasa-Hito-2022-22). Experimental task The objective of the learning task was to infer causal attributions and construct concept maps based on the attribution theory. Two types of experimental materials were used: a learning  text and an episode. The learning task comprised an individual phase followed by a collaborative phase. Participants used a concept-mapping tool (Shimojo & Hayashi, 2020) that allowed simple creation and labeling of nodes and links; no advanced technical skills were required. All participants were novices in attribution theory and concept mapping.  The learning text introduced the attribution theory, focusing on the causal explanations of success and failure. The episode material was a Japanese translation of the counseling session content reported by Weinberger et al. (2006), in which students expressed anxiety about the upcoming semester. During the 15-minute individual phase, participants read the text, inferred causes of the protagonist’s anxiety, and independently constructed a concept map (e.g., anxiety– internal–effort). In the 20-minute collaborative phase, pairs explained their reasoning, referred to their individual maps (displayed on the right), and jointly created a new map (displayed on the left).

## Experimental procedure

The learners first read a text  on the attribution of success and failure. They then completed a pretest assessing their prior understanding, which asked, “Please describe what you know about the attribution of causes for success and failure.” Following this, they engaged in the individual and collaborative phases. After completing the task, a posttest was administered to evaluate participants’ understanding, which stated, “Based on the content discussed, describe what you know about the attribution of causes.” Measurement In this study, two outcome measures were used: (1) the collaborative learning process, and (2) learning performance.

Collaborative learning process Learners’ utterances were coded using the ICAP framework into three categories: Active (repeating/summarizing text), Constructive (generating or elaborating ideas), and Interactive (building knowledge with a partner) based on Chi & Wylie (2014) and Wang et  al. (2015). A primary coder scored all data, with 20% independently coded by a secondary coder (Krippendorff’s α =.68). Although the reliability coefficient was slightly below the conventional threshold, all instances of disagreement were discussed between the coders until consensus was reached, and the coding scheme was refined where necessary. The agreed-upon codes were then reflected in the final dataset used for analysis.  ENA was applied to visualize and quantify co-occurrence patterns among utterances. Connections were computed using a sliding-window of seven preceding utterances, and network structures were compared between young and older adults (GroupID). Principal components (MR1, SVD2) and connection strengths were calculated and statistically compared using the Benjamini –Hochberg FDR correction. All ENA analyses were conducted using the rENA package in R (Marquart et al., 2018). Learning performance Learners’ understanding was assessed using pretest–posttest difference scores on an open-ended question about attribution theory. Responses were scored 0– 5 based on the degree of causal attribution and explanation elaboration. A primary coder scored all responses, and 20% were independently coded by a secondary coder (Krippendorff’s α =.91). Coders were blinded to conditions. Logistic regression was used to examine differences 315 in learning performance, with the difference score as the dependent variable and ENA dimensions and cohort as predictors.

Differences in collaborative learning processes between young and older adults In this section, we discuss ed how cognitive engagement differs between  young and older adults during learning (RQ1). Previous studies on older adults’ collaborative learning have demonstrated that cognitive abilities, such as memory, executive function, and processing speed, naturally decline with age (Salthouse et al., 2003). Moreover, the literature on aging has often treated memory as an independent and solitary phenomenon that has been studied individually (Mahr & Gsibra, 2018). By contrast, we examined how cognitive engagement manifests in older adults in the context of collaborative learning. Wolfe et al. (2023) emphasized that the outcomes of collaborative learning depend on the nature of the task. While several studies have focused on cooperative learning, where roles can be clearly divided, our study emphasized collaborative learning, where tasks are less easily partitioned and meaning must be co-constructed through interaction and dialogue. These differences can be theoretically explained using the ICAP framework (Chi & Wylie, 2014). Young adults externalized their ideas (Constructive) and reexamined them through others’ input (Interactive), reaching the interactive level, which can yield the most profound learning effects. Conversely, older adults experienced a more surface-level connection between the Active and I nteractive levels, devoting their cognitive resources to recall and questioning rather than to co-constructing knowledge. This suggests that the socially shared regulation of learning (Hadwin et al., 2011) is more readily established among younger adults but poses greater challenges for older adults.

Younger learners externalized their knowledge through C-1 and repeatedly engaged in mutual confirmation, thereby sharing “who knows what” and managing knowledge in a distributed manner across the group. Furthermore, these findings support the importance of collaborative meaning  making as emphasized in CSCL research (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995). Therefore, learning performance is promoted not by the mere recall of knowledge but by the dialogic processes in which externalized ideas are mutually confirmed, revised, and co-constructed.

## Limitations and future work

The small number of ICAP utterances limits generalizability, and the sample may not represent typical young or older adults. The study design does not allow definitive causal claims about aging effects, and some large effect sizes and odds ratios may reflect model instability or overfitting due to the small sample size; thus, results should be interpreted cautiously. Although the tasks required no prior domain knowledge, it remains unclear whether they were truly age-neutral, as factors such as digital fami liarity or processing speed may have differed across groups, limiting internal validity.  In addition, although the learning outcome measure demonstrated high reliability (α =.91), the mean scores were moderate (pretest: 2.25; posttest: 2.60), suggesting limited ceiling effects but also somewhat restricted score variance. This restriction may have constrained the ability to fully capture differences in learning gains.

Future studies should increase the sample size, extend task duration, and employ model-based approaches to examine underlying cognitive processes and supports for reducing age-related disadvantages. More precise analyses of how constructive engagement (e.g., C-1) can be facilitated and how cognitive engagement levels emerge from specific processes are alsoneeded.
