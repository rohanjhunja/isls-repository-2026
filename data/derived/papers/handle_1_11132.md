# Contextualizing Moments of Learning: Entangled Practice

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Children develop repertoires of practice through engagement across communities. Dominant conceptualizations of classrooms-as-containers do not make space for practices developed outside of school. I examine how a metaphor of learning as entanglements supports understanding how practices can and should be entangled with (not transformed by) classroom learning. I ask, how can entanglements as a metaphor support identifying how cultural histories shape present learning and emergent futures? I argue this approach allows for interaction analysis of the impact of historicity and power in interactions. I also claim that the metaphor of learning as entanglements supports recognizing interactional moments through which onto-epistemic heterogeneity can be affirmed and drawn upon as a resource for teacher and student learning, contributing the emergence of hybrid/third-spaces.

## Introduction

How we conceptualize learning has implications for what we notice, the explanations we create for chil dren’s experiences, and subsequently what we conclude abou t directions for future design and research (e.g., Warren et al., 2020). Historically (and perhaps still in the popular imagination of many), settings like classrooms have been conceptualized as isolated containers where childre n set down their differences to learn together (Lea nder et al., 2010). Others describe permeable classroom boundaries, like a porous conta iner, supporting students to draw on prior experience with disciplinary phenomenon as those experiences are deemed relevant to learning. For instance, The Framework for K-12 Science (NRC, 2012) states, “children entering k indergarten have surprisingly sophisticated ways of thinking about the world, bas ed in part on their direct experiences” (p. 24) and  that “by listening to and taking these ideas seriously, educ ators can build on what children already know and c ando” (p. 24-25). While the Fra mework authors recognize both knowledge and practice a s essential elements to science, descriptions of how to draw on children’s experience focus on children’s ideas about phenomena rather than their practices for exploring phenomena. In porous container classrooms, some practices make their way in if educators deem them relevant, but even those practices will be “transformed” towards Western understandings of science. More radical conceptualizations of classrooms recog nize that cultural repertoires (Rogoff & Gutiérrez,

2003) cannot be left at the door and must indeed be  drawn upon in classroom learning. Hudicourt-Barnes (2003)

provided an example demonstrating children’s brilliance when  they were afforded use of familiar language practices. In line with this work, Warren et al. (2 020) conceptualized the nature of classrooms as pla ces of ontoepistemic heterogeneity based on decades of work by  scholars focused on learning  across multiple disciplines including Ananda Marin, Ann Rosebery, Beth Warren, Carol Lee, Ed Taylor, Katie Headrick Taylor, Kris Gutiérrez, Na’ilah Suad Nasir, Megan Bang, Shirin Vossoughi, and Thomas Philip. Warren et al. begin by saying: We explore what is required of us, as researchers and educators, to disentangle received disciplinary knowledge and ways of knowing from wha t Morrison names as “the apparatus of control,” and to enable expansive and insurgent way s of learning, being, and acting in, with, and across disciplines. We posit that engaging in liberatory forms of disciplinary learning within a framework of decolonization and onto-epistemic heterogeneity is necessary for equitable, dignified, and just forms of education that support learners in who they imagine  themselves to be and might become (Espinoza & Vossoughi, 2014). (p. 278) In liberatory disciplinary learning, it is necessary to assume classrooms are heterogeneous. Taking this as a starting place, I extend understandings of lines of experience (Ingold, 2007) to support conceptualizing entanglements as a metaphor for learning  to replace container metaphors  that are more likely to support continued settled expectations (Bang et al., 2012). I then demonstrate how an interaction between a young child and her presch ool teacher is an entanglement to illustrate the value of this metaphor for recognizing onto-epistemic heterogeneity. Framework Lines of experience Ingold’s (2007) exploration of lines allows for tracing experience within and across settings. Wayf aring is the experience an individual has as is “one who participates from within in the very pr ocess of the world’s continual coming into being and who, in laying a trail of lif e, contributes to its weave and texture” (Ingold, 2 007, p. 81). I describe these “trails of life” as lines of experience. Conceptualizing lived experience along lines is hel pful in several ways. First, the conceptualization of lines  emphasizes the connected lived experience of children to push back against traditional conceptualizations of sett ings as isolated containers (Lea nder et al., 2010). Second, a conceptualization of lines encourages tracing exper ience by analytically making connections between ch ildren’s participation in valued practices across settings (see Azevedo, 2011). Thus, lines of experience supp ort analyses that consider multiple time points through examining assemblages of moments. This provides opportunities to contextualize individual moments of learning within  a broader understanding of lived experience. By coming to understand prior experience s, repertoires of practice, and learning in multiple cultural co mmunities, an analyst can understand contextual nuances of the present mo ment from participants’  perspectives (e.g., children’s perspectives). Third, a conceptualization of lines acknowledges that pathways may wander, circle back, and move in multi-dimensional ways. Such analysis allows for asking questions about how prior experience across settings shapes children’s interactions, and how histories and futures become entangled within those interactions. Finally, the idea of lines of experience foregrounds the unique pathways individuals trace as they participate in overlapping cultural communities. In the case of the present exploration, this means recognizing that classrooms serve as a nexus of connections as children, teachers, and car egivers move within and across the physical bounds of the classroom. Lines of experience become entangled in classrooms, as in any other interactional setting. Conceptualizing entanglements Ingold (2007) sets the stage for conceptualizing entanglements in a story he told of a conversation among women on a ferry-crossing during which “the life-histories of the three women were momentarily entan gled in their conversation” (p. 51). This concept of entangled life-histories, or lines of experience, emphasizes the w ays that experience can retrace prior paths. He develops this idea as he describes telling a story as the act of sharing: …the occurrences of the past, retracing a path through the world that others, recursively picking up the threads of past lives, can follow in the process of spinning out their own. But rather as in looping or knitting, the thread being spun now and the thread picked up from the past are both of the same yarn. There is not point at which the story ends and life begins. (p. 90). To emphasize his point that lines of experience are not bounded but are spun throughout an individual’s life (and arguable still entangled in other’s futures), Ingol d shares an image (re-interpreted here by the author) of lines of experience becoming tangled up as stories of past lived experience are shared in the present moment (Figure 1a). Much prior work on entanglements has drawn on Barad’s

(2007) use to reference how interactants define each other through their interactions. Nxumalo (2017) focuses on

the relationships formed through interactions as she describes how  rocks may become entangled with children’s explorations, moss, and rain’s lines of experience.  These ways of seeing entanglements have been taken  up to understand entanglements between humans, materials,  and animals to expand units of analysis (e.g., Nxu malo, 2017; Sheridan et al., 2020). Like this prior work on entanglements, my purpose here, too, is to expand analysis. I conceptualize entanglements as moments during which participants engage in collabo rative sensemaking making relevant their histories, futures, and ideas. I developed this conceptualization as a result of trouble in prior analysis; I realized that to fully understand a focal moment, I needed a broader understanding of related repertoires to contextualize individuals’ p erspectives. I analyzed entanglements of participants’ lines of experience before and after the focal moment illustrating how  prior experience and future possibility exist ed simultaneously in the lived embodiment of practice in that moment. Thus, analysis of moment-to-moment interaction was enhanced by understanding connected practice (e.g., repertoires of practice, funds of knowledge; Rogoff & Gutiérrez, 2003; Moll et al., 2006) and em ergent futures (e.g.,  third/hybrid spaces, barriers to participation; Gutiérrez, 2008; Heath, 1983). The present effort is aligned with efforts to iterate Interaction Analysis methods to attend to power (e.g., Daniel e t al., 2023; Keifert & Marin, 2018; Philip et al., 2018)  and a historicity that conceptualizes past/present/future as mutually constituted (Hirsch & Stewart, 2006). Illustrative data source In this conceptual piece, I re-examine data from a prior International Conference of the Learning Sciences long paper. In Keifert (2012), I examined how Catherine (previously Marie [1]), a white, European-heritage child, engaged in a family inquiry practice (see Keifert, 2015, 2021). In the present conceptualization of entanglement, I take up this data to illustrate how this metaphor support s a shift in conceptualizing classrooms and children’s contributions. The data come from the Early Learnin g Across Context study; ELAC captured the complexity of the social environments in and through which young children learn. ELAC was a longitudinal video-observation study of young children (ages 2-4) across home and preschool, and the same children (ages 6-8) across home and early elementary. Data consist of over 800 hours of video in total.  In Keifert (2012), I examined approximately 35 hours of preschool-phase data of Catherine at home and in her preschool classroom using Interaction Analysis traditions (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) with careful attention to acknowledging Catherine’s agency and contributions (Ochs, 1979). I focus here on the conceptual idea of entanglements rather than  the method of its development. I center Catherine’s lines of experience based on p rior analysis of her family culture (40.47 hours of video data; see Keifert, 2021) in relation to he r teacher’s lines of experience based on data of th eir classroom (27.18 hours; note: this teacher was not present in ever y classroom video). Doing so allows me to foregroun d a child’s lived experience in contrast to the adult-centered power norms typically deployed in analysis. Illustrating case I analyzed Catherine’s engagement in a family practice while observing crickets in her preschool class room. At home, the practice began by asking a “what if?” to propose imagining themselves in an undesirable circumstance, followed by characteristic interactional turns of f urthering (e.g., ‘tell me more ’ turns), exploration (e.g., articulating what would happen), and expanding (e.g., revising the circumstance) (see Keifert, 2021 for a thorough description of this family practice). In Catherine’s classroom, she opened this practice with Teacher Stacey but was not successful in engaging in the full range of  characteristic interactional turns of the practice. Coordination of practices during interactions often result in such transformations (Engeström, 1999; Greeno et al., 1993). As several children gathered around a table for morning snack, they examined a terrarium filled with crickets. As illustrated in Table 1, Catherine identified a phenomenon of interest (the crickets) and initiated a thought experiment to explore that phenomenon. Her first turn proposed a thought experiment that placed herself (and others) in an undesirable circumstance of havi ng the crickets loose running around them, allowing  her to explore her relationship with the crickets. Teacher  Stacey replied by saying it couldn’t happen becaus e they had a good cricket house, correctly assessing that this  was an undesirable event but denying it as an im aginative possibility and drawing on caring for the crickets as a valued practice. After this opening pair of tu rns, both Catherine and Teacher Stacey maintained this pattern of Catherine proposing the thought experiment and Teacher Stacey describing why it couldn’t or shouldn’t happen (Table 1). Each repeating of Catherine’s question suggested that she had not yet received a satisfactory answer, resulting in a back-and-forth where each of Catherine’s turns re-envisioned ever more exciting parameters tailored to push past  Teacher Stacey’s responses to imagine the cricket escape. In response Teacher Stacey re-envisioned ways that constrained Catherine’s thought experimen t within the bounds of what should actually happen as they collectively care for the crickets. A conceptualization of past and present as mutually constituting one another shows a disconnect between the pattern of Catherine’s past engagement in her f amily inquiry practice and the present back-and-forth with Teacher Stacey: while Catherine used similar opening turns, respons es fr om Teacher Stacey led to a transformation. In Crickets, Catherine first envisioned parameters that proposed exploration in the imaginative (and undesirable) realm followed by Teacher Stacey re-envisioning parameters that constrained the explora tion based on realistic animal care concerns and Catheri ne continuing with newly re-envisioned expansions. From Catherine’s perspective, Teacher Stacey responded with appropriate next turns, but not the relevant-next turns of Catherine’s family practice. Rather than an exploration of the relationship between Catherine (and others) and the crickets which Catherine proposed, or an exploration of how and why to care for animals  with which Teacher Stacey countered, the resulting interaction resembled a one-upping game of expanding and constraining turns. Conceptualizing entanglements The metaphor of entanglements encourages a shift in  perspective to understand how each line of experie nce informed interaction in the moment.  Considering a multitemporal historicity—past, present, future  as co-present—in a moment allows articulating the role of Catherine’s experience with her family practice, Teacher Stacey’s animal care practice, the power of teachers, a playful game, and emergent barrier. The transformation of Catherine’s thought experiment to a one-upping game may be explained by tension s between Catherine’s prior experiences within her family’s practice and Teacher Stacey’s own practices for animal care. While Catherine’s family practice valued exploring the undesirable through inquiry, evidence of Teacher Stacey’s values suggests a primacy of concern for animal welfare, care, and co ntrol. While both Catherine and Teacher Stacey want ed to attend to the crickets, Catherine and Teacher Stace y may have understood themselves to be participatin g in different activities, even as they were interacting in the same moment with each other in the classroom. With this potential explanation in mind, I present analysis that assumes two interacting activity systems (Engeström, 2001) as lines of experience become ent angled in interaction: one system represents Catherine’s demonstrable understanding of the interaction infor med by her history of participation in her family i nquiry practice, and the other system represents Teacher Stacey’s demonstrable understanding of the interaction informed by her history of talk and action regarding caring for animals in the classroom. Although Catherine and Teacher Stacey had an apparent shared goal of attending to the crickets, this analysis will show how this similarity was superficial. While Teacher Stacey’s response was reasonable from a general standpoint, it was not reflective of Catherine’s proposal for what they were up to in that situation.

## Results of the interaction: No more “What If?” in the classroom data corpus

What I call the transformation of a practice in Cat herine’s interaction with Teacher Stacey may not se em problematic, particularly as the transformed outcome was playful in nature. Certainly, it is a relatively low stakes encounter. However, combined with the lack of any additional proposals from Catherine for engaging in thought experiments within observations of her activity in her preschool classroom, it presents a more troubling image. Taking up an understanding of the mutual construction of present and future, the possible harm from one moment becomes a potential pattern of similar entanglements that could lead to emergent barriers to Catherine’s classroom engagement and a loss of opportunity for all children in the classroom to benefit from her family inquiry practice as a contribution to classroom culture. I now explore Catherine’s lived experience to foreground the importance of taking very young children’s perspectives and understanding such present/future barriers. Participants may experience consistent constraints in drawing on familiar ways of perceiving and knowing across settings with significant consequenc es for feeling seen in classroom learning. Though C atherine seemed to enjoy the one-upping game with Teacher Stacey, Catherine did not receive a demonstrable satisfactory response from Teacher Stacey as indicated by her six repeated “what if” questions. Moreover, in the entire record of Catherine in her preschool classroom, this was the only instance of her init iating this family inquiry practice. This makes it difficult to understand Catherine’s l ived experience as there is little data about her a ttempts to engage in this family practice. It is possible that  Catherine had already encountered many such challe nges to asking, “What if?” in familiar contexts in her preschool classroom, and the lack of similar instances are a result of her already experiencing a “felt” barrier—a sense that her family inquiry practice, as she knew it, would not be recognized and drawn upon by others or incorporated into classroom practices. Such a felt barrier would explain why at home she perceived and acted upon many oppor tunities for the practice (see Keifert, 2021 for a summary of both the density of occurrences in short time periods and continuity of occurrences over years of observation), but in school demonstrated acting on  only one such opportunity (with the crickets). Thus, the lack of data of Catherine drawing on her practice in preschool suggests that she already recognized th is as a present boundary, much like Heath’s (1983) study of the rules for engagement in play across settings leading to “children gradually [giving] up trying to play in those areas of the room where they could not bring their home play habits” (p. 274). I raise this as a n example of understanding how patterns in entangle ments can be understood to knot-off— disconnect—children’s repertoires from sensemaking in classrooms leading to harmful emergent futures. Patterns of knotting may result in children experiencing challenges to their educational dignity (Espinoza et al., 2020), yet for Catherine it seemed a different outcome occurred. Although I did not explicitly question her about this episode in the ELAC elementary phase, I did ask Catherine (age 7y 3m) i f she got to “try things out” in school like she described at home (another family inquiry practice; Keifert, 2021). Catherine replied no, that at school she does “testing” not “experimenting”, where in “testing the teacher knows what’s gonna happen” and in experimenting they get to explore and find out toge ther. By age seven, Catherine described the very emergent barrier we hope to avoid, yet claimed science as her own (likely as both parents identified as scientists). It is important to position findings in sociocultural understandings of the relationship between power and historicity. “Central to the idea of historicity is  the recognition of systemically constructed power dynamics that structure relations between and among individuals, communities, and institutions (involving humans and  morethan-humans)” (Learning in Places Collaborative, 2021, p. 1). Thus, analytically I can recognize how Catherine’s experience as a European-heritage child whose parents were STEM professional s allowed her to claim science practices as her own (over and above school) rather than feel that she c ouldn’t do science. This is quite different from experiences of children from marginalized communities who are often placed in “untenable epistemological positions that work against engagement in meaningfu l learning of scientific ideas, practices, and phen omena” (Bang et al., 2012, p. 304). For instance, Warren et al., (2020) discuss how Jonathan—a young African American boy—was labeled as having “problematic” science engagement because of his contributions. However, data show Jonathan contributed valuable ecological perspectives that disrupted settled expectations. While schooled ways of knowing constrained Catherine’s sensemaking, the  cost for Catherine was small; she did not get to e ngage in her family inquiry practice. She was not punished, accused of proposing cruelty to animals, chastised, or labeled problematic. Yet emergent futures based on patterns of similar i nteractions for Catherine and Jonathan meandisconnects between their repertoires of  practice and classroom practice and a limiting of o nto-epistemic heterogeneity in the classrooms with harm to them and their classmates (Bang et al., 2012; Warren et al., 2020).

## Discussion

Entanglements are an omnipresent social phenomenon. Entanglements occur when families gather at dinner time to tell stories about their day (e.g., Ochs et al., 1992), when children draw upon practices familiar in their outsideof-school lives (e.g., Heath,  1983), and even as you read this manuscript and entangle your sensemaking with Catherine and Teacher Stacey’s activity and my argument. Entanglements as phenomena are a part of the nature of human existence and the nonlinearity of learning. Entanglements as a metaphor for conceptualizing moments of learning highlights the importance of participants’ experien ce across settings as made relevant during interactions with others and supports understanding the inherent onto-epistemic heterogeneity of classrooms. The above analysis demonstrates that entanglements support identifying at the interactional level how cultural histories shape d a  moment of learning  and led to emergent classroom patterns. I show ed that entanglements help shed light on  a moment of learning  with respect to  how lines of experience (cultural repertoires, past experience, and future practice) become relevant and entangled  with powered systems. I also showed how analyzing Crickets from the lens of entanglements was necessa ry to understand how cu ltural practices were being intertwined  by two equally competent cultural practitioners and  how a teacher’s power shaped the moment and present/future barriers.

This use of entanglements is a model for  how interaction analysis can recognize sociocultural systems (e.g., ideologies; see Daniel et al.,  2023; Keifert & Marin, 2018)  in a historicity of related past/present/future through analyzing historical substrates and emergent futures (including potential hybrid/third-spaces) as relevant in-the-moment. I suspect much research on young children’s learn ing might look different if children were assumed to be competent, and similar analyses were conducted to understand interactions from their perspectives, a perspective too often missing from research (Cannella, 1999; Keifert & Stevens, 2019). It is also an interactionallevel example of emergent classroom practice that c an lead to barriers restricting learning or to weav ing of multiple repertoires in a classroom center ing onto-epistemic heterogeneity towards liberatory learning (e.g.,; Gutiérrez, 2008; Heath, 1983; Warren et al., 2020).

## Significance

Understanding the multitude of phenomena that const itute learning and culture at multiple scales is necessary to understand the complexity of learning. While prior work attended to how schools harm children as they force cultural assimilation based on European-heritage, white-dominant cultural understandings (e.g. Bang et al., 2012; Warren et al., 2020), it is important to also exami ne the role of culture at the scale of families (Go odwin, 2007; Keifert, 2021) in shaping children’s learning experience across settings. Similarly, in addition to looking at wholeclassroom patterns of interaction (e.g., hybrid/thi rd-space), it is important to understand particular mo ments that contribute to those patterns. Drawing on  entanglements as a metaphor for learning sheds ligh t on children’s experience as they attempt to draw on family inquiry practices in classrooms in which teachers may not be familiar with such practices; this in turn highlights tensions between children’s and teacher’s interests  and concerns that can lead to learning opportunities for both that center onto-epistemic heterogeneity as a strength, or conversely to barriers that maintain settled expectations. This approach attends to historicity and power by r ecognizing interrelations between past, present, and future  of multiple competent humans (little and big). As educators and researchers better understand the work of interweaving diverse repertoires of practice  in entanglements, opportunities for weaving that allow teachers to not ice and sustain young  children’s practices, they can then support centering onto-epistemic heterogeneity towards liberatory learning.
