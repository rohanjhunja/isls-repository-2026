# Mapping the Relational Dynamics: Asymmetry with GAI Chatbot and Reciprocity with Peers

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This case study explores how learners form and negotiate relational ties with Generative AI (GAI) chatbots and human peers during two reflective writing activities. We found learners formed a hierarchical, task-oriented relationship with GAI chatbots, whil e maintaining an equal relationship, reciprocity, and socio-emotional connections with peers. These findings reveal learners’ strategic negotiation to balance writing agency and socio-emotional needs with human-computer collaborative environments.

There is a growing interest in Generative AI (GAI) chatbots on supporting learners’ writing skills (e.g., Mahapatra, 2024; Mekheimer, 2025). However, little is known about learners’ different relational perceptions when interacting with both GAI chatbots and human peers. The relational dynamic is crucial, as it influences learners’ motivation in writing (Ainley & Ainley, 2020), and potentially reshapes learners’ perceptions of agency of writing and collaboration (Holland et al., 1998). This study focuses on the relational ties by comparing GAI chatbots support with peer support in learners’ reflective writing experiences. We aim to understand the dynamics between human-and-GAI chatbots and peer-to-peer interactions in writing processes in answering the following question: How do learners form and negotiate relational ties with GAI chatbots and their peers during reflective writing processes?

From the sociocultural perspective, we conceptualized reflective writing (RW) activities as social and shared practices, where meaning-making consists of introspection and emotional expression (Moon, 2004; Ryan, 2011). These practices are accomplished by i ndividuals and learners’ interactional environments, including the mediational tools (i.e., GAI chatbot/peer interactions) to holistically understand the learning processes (Nathan & Sawyer, 2022), and to identify relational dynamics to understand the way learners form and negotiate relational ties and agency (Esmonde, 2017). Drawing on this lens, we focused on relational agency (Edwards, 2005) to unpack the dynamics in this study. Relational agency refers to the ability of individuals to identify and utilize the expertise of others/tools to expand their “objects of practice” (Edwards, 2007). In other words, this concept shifts the analytical focus from isolated individual actions to joint action with others, and thus enhances understanding and addresses problems learners encounter. That is to say, relational agency expands the explanatory scope of personal agency and emphasizes the centrality of mutual responsibility in social interaction. This paper uses a single-case study (Yin, 2015), with eight participants from diverse majors (e.g., engineering, science, and education, etc.) at a northeastern U.S. university. Participants were randomly assigned to either Group A (interacting with human peers first, followed by GAI chatbots) or Group B (interacting in the reverse order) for two one-hour writing activities. We conducted two one-on-one interview sessions in between the two writing activities. Each participant selected a specific GAI chatbot (e.g., ChatGPT 5, Gemini, Copilot) to work with during their writings based on their preference and accessibility. In both writing sessions, participants were given just one prompt. We intentionally designed this prompt to elicit open-ended reflection by asking participants to revisit a meaningful educational experience and connect it to their imagined futures. As such, this reflective task provided learners with interactive opportunities to explore the presence and tension of relational ties with peers/GA I chatbot.

We collected five main data sources, i.e., participants’ pre-and post-interview transcripts, reflective writing samples, GAI chatbot interactional logs, peer interactional videos, and researchers’ fieldnotes. We used interpretive qualitative analysis following three coding processes (Emerson, 2011). First, we open coded the transcripts line-by-line and identify any recurring phenomena, e.g., relational patterns (e.g., “I want your help, but not that much”). In the focused coding, we fine-grained the topical codes that aligned with our research question, e.g., “controlled collaboration” and “respectful reciprocity.” The researchers consistently documented their integrative memos about the observed phenomena to clarify and link analytic categories (p. 171). 2910

## Preliminary findings and conclusion

We selected Dana as our critical case, as she provides a rich vignette that demonstrates the intricate relational ties she formed with both modalities (Yin, 2015). Our preliminary findings showed a salient contrast between the hierarchical, instrumental relationship between Dana and GAI chatbot, and the socio-emotional and equal relationships between peer to peer.

In working with GAI chatbot (i.e., ChatGPT 5), we found Dana formed an asymmetrical, task-oriented relationship with the chatbot. This relationship centers on Dana’s maximized control over the chatbot, prompting it given the task efficiency and text output. Dana explicitly views ChatGPT as a “writing/research assistant,” which positions herself as a goal-oriented “boss” to emphasize the necessity of giving “ really clear direction to the (ChatGPT) I’m working with/working for me … so that (ChatGPT) got the guidelines/direction can actually do a better job.” This guiding strategy and evaluating GAI chatbot’s output through precise prompting treats the chatbot as a controllable resource serving her writing objectives. Also, it’s interesting to note that Dana used a strict timing control strategy. She consistently claimed “ I didn’t want it (ChatGPT) to intervene too early ” to disrupt the flow of her thoughts until she completed her “ rough draft,” with confirmation of the writing “ingredients.” Only after her personal ideas were organized did she submit the draft to ChatGPT for the next stage (e.g., grammatical check and/or reviewing for suggestions). This action showcased that she prioritizes authorship agency over tool functional optimization. Dana’s relational agency manifests in refusing to be drowned by the voice of ChatGPT. When ChatGPT’s revisions “ changed too much, exceeding my  (Dana’s) expectations,” she went back and redirected ChatGPT’s output. In interactional logs, she identified and rejected vocabulary she believed were “exaggerated” or “too professional” (e.g., “aspiring” or “profoundly”), since these “don’t represent what I (Dana) was going to say, I wanted to say, then I will just ditch it (ChatGPT’s output).” In viewing this, we found a strong agency to preserve her own voice and thoughts in the writing. In contrast to GAI chatbot, the relationship between Dana and her peer (Lucy) are characterized by equal relationship, reciprocity, and a strong emphasis on maintaining socio-emotional connections. First, unlike the controlled approach of “finishing rough draft on my own, then consulting with GAI chatbot,” Dana initiated the adopted her opinion. Lucy also shared her thoughts on choosing the statistics  class experience with Dana’s encouragement. This joint discussion of the writing’s direction established equal participation for both of them. Then, in completing their writing and exchanging their texts, Dana and Lucy noticed a salient difference in thei r writing styles. Dana’s style was more formal, while Lucy adopted an informal, casual tone. This difference became an important co-reflective opportunity to explicitly explore the spectrum of expressive possibilities in reflective writing. Meanwhile, Lucy followed up on more about Dana’s teaching experience and Dana asked for Lucy’s statistics course experiences. Lucy immediately shared specific course recommendations and information on course difficulty levels. This interaction showcased the mutual sharing of lived experiences. We found this balanced and non-hierarchical support was in much contrast to Dana’s hierarchical, one-way directives to ChatGPT.

Also, interestingly, we found a shift from “tool function optimization” to “emotional connection.” While reading Lucy’s work, Dana expressed empathy, saying she could “ picture myself in her (Lucy’s) shoes” and praising her writing as “so alive” and full of emotion. Moreover, though Dana noted both of their writings might contain “grammatical errors or areas for improvement,” they jointly decided “ not to modify anything.” She explained this in the post-interview, saying she “didn’t want to touch her work,” and viewed Lucy’s as “completed personal work.” Dana defined her role here as “respectful or appreciative audience.” However, Dana also expressed hesitance in asking during the writing process, concerning her thoughts or moves could interrupt Lucy’s writing or invade her peer’s privacy, “I don’t know if she (Lucy) is willing to share personal information.” She even asked Lucy if she was willing to exchange essays, ensuring the content was “ good to go, nothing to hide. ” When interacting with others, Dana noted many factors to consider (e.g., “ think about when to ask ” and that “I can’t do whatever I want to do there.”). This reflection echoes what Edwards (2007) argued the situational constraints that social relations place on individual agency, contrasting to when faced with the GAI chatbot with less emotion. Dana could freely provide instructions without worrying a bout emotional or social risks, which displayed a high degree of social caution in interpersonal interactions. This caution reflects the inherent constraints on individual agency imposed by the meshes of mutual responsibility (Edwards, 2007). Our initial findings contribute to Edwards’ (2005) relational agency framework by expanding the concept of “others’ expertise” from human to impersonal GAI chatbots with anthropomorphic behaviors (Salles et al., 2020). Dana emphasizes the “more room to bother ” nature of GAI chatbots, which can be interpreted as GAI chatbots allow them to avoid social risks and engage in emotional labor (Morton, 2025), while equipping learners with the optimized functionalities of GAI Chatbots.
