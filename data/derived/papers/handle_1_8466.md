# Applying Deweyan Perspective of Inquiry to Teaching Experimentation Using Simulation

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We investigated whether and how learning of experimental inquiry can be facilitated with computer simulations. As a theoretical framework, we built upon Dewey’s concept of the continuum of inquiry and identified three types of requisite knowledge that provide support for, and evolve with, experimental inquiry. Preliminary findings from two teachers’ classrooms indicate that teacher framing styles identified in the classroom discourse may be a factor in explaining contrasting student experimentation behaviors.

## Introduction

The current vision for science education calls for engaging students in science practices so that they can learn how to do science, not just learn the end products of science, thereby being able to understand how scientific knowledge is developed (National Research Council [NRC], 2012). The pivotal role that experimentation has played for the development of scientific knowledge, especially in physical sciences, is well described in the philoso phy of science (Hempel, 1966). Hence, an important aspect of physical science learning has to do with experim ental inquiry, which is carried out by asking an investig ative question, making hypotheses, collecting, and analyzing data, formulating explanations and conclusions based on evidence, and communicating findings (NRC, 1996). As the emphasis on learning scientific content through science practices has grown in recent years (NRC, 2012), the need for reconceptualizing the practice of planning and carrying out investigations has increased (Manz, Lehrer, & Schauble, 2020). We found Dewey’s concept  (Dewey, 1938) of the continuum of inquiry  useful in building a theoretical foundation for experimental inquiry. Inquiry process seeks to obtain a warrantable assertion (knowledge objective) while building on and making use of knowledge resources available. Inquiry is dynamic not just in the trivial sense that the knowledge ob jective is formulated over time but also in the non trivial sense that knowledge resources go through concomitant re-examinations and temporal modifications. In turn, t he knowledge attained at the conclusion of the inquiry can act as a resource for a new inquiry and will go through its own dynamic update. In this continuum of inquiry, no knowledge, even that of logic, can be absolutely taken for granted; there is only the self-corrective process of inquiry whose conclusions approximate the truth in the long run. Applied to students’ experimental inquiry, this d ynamic nature of inquiry requires students to adapt  their conceptual and practical resources by constantly re sponding tonew experiences. The resources include,  as we discuss below, three types of knowledge: what students understand about the phenomenon under study (science content), what students know about how to conduct scientif ic experiments (method, logic of inquiry), and how students interact with the external world entities (materials) such as the physical setups with which to create a scientific phenomenon, measurement tools, and software to collect, simulate, or analyze data. Scientific experimentation can be used to confirm an existing theory in the form of hypothesis testing or to discover patterns in the data while a theory to explain the patterns is yet fully developed (Hacking, 1982). In both cases, especially for the latter, students not  only need to reason between their knowledge space and the empirically obtained data space (Klahr & Dunbar, 19 86) but also must productively interact with materi als to obtain the data so that they can make warrantable conclusions based on the evidence (Miller et al., 2018). In this study, we focus on scientific experimentati on of a physical system based on a computer simulation. Part of interest in employing simulation-based experimentation is to take some cognitive load off from physical experimentation. Especially in a remote-learning situation, computer simulations can be valuable for the purpose of enacting experimental inquiry. Simulations are “dynamic computer models that allow users to explore the implications of manipulating or modifying parameters within them” (Honey & Hilton, 2011, p. 2). Simulations are theory-based models and capture some but not all aspects of reality being experimented. Thus, simulations act as materials through which students can gain access  to a theory-laden version of the real physical wor ld. Using the classroom videos and computer log data of two teachers’ classrooms, we investigated two research questions:

(1) how the teachers framed simulation-based scientific experimentation and (2) how students’ experimentation

patterns were impacted by the teachers’ framing styles.

## Theoretical framework: three legs of an experimental inquiry

According to Dewey (1938), the outcome of a compete nt inquiry is a w arranted assertion, i.e., a piece of knowledge. In experimental inquiry, a common form of knowledge to be created would be a data-based ans wer ICLS2022 Proceedings 1281 © ISLS to the question “Does the quantity Y depend on the quantity X, and, if so, how?” We assert that three legs of requisites as shown in Figure 1 are necessary for t he completion of a competent experimental inquiry. In describing these three requisites of experimental inquiry, we shall use an example question, “Does the acceleration of the car on a ramp depend on the mass of the car?” Figure 1 Three Requisites Essential to Support a Successful Experimental Inquiry

1. Content: The experimenter’s scientific content knowledge o f the phenomenon under investigation is a

necessary element. Adequate understanding of the main science concepts and their relations is necessary not only for motivating, designing, and understanding a n experiment, but also for dynamically steering the experimental inquiry in the productive direction when faced with unexpected outcomes along the way.  For the above example question, the experimenter’s disciplinary content knowledge must cover the kinematics of the motion (position, velocity, acceleration) and the variables of the setup that affect the motion (mass, friction, gravity, and ramp geometry).

2. Method: The experimenter should know how to carry out exp eriments towards a clearly formulated result

without involving confounding variables. This required knowledge is not a type of understanding that applies to a particular experiment or a particular science domain but is a formal and logical understanding of the practice of experimentation typically acquired through experiences with epistemic reflections. Namely, it is understanding of scientifically valid experimental strategies to achieve the most unambiguous outcome that can identify or establish causality. For the above example question, the target strategy is control of variables (COV) (Chen & Klahr, 1999; Kuhn, 2000; Schwichow et  al., 2016), where all variables (such as friction, ramp angle) are fixed other than one independent variable (mass). COV is an ideal strategy, if available, for deducing the relationship between the dependent var iable and the independent variable. While outside t he scope of this paper, statistical understanding of data also belongs in this category.

3. Materials: This category concerns interacting with the exter nal world. The experimenter should be able to

competently interact with equipment, specimen, software, and the general physical setting of the experiment to produce quality data with confidence. In the simulation-based experimentation, simulation itself becomes the material setup. Different from the method, this  falls into the category of an actual doing of a specific experiment under a specific circumstance and concerns the knowledge necessary to recognize not just ho w the materials operate and how they can be controlled but also how the phenomenon under study is represented in the material setup and how measurements should be drawn from that setup. While the three legs of support are formulated as r equisite resources for an experimental inquiry, their characteristics are also dynamic, a la Dewey (Dewey, 1938); they are also a result of the inquiry in the sense that they change or grow with the inquiry process. They need not, and cannot, be in a perfect state when th e inquiry begins. The experimenter’s knowledge of phenomenon, method, and materials needs to be only sufficiently settled for the inquiry to begin. In Dewey’s words (1938), “In scientific inquiry, the criterion of what is taken to be settled, or to be knowledge, is being so settled that it is available as a resource in further inquiry; not being settled in such a way as not to be subject to revision in further i nquiry” (p. 8-9). This view of inquiry and knowledg e is summarized by what Dewey terms as the continuum of inquiry: “It is the convergent and cumulative effect of continued inquiry that defines knowledge in its general meaning” (p. 8). Therefore, any one inquiry process must be viewed as an open process spawning further inquiries, not a process closing upon conclusion. All involved knowledge evolves through inquiry. In the symbolism of Figure 1, the inquiry process will transform the  whole table. It will transform not only the tabletop, representing the inquiry activity that occurs to produce the knowledge objective (“Y vs. X”), but also the legs, the three knowledge requisites of the inquiry. In a classroom setting where ICLS2022 Proceedings 1282 © ISLS the experimenter is a student or a group of student s, which legs of knowledge will have progressed mos t prominently through the inquiry may be influenced by how the lesson is structured by the teacher. The analytical distinction among three types of requisite knowledge is valuable, but we realize that they cannot be cleanly separated in any given situation. Dewey noted (1938), without “the sensitivity to the quality of a situation as a whole” (p. 70; emphasis by us), an experimenter cannot avoid “two evils” (p. 70): (1) mindlessly collected data leading to nowhere and (2) prejudiced data collection forced into the predetermined conceptual and theoretical scheme. Thus, it is critical that an experimenter be situated in a holistic mixture of all three requisites. In sum, the continuum of inquiry perspective characterizes inquiry to be open, dynamic, and holistic.

## Learning context and methods

This study examined a simulation-based experimentation activity embedded in a four-week, technology-enhanced physics module for high school students. Two participating teachers, Teacher S and Teacher J, taught the physics course to 17 and 27, respectively, ninth grade students in the Northeastern part of the US. Teacher S had 20+ years of teaching experience and held a Ph. D. in microbiology while Teacher J had 10+ years of teaching experience in physics. Teacher S’s school was in the moderate to high socio-economic neighborhood while Teacher J’s school was in the moderate socio-economic neighborhood. We collected and analyzed videos of the whole class as well as students working in small groups. Among the stud ents, 48% identified as male and 48% as female; 22%  had minority backgrounds; 99% spoke English as their fi rst language; 68% used computers regularly for scie nce learning prior to the module.  The module addresses physics concepts such as displ acement, velocity, and acceleration related to a car’s motion on a ramp. S tudents carry out the sensor-based physical experim entation with a toy car on a ramp and then simulation-based experimentation. The ramp simulation allows students to vary five independent variables such as car mass, ramp a ngle, distance up the ramp, gravity, and friction. Students investigate how the acceleration of the car depends on one of the independent variables (of their choice). ## Results and discussions

Table 1 summarizes the major themes emerged from th e video analysis. We characterize Teacher S as the supporter of individual experimental inquiry and Te acher J as the orchestrator of the whole class expe rimental inquiry. Teacher J emphasized the conceptual modeli ng (i.e., content knowledge) of the ramp system whi le Teacher S let students explore and experiment freel y. Teacher S used content as a resource for carryin g out experiments and making measurements and did not go over the content as forcefully or intentionally as Teacher J. While Teacher S’s goal for experimentation was to explore the system under study and carry out investigation to collect data that was worthwhile, Teacher J’s go al was to create community knowledge based on the contribution from each group. Nonetheless, the affordances of the simulation (e.g., adjustable gravity or perfectly reproducible data) were emphasized by both teachers  as the simulation became the main tool of experime ntal inquiry. Teacher S had a balanced emphasis on all t hree requisites of content, method, and materials, while Teacher J over-emphasized the content requisite. Students in Teacher S’s class were given ample time to  get to know the materials with a holistic goal of carrying  out the experiment to get “really good data eventu ally.”  In ICLS2022 Proceedings 1283 © ISLS contrast, students in Teacher J’s class were urged to experiment only one single independent variable with the simulation so that each student group could contrib ute to the class wide discussion of the physics con tent. Surprisingly, in Teacher J’s class, nearly half (48 %) of the log data sessions related to simulation usages showed no COV enactment (showing chaotic exploration of the independent variables) despite the fact that the COV was forcibly directed at students. In Teacher S’s class, only 30 % showed such a behavior. We postulate that playing as a way of familiarizing with the material such as  simulation naturally happens whether the teacher e xplicitly allows it or not. We further postulate that familia rizing material as a tool for inquiry was critical for students in Teach S’s class to make transition from pure play f or exploring the tool to productive experimentation  for collecting serious data. Indeed, in Teacher S’s class, more than half (52 %) of log data sessions showed evidence for “rich” COV enactment: COV behaviors mixed with other variable exploration or showing consecutive COVs with different independent variables. In contrast, only 17 % showed the rich COV in Teacher J’s class.   The remaining data showed a “strictly regimented” COV behavior (pure single-variable COV with no play), 35 % for Teacher J and 17 % for Teacher S.

While a potential connection between teacher framing and student experimentation patterns exists, it was not possible to tell whether there was any causal relationship between the two due to a small sample size and other student, teacher, and classroom factors that were n ot considered in this study. What seems to be clear, though, from the Deweyan perspective of inquiry is that Teacher S’s framing was more conducive to student learning of an experimental inquiry as a dynamic whole supported by the balance among the three knowledge requisites. On the other hand, Teacher J’s framing might have deva lued students’ engagement with the method and mater ials legs of inquiry, which diminished their chance to, in Dewey’s sense, sensitively and productively interact with the inquiry situation as a whole.

Our results show that it is possible to use computer simulation in a productiv e way to teach important aspects of the experimentation practice. Simulation s can be more effective in teaching the experimenta tion practice such as COV than physical setups thanks to their affordances such as the absence of noise/imperfection in physical data. However, simulations’ affordances are also their weaknesses (lack of fidelity to the phenomenon under investigation), thus requiring teachers and curriculum developers to prudently mix physical experimentation with simulation experimentation to maximize student learning. Lastly, this paper focused on experimental inquiry, but the theory outlined in this paper may be applic able to other types of inquiry such as observational inquiry if each requisite knowledge is recharacterized accordingly.
