# Analyzing Teacher-AI Interaction Patterns in Student-Centered Lesson Design

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates the interaction patterns of elementary school teachers utilizing generative AI for student-centered lesson design. By analyzing teachers with varying levels of teaching experience and AI proficiency, this qualitative study employs CORDTRA diagrams to examine teacher-AI interactions. The findings reveal seven distinct interaction patterns, with distinctive differences based on teachers’ experience and AI proficiency. These results highlight the need for differentiated AI training and tool development that accommodate teachers’ varying levels of expertise and interaction styles.

## Introduction

With recent advancements in AI rapidly transforming education (Baskara, 2023; Whalen & Mouza, 2023), various generative AI tools have impacted education by integrating new technologies and teaching methods into curricula (Trust et al., 2023). Numerous studies thus explore its use in lesson planning and educational applications (Davis & Lee, 2023). However, many of these studies lack m ethodological rigor and leave a gap in understandin g how teachers interact with AI for co-designing lessons. Moreover, the li terature demonstrates that teachers face challenges in designing and implementing student-centered lessons and instruction (Weimer, 2002), wh ich highlights the need for more research to guide teac hers’ pedagogical practices. From a social construc tivist perspective, lesson planning is not merely an indiv idual cognitive process but a socially situated  process that evolves through interaction with others  (Vygotsky, 1978). Similarly, distributed cognition theory suggests that teachers’ cognitive processes are not solely internal but occur within a distributed cognitive system (Hollan et al., 2000; Salomon, 1997). Based on these theoretical perspectives, this study investigates how teachers engage with AI as part of a broader sociocognitive system in lesson planning by focusing on how teachers' teaching experience and AI proficiency influence their interaction patterns with AI during lesson design. Using CORDTRA diagrams (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2008), the study analyzes the AI int eraction behaviors of elementary school teachers, providing insights into tailored teacher support strategies for optimizing AI-assisted lesson design.

## Methods

Ten elementary school teachers with prior experienc e using generative AI participated in the study. Ba sed on Huberman’s (1992) teacher development model, participants were classified into low-experience teachers (≤3 years) and high-experience teachers (≥7 years). Their AI proficiency was also categorized as high or low based on average scores by using Celik’s Intelligent-TPACK survey (2023). Combining these two factors, the teachers were divided into four distinc t groups. This study employed a two-phase qualitative case study approach. In the first phase, teachers attended an orientation on AI  usage and student-centered lesson design principles before individually creating a lesson plan using ChatGPT in a 60-minute session, during which the think-aloud protocol was employed. They designed a one-session lesson on modern communication methods for a third-grade social studies class. In the second phase, in-depth interviews were conducted to explore teachers ’ experiences, challenges, and perceptions regarding AI-assisted lesson planning. Data were analyzed using Braun & Clarke’s

(2006) thematic analysis, applying both deductive a nd inductive coding, which resulted in nine key cod es.

Additionally, CORDTRA diagrams were used to vis ualize teacher-AI interaction patterns in real time  (HmeloSilver et al., 2008). The unit of analysis was defined as a “cord box,”  representing a single cycle from the initial AI prompt to the final lesson-planning action. To ensure reliability, two primary  coders conducted the analysis, with independent reviewers cross-checking CORDTRA diagrams against the original reco rdings. To ensure coding consistency and accuracy, a continuous discu ssion process was maintained until 100% inter-rater reliability was achieved.

## Results

Teacher-AI interaction patterns From the analysis of 80 cord boxes, seven frequently occurring interaction patterns between teachers and AI were identified. These patterns reflect distinct sequences of teacher behaviors and AI responses during lesson planning. The patterns included: (1) Initial Prompting → Accepting AI Response → Lesson Planning, (2) Initial Prompting → Accepting AI Response → AI Elaborating → Lesson Planning, (3) Initial Prompting → Rejecting AI Response → Lesson Planning, (4) Initial Prompting → Rejecting AI Response → AI Re-prompting → Accepting AI Response → Lesson Planning, (5) Initial Prompting → Follow-up Prompting → Accepting AI Response → Lesson Planning, (6) Complex Interactions, where bo th response acceptance and rejection occurred simultaneously within a single  pattern, and (7) Initial Prompting → Lesson Planning without Incorpora ting AI Response.

Differences in patterns by teachers’ experiences and AI skills Teachers with high teaching experience and high AI proficiency showed a high frequency of interaction with AI, as indicated by the number of cord boxes (9, 12, and 7). Pattern (2) (33%) was most frequently used, r eflecting their ability to critically evalu ate AI responses and revise them to suit the specif ic educational context. These teachers employed a wide range of prompting strateg ies, including re-prompting and follow-up prompting, adjusting their interactions flexibly based on situational needs. Modifications considering students and classroom context were notably observed at time intervals suc h as 16, 32–33, 35, 45, and 56 –57 minutes (Fig 1). Teachers with high teaching experience but low AI proficiency had fewer cord boxes (3 and 6), and re-prompting or followup prompting was rarely used. When AI responses were unsatisfactory, they relied on their professional expertise or textbooks to revise content themselves. Rather than passively accepting AI outputs, these teachers often made instructional decisions by considering students and classroom context. Teachers with low teaching experience but high AI proficiency also had a relatively small number of cord boxes (5 and 6), but mainly used response-acceptance patterns such as Pattern (2), (5), and 1. While they actively modified and elaborate d on AI responses and incorporated them into their lesson plans, they  rarely considered individual student characteristi cs or instructional context, instead relying primarily on  AI-generated content. Teachers with low teaching experience and low AI proficiency demonstrated a high number o f interactions (10, 8, and 10 cord boxes), but Patt ern (1) (direct use of initial AI response) was most frequently observed. There was minimal evidence of re-prompting or refinement, suggesting a tendency to accept AI outp uts as-is. Although these teachers were eager to engage wi th AI, they showed limited critical evaluation or contextual adaptation in their lesson planning. ## Discussion

This study examined how elementary school teachers interact with generative AI, specifically ChatGPT, during the process of student-centered lesson design. Using the CORDTRA diagram, the study identified seven distinct interaction patterns. Among the teachers categorized into four groups based on their  teaching experience and AI proficiency, h ighly experienced teachers with high AI proficiency  engaged in strategic interactions, critically adapting AI responses based on pedagogical expertise (Hogan et al., 2003). In contrast, low-experienced teachers with low AI proficiency tended to accept AI-generated responses passively, applying them with li ttle or no modification. This suggests that AI may risk functi oning merely as a static content provider in such c ontexts. These findings indicate that effective AI integration in education requires more than technical access; it demands pedagogical design expertise and informed instructional judgment (Karaman, 2024). AI should be positioned not as a replacement for teacher autonomy and expertise, but as a tool that complements and extends them. To achieve this, targeted AI literacy training, hands-on workshops, and collaborative learning environmen ts among teachers are essential (Ellingson & Roehrig, 2024). Theoretically, this study supports distributed cognition theory (Hollan et al., 2000), demonstrating that AI functions as an extension of the teacher’s cognitive processes. Moreover, from the perspective of social interaction theory (Vygotsky, 1978), teacher-AI interactions are not merely instrumental but represent a socially situated process of instru ctional design. Therefore, AI should be viewed as a partner that supports and extends teachers’ thinking and decision-making, not as a replacement for their professional expertise.
