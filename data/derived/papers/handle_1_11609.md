## Introduction

When students pose questions in college science lectures, the most common action by the instructors is to answer them. Sometimes, though, instructor s reflect questions back to the class, leading to open discussion s that might last several minutes without resolution. That is, t he instructor may see question s as opportunities for students to engage with uncertainty  — considering multiple perspectives, exploring evidence and reaso ning, perhaps engaging in argumentation. We refer to these discus sions as “public debates,” public in the sense of taking place in plenary for the course as a whole, and “debates” in the loose sense of considering multiple perspectives. Research on learning has argued mainly in epistemic terms fo r the value of public debates in science classes, including to contend they support students’ engagem ent with uncertainty (Driver et al., 2000; Engle & Conant, 2002; Ford & Forman, 2006; Ryu & Sandoval, 2012). Some work has begun to consider social aspects, along with epistemic, and analyses of debates have shown entangled social and epistemic dynamics (Appleby et al., 2021; Krist, 2020; Watkins et al., 2018). Broadly within school contexts, students experience uncertainty as a liability, both pedagogically and socially: They risk losing points in the course and losing face with classmates. In an ongoing study, we are as king: (i) what social dynamics do students experience  during public debates in science classes, and (ii) how do these social dynamics support or hinder their engagement with uncertainty? Here we report on early findings from interviews of students from an upper-level college course in biochemistry. Evidence supports accounts of entangled social and epistemic dynamics and highlights positive and negative impacts of social dynamics on students’ engagement with uncertainty.

## Study context

The interviewees were students in the third author’ s course, Biochemistry II with 60 students enrolled, most of whom were majoring in the field and/or intending to  continue to medical or dental education after coll ege. The instructor had recently redesigned the course to center uncertainty, including in class discussions, choosing in some moments to open a question to the students for  public debate, rather than providing an answer her self. Throughout the course, she described how uncertainty is a central feature of science and medicine. In the semester after they completed the course, th e second author recruited students to talk about their experiences with uncertainty. 15 students consented and participated in semi-structured interviews. 14 interviews included a video clip of a public debate that started from a student’s question:  Why is eating a lot of carbohydrates perceived as bad, when they had learned carbohydrates are essential for multiple chemical pathways (i.e., ami no acid synthesis) in the body? The instructor saw this as a generative question for everyone to consider, with multiple possibilities related to matters they had discussed  as well as to everyday experience, and so she chos e to reflect the question back to the class for public debate. The clip showed about a minute of a lively discussion and ended with a commotion of students talking with each other. Here we focus on the students’ reactions to the clip. We transcribed the interviews and analyzed data through methods of grounded theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967), with an interest in students’ perspectives o n epistemic, social, and emotional aspects of their  experiences with uncertainty. The first author watched the inte rview recordings, identified relevant data in the transcript, and discussed the interpretations with the other authors (Engle et al., 2014).

## Results

13 of 14 students commented on  social dynamics after watching the debate  clip, highlighting ways social dynamics either supported or hindered their engagement with uncertainty. Students who mentioned multiple types of social dynamics were counted more than once in Tables 1 and 2. Social dynamics that supported students’ engagement with uncertainty Seven students explained that the public debate allowed them to feel a shared sense of uncertainty, as summarized in Table 1. Five of them explained that it validated their own questions, while the other two took up the question as interesting to explore. Like the student in the video, they wondered why common wisdom is to limit intake of carbohydrates, given their essential roles in metabolism. Three students  identified the value of receiving feedback from other students about their line s of reasoning: When they contributed to the discussion, they were still unsure about their answers. They wanted to “take a shot at it” and see how others would respond to their ideas. One of the three expressed appreciation for how students openly voiced their disagreements with one another, because doing so meant that students took each other’s ideas seriously.

Two students spoke more generally about the value o f the collective experience of not-knowing. One explained how the  debate showed that everyone was at a similar place of “put(ting) all this stuff together” and “reconciling our uncertainty by asking questions.” The debate helped normalize experiences with uncertainty. ## Discussion

Prior works have discussed the value of public debates in epistemic terms: Students learn to consider multiple perspectives and construct arguments (Driver et al., 2000; Engle & Conant, 2002; Ford & Forman, 2006; Ryu & Sandoval, 2012). Some recent works have considered the value of debates also in social terms: Students’ trust and care for one another leads them to listen to each other’s thinking and vice versa (Appleby et al., 2021; Krist, 2020). Further, students taking a public position of not-knowing helps make it socially acceptable (Watkins et al., 2018). The present study contributes data from interviews of college students. It suggests that public debate s can present both helpful and challenging social dyn amics for students as they learn to engage with unc ertainty. This nuance s the overarching idea in the literature that public debates are generally productive for students’ engagement with uncertainty. How students come to engage with uncertainty in public debates, in which various social dynamics are present, is a complex, context-sensitive phenomenon. Some students for whom  social dynamics hindered their public engagement with uncertainty  were not only afraid of other’s opinions  but also overwhelmed with the information about the topic (i.e., amino acid synthesis). These students faced risks both at the social and pedagogical level: they felt at risk for losing face by their peers and for not understanding the content that they felt they were expected to understand. We wonder what would have helped them to feel less at risk. Perhap s they would have felt less overwhelmed and tried t o engage with the debate, if the instructor emphasized not only the value of not knowing what is unknown in the fie ld but also the personal value for a learner of not knowing established ideas. Future research can investigate ways that these students can be supported in their learning to engage with uncertainty.
