# Walking Urban Green Spaces: Secondary Students’ Descriptions of Returning to Embodied Relations with Nature

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While interactions in nature and urban green spaces have been studied for their benefits to developing, wellbeing, and quality of life, this paper draws on research with secondary students engaged in a walking curriculum to examine how they describe what they are learning through walking, as well as their relations with nature. We utilized central concepts from cultural-historical approaches to the learning sciences —including learning on the move and learning as movement—along with literature from critical geographies to focus our analysis on surfacing embodied relations with nature. We address three themes in this paper: walking as a return to the senses, walking as a return to the body, and walking as a return to relationality. Walking led to learning that was embodied, organic, and emotional, linking curriculum, students’ lived experiences and their values, with real and imagined embodied and emplaced social futures.

Historically, interactions with nature and natural spaces have been seen to support healthy development and quality of life from early childhood through adulthood, even as that interaction has become problematized (e.g., Ingold, 1994).  Current systematic reviews have shown the significance of interactions with nature across the lifespan (Callaghan et al., 2021). More specifically, systematic reviews have shown the importance of interactions with nature in research focused on young people, for example, establishing the important role of space for: 1) youth development and health outcomes with children aged 2 to 18 years through longitudinal and experimental studies (Sprague et al., 2022) and 2) preventing anxiety and depression in youth aged 14 to 24 years through interdisciplinary studies (Reece et al., 2021). In addition, although some have argued that humans have an innate affinity for nature (Wilson, 1986), others have noted the extent to which nature-human relations are reduced, disrupted, and threatened given new technological relations (Kahn et al., 2009). Currently, research on the importance of access to and engagement in green spaces focuses especially on children in elementary schools, for example, green schoolyards have been found to be particularly significant for the development of children in low inc omes areas (e.g., Bates et al., 2018). Other studies have examined approaches and methods for enhancing opportunities for access to green  spaces (e.g., Tallis et al., 2018). This finding is similar internationally as well (e.g., Myung et al., 2025). The significance of green spaces for middle grade and secondary school students has also been studied, most  commonly with an emphasis on standard quantitative and qualitative methods of research—including self-reports and surveys—to generate data related to wellbeing and quality of life (e.g., Ekenga et al., 2019). Alongside the literature on the importance of urban green spaces for wellbeing, mental health, and quality of life, there exists less literature focusing on the experiences of students  in nature and urban green spaces (however, see, e.g., Kumpulainen et al., 2023). Our interest is on learning with and from secondary students what their experience walking in nature is like, including what is learned from these experiences, through embodied approaches to research, such as walking methodologies and, in this case, a walking curriculum. The purpose of this study was to examine more deeply the learning opportunities created with secondary students engaged in a walking curriculum. For this research, we asked:

1) How do secondary students describe what they have learned through a walking curriculum?

2) How do secondary students describe their relations with nature?

Walking alongside the teacher and students, and analyzing journals and interviews, the first author gathered evidence of what they learned and the kinds of relations with nature that they described.

## Literature review

This research is grounded in cultural-historical approaches to learning on the move  (Marin et al., 2020) and learning as movement  (Gutiérrez, 2020). As an approach to learning, learning on the move  provides a lens for accessing the co-construction of knowledge through engagement in social practices, such as walking with family 1882 in the forest (Marin & Bang, 2018), and riding bikes through the city (Marin et al., 2020). It traces  pathways through places attending to the past, present, and future of relations with what exists in the natural and built environment, including routes and signs, as well as land, water, and ecologies. Learning as movement  helps to unsettle what has historically been taken for granted in learning, teaching, and educational spaces, including what counts as learning and where we see learning occurring in orde r to shift perspectives and, potentially, craft new relations and spatial configurations for learning and teaching. In addition, the integration of these two approaches is elaborated through perspectives emerging from critical geographies (e.g., Soja, 1996; Vadeboncoeur, Monestel Mora, & Brooke, 2025). Soja (1996) explicitly noted that human relations with nature were included in an “expanding roster of struggles” (p. 12), and recognized the ways in which Marx’s critique of capitalism—with the binaries of capital and labour, for example —may lead to an “estrangement of the critique from nature, the soil, the earth, the production of space” (p. 48). Drawing on feminist scholars, including bell hooks and Gillian Rose, Soja (1996) wrote of reimagining “new sites and spaces of resistance from which to redesign cityspace,” while also highlighting that “the center of attentio n has shifted back to urbanism, to everyday life within the built (socially produced) environment of the city,” and away from nature (p. 108). Lamenting the overemphasis of time over space, history over geography, he concluded the first part of his book wi th the statement: “a simple exercise is helpful: whenever you read or write a sentence that empowers history, historicality, or the historical narrative, substitute space, spa tiality, or geography and think about the consequences” (p. 183). We weave an iteration of these perspectives together to contextualize the use of walking curriculum and walking methodologies in this study (Vadeboncoeur et al., 2021).

## Research design

In the lower mainland of British Columbia, one teacher and 19 students were invited to participate in an exploratory study of walking curriculum through a Grade 11 course on environmental science. The teacher created a walking curriculum around five learning opportunities to move through urban green spaces within 5-10 minutes on foot from the school. The walks were loosely structured around topics: Mind Up, Place, Neighbours, Plastic, and No Impact. General instructions were offered, including encouragement to “notice” the environment, consider the course content (including topics like mutualism, competition, and commensalism), and write freely about their experiences. While the teacher sometimes encouraged links to content, the students were encouraged use their journal to explore, take notes, and draw, as well as develop more complete ideas and responses. The journals were graded as completed or not, rather than with a letter grade. The intention of the teacher was to provide the students with the experience of walking in nature, and journaling about their experience, without confining them to typical academic expectations.

The central organizing concept for the logic-of-inquiry was the notion of a shared horizon (Lee & Ingold,

2006) and the way in which a shared  view and purpose may enable diverse participants to, in the moment of

participation, become a shared community with a shared  goal. The first author utilized aspects of walking methodologies (e.g., Springgay & Truman, 2022) to walk alongside the teacher and students, observe and gather field notes, and take photos. Interviews with the teacher and students were also com pleted. Along with the observations and field notes of the walks, and the journals written, semi-structured artifact mediated interviews were conducted with the teacher and students mediating between what was observed on the walks and what was written in the journals. Thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2019) was  utilized for written observations, field notes, journals and interviews. This paper draws on themes that emerged from both the journals and the interviews. It includes photographs of the walks, but the photos have not yet been analyzed. Emerging themes Three main overlapping and interrelated themes are the focus of this paper: walking as a return to the senses, walking as a return to the body, and walking as a return to relationality. We began thinking about these themes loosely as opportunities to respond to challenges posed by the denial of the body, the focus on instrumental rationality, and the separation of nature and culture, and as an opportunity to reimagine human-nature relations as self-other relations.

Theme 1: Walking as a return to the senses This theme emerged as students took note of the nature around them and acknowledged with each of their senses even the smallest natural elements in their environment. Through their senses, and through the recognition of continuous change, students highlighted how they experienced nature. Students wrote and spoke about engaging with nature visually as: “big trees overlapping the sky,” “sunlight filtering,” and a “winding walking path.” They noted audible sounds, such as “dogs barking”  and “birds singing.” They also noted connections between visual and audible sense-making through “leaves falling” and “the sounds of the ripples on the stream.” They felt the 1883 water and the wind and noted these sensations, along with the chill and the “wet air.” Nature was about “being outside,” “camping with my dad,” “going for hikes,” and “exploring,” highlighting the importance of doing with, engaging with, and taking action in and through nature. Nature was also a place of “taking solace” and “being at peace,” “a relaxing place.” Students’ sensory descriptions highlighted movement and change, including changing perspectives. In general, students wrote about changing temperatures, season s, and conditions, and emphasized how conditions change in relation to time of day, wind, and even what they heard and smelled. They wrote and spoke about nature “in flux,” as “a sea of change.” At the same time, they wrote about humans as being “slow to change,” stuck in “unsustainable" patterns of behaviour, routine oriented, and mechanical. Walking in urban green spaces enabled the students to return to their senses, and through their senses, return to nature  (see Urban Green Space, Image (a)). Theme 2: Walking as a return to the body This theme emerged as students noted what they were feeling, how the walking curriculum surfaced a deep appreciation for nature  through the lenses provided by the experiential curriculum, alongside a deep worry for nature, given climate change. T he coupled experience of appreciation/worry was something they carried with them when they returned to the school and throughout their day, a unified, embodied experience of emotion and cognition. A variety of feelings and emotions emerged as space was created for new emotional configurations, or relationships constructed “between feeling, sense making, and practice” (Vea, 2020, p. 313), in this case, the social practice of walking together in urban green spaces. Here, emotion emerged and became meaningful as entangled with sense-making in a social practice (Vea, 2020). Emotional configurations emerged in students’ journals from their experience walking in the following ways: “I think a consistent fear is about how much [human actions] might not change” (FJ, T1); “healthy environment equals happy earth”; and notes about hope, including “hopes for the future: that its better,” and “hopes for learning less well known facts about how to help the environment and how we can make it  a better place for future generations” (see also, Chawla, 2020). Students also noted experiences of being and feeling one with nature, of being connected to and through nature, of being more “at peace” in nature. One student wrote: “Nature is so many things, but that earthy feeling that nature gives me can never be filled by anything else” (J1, Z7). The most common response emerging alongside the emotions described was a desire to care for and care with nature to act toward ecological justice by changing t heir actions and consumption (see Group Walking, Image (b)).

Theme 3: Walking as a return to relationality This theme is defined through the evidence students gathered reflecting the effect of humans on nature, as well as their ability to be affected by nature. They wrote about the direct impact of urbandevelopment on nature: “I wonder how the construction of the train affected the soil and ecosystems here” (FJ, AS3). They also highlighted the impact of politics and policy on nature: “[W]hen will the government learn that it will affect the future of us, the younger generations” (FJ, T1). Their concern for nature and about climate change became a demand that: “Others should care about this ongoing problem because it is a problem that affects everything in the future” (J1, S6). At the same time, they also made note of powerful ways in which connecting with nature helped them become more aware of their agentic role in the world: “Connecting with your environment and earth makes you feel more aware of what you are a part of and how what you do affects things around you” (J2, V6).  This theme seemed to challenge the t ypical instrumental approach to commodifying nature by surfacing concern and care given our relation with nature and, in turn, through our relation to a form of answerability  (Bakhtin, 1993) (see Journaling, Image (c)).

1884 Concluding thoughts Guided by our research questions, we sought to explore: how the participants described what they learned through a walking curriculum, as well as their relations with nature. In this study, we integrated learning on the move and learning as movement, with perspective s from critical geographies, to contextualize the learning opportunity created using a walking curriculum.  We also drew upon what Gardiner (1993) describes  as Bakhtin’s “Green” social theory in highlighting three overlapping and interrelated themes: walking as a return to the senses, walking as a return to the body, and walking as a return to relationality.  These themes are responses, in part, to concerns raised regarding the overemphasis in Western culture on instrumental rationality and mechanical thinking, the artificial separation between nature and culture, and the denial of the body, aspects of which have shaped the structure of schooling given its basis in the early 20th century factory model. Through their written journals and interviews, the secondary students responded passionately and thoughtfully to the opportunity to experience and reflect on walking in nature. Results indicate d that walking together in natural environments enable d unique learning opportunities related to students’ understandings of relationality with the natural world: between themselves and nature, between human actions and nature, and between politics, policies, and nature. The nature of students’ observations suggests that iteration and exploration as pedagogy in this research led to learning that was embodied, organic, and emotional, bridging between curriculum, students’ lived experiences, and their values, and extending to real and imagined v ersions of social futures.

We have written elsewhere about building from the  “right to the city ” (Soja, 1996) to  claim a parallel “right to be with nature,” and to claim this right not as a traditional Western form of dominance, extraction, and commodification, but as a right to engage with and relate to the natural world in a manner that foregrounds, instead, honour and acknowledgement for its importance in s ustaining us and our concomitant answerability in sustaining more-than-human others and the more-than-human-world. Bakhtin (1993) would acknowledge that in our answerability—our response to the reminders from nature of the ongoing harms caused by human impact — we have no alibi. As once-occurrent beings, as tasked with crafting our own life history from each individual act, we must recognize that “[t]o be in life, to be actually, is to act, is to be unindifferent toward the once-occurrent whole” (p. 43). It is this challenge to be “unindifferent” to human impact on nature that re-emerges through returning to the senses, the body, and relationality, and that builds through self-other relationships extended to the more-than-human-world.

Yet as attention to urban spaces increases, and access to nature decreases, we need to return to human relations with nature in our “roster of struggles” (p. 12), informed by current understandings of “race, gender, and class … sexuality, age, nation, regio n, nature, empire, and colony” (Soja, 1996, p. 12) as expanded through current notions of intersectionality. Here, we would reassert a sustainable “right to be with nature” that recognizes the dangers of and moves away from human exceptionalism and works to rebalance nature-human relations. The work of Indigenous scholars, like Robin Wall Kimmerer (20 24), who braid traditional western knowledge with Indigenous forms of knowledge, may hold the key to rebalancing our relations. Thus, while we would seek to move beyond dichotomies, braiding Kimmerer with  Soja, we might paraphrase Soja (1996) with insights from Kimmerer (202 4) to say the following: “A simple exercise is helpful: whenever you read or write a sentence that empowers urban  spaces, instead substitute green  spaces, and think about the importance of reframing nature, not as commodity, but as gift that transforms the relation between giver and receiver as an act of care.” Going beyond what we have written previously, we focus in on the importance of the ongoing gift of nature: the invitation to return to our senses, our bodies, and our relationality. When  made available through walking curricula, it is a welcome lesson that needs continual relearning  and renewal not just for developing, wellbeing, and quality of life, but also as the basis for all learning as central to life on Earth.
