# Resilience and Ingenuity: A Connected Arts Learning Journey

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Culturally organized learning pathways are related to identity, cultural practices, and are relational to structures and institutions. Learning in the arts is often withheld from children of color and those from marginalized communities in the U.S. By applying a connected arts learning model to an individual learning journey, this paper aims to address how youth may overcome obstructions to arts learning opportunities and what happens when they enter institutional arts learning environments and create their own, culturally connected arts learning and making experiences. This study pushes at a need to move more quickly and decisively toward both providing and diversifying pathways for arts learning.

## Introduction

An individual’s trajectory of learning is often called their learning pathway (Bell et al., 2017), indicating a learning road traveled throughout a learner’s life. Cultural ly organized learning pathways (Nasir et al., 2020)  are related to identity, cultural practices, and are relational to structures and institutions (Cawelti & Peppler, 2023). Learning journeys encompass many paths, as learners negotiat e their interests within social and cultural spaces, on their journey of becoming. In the United States, an individual’s pathway is of ten directed or obstructed by their place in society based on race, socioeconomic status, and other intersectional identities; “systems and institutions often have the power to facilitate or constrain individuals’ access and opportunities” (Nasir et al., 2020, p.206). The arts are among many learning opportunities often withheld from chi ldren of color and those from socioeconomically disadvantaged communities (Bowen & Kisida, 2019). T his puts these students at a disadvantage as t he arts can play a key role in identity, creativity, and langua ge development (e.g., Halverson & Sheridan, 2022), and where an artist’s activity, their artmaking, is an expression of self (Sullivan & McCarthy, 2007). Connected learning (Ito et al., 2013) is a framework for understanding learning across in-school and out of school learning contexts. In connected learning, meaningful learning occurs when youth interests, relationships (e.g., mentor, peer), and academic or career-oriented opportunities intersect. For this paper, I approach  interest driven learning from a Connected Arts Learning (Peppler et al., 2022) perspective, where the learn ing model emphasizes how culturally sustained practices in arts education contribute to more equitable learning and, in turn, youth artistry. Connected learning posits that mean ingful learning occurs at the intersection of inter ests, relationships, and opportunities. In cases of students from marginalized backgrounds, those opportunities, around technical studio instruction or making career connections, may be limited by the structures and institutions around them. Where their peers from more affluent backgrounds not only have resources for more connected out of school time with studio lessons, online access and technology, and tools and materials like instruments, they also have better equipped schools with instruments, theatre spaces, and art supplies available for student use. In interest driven connected learning, youth seek out and create communities for learning the things that interest them most. As young artists practice and grow, their understanding of their arts learning within social and cultural contexts emerges, including what kind of a rt learning is prioritized in different spaces and when certain arts practices are prioritized or marginalized. While interest driven connected learning is a dynamic model for understanding growth and creativity in the arts, I am interested in  what happens when youth do not have the resources to find meaningful relationships to suppo rt their arts making, or when they cannot  access academic, leisure, or studio arts opportunities. I seek to connect interests and opportunity in cont exts where students may struggle to join or create connected communities and wonder how under-resourced students may still pursue a culturally oriented learning pathway. This inquiry leads me to the driving questions for this paper, where I look at one young artist’s story  to try to understand the following: How did a young artist foster their interests in the arts amid limited arts learning opportunities?  What challenges d id they encounter when entering academic or studio arts learning environments? How did they respond?

## Methods

This paper is rooted in a broader study on identity and arts learning  among young artists. Ten participants were recruited in  January 2023. All are in emerging adulthood (ages 18-25) and are undergraduate performing arts majors at a university in Southern California. Participants are also affiliated with a grassroots theatre company on campus focused on telling stories of, by, and for t he Latine community. This theatre company is import ant to bounding the context of the study as most participa nts have similar heritage identifiers  and culturally connected reasons for participating in this theatre company. For the full study, we began with co-designed drama activities (Boal, 2022) built around embodying histories as we moved through personal timelines, and geographies through arts-based cultural mapping activities where we moved through maps and space. After the drama-based activities, participants responded to written prompts and created artwork based on their personal learning journeys. Finally, I conducted semi-structured retrospective interviews where the young artists described their artist journey artwork then responded to questions designed to encourage r eflection on their learning experiences, their interests and involvement in the arts, their supportive relationships, and opportunities opened through participation in the arts. Interviews lasted approximately one hour and 15 minutes each and were conducted on zoom. From this rich data set, I’ve chosen to focus on one case for this paper. Tracy (they/them pronouns) is a young theatre artist who encountered obstacles to connecting their interests in the arts to the re lationships and opportunities that would help foster those interest s and lead to richer learning, particularly in their learning journeys before college. Despite those struggles, they still chose to major in drama and pursue the arts professionally. I selected Tracy’s case because as their story emerged, I became interested in understanding how they overcame obstacles to connected learning, as w ell as more about the institutional contexts that h ampered their progress. ## Findings

Fostering interests in the arts amid limited arts learning opportunities Elementary and middle school. Tracy’s timeline reveals a strong throughline of ma intaining their interest in the arts even though there were few opportunities to pa rticipate in arts learning or arts making. For Tracy, initial introductions to the arts came from TV and movies, especially Disney musicals. Tracy was drawn to singing and dancing, and these movie musicals provided emotiona l connection and left them feeling happy and hopefu l. As they explain, “I think that's what kind of made me really like it, because they make a tie in for an emotion of hope, and that like, was something that made me want to pursue  (the arts).” Tracy reflects on those early experiences, and connects them to their current arts practice, “this gave me this hope, but like now I can pursue it in a different way.” Here they describe capturing the emotion and trying to replicate it in new artistic ventures. Tracy’s early arts experiences were solitary. Their  interests were disconnected to relationships and opportunities at this point as no one else in their  family felt the same spark with the arts. Tracy ex plains that in their home, “no one likes doing art at all, or danc ing, or anything. I think I was a little odd one ou t for them.” In their community, Tracy participated in their school  and church. When asked about school, Tracy laughed  and said, “No, I don't think there was opportunity to be arts driven.” They further explained that there was sometimes a school dance program that was on and off dependin g on whether it was funded. A highlight in their ea rly experience occurred in middle school when a dance c lass was offered, “But it was only for a semester, and then it got cancelled, and then we didn't have any from there. That's when it stopped.” When asked about afterschool programs, Tracy explained that their parents wanted them at home where they were safe. They knew a friend who took gymnast ic classes, so they approached their parents about taking a dance class. This idea did not pan out as Tracy’s p arents said classes were too expensive. At church t hey found the one opportunity to join in the arts as they san g in a worship group at a young age, however they s witched churches in middle school, so that ended too. Ultim ately, Tracy persisted in their arts interest by co ntinuing in their solitary journey and making up songs and performing them on their own at their school. High school. This pattern of trying to get connected to arts lea rning continued into high school. In high school, Tracy signed up for music class. They earne d the orchestra course credit but never learned an instrument because the school could not afford to provide instruments to the students. They tried to take an art or drama class, but it was scheduled in conflict with advanced placement classes. Tracy explained that in their school, “you're an AP student, or you're an art student. You can ’t be both. And I was trying to get into college, so I  was like, oh, I can't do theatre.”  Friends also presented a problem. Tracy remember ed how her high school friends never saw them as an artist. When Tracy would share with frie nds about their interest in the arts, their friends  would say, “‘Oh, but like you're not creative.’” Tracy responded, “ I think I am. You're wrong; I think I’m actually really creative.” Though they continued to try to engage in arts lear ning, they were stymied by a lack of school and community institutional support, funding and resources, and family or peer connection. Tracy’s story finally hits a bit of a turning point in high school when they join cheer and when an English teacher in their senior year encouraged Tracy’s writing. Tracy describes participating in cheer as fun and a chance to dance. In English class, Tracy describes the rel ationship with their teacher as important to encour aging them toward the arts. “I had an English teacher in my senior year, who was a drama teacher. We would do like journals, and she would always tell me I was like a good writ er in my journal.” This teacher also encouraged Tracy to audition for school shows, however as Tracy explains, “I would audition for her stuff (plays), but I’d never get in because I didn't have experience.” As Tracy tells this part of their story, it does seem that the encouragement they received toward writing far outweighed their perception of failing to get a role in the school play. Challenges and responses when entering arts learning environments Challenges. Arts learning experiences finally begin for Tracy in college. After entering university as a declared political science major, Tracy shared an important moment when, “someone told me that you could take dance for free, and I was like, ‘Wow! That's amazing!’” They signed up for a jazz cl ass and even wanted to become a dance major, but then realized, “I couldn't be a dance major, because it's rigorous, and you have to auditi on, and you have to be like the best ballet person in the world. ” Undeterred from their arts path they decided to t ry for a music major but discovered, “you have to audition with an instrument, or with your voice and I don't have enough experience to do that.” Even though Tracy now had a ccess to the arts classes and experiences they had wanted since early childhood, their lack of experience prevented them from pursuing an arts major. Things changed for Tracy when someone told them that you didn’t need experience to take acting classes and be a drama major. Tracy decided to take the int roduction to acting course series and explains, “ it was very difficult, and I was uncomfortable.” They enjoyed having classes where the learning was engaging and active; the acting classes were fun. In the second acting class, Tracy decided they needed, “ to act like I'm confident. So, I did. And then the teacher thought I was good becaus e of that.” Despite declaring the drama major and f inding a place in these classes, Tracy explains, “I never thought I was like doing as well, and it was very hard for me to catch up.” A lack of experience in elementary and high sch ool continued to plague Tracy. They expressed their insecurity, saying “I wasn't at the level of other people, because I didn 't get those skills that other people might have. That would always hold me back from performing, beca use in comparison to other people, I haven't been doing theater since (as long as) they have.” Responses. Tracy does have a response for the challenges they encountered when entering university arts spaces. First comes from advice from a singing teac her who simply asked, ‘Why did you choose to do art ?’ As Tracy reflected on this question, they realized tha t the “goal is that you want that feeling of performance, and it doesn't matter where it is, you know it's like, that's what brings the joy.” Tracy returned to their childhood where they were initially interested in the arts because of the joy that stories, movies, and singing brough t. In addition to capturing the joy through personal practice and performance, Tracy has found joy and connection through their work with [blinded] theatre company. As they explai n, “I joined because it is a space on campus that allows you a chance to practice theatre without prior experience. Also, in this space I am allowed to challenge the university’s theatre program and help create a theatre company that is inclusive and honors various cultures.” Participation in [blinded] theatre company has given Tracy an opport unity to redefine the skills and knowledge needed t o make theatre art, to reconsider what it means to make ar t, and to push back on the institutional norms that  have put up walls and excluded their and other’s cultural backgrounds and stories.

## Discussion

Tracy’s timeline reveals a strong throughline of  struggle, hope, and resilience. They were aware of their interest in the arts at an early age through the media they watched, as they sang along to High School Musical,  but they encountered obstacle after obstacle as they tried t o participate in arts communities and arts learning. Solitary practice and a strong sense of self  seem to be the primary method s Tracy used to foster their own arts interests despite adversity. Making up songs, writing in thei r journal, and trying to participate every chance t hat came up were all important moves. Just as important was Tracy’s resilience when a program was cancelled, an instrument was unavailable, or a friend told them they’re not crea tive enough to be an artist. Through all of this, Tracy maintained hope that they could become and artist, and this hope led them to ultimately pursue a career in the arts. As Tracy entered academic, cultural, and studio arts settings in college, they encountered challenges with gatekeeping based on lack of “formal” arts knowledg e. This started with their high school audition whe n they didn’t have enough experience to land a role and continued with their inability to declare a dance or music major because they didn’t enter the academy with prior di scipline specific skills or knowledge. Of course, therein lies the rub, as Tracy tried everything they could to gain those skills and knowledge. Tracy returns to their themes of resilience when they describe a turn toward capturing the joy of making art. They also shift their story when they join [blinded] theatre company and begin making art  with peers who have similar backgrounds and experi ences. With [blinded], Tracy is working with peers to push back on institutional norms and creating their own, culturally connected arts learning and making experiences.

Tracy’s story illuminates a critical social justice  issue in U.S. schooling. Their lack of access to b asic classes or programs in the arts, the choice between AP or art class, are not realities for more affluent or resourced students. The gates held at the academy doors for arts majors prevent students with less experience or means from entering learning pathways and ultimately entering arts focused careers.  This study, while just one case, likely represents the experiences of many students. This study pushes at a need to move more quickly and decisively toward both providing and diversifying pathways for arts learning, incorporating culturally sustaining learning in traditional arts studio settings, and confronting university and cultural institutional biases toward Eurocentric arts traditions and expectations for students entering studio arts programs.
