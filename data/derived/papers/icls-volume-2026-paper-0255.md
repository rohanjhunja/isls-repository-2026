# Knowledge-as-Network, Creation-as-Navigation: A Complexity-Informed Multilevel Model for Educating Creators

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Large language models perform a variety of tasks, challenging education systems built for routine recall and application. This paper proposes an adapted three-world model for educating creators who can coordinate reality, concepts, and language/public symbolic forms. We synthesize learning sciences research on complexity to motivate a multilevel framework of knowledge and creators (Levels 0+5). In particular, Levels 3-5—disciplinary big picture, generalizable ways of thinking, and meta-knowledge of learning—serve as advanced knowledge generators, outlining pathways to operationalize creator cultivation in the AI era.

As artificial intelligence, especially large language models (LLMs),  become broadly competent at many school-valued tasks, education that trains only routine retrieval and standard application risks converging on what machines already do well. The alternative is not to reject AI, but to refocus human learning on creator capacities: posing valuable problems, designing new concepts and methods, and cultivating communities capable of doing both. This paper lays the conceptual and design groundwork for such a pivot. We first clarify how LLMs inhabit the world of language while variably reflecting the world of concepts and remaining weakly grounded in the world of reality. We  then draw on decades of learning sciences research on complex systems, which gives us a vocabulary and toolkit for designing learning that is hierarchical yet emergent, structured yet adaptive. With that scaffolding in place, we introduce a multi-level model of knowledge and creators and show how to build the learning technologies and assessments that make it actionable. LLMs, world models, and the three worlds Over the last five years, large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated broad competence across languagemediated tasks, including few-shot reasoning, code generation, summarization, and exam-style question answering (OpenAI, 2023; Team Google, 2023). When augmented with prompting strategies such as chain-ofthought reasoning (Wei et al., 2022), these systems can solve multi-step problems that previously required specialized pipelines. Yet this success raises a deeper question: what kind of “knowing” do LL Ms possess? Yildirim and Paul (2024) distinguish instrumental task competence from world models that represent causal and counterfactual structure. Because LLMs learn primarily from text, their performance may rely on regularities in language rather than grounded models of reality.

With this distinction in view, it becomes natural to articulate a framework that keeps separate—and then carefully reconnects—the domains that contemporary LLMs conflate. We  therefore introduce an adapted threeworlds account of learning and knowledge inspired by Popper’s classic tripartition (Popper, 1978) that will anchor the rest of the paper. Popper’s original formulation is broader: World  1 is the physical world, while World 2 concerns subjective or psychological states/processes, and World 3 concerns the products of the human mind. For representing knowledge creation, we extend World 1 as the world of research objects and their activities of all disciplines. Therefore, for instance, for creators in education/psychology, the subjective process, which in Popper’s view belongs to World 2, live in the World of Reality (our world 1) in our three-world framework. The concepts to describe and the ways of thinking to create and use these concepts, and to ask the prevalent questions belong to our W orld of Concept (our world 2). The World of Language  (our world 3)  is composed by the language/symbols that representing the objects in the World of Reality and World of Concepts. One should be able to see that our three worlds and Popper’s classic tripartition are related in certain sense but quite different. Human learning for creativity operates across all three worlds: we learn from the World of Reality (direct experience of objects and phenomena), we abstract those experiences into the World of Concepts (forming mental models, theories, and conceptual knowledge), and we use the W orld of Language to express and communicate objects in the other worlds. Learning, on this view, is not merely the accumulation of sentences but the orchestration of movement among these worlds. We abstract concepts from engagements with reality; we stabilize and scrutinize those concepts in language; and we project them back into reality as guides for measurement, design, and intervention. Importantly, the knowledge levels introduced later are not one-to-one equivalents of worlds: a 1757 level may be primarily anchored in one world while still drawing on the others. The educational consequence of the LLM era is therefore not that language has become less important —if anything, the linguistic surface has become more tractable and thus more tempting to overemphasize —but that we must intentionally design for the reality–concept loop, the capacities that LLMs currently lack and that societies will increasingly need. Knowledge and learning as complex systems: Foundations for a multilevel model for meaningful learning To help learners navigate the three worlds, a powerful theme in the learning sciences —education is a complex system to be designed as such (Goldstone & Wilensky, 2008; Jacobson et al., 2019)—offers the foundation. Research in the learning sciences increasingly frames education as a complex system (Goldstone & Wilensky, 2008; Jacobson et al., 2019). Complex systems are characterized by hierarchical organization, feedback, and emergence, where local interactions can produce macro-level patterns of understanding (Simon, 1962). In educational contexts, this perspective implies that conceptual change cannot be fully scripted; instead, instructional environments should scaffold the conditions under which new patterns of kn owledge emerge. Several design implications follow. First, instruction should make levels of knowledge explicit, enabling learners to move between concrete experiences and abstract frameworks. Second, tasks should support emergence through cycles of prediction, feedback, reflection, and revision (Schwarz et al., 2017). Third, educators should promote lateral connections across domains using analogical reasoning and representational tools that reveal structural relations (Gentner, 1983; Novak & Cañas, 2006). Finally, assessment should attend not only to correctness but also to the connectivity and generativity of knowledge networks, using analytic approaches that capture structural growth (Shaffer et al., 2016).

These principles not only cohere with the complexity literature but also expose a gap: while the field encourages us to teach for multi-level interaction, emergence, and transfer, it has not fully articulated how the multilevel organization of knowledge  itself can be systematically modeled, taught, and assessed.  Yet without an explicit, teachable schema of levels, educators lack a shared language to (a) set goals (“What kind  of knowledge are we aiming for here?”), (b) design tasks (“Which vertical or lateral moves  does this activity elicit?”), and (c) assess growth (“Has the learner’s network become more coherent, deep, and generative?”).  This is where a multilevel model for meaningful learning becomes essential. The next section introduces such a model and shows how it provides relatively an actionable, unified representation of what those levels  of knowledge are and how it translates these complexity-informed design principles into concrete educational aims, practices, and assessments. A multilevel model of knowledge and creators What kind of knowledge should we teach if our goal is to cultivate creative thinkers and innovators? Our model posits that advanced knowledge – the kind that enables knowledge creation – must be at the center of learning goals. To define advanced knowledge, we introduce a multilevel framework of knowledge  consisting of 0 + 5 levels (that is, a zeroth level plus five subsequent levels). Levels 0–2 summarize widely studied strata (experience, facts/procedures, concepts); Levels 3 –5 articulate advanced knowledge generators that coordinate multiscale structure, emergence, and meta-level regulation. In parallel, we distinguish creator levels (0 –5) to indicate the kind of creative work learners cando as they master each stratum and the moves between strata. This framework is meant to complement rather than replace existing level-based accounts. We agree with Bloom’s revised taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001)  that a learner might memorize, apply, analyze, evaluate, or create knowledge and those are indeed at different levels of mastery, however, what we intend to emphasize in our levels of knowledge is that in order to memorize, apply, analyze, evaluate, or create knowledge what kind of knowledge are required respectively, would them be different and so different so that they are in fact not at the same level and as we will show soon they are. In this sense, our level of knowledge can be regarded as an operative implementation of Bloom’s revised taxonomy. In a forthcoming paper, we will clarify the distinctions between our model and existing frameworks, including Bloom’s revised taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001), Webb’s (2002) depth of knowledge and Bateson’s (1972) levels of learning. Frankly, we should acknowledge that knowledge exists at multiple levels, and that effective knowledge creators primarily operate at higher levels —engaging with scientific methods, critical thinking, abstraction, and relational reasoning —rather than focusing solely on discipline-specific concepts or routine skills such as mathematical manipulation. This distinction is well understood by experienced researchers in mathematics, physics, and other fields. However, these varying levels of knowledge have rarely been explicitly conceptualized. In this sense, our work can be seen as an attempt to articulate and formalize this largely tacit understanding of knowledge hierarchies.

1758 Levels 0-2: Well-studied knowledge strata and creators they enable Level 0—Pre-knowledge (experiential/embodied) & memorizers. Direct interaction with phenomena in the World of Reality (perception, manipulation, observation) precedes abstraction. Work on experiential learning, ecological perception/affordances, and grounded cognition emphasizes how perception –action coupling provides the substrate for conceptual growth (Barsalou, 1999; Kolb, 1984; NASEM, 2018). Broader perspectives on cognition as embodied, distributed across people and artifacts, and in some cases extended into tools and representational systems further highlight the role of interaction with tools and environments (Wils on, 2002). It corresponds to Level-0 creators (memorizers/searchers) who can notice and recount experiences but do not yet transform them conceptually or procedurally.

Level 1—Factual and procedural knowledge  & routine appliers. “Knowing-that” and “knowing-how” provide the operational basis for many school tasks. Skill acquisition research describes how declarative knowledge becomes proceduralized through practice (Anderson, 1996). However, without conceptual integration such knowledge often remains inert (NASEM, 2018). It supports Level-1 creators (routine appliers) who reliably execute established procedures and reproduce known results, e.g., instructions, recipes,  and worked examples, typically through established representations within the World of Language. Level 2 —Disciplinary conceptual knowledge  & problem solvers. Concepts organize facts into explanatory structures that generalize across situations (e.g., force, equilibrium, function). Research on conceptual change (Chi et al., 1981; Vosniadou, 2019)  and knowledge-in-pieces (diSessa, 1993)  shows how learners reorganize intuitive resources into disciplinary models. It enables Level-2 creators (problem solvers) who apply concepts flexibly to non-routine but given problems—moving more deliberately between the World of Concepts (principles, models) and the World of Reality (data, phenomena), though typically within a single discipline. Levels 0-2 are necessary but insufficient for creator education: by themselves they privilege recall and routine application. Many of their language-visible performances—especially recall, routine procedure following, and some forms of concept application—are increasingly supportable or partially  automated by LLMs trained on the World of Language alone.

Levels 3-5: Strata for advanced knowledge and higher creators Level 3—Disciplinary “big picture”  & problem formulators. This level represents an integrated understanding of a discipline’s overarching frameworks. We characterize it in five facets – a field’s objects of study, typical questions, epistemic forms, methodologies, and values —the architecture that situates and organizes Level-2 concepts. In essence, L evel-3 knowledge is a meta-conceptual grasp of how a field is structured and how it operates. For example, a L evel-3 understanding of mathematics would include recognizing that mathematical objects of study are abstract structures, its mode of thinking emphasizes abstraction and logical deduction and proof, its methods include axiomatization and rigorous argument, and its societal role is to provide a formal language for science and technology. Achieving Level-3 knowledge means seeing the forest beyond the trees of facts, disciplinary concepts, propositions, and formulas–understanding what makes the discipline tick. It catalyzes Level-3 creators (problem formulators) who pose novel, field-internal questions by spotting gaps, anomalies, and promising directions and allows them to navigate and connect concepts within the field strategically. Level 4—Generalizable ways of thinking (cross-cutting cognition) & strategic innovators. Beyond any single discipline, Level-4 knowledge consists of general cognitive strategies and ways of thinking that can apply across domains. These often arise by abstracting methods from specific fields into broadly useful forms, such as relational thinking, abstraction, mathematical modeling, optimization, causal analysis, analogical mapping, critical thinking. They function as transfer engines. Level-4 knowledge equips learners with transferrable thinking tools, enabling them to be Level-4 creators (strategic innovators) who compose and adapt cross-domain structures to frame high-impact, transdisciplinary problems and solution pathways. Level 5—K nowledge of learning (teaching and learning methods) & cultivator of innovators: The highest level in this framework is knowledge about how to facilitate knowledge acquisition itself.  Achieving Level-5 knowledge means the individual not only has mastered Levels 1–4, but has also developed reflexive expertise in orchestrating Levels 0 –4 for oneself and others. T his pedagogical or metacognitive expertise –knowing defines Level-5 creators (cultivator of innovators) who amplify creation at scale by designing sociotechnical environments (e.g., multilayer concept maps, modeling practices, assessment for transfer) that seed productive emergence across communities—treating learning ecologies as complex adaptive systems. For example, a mentor might deliberately sequence field observation, modeling, critique, and reflection so that novices move from raw experience to transferable principles rather than only consuming finished explanations. Knowledge-as-network, creation-as-navigation 1759 We describe creative work as navigation across levels and worlds. Novel work rarely comes from a single upward leap; creators move among phenomena, concepts, and representations to reinterpret problems and generate artifacts. The 0+5 knowledge levels provide a structured map of the landscape learners must navigate; the 0– 5 creator levels describe their expanding agency (see Figure 1). Three types of moves are especially important: (1) Bottom-up vertical moves (formation): lower-level knowledge is abstracted into higher-level structures. For example, a specific fact (Level 1) is synthesized into a broader concept (Level 2), and the processes used to construct Level 2 concepts become the foundation for acquiring and mastering Level 3+ knowledge; (2) Lateral moves (assimilation): knowledge at the same level is integrated to expand or reorganize understanding. For instance, multiple Level 2 concepts may combine to support comprehension of another Level 2 concept, or Level 2 concepts from different disciplines may be connected to form interdisciplinary insight; and (3) Top-down vertical moves (generation): higher-level knowledge produces or refines lower-level knowledge. For example, Level 2 knowledge may generate new procedures for solving specific tasks (Level 1), while Level 3+ knowledge may frame and resolve questions that lead to the creation of new Level 2 concepts. This multilevel perspective transforms learning from linear accumulation into dynamic network traversal—and clarifies how advancing knowledge levels systematically expands creator levels, moving learners from routine performance to problem formulation, strategic innovation, and eventually the cultivation of a new generation of creators.

Figure 1 Relation between the Three Worlds and Knowledge Levels This paper has argued that in an era when LLMs increasingly master the manipulation of language and routine problem solving, education must pivot toward cultivating creators who can traverse and connect the worlds of reality, concepts, and language. By syn thesizing insights from the learning sciences and complexity theory, we proposed a multilevel model of knowledge and creators —a framework that views knowledge as a complex, layered network and learning as the process of navigating and expanding that networ k. Framing education through this complexity lens foregrounds emergence, feedback, and cross-level coordination as the mechanisms of deep learning and creative production. It also offers a coherent language for designing, teaching, and assessing creativity: educators can now specify which level of knowledge an activity targets, which vertical or lateral moves it elicits, and which level of creator it develops. This framework is intended as a design heuristic, not a strict developmental sequence or a claim that tasks belong to only one level. Different disciplines may instantiate the levels differently, and sophisticated work often recruits multiple levels at once. Future work should operationalize the model empirically: designing tasks and rubrics that distinguish level-specific performance, tracing navigation moves in discourse and artifact production, and testing whether interventions aimed at Levels 3-5 improve transfer, problem formulation, and collaborative knowledge building. Early steps include research on neuro-cognitive mechanisms for cross-level abstraction  (Li et al., 2024), scientometric analyses for quantifying knowledge emergence  across disciplines (Zeng et al., 2017), and optimizing pedagogical sequences according to knowledge levels (Cao & Wu, 2025; Yan et al., 2013).
