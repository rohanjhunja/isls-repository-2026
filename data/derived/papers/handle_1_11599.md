# Developing Active Learning Pedagogy in Teacher Candidates: Bridging Theory to Future Practice

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores active learning pedagogies in a Secondary-Level Math Pedagogy course at a large teacher’s training faculty in Canada. Using the ICAP Framework, the research presents various active learning activities to develop teacher candidates' (TCs) active learning pedagogy. Observations and interviews reveal that smaller activities, such as problem-solving on whiteboards and mind-mapping, effectively engage TCs. Larger activities, like a Collatz Conjecture Representation activity, create authentic mathematical problems that provide deep, interactive, and domain-specific active learning experiences. The study suggests that simple, adaptable activities can significantly prepare TCs for implementing active learning in their future classrooms.

## Introduction

Active learning comes from a long lineage of pro-education reformer theorists (Page, 1990). The phrase has been advanced as an umbrella term encompassing various educational instructional approaches, such as problem-based learning and student-student teaching. These approaches are argued to be  more effective than traditional lecture methods and are important for improving educational outcomes (Freeman et al., 2014). Chi et al. (2018) have offered the ICAP Framework to define “active learning,” which includes four activity levels (interactive, constructive, active, and passive). In Passive, students listen without manipulating the information; in active, students manipulate information only within the current lesson’s domain; in constructive, students manipulate information and connect new information to previous learning; and in interactive, students engage in constructive-level thinking with others. Chi argues that the student’s work (e.g., worksheets, mind maps, debates, conversations, etc.) can show the interconnections between knowledge, allowing the categorization of active learning behaviours. In ICAP, inter connections are thought to develop most frequently at the interactive and constructive levels. These interconnections avoid the encapsulation of  new knowledge, allowing the knowledge to be more easily retrieved and applied more broadly. Niemi et al. (2016) argue that active learning in teacher education res ults in more responsive teachers. However, there is little information on what educat ional frameworks, learning activity examples, and t hinking teacher educators (TEs) use to enculture teacher ca ndidates (TCs) to active learning. One proposed solution to this problem is integrating active learning into te acher education programs (Slotta, 2018, 2020; Slott a & Jacque,

2019) to help TEs develop these skills early. Burron et a l. (2023) discussed that TEs may struggle to  translate

active learning pedagogy effectively because they engage their TCs in active learning as student participants rather than as future practitioners. To that end, this paper demonstrates methods active learning TEs can encourage active learning pedagogical development in TCs.

## Methods

This case study examines the instructional behaviour of Dr. C a Secondary-Level Math Pedagogy course instructor at the University of Toronto. Dr. C, has taught mathematics pedagogy for 5 years and is part of t he Teacher Education Technology Enhanced Active Learning (TEAL) Group at the University of Toronto, which encourages active learning pedagogy in teacher education. Dr. C joined the TEAL Group because he self-describes as an active learning instructor and wanted to enhance his existing active learning pedagogy. The lessons described all occurred in a TEAL classroom with movable furniture and 18 whiteboards (6 mobile) that encourage TEs to rearrange the room to their needs and have students work on the whiteboards rather than at the tables. Observations and field notes of Dr. C’s class captured approximately half his total lectures (18 hours). The class’s activity was captured as time-stamped field notes describing Dr. C's active learning activities and how he scaffolded active learning pedagogical development in his TCs. Dr. C's reflections were recorded as structured interviews after each observed class. These interviews captured Dr. C’s impression of each class's effectiveness and how he scaffolded active learning development for his TCs. Cross-referencing observations and student work against ICAP allowed us to gauge whether active learning was occurring and at what level.

## Case Study: Dr. C

Dr. C’s active learning activities can be divided into smaller, everyday-use, and larg er single-use activities. Smaller activities were components of multiple classes and in numerous use cases. In comparison, larger active learning activities would take up the entirety of t he class or multiple classes and would not be used again. After each activity, Dr. C would discuss the applicabilit y of the activity with his TCs. The selection of activities presented below constitutes interactive-level active learning because the TCs work ed as a group, had to justify their thinking to others, and displayed their work for analysis by Dr. C and the class. Dr. C did not initially consider his smaller activities as noteworthy active learning. However, the smaller nature of these activities allow ed for greater flexibility and made them applicable to different disciplines and instructional cases. In one example, Dr. C had his students select a random mathematical problem to solve on the whiteboards in a randomly assigned group. Dr. C’s goal for this activity is not to have the TCs practice math problems but instead to have the TCs practice whiteboard skills and effectively explain their solution using these skills. To this end, the students work ed together to solve the problem and then justif ied how they presented their solution to Dr. C and other classmates. Students who arrived late to class were asked to question the TCs’ solution or have them explain how their solution work ed. Dr. C expressed that this activity encouraged TCs to consider how their teacherly board work should be presented and believed that the TCs could adapt the activity for their future students to showcase and justify their thinking. Another example was mind-mapping activities that asked the TCs to organize their learning as they worked on a problem and to scaffold that understanding to fellow TCs. For example, TCs were expected to map curricular outcomes of math disciplines across different grades on the room’s whiteboards. Each group was given a differen t discipline to investigate. Once done, the TCs presented their mind maps and explained their connections to the class. These mind maps allowed the TCs to jigsaw their collective understanding of the curriculum with their fellow students. Dr. C expressed that using mind maps made the TCs learning public, allowing them to see and build on everyone else’s ideas. Notably, in subsequent unrelated activities, all the TC groups would consistently use these techniques to think together -- unprompted by Dr. C. One of Dr. C’s larger activities asked the TCs to represent the Collatz Conjecture using available crafting supplies. This class started with a 15-minute instructor-led lesson where difficulties conceptualizing the conjecture were addressed, and examples were explored. Once the grounding le sson of the lecture was complete, Dr. C showed examples of how the conjecture had been prev iously represented  and asked the TCs to design a representation. Dr. C  expected the TCs to  mimic the existing representations in a larger form at. However, the students created entirely new representations, including one not described in the literature. Surprised by this discovery, Dr. C encouraged the group to publish th eir result. The lesson finished with a consolidation activity that encouraged the students to connect the activity they just completed to how they might do a similar activity in their future teaching. In his reflection, Dr. C expressed that the activity seemed to challenge the TC’s conception of math instruction. Dr. C was pleased with the overall positive reaction to the technique and positive comments expressed by the students. Dr. C also discussed tha t the interview question used to prompt his reflections, “What in the class supported your TCs’ development of the ir active learning pedagogy?” drove him to recontex tualize his activities to include this outcome.

## Findings and conclusion

Dr. C’s smaller activities highlight that active le arning doesn’t need to be an all-consuming activity type. The start-up problems activity is no longer than 10 minutes while meeting the critera for ICAP’s interactive level. The activity could also be adapted to other lessons and disciplines by changing the questions. Dr. C’s larger activities tended to package smaller activities into a cohesive lesson to create an authentic disciplinary experience. Reflecting on incorporating scaffolding for TC’s future role as an active learning practitioner also encouraged Dr. C to add these components to his lessons. The TC’s positive emotional reactions to the lessons may also encourage TCs to adopt these practices, as positive experienc es of active learning during a teacher’s education tend to promote its adoption in future teachers (Burron, 20 22). Effective active learning enculturation for TCs may be a mosaic of active learning activity types paired with intentional scaffolding to encourage TCs’ future use. This selection of Dr. C’s active learning techniques shed s some light on effective strategies to scaffold active learning pedagogy to TCs. However, the most effective active learning techniques  for this goal remain unclear. The continued collection of active learning techniques to find common elements would be helpful to encourage active learning development in TCs further.

As ISLS acts as an effective community for developing active learning activities, the society must also consider how best to deploy those activities to in-service teachers and TCs. Workshops and professiona l development classes may benefit from including activities that purposefully bridge attendees from a participatory role into the role of a future practitioner of the technique. Further development in this area may be  critical for effectively translating active learning techniques developed in academic environments to classrooms.
