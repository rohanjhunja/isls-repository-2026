# Learning through Partnerships: How Co-Design and Embodied Play Can Shape a Playful Learning Ecology

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
What happens when teachers and researchers partner to co-design a tangible, embodied game to foster computational thinking? Byte Beads is a game in which children compose and perform sequences of beads associated with actions to represent computational patterns. Initially conceived within computing education, the game evolved as teachers helped shape a design supporting pattern-based reasoning, collaborative embodied sequencing, and executive functioning skills, including attention and cognitive flexibility. Drawing on constructionist and embodied learning traditions, we examine how researcher-practitioner partnerships shaped the evolution of the game and the pedagogical practices surrounding it. Tracing these interactions across relational collaborations, p rofessional development, and classroom enactment, the paper illustrates how partnership itself becomes a site of learning where design, pedagogy and play co-develop. This work contributes to the ISLS 2026 theme of Partnerships with Purpose by conceptualizing partnership as an ongoing process of co-designing and sustaining environments supporting playful embodied learning.

Partnerships in education often begin with shared intentions and evolve into dynamic relationships that shape what and how we learn together. Our work began as an exploration of how tangible and embodied play could foster children’s computational thinking, yet continued partnerships with teachers revealed how the game could support broader learning aims. In co-design meetings and a teacher professional development (PD) workshop, educators helped refine how Byte Beads could effectively engage students in classroom learning and suggested possible extensions to mathematics, music, and biological concepts. The paper examines how learning unfolded through partnerships, threading relational, ecological and pedagogical design processes across the creation and early classroom use of Byte Beads.

Byte Beads (Renteria et al., 2024) is a tabletop, “unplugged” game that engages players in embodied collaborative play. Players thread colored beads onto strings to build sequences, assigning each color a distinct sound and a physical action. On each turn,  players perform their full sequence in order, combining sound and gesture together. A spinner introduces rule-based actions such as Combine, Split, or Simplify, prompting each player to modify their sequences. Wild Beads add new sounds and are earned when a player makes an error, serving as a built-in scoring mechanism that reframes mistakes as playful opportunities. Full game materials are openly available at The Bead Education Organization (Renteria et al., 2024). Byte Beads was conceptualized and refined by an interdisciplinary research team consisting of members from the domains of design, engineering, STEM education, cognitive science and neuroscience. Over the course of one academic year, the design evolved through three forms of partnership: (1) relational design, grounded in shared inquiry with two classroom teachers and researchers who co-developed and tested the game; (2) ecological design, centered on classroom enactment co-facilitated by researchers and one partner teacher with seventh-grade students; and (3) pedagogical design, focused on adaptation and translation during a two-day PD workshop with a broader group of educators. Examining these interlinked phases, we argue that partnership itself functions as an ecology through which learning, play, and design co-develop.

## Conceptual framework

Our partnership-based design work is grounded in constructionism, embodied cognition, and the learning ecologies perspective. Constructionism (Papert & Harel, 1991; Ackermann, 2004) positions learning as an active, expressive process in which learners cons truct knowledge through the creation of personal and socially meaningful artifacts. Byte Beads extends this premise by inviting children to externalize computational reasoning 2152 by threading bead sequences that encode rhythm, repetition, and rulemaking. In creating and performing these sequences, learners give tangible form to abstract computational structures, linking symbolic logic to sensory and social experience.

Embodied and distributed cognition theories provide a complementary lens (Hutchins, 1995; Barsalou, 2008; Rogoff, 1998; Cook & Goldin-Meadow, 2008). From this perspective, cognition arises through the coordination of bodies, tools, and environments, and students enact computational thinking through coordinated sounds and gestures distributed across people and artifacts. These enactments illustrate how learning depends on the interplay of representation, embodied action, and social coordination. The addition  of extensive repetition solidifies sequential learning and memory (Bellafard, et al., 2024). Finally, we situate our work within a learning ecologies framework (Barron, 2006), which views learning as emerging from interdependent systems of relationships, materials, and practices. Within this ecology, partnership serves as both context and a catalyst for design, mediating how ideas evolve across people and settings. To analyze how these ecologies take shape, we employ a tripartite lens of relational, ecological, and pedagogical design. Relational design attends to shared inquiry among collaborators;  pedagogical design focuses on the translation of the game into teachable practices; and ecological design examines how ideas transform during classroom enactment. Together, these orientations articulate how partnership-based design can generate playful learning ecologies-dynamic systems of design, play, and reflection that sustain learning and innovation across contexts.

Our progression from small-group collaboration to broader professional development and classroom enactment parallels patterns in participatory curriculum design (Voogt et al., 2015). However, rather than treating these as discrete steps in a design pipeline, we analyze them as interdependent layers within a learning ecology of partnership. This ecology is animated by Byte Beads which foregrounds embodied sequencing and material interaction as integral to sense-making. In this way, partnership is reframed not merely as a means to implement the design, but as the medium through which the learning and design co-evolve. We approached this work as a teacher –researcher partnership, using a qualitative design narrative methodology to document and interpret how our collaboration evolved. In the first phase, our group of co-authors, including two classroom teachers and researchers, met frequently to engage in design iterations, test game prototypes, and reflect on gameplay interactions. Their sustained engagement reflected a relational partnership, grounded in curiosity, trust, and shared investment in shaping the game and its classroom relevance. The second phase consisted of classroom enactment with a class of seventh-grade students co-facilitated by one of the partner teachers and the researchers. This represents the ecological partnership that focuses on the dynamics of learning, when the students and teachers interact with the materials in real-time. The third phase was a two-day professional development (PD) at the San Joaquin County Office of Education in California with 17 teachers participating in hands-on play, reflection, and lesson mapping. This collaboration represents a pedagogical partnership,  where Byte Beads could be adapted for classrooms and mapped to curricular goals.

Data sources included field notes, meeting transcripts, teacher feedback forms, and classroom observation logs. Analysis proceeded thematically across these sources, with each researcher independently coding interactions, design decisions, and reflections against the three partnership lenses (relational, ecological, pedagogical). Codes were then discussed collaboratively to build consensus, with specific data sources anchoring 2153 observation logs and researcher field notes informed the ecological phase; and teacher feedback forms and PD observation notes informed the pedagogical phase.

Design insights across partnership phases Relational design: Shared inquiry through established trust The relational partnership between the two teachers and the researchers formed the foundation for design. A distinctive feature of this collaboration was that two of the researchers were the adult children of the two partner teachers, making this an interg enerationally rooted partnership. This familial relational infrastructure meant the group could move immediately into creative exploration rather than rapport-building, as trust and shared values were already constituted through long-standing personal bonds. Over the course of the academic year, teachers and researchers communicated weekly, exchanging prototypes, reflections, and classroom-oriented suggestions. The teachers were committed as equal partners in shaping the game’s design and pedagogical direct ion, contributing perspectives that bridged classroom realities with emerging learning goals. Feedback often reflected pedagogical expertise and design sensibility. Teachers proposed changes that prioritized accessibility and engagement, such as using brighter colors, reducing the number of beads to simplify play, and replacing the ‘opponent bead’ with a more positive sounding ‘wild bead’. The initial version of the game featured a separate scorecard, but one teacher suggested integrating scorekeeping directly into gameplay to minimize cognitive overload. This led to the incorporation of Wild Beads,  special beads earned when a player made errors in performing the embodied sequences, becoming a built-in mechanism to keep score and a reflective marker of performance.

Game playtesting delineated aspects of executive function support and development that could be incorporated into the game, thereby leading to an additional branch of co-creation. Introduction of wild card actions such as Stop and Reflect now facilitated executive-functioning challenges like inhibition control and cognitive flexibility. These additions deepened the game’s pedagogical value, linking embodied play to metacognitive awareness. The group also converged on a shared phrasing across the game’s components: players complete an action then perform their sequence, exemplifying the collaborative precision of this phase. These sessions built shared ownership in the design process. Teachers learned to articulate their reasoning as design insight, while researchers learned the subtle classroom micro-decisions that make activities flow. The relational phase thus foregrounded partnership as a space of shared inquiry and co-theorizing, where pre-existing trust allowed creativity and reflection to take center stage. Shared intent and trust, rather than predefined outcomes, became the primary driver of progress. These experiences point to a transferable co-design principle: embodied play as a design medium surfaces the tacit pedagogical knowledge that verbal-only co-design processes may overlook. The most generative design contributions such as renaming the opponent bead, keeping a scorecard, and identifying executive function affordances, emerged from the act of playing the game, not from planning discussions. This suggests that co-design partnerships working with embodied or tangible materials should treat playtesting not merely as evaluation but as a primary site of pedagogical insight.

Ecological design: Learning as emergent enactment In the classroom, the design took on ecological properties. Students performed sequences collaboratively, responding to one another’s timing and improvisations. When a student erred, peers reframed it as a collective debugging challenge, “That’s a bug, how  do we fix it?”, transforming mistakes into learning opportunities. Students also began proposing design modifications, such as “make Uncombine bigger on the spinner,” revealing emergent reasoning about probability and fairness in gameplay. The classroom activity revealed new forms of embodied reasoning and moments of ambiguity. Students often debated whether a rule should apply to everyone or only to the player who drew the card. For instance, they questioned whether a Reflect or Combine action would alter everyone’s sequences or just that of the player. These reducing its playfulness. One researcher observed that some confusion seeme d pr oductive, as it encouraged students to explain, negotiate, and revise their understanding rather than simply follow instructions. The classroom operated as an adaptive system. Students worked together to create shared conventions, such as selecting sounds that kept the group’s performance comfortable and cohesive. They also modified rules as new situations emerged. The teacher guided play by posing reflective questions instead of giving direct answers, allowing agency to move between students, materials, and rules. These patterns showed how classroom enactment became an evaluation and an extension of design. Ecological design thus highlights how 2154 implementation itself is a form of co-design: each enactment redefines the boundaries of the game and of the learning it situates.

## Pedagogical design: Translating play into practice

Building on the relational and ecological phases, pedagogical design functioned as a hinge that extended the partnership ecology into collective inquiry for classroom translation. We facilitated a two-day, in-person professional development (PD) at the San Joaquin County Office of Education with 17 elementary and middle school teachers as part of their Hands-on CS in STEM event which supports educators in exploring approaches for integrating computing into their classrooms. We co-created the curriculum for the PD with our partner teachers to align with instructional contexts and current teaching practices. The classroom enactment preceded this workshop, which allowed us to bring the lived experience of students interacting with the game directly into the PD, grounding the translation work in observed practice. The first day was dedicated to introducing the idea of gameplay as a modality for learning. Teachers were familiarized with the early design intentions and the science of learning principles that informed the design of Byte Beads. Teachers were presented with the Byte Beads game and participated in an initial 30-minute play session.

The second day of the PD focused on a structured mapping of the game to computational thinking concepts. An example lesson on arrays was introduced to demonstrate how foundational programming concepts could be introduced through Byte Beads. Following the demonstration, teachers engaged in a guided reflection activity, documenting the strengths and improvement areas of the lesson– game integration, as well as opportunities for cross-disciplinary adaptations. Teachers emphasized the importance of adaptable rules and collaborative invention, noting that “students could create their own spinner actions.” One teacher remarked, “Each sequence feels like a loop where our bodies are repeating code.” Others related the experience to their own fields: “This connects to rhythmic patterns in music,” and “It could introduce math grouping.” This activity also revealed key sources of ambiguity that could hinder the game’s effectiveness in classroom settings. Addressing the feedback from this activity and a written feedback f orm led to game improvements for better flow and conceptual learning.

While the PD was brief, it served a pivotal pedagogical function within the larger partnership ecology. This phase contextualized relational-phase ideas to match classroom realities, while teachers gained confidence to experiment with embodied computing. The design thus became a site of shared pedagogical reasoning where meaning was co-constructed through dialogue, rather than imposed through instruction. Across relational, ecological and pedagogical levels, the Byte Beads project demonstrates partnership as purposeful ecological maintenance (Penuel, 2019). Relational design fostered trust and shared language; ecological design illuminated learning as emerg ent, embodied enactment; and pedagogical design enabled translation into curricular practice. Together, they form an iterative system that sustains learning across contexts. Beyond influencing the game’s evolution, the co-design process itself became a setting for teacher creativity and professional growth. Through joint naming, rule revision, and reflection, teachers contributed as co-authors of both the game and its classroom meaning, building on their knowledge while expanding their confidence and agency as designers. Teacher participation often faces the pressure of competing goals and limited time (Tayne et al., 2024). The phased partnership structure provided space for fl exible engagement that changed in depth over time while maintaining shared intent and continuity. We view partnership not as a method of implementation but as a medium for learning. By centering teachers’ expertise and students’ improvisational agency, it creates conditions for jointly making sense of practice and enacting equity grounded in local values. The classroom functioned as a living ecology traversing multiple settings: design knowledge generated in research meetings traveled to a seventh-grade classroom, and then into a regional PD workshop, accumulating new meaning at each transition. This is  precisely what a learning ecologies lens makes visible, that the boundaries of a learning environment are continuously redrawn by the relationships moving through them.

Rather than treating the relational conditions of this partnership as a limitation, we invite future partnerships to ask what relational infrastructure in their own community, whether familial, cultural, or historical, can serve as their bridge. Different communities will find different answers, and surfacing those answers is itself a productive act of partnership design. For learning scientists, this framing invites a shift from designing interventions to cultivating ecologies of participation. Purposeful partnerships, such as those in Byte Beads, show how inquiry can grow from shared intent, how design can evolve through trust and teaching expertise, and how learning can take shape through embodied, collective action.
