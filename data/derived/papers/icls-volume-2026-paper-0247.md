As nations address unprecedented socio-economic and environmental challenges, the critical need to cultivate individuals capable of leveraging their knowledge to solve real-world problems is paramount  (Songer & Kali, 2022). The international science standards and guidelines emphasize this necessity, highlighting that students must actively apply their knowledge to make sense of the world (National Research Council, 2012; OECD, 2023). The goal of science learning has shifted  from the memorization of isolated  facts to the application of knowledge (Sawyer, 2022). This focus on Knowledge-In-Use represents a central aim of contemporary science education, encouraging students to integrate scientific knowledge and practices to explain compelling phenomena and solutions to complex problems. In the U.S., this vision is reflected the NGSS performance expectations [PEs] that integrates the three-dimensions [3D] of scientific knowledge: disciplinary core ideas, science and engineering practices, and crosscutting concepts (NGSS, 2013).  To support and sustain student growth to develop usable knowledge, assessment tasks must integrate all three dimensions and teachers need to track progress  toward the PEs (Gane et al., 2024). Learning p rogressions [LPs] meet this need by providing a developmental perspective that delineate increasingly sophisticated pathways of understanding through a domain (Duncan & Castro-Faix, 2024). LPs can guide instruction, curriculum, and assessment  and help teachers monitor how students integrate and apply knowledge over time. Consequently, LP-based assessments provide diagnostic information, allowing students to reflect on their performance and advance toward proficiency (Kaldaras et al., 2024). However, analyzing and scoring student-generated models is time-consuming and presents challenges for teachers. Leveraging artificial intelligence [AI], researchers have developed diverse approaches to automatically assess student performance, often showing convincing accuracy and agreement with human scoring. However, a remaining challenge is in constructing a validity argument for AI-based scores, specifically in how AI-generated scores align with the response features or underlying construct on the rubric of LP-based assessment (Ferrera & Qunbar, 2022).  Establishing this transparency would substantially enhance the quality and trustworthiness of information derived from automated scoring systems. For example, student-generated models are a powerful way for students to demonstrate 3D learning performance, and several studies have successfully developed image recognition to evaluate students’ models as a classification task (Lee et al., 2023). Despite the favorable performance of AI in scoring student models at the image scale, the classification approach  often fails to provide interpretable features o f the model itself, such as the number or type of specific  symbols. This failure is critical because scientific modeling proficiency extends beyond superficial artistic expression. The modeling practice requires students to express their understanding in ways that reflect the deep integration of 3Ds of scientific knowledge to explain phenomena (Schwarz et al., 2017). Current classification methods can miss this, integrated proficiency, often confusing scientific reasoning with detailed drawings.

To address these issues, we conceptualize evaluating student models as  an object detection task. This enables identification of both  the type and location of specific symbols and conceptual components, which are 1717 then used to score student performance. These specific symbols are key features of the rubric and are directly aligned with the LP levels. This study explores an image recognition scoring system using a dual-model architecture to improve the accuracy and consistency of LP-based assessment. Further, the system  ensures the validity of the scores against the LP levels, which is a necessary foundation for providing meaningful feedback to students in developing usable knowledge. The research questions are: (1) How accurately and consistently does the object detection model identify and localize specific  symbols within diverse student-generated models? (2) How accurate is the scoring system in evaluating students’ performance in LP-based assessment?

## Conceptual framework: LP-based vision using AI approach

We use the LP-based vision as the conceptual framework to guide the development of student 3D performance integrating an AI approach to efficiently and reliably analyze complex performance-based tasks (Kaldaras et al., 2024). This framework is  structured through three stages (Figure 1a): Stage1.Developing task and collecting responses: We applied a modified evidence-centered design process to develop assessment items, rubrics, and LP levels. Ensuring alignment between the assessment and the LP is critical for measuring 3D learning. The LP articulates the validated pathways for how understanding and practices develop, providing the foundation for valid inferences about student performance. Therefore, tasks and rubrics must be explicitly aligned with the LP to ensure they accurately capture students’ progressive sophistication in achieving t he targeted PE (Kaldaras et al., 2021). Stage 2. Analyzing student’s responses: Student performance is assessed via the image recognition scoring system. By applying the LP-aligned rubric, the system identifies meaningful symbols on student models, transforming this information into input for the scoring model, which works to enhance accuracy and consistency with human score. Stage 3. Providing feedback: To support student growth along the progression, grouping rules were designed to categorize and characterize performance into distinct groups, enabling the provision of targeted, meaningful feedback aligned with the LP. ## Method

We randomly selected 654 students’ electronically drawn models from the “Electroscope” modeling task within a high school physical science curriculum, which prompts students to model why metallic foil leaves move apart as a charged rod approaches an electroscope (Figure 2a.1). Students used icons or pencil tools to represent point charges and arrows to indicate the strength and direction of forces, or  charge movement (Figure 2b. 2). These models were previously human scored using the rubric in Figure 2c with high inter-rater reliability (Krippendorf’s alpha > 0.8) (Li et al., 2025). Owing to restricted log-files from the platform, researchers could only access time stamps and image files. Because the logs did not capture individual symbols, we manually labeled the models for five specific symbols based on the codebook (Figure 2b. 1). This process showed high agreement between two raters: agreement on the symbol type > 0.95, and the Intersection over Union [IoU] reached 0.95 for IoU.50. We then followed a workflow (Figure 1b) to develop the image recognition scoring system. Codebook for manual labeling task To identify specific symbols in  student models, we started  by unpacking the analytic rubric to define necessary symbols and expected locations for  each category (Figure 2c). This initial step is critical because the rubric statements are directly aligned with the LP levels, making accurate symbol detection foundational to valid assessment. For example, Category 1 requires point charges on the rod in scenario A, where the charges are the specific symbols, and the rod denotes location. These symbol-location pairs were compiled into a final codebook (Figure 2d). We define a meaningful symbol as one that is both correct and correctly placed, which allows the system to filter out non-essential or misplaced symbols during the automated scoring process. 1718 Figure 2 The Assessment Task, Student-Generated Model, Analytic Rubric, and Codebook for Manual Labeling Task Developing image recognition scoring system We developed the image recognition scoring system using a dual-model architecture connected sequentially in a single pipeline. The first model is an object detection model that functions as the feature extractor. We fine-tuned the YOLOv9 model (Wang et al., 2024) on a custom dataset to precisely identify and localize specific symbols within student models.  The second model is a multilayer perceptron [MLP] scoring model employed for performance evaluation. This MLP predicts student performance for each rubri c category. As illustrated in Figure 1b, we divided this workflow into three distinct steps: Step 1: Data preprocessing. We divided the collected images into two sub sets: the custom dataset (n=496) for model development and the evaluation dataset (n=158) for system evaluation. All images were initially manually labeled for five specific symbols according to the codebook and critical for LP-level assignment (Figure 2b.2). For the custom dataset, these images were divided for model training and testing in Step 2: training (n=396), validation (n=50), and testing dataset (n=50). Step 2: Model development. The object detection and the scoring model in our system were both developed using the same set of images. For object detection model, we utilized NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs and the YOLOv9 model’s default parameters for our setup. During training, we employed an early stopping mechanism, to prevent overfitting and to optimize performance. To evaluate the fine-tuned model, we assessed its performance using mean Average Precision [mAP] and F1 score, examining these metrics for both overall and individual label s within train ing and testing phase  (Table 1). For  the scoring model, we employed the MLP designed for multi-label classification. This MLP predicts scores in five  rubric categories based on the feature s derived from the object detection model.  The MLP model was trained using the Adam optimizer and BCEWithLogitsLoss with early stopping. Step 3: System evaluation. We evaluated the image recognition scoring system using the evaluation dataset. After detecting and filtering symbols according to the rubric, a scoring model predicted student performanc e. The system’s overall performance was then determined by evaluating these AI scores against expert assigned scores.

Addressing the challenges of AI-based image analysis, we employed the YOLOv9 model for object detection and aligned its training with the rubric-aligned symbols.  The YOLOv9 model effectively recognized the specific symbols in students’ generated models, ensuring that each symbol was  accurately identified and localized. For instance, the model successfully detected negative charges  and bidirectional arrows in students ’ models (Figure 2b.2). These meaningful symbols are critical indicators of student proficiency, as they directly align with the LP levels. For instance, detecting point  charges and arrows provides evidence that students understand how  charge transfer produces charged objects from neutral ones  and the magnitude and direction of the electric force are related to the movement of the leaves. The model’s ability to accurately identify these meaningful symbols directly supports the valid assessment of the integrated knowledge application required to a chieve specific LP levels. Then, we evaluated model performance using F1 score and mAP, comparing YOLOv9 to Faster R-CNN, another object detection m odel. The results indicate that YOLOv9 outperformed Faster R-CNN in mAP, achieving the mAP.50 of 0.91. In comparison, Faster R-CNN recorded a lower mAP.50 of 0.86. YOLOv9 performed well across 1719 individual symbols, with most achieving high mAP value (Table 1). This reflects strong symbol-level reliability, ensuring accurate identification of features of student models an essential for interpreting conceptual components. After fine-tuning both the object detection and scoring models, we evaluated the entire image recognition scoring system using the evaluation dataset. T he system demonstrated strong and reliable performance in predicting students’ performance across five  rubric categories. Furthermore, the system achieved a high subset accuracy (0.84), which indicates  a performance for correctly identifying all categories within a single student model. Evaluation metrics for each individual category further indicated strong reliability (Table 2). These results confirm the system’s ability to accurately predict students’ performance across the entire progression of complex conceptual understanding. Notably, Category 5 exhibited the highest productive performance. This high accuracy is crucial, as this category assesses the complex conceptual component of electric force. Since this  advanced proficiency is necessary for assigning higher LP levels, the system’s robust performance indicates it can reliably measure students’ LP levels grounded in complex conceptual understanding. While previous research has demonstrated that AI can achieve high accuracy in scoring student’s model (Lee et al., 2023), there remains a gap in how AI can extract evidence to interpret the specific nature of student understanding and align those models with LP levels. This study addressed this gap by developing an image recognition scoring system tailored for LP-based assessments. Our results demonstrate that a dual-model architecture effectively mitigates current automated assessment limitations. The object detection model demonstrated high reliability in identifying rubric aligned symbols (mAP.50=0.89), and the full system achieved strong agreement with expert scoring (F1 score=0.90; subset accuracy=0.86). Specifically, the YOLOv9 model proved to be a robust tool for detecting the location and type of rubric-aligned symbols with high accuracy (mAP.50 range from 0.65 to 0.99). Subsequently, the MLP utilized this granular data to achieve high agreement in scoring student performance across all rubric categories (Accuracy range from 0.93 to 0.98; Kappa values range from 0.76 to 0.95). We argue that identifying specific symbols (e.g., point charges and arrows) within student-generated models is essential for bridging the gap between AI automated scoring and conceptual interpretation. Because these symbols are explicitly derived from the LP-aligned rubric, they function as construct relevant features of student’s performances. By grounding the scoring decisions based on detection of these rubric defined symbols, the system produces AI-generated scores using observable evidence in student models directly tied to the LP.  This alignment strengthens the validity of score interpretations beyond surface level image classifications. The core innovation of this study lies in coupling object detection with an LPaligned rubric to assign meaning to the identified specific symbols. By accurately localizing specific symbols, the system measures the representational features of integrated 3D learning proficiency. This symbol-level interpretability is vital for providing meaningful, LP-aligned feedback and advancing in developing usable knowledge. This approach represents a fundamental shift from holistic image classification to symbol-level detection in student-generated models.
