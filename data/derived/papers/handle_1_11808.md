# Data Literacy Beyond Logic: Entanglements of Logical Reasoning and Emotion in a Data Science Camp

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Both dis-and misinformation based on data exploit the belief that more data inevitably leads to more informed decisions. This study explores how logical reasoning is entangled with emotion and technology in sense-making processes. We present a case study of a middle-school student working with environmental data in a data science camp, a context paralleling “in-the-wild” misinformation. Drawing on current scholarship on embodied and affective dimensions of data work, we argue that emotional entanglements shape how students interpret evidence. Results point to the need for educational approaches that move beyond logical reasoning to include these affective dimensions.

## Introduction

In a podcast episode, author Yuval Harari rhetorica lly asks: How can humans be so intelligent and, at the same time, act in so many ways that might cause harm to society, the planet, and ultimately our species its elf? Harari suggests that the answer concerns a widely held bel ief that if people have more data, “better decision s” will be made (Noah, 2024). That assumption has roots in Cartesian rationalism and undergirds both well-intentioned factchecking initiatives a nd school curricula: people can find the  truth (or canonical knowledge) themselves if you simply provide them with the facts. The problem is that this assumption overly relies on individual ra tionality while overlooking other sense-making dimensions that are as important. In this paper, we explore the interweaving of logical reasoning, emotions, and technology as children interpret evidence collected first-hand in a data science workshop. Our goal is to provide a glimpse into the  complex processes taking place when individuals ca n “do their own research” about personally relevant issues. We hope to draw parallels between educational and “in-thewild” settings where those calls for agential inquiry are weaponized for political ends. This study is part of a broader research agenda on mis/disinformation (Russo & Blikstein, 2022; Russo & Blikstein, 2023; Russo et al., 2024) in which we investigate twisted knowledge construction—that is, reasoning processes that superficially follow logical steps but lead to unwarranted conclusions. We draw on research about how ill-intentioned groups exploit humans' willingness to l ink evidence to promote ideological agendas that ar e harmful to democracy (Marwick & Partin, 2022), incl uding by distorting the process of knowledge constr uction and evidence interpretation to promote misogyny (Ro thermel, 2023) and radical nationalism (Willaert, 2024). In previous work, we analyzed social media data to inv estigate how authoritarian political leaders in the  Global South explored the sociotechnical affordances of so cial media and strategies of collective sense-making to keep their constituency mobilized around an extremist ag enda, adopting linguistic and rhetorical devices th at implied epistemic agency (Russo & Bli kstein, 2021, 2022; Russo et al., 2023, 2024). In the same way that individuals collect information and evidence about political ev ents to inform their voting, we are interested in s imilar types of agentic investigative processes that children em ploy when collecting and interpreting data to exa mine meaningful issues in their communities. This paper extends our previous work on the entanglement of lo gical reasoning, affect, and knowledge construction—often mediated by technology—by presenting empirical evidence gathered during a data science camp for youth.

In this paper, we seek to demonstrate that factors usually perceived as adequate supports for learning, like personal experience and availability of data and evidence, can result in outcomes that diverge from designers’ and facilitators’ expectations of an educational ex perience from a constructivist perspective. That is, instead of setting the learner on track to formulating more ad vanced hypotheses, those supports can result in “di storted” learning that confirms previously-held hypotheses. In such cases, technology can legitimize seemingly sound but incomplete meaning-making, reinforcing assumptions that diverge from canonical knowledge. We argue that such learning outcomes emerge both in structured environ ments like our data science camp and in chaotic on es like social media, where learning is a secondary but consistent outcome. This paper is structured as follows: we first intro duce related literature that illuminates the connec tions between digital media-based distorted knowledge construction, contemporar y approaches to data science instruction, and epistemic cognition, that is, how we “vet, acquire, understand, justify, and use knowledge” (Muis et al., 2021). Next, we lay out our methodology, including how we obtained, pre-processed, and analyzed our data and details about our decision on the case study we  present. Following that, we present our findings i n two subsections: (1) a vignette that shows a camper’s p rocesses of sense-making of environmental data; and (2) an analysis of a camper-led investigation; in both sections, we intended to highlight the blending of logical reasoning, emotion, and reliance on technology as the camper interpreted evidence and derived conclusions (see Table 1 for explanations of each of those dimensions). We concl ude with a reflection on the implications of our fi ndings for future research and instruction in similar settings, as well as the next iteration of our camp in the following cycles of our design-based research trajectory (Edelson, 2002). Particul arly, the findings point toward special attention to the fact that, depending on curricular design and pedagogical decisions, learners might leave such data-science camps or curricula with the false impression that g athering abundant evidence is sufficient to reach t he “right” conclusion, even when their datasets may warrant several interpretations.

## Related work

The use of evidence to support thinking has turned from a gold standard in progressive education into an ambiguous, animating principle in subcultures marke d by intolerance and bigotry. Such diversion has be en epitomized by extremist groups who overtly employ t he slogan “do your own research” to promote ideolog ical agendas that present threats to democracy (Tripodi et al., 2023). In online spaces where the extremist group QAnon thrived, users employed strategies to decrypt messa ges that included search and collective evaluation of “evidence” to promote a democracy-threatening conspiracy (Marwick & Partin, 2022). Ot her examples abound of communities whose members engage in intolerant s peech, supposedly supported by evidence. Rothermel

(2020) identified “evide nce-based” misogyny in online communities; Willaert (2024) investigated how some

communities replicate dynamics that resemble schola rly research practices to construct superficially c ohesive claims based on recontextualization of scientific e vidence. Commo n to those examples is a weaponization of rational thinking, science, and technology to advan ce ideologically laden new “truths” that intentionally ignore the social and political dimensions of data and evi dence. A varnish of individual agency permeates that weaponization, adding an impression of autonomous l earning, perverting criticality and putting it at s ervice of a type of informal miseducation (Greenhalgh et al., 2021). Having discussed the challenges posed by knowledge construction that is superficially evidence-based and agential, we now turn to approaches in data sci ence education that might help address the social, cultural, political, and affective dimensions of the  discipline. In their call for “humanistic data scie nce,” Lee et al. (2021) propose a “socio-political layer of mediation within data practices, ” which questions the purposes of using data and evidence, often following the agendas of those in power. Ot her alternative takes on data literacy support critical appreciation of entanglements of data and personal significance. One example is D’Ignazio's (2022) proposition for creative data literacy, which presu pposes democratization of data work and data litera cy in favor of emancipation, similar to other types of literacy. Emancipation is also a stated goal for critical d ata literacy (Tygel & Kirsch, 2016), which draws directly from F reire's critical pedagogy (Freire, 1968/2014) and p opular education. Lanouette (2024) identifies entanglements of emotions, place, and science practice in work with young students and how some emotions shape children's sen se-making of data and sampling strategies. Stornaiuolo (2020), in work involving data practices with high schoolers at a media makerspace, reported how students’ emotions permeate data collection and reporting dyn amics and become part of stories told with data. Le e et al.

(2022) review recent scholarship on data science ed ucation and identify principles of data feminis m (D’Ignazio

& Klein, 2020) in educational interventions reporte d. “Elevating emotions and lived experiences,” one of those principles, prioritizes embodiment, affect, and personally and culturally meaningful experiences over standardized and minimalist standards for work with data. Those examples point to the comingling of sociocultural, emotional, and technical aspects of work with data, suggesting approaches to research and instruction that go beyond purely cartesian rationality in data science instructi on. In this paper, we focus on three of those dimen sions: emotional, technical, and logical.

Previous research has explored the intertwining of emotion, embodiment, and logical reasoning in evaluating information. Chinn et al. (2014) highlight the importance of presenting science as a process permeated by emotions —they argue that general belief in science as a uniq uely logical endeavor may cause discredit of scientific claims when the process is known to have  been influenced by  “non-logical” factors—like emotions. That belief in science as devoid of affect conflict s with scholarly work demonstrating that emotions a re part of knowledge production. For example, Jaber and Hammer  (2016) triangulate reports from professional scien tists and classroom data to argue that emotions are fundamental to “doing science” and that pedagogies should reflect that. S imilarly, emotions are critical components of epist emic cognition, that is, how people "vet, acquire, understand, justify, and use knowledge" (Muis et al., 2021). Cognitivist accounts of learning sustain that specific epistemic emotions—such as confusion and curiosity—predict exploratory behavior (D’Mello & Graesser, 2014), whereas others—e.g., enjoyment— emerge after the expression of beliefs such as the need to justify conclusions using multiple sources of information (Muis et al., 2015). This body of research highlights the importa nce of investigating how emotions and logical reasoning sh ape each other in children's sense-making of evidence they generate firsthand. Such investigations may offer insight into broader social phenomena, such as how individuals become susceptible to ideological agendas based on a "do your own research" claim. This brief literature review connects three complementary fields of inquiry that form the foundations for our study design and analysis. First, ideologically-laden sources that claim to present evidence are involved in the propagation of intolerance and hate speech, highlig hting the importance of attending to values that un derlie evidence rather than relying only on cold rationali ty. The maxim “do your own research” exemplifies ho w that reliance may lead individuals through a trail of mi sleading, harmful e vidence. Second, research in data science instruction has evolved to emphasize personal, cultural, and affective layers of data practices, which opens up an opportunity to reflect on evidence beyond its face value. Finally, research in the learning science s and related fields explores the intersection of rationality and  emotion and their associations with evidence-related sense-making. Together, these three strands frame our inv estigation into the intertwining of logic and emoti on in the sense-making of evidence gathered first-hand.

## Methods

Setting This study is part of a larger research project inv estigating opportunities for community-based data science learning using low-cost sensors connected to a low-cost educational robotics board (GoGo Board). In a series of six-week-long camps at a science museum in the Northwest US, 11-and 12-year-old youth (approximately 20 in each camp) work with portable, low-cost environmental sensors to explore a topic of in terest (framed as “something they want to know more about in their community”). Camp facilitators guide campers through framing questions, collecting data, visualizing and interpr eting it, and creating posters to share their resea rch and conclusions. The case study reported in this paper is based on data collected during the Summer of 2024.

## Focal participant

We chose a case study approach to illuminate how em otions, logical reasoning, and technology enmesh wh en children interpret evidence, as has been used in si milar work (Lanouette, 2022). We chose our focal ca mper, Isabella, following a critical sampling app roach (Flyvbjerg, 2024): following preliminary anal ysis of our larger dataset, we understood that Isabella derived strong claims from her limited corpora of evidence. We hypothesized that this discrepancy could offer insight into how individuals align claims, evidence, and reasoning based on data and associated with affects and personal experience.

Isabella self-identified as a mixed-race U.S. national whose primary language is Englis h at home and school. At the time of camp, she was an upcoming 7th grader attending a public school in the city where the camp was located, where she was born and raised. Her int erests include watching movies, and she reported th at were she not at camp that week, she would be “at the bea ch or the swimming pool or hanging out with friends.” She was attending a summer camp for the first time. Her  stated topic of interest va ried during camp but generally revolved around water quality in the local river and littering in her community. Given the breadth of her interests and camp curriculum, she tried several types of env ironmental sensors throughout the week; for her pro ject, she worked with a sensor for Total Dissolved Solids (TDS).

Data sources and analysis The analysis included data from curricular activiti es and structured data collection tasks run by rese archers. The corpus, therefore, included end-of-day reflections (video); group work (video); 2 roun ds of in-depth interviews (video); and first-person camera recordings of museum floor exploratio ns (video); camper's final project report poster and other artifacts, such as a community map  and worksheets (pictures). Daily exit tickets answered by campers were consulted as complementary data. Artif acts were analyzed using Freeman and Mathison’s (20 09) framework focusing on subject matter and image creation. In the subsection below, we explain how we analyzed video data, which constituted the basis for our analysis—Figure 1 visually explains the analytical steps we took. ## Findings

In this section, we present an analysis of Isabella ’s investigation where we triangulate video data and camperproduced artifacts (final report poster, community map), providing a comprehensive portrayal of the entanglements of emotion, reason, and reliance on t echnology. First, we refe r to a part of camp when Isabella debates whether to focus on the relationship betwee n water pollution and the ferry that operates on a local river or the problem of littering and rubble from constru ction works that she suspects affect both the river  and streets in her neighborhood. In the second part, we analyze her final report poster and support our analysis with segments of our video data containing references to the process of creating the poster. Defining the focus of investigation: emotion filling logical gaps. Isabella's reasoning was significantly shaped by af fect related to community, especially those associa ted with places and people. That affect acted both as motivation and as a lens for interpreting  what she observed. When discussing ideas to focus on, she often displays frustration with other people in her neighborhood, who are “jus t gross” because they litter, vandalize trash cans, and throw trash in the river. That causes pollution, she infers, but also closures to the swimming pool in the park (“la st week, a kid pooped in the pool”), which she regr ets. Affect/emotions and logical reasoning clearly inter twine at other different points in our data set. Fo r example, in the segment below, Isabella is explaining to a researcher why she decided to focus on water pollution in the river instead of investigating the littering problem in her neighborhood (about which she had initially been vocal): There is a lot of construction, and the constructio n leads to a lot of the rats because there is already construction, but then, people litter a lot, so this causes even like, food, food for the rats to eat, and they keep coming, so… Here, we identify a clear albeit fragmented causal chain: she associated construction rubble (present in the community map she drew on the evening before) and p eople littering with an increase in the rat populat ion. However, she did not substantiate the claim with objective evidence. Rather, percolating through the logic-based causal chain, there are at least two emotional comp onents: her frustration with people who litter (whi ch she verbalized a few times during the camp) and the loc al connection, both associated with her home (aroun d where scaffolds abound, according to her community map) a nd the river (where she used to go swimming, “befor e she knew any of that”). Those emotions work to “fill the gaps” left by the limited explanatory power of logic alone. Embodiment and technology shaping reasoning Another (incomplete) causal chain is present in other episodes, such as the dialogue below: Researcher:  So, what made you change your mind and pursue a different question? Isabella:  I feel like rats are a big problem, but also pollutio n is a big problem, too, because it affects the planet way more. And rats li ve (...) here, and we can't really get rid of them because this is their home, so... [giggles] In that dialogue, Isabella states that rats “live here,” so “we can't really get rid of them,” the logic here being that we could not “evict” the rats because this is their home as well as humans’. The comparison between the scale of the problem is also no teworthy: rats are seen as a local-scale issue, whereas pollution has planet-wide consequences, which denotes some level of gesturing to local-based affect. There is an intriguing rationale behind her decision, as she chooses to employ technology r esources to investigate an issue whose solutions are further from her reach than the one she formally abandons (formally because, as we will see below, she keeps revisiting the littering problem throughout the camp). In an a pparent paradox, she leaves aside an issue with who se roots she seems closer —her “Next Steps” include community activism work su ch as convincing neighbo rs to stop littering.

Following that, the researcher openly asks her about her feelings toward the issue: Isabella:  Do you think there is fish in the Western River? Here, Isabella is conjecturing about  the consequences of trash in the ocean to assess th e importance of her work, which is supported by her affect toward s ea life (e.g., turtles). Embodiment and physical cl oseness are relevant in her decision-making process, as she feels first-hand the effects of trash in the river, a place whe re she used to go swimming. The segment illuminates the en tanglement of logical reasoning and affect/emotions  in situations closely connected to Isabella's process of defining a topic. However, we understand that there may have also been some lure in the science and technology that enabled the sensor set at various points in the early stages of the process. The sensors and board set became epistemic supports that lent scientific credibility to the process. For Isabella, the GoGo Board and sensors look “so sci-en-tif-ic,” suggesting that the apparatus had an aura of scientific objectivity. In an early end-of-day reflection she says she feels surprised by the size/capacity of the board (“it's so small, but there's so many things you cando with it”). When explicitly prompted about things that surprised her in camp, she mentioned the GoGo Board  and that it was “really cool that (...) a group of  people created that themselves.” In context, those quotes de monstrate how the sensor gear provided the process of interpreting evidence with an objectivity conventionally associated with science. Crafting a poster: beyond the technical dimensions in community science inquiry In the section below, we describe Isabella's final report poster and how that artifact materializes the commingling of emotion, logical reasoning, and interaction with  technology. Isabella chose to formally focus on the issue of pollution caused by the ferry that runs in the loca l river—formally because, as we demonstrate below, she still brings data, arguments, and reflections about litte ring. Although at times we opted to highlight one o f the dimensions, that was a stylistic decision—we argue that emotion, logical reasoning, and technological mediation interweave, and they all emerge concomitantly, just at alternating intensities. This artifact is relevant in this context for a few  reasons. First, the poster is where the camper rec orded her research questions and conclusions, which were supposed to be, respectively, the object of her inv estigation and what she found out by coordinating technology (the sensor set, digital interfaces) and logical rea soning. Second, the poster was meant to be a public artifact summarizing a week's worth of work, representing emotional involvement both in its crafting and presentation, as we will see below. Thus, both the finished poste r and the process of creating it presented intertwinings of e motion and logical reasoning. We first provide an a nalysis of the poster based on Freeman and Mathison's (2009) g uidelines for the analysis of visual data. We dec ided to present evidence both from the “Research question” section of her poster and other areas, as we unders tand that, besides the conclusion explicitly stated under her “Research question,” parts of her poster, such as “ Next steps,” also offer us a glimpse into those interweavings.

Emotional engagement in layout components, process, and in dialogue with technical dimensions Unlike a purely technical report of her process and findings, Isabella adds some personal touch to the poster: handdrawn smileys, hearts, and stars decorate the area where she claims authorship as well as the “Next St eps.” Additionally, personal expressio n blends with technical aspects in the title of the  section containing the charts: she includes two exclamation marks (“DATA Graphs!!”) and replaces the dots beneath them with hand-drawn hearts. Place-based affects are present in a picture she included: it shows the bathing area in the river, “where people try and swim” near the camper's home —she had included a drawn version of that location i n her community map. The picture shows graffiti and debri s and a makeshift rock pathway leading to the water. She circled two areas that look particularly bad (a gap b etween a stone plate and the floor, possibly indica ting spots that are dirty), and the picture label says that, t o reach the water, “you have to step in all that di rt which is not sanitary.” The place is where she used to go swim “before she knew any of that.” Isabella felt anxious about how her audience would perceive the poster during the showcase happening on the last day of camp. Before even starting to wo rk on the piece, she verbally expressed worry that “nobody will understand” one of her charts—one of the default plots generated by the digital graphing tool CODAP. That automation did simplify the process of creating cha rts but might have omitted significant contextual i nformation while still offering a superficial impression of ob jectivity. She ultimately included that chart on the poster, even though the graph did not contribute much to her arg umentation.  Also, the day before the presentation,  she said she felt anxious presenting to others (her parents and other campers), which was also a reason for her  to feel “stressed out.” Associated with those expressions o f anxiety was her desire for her project to look go od because that is what it means “to have a good research proj ect.” Those markers indicate how emotions (stress, anxiety, place-based affect, aesthetic qualities) and rationality (charts, visual evidence) alternate in importance during her process and on the final product.

Local environment and community identity An intriguing thread in Isabella's work throughout the camp was her shifting attention across scales of the pollution issue, as seen in her defining the focus of investi gation. On her poster, this shifting is visible thr ough the divergence between her rese arch questions, most of the evidence presented, and  the next steps she devised. One of her stated research questions was: “Does the river get dirtier as the ferry drives by?” to which she answers that “Yes it does. The ferry releases a lot of toxic fum es which end up going into the river which makes it pol luted.” Despite the claim that the ferry is the cause of ri ver pollution, the evidence she presented on her po ster does not fully support that conclusion: she shows charts of total dissolved solids (TDS) levels in the river water that do not account for possible variations caused by the ferry; the picture shows a part of the river bank whose sanitary conditions do not seem to be related to the ferry (graffiti, rubble); an annotated map where she drew the ferry route and circled the spot where the bathing area i s (“a tiny beach which (sic) sand that allows you t o swim but people don't know how dirty it really is”).

Her “Next Steps” section reflects her personal invo lvement with her community but nothing directly related to her formal investigation of the ferry's impact on the river. That assemblage of evidence, s tated conclusion, and action points demonstrates a range of affects related to location: saying that most pe ople (in her community) do not know how dirty the river is implies that Isabella sees herself detached from that group (because now she knows about the river water pollution). Sti ll, there are some signs  of hope in individual, local action in her next steps: her words demonstrate that she is willing to try and persuade neighbors to stop behaving in certain ways (either littering or bathing in the dirty beac h). Preventing those behaviors would benefit comm unity members (who could bathe in the area again) and sea life (protected from the impact of trash).

## Discussion

In our study, we sought to identify mechanisms that  cause the emergence of unintended consequences of overreliance on logical reasoning for the interpret ation of data and evidence. In a summer camp, we sa w that, counterintuitively, some elements that are usually seen as effective supports for learning (like personal experience and technology) can be misleading. We argue that in  cases where those elements are manipulated by ill-intentioned agents, the consequences can be disastr ous. In the real world, examples go beyond charts d isplaying water quality data and involve “science-backed” charts that allegedly display the prominence of men, white people (Willaert, 2024), or citizens from wealthy nations —to cite a few examples. Those charts, perceived as a materialization of scientific evidence, might potentially be paired with isolated interactions with minoritiz ed groups to steer seemingly autonomous processes of s ense-making through ideologically laden conclusions. Indeed, the problem is much larger than a data science camp as people spend years “learning” informally beyond their school trajectories through social media and other means. Also, we understand that although our case study provides deep insight into a camper's processes of interpreting evidence, we do  not intend to assume that our findings generalize beyond this context: we acknowledge the limitations of our study, most notably regarding age difference and distinct uses of technologies and me dia. Still, we understand that this study can help other researchers identify structural parallels in constructs  like overreliance on personally meaningful but wea kly supported claims legitimized by technology. With those caveats in mind, we draw considerations on what can be improved in our data science camp in the spirit of design-based research. Design recommendations Exercise critical self-assessment of emotion's role in interpreting evidence. Although it may seem trivial that affect is part of  reasoning, it is critical to acknowledge that affe ct tied to personally relevant issues like pollution can sometimes conflict with reasoning based on firsthand-collected data. For example, in our case, Is abella frequently saw trash in the river and displa yed frustration about people who littered in the vicinities of the waterfront. As we  observed in her poster, she connected those two fa cts—water pollution and littering—even though her sensor readings did not directly support her causal claim. Isabella's case illustrates how affect interacts with logical reasoning as one interprets data, especially when the issue is personally relevant, as river water pollution was to Isabella.

We do not intend to question the impact of litterin g on water pollution —indeed, trash disposal might have been one of the causes of poor water quality. However, in Isabella's case,  other causes of pollution might have been overlooked (such as the ferry). Also, we do not mean that affect is always negative for reas oning with data: we agree with the growing body of research th at advocates for the inclusion of subjective, personal, and embodied dimensions in data work (e.g., Lanouette, 2024). Rather, we argue that data science instruction should attend to these dimensions that are often disregarded based on the assumption that data is inherently objective. Emphasize that explanations exist beyond what can be measured quantitatively. Another way to see Isabella's experience in interac tion with her data is to consider that her experience and affect amplified her data's explanatory power. That leads us to the conjecture that curricula sh ould emphasize that explanations go beyond what can be measured quantitatively, especially in structured learning settings like a data science camp, thus encouraging students to critical ly evaluate the lim itations of data. In our example, Isabella highlighted in her poster the charts that proved that she o btained readings of TDS close to 300 —the threshold value that she found in a Google search. Interestingly, her attempt to explain water pollution went beyond the data she collected and included her experience of taking  the ferry and seeing water pollution firsthand. Al though her conclusion was not entirely correct, Isabella's rel iance on both quantitative data and her experience hints to the potential of combining both—as long as there are curricular safeguards to limit  the perceived explanatory power of each. Curricula could provide supports for learning that guide students through reflection on the limitations of data and affect when studying a topic. This is not to say that affect should replace data, but perhaps  it would be beneficial for students to see the complementary roles of each of those resources.

## Emphasize the nuances of assumptions, evidence, and conclusions

One important consideration to be made concerns the  nuances of logical components of campers’ reasonin g processes and consequential research practices. As we engage in the upcoming iterations of this multi-year research project, we understand that care should be taken in guiding campers in their exploration of assumptions, hypotheses, interpretations of evidence, and other steps in the process. Indeed, some other types of i nformal learning experiences have the benefit of lower stakes trial and error—consider a robotics camp in which children can experiment with digital tools and create fun pr ototypes; at the end of the workshop, a miniature e lectric car might be faulty and have each of its wheels spin in  a different direction. That “failure” will be frus trating, of course, but that will hardly have negative epistemo logical implications: on the contrary, if a prototy pe does not work as expected, there will be practical opportuni ties to learn about the failure in very concrete wa ys: maybe tweaking programming blocks will fix the problem, or using a different type of axis. Conversely, in a data science camp, the consequences of having faulty assumptions  validated by the grown-ups in the room might be long-lasting. In the next iterations of the camp, participants should be able to discuss in more detail the nuances involved in interpreting evidence and the limitations of the evidence they gather using environmental sensors.

## Conclusion

In this paper, we intended to demonstrate how emotion, technology, and logical reasoning interact in situations in which individuals have the opportunity to “do their  own research” about a topic of their interest. Our  ultimate goal was to demonstrate that those dimensions deeply intersect in ways that may affect how we reach conclusions based on evidence and communicate those conclusions. Even though we worked with children in a setting with low political stakes (at least those related to ins titutional and party politics), we identified proce sses that may be present in the future when those children examine m ore complex topics. We conjecture that those intert winings are even more intense in highly controversial topics, which are, by nature, associated with emotion and, by design, influenced by technology —such as institutional and party politics. Consequen tly, initiatives that overly rely on people's rationality to prevent the proliferation o f (faulty) evidence-based intolerance and polarization will unlikely yield satisfying results. What is needed instead ar e approaches that attend to convoluted reasoning processes motivated by emotion and reason and suppo rted by technologies that are susceptible to flaws or are simply not designed to inform decision-making.

We end with a cautiously optimistic response to Harari's question that opens this paper: the answer does not imply more data, collected by more and more adv anced gadgets, with ever-expanding processing power. As the adage goes, technology is only as good as the use we make of it. That is true in the case of data-based reasoning, both in a highly-structured educational setting and in settings like  social media, where a chaotic web of relationships, content, and claims prevails. We hope our design-based study can inspire curricula and public policy intended to hinder the spread of hatred and misinformation allegedly based on faulty data and evidence.
