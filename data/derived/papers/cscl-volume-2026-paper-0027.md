# Bridging Group and Individual: Transitional Spaces in Collaboration

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how learning traverses between collective and individual levels in collaborative learning by introducing the concept of Transitional Space, the temporary and interactional zone where collective knowledge constructions outpace or below individual understanding. Using an approach that integrates students’ knowledge complexity levels and participation patterns, we model both group-level dynamics and individual trajectories. Results from two groups reveals that while higher-level engagement often align with increased knowledge at the group level, most individual students within the group deviate significantly from these patterns. These divergence highlights the fragile and contingent nature of transitions, which indicates that students must need a space to reconcile group-generated ideas with their own thinking, the Transitional Space. By identifying occurrence of such divergence, this study makes transitio ns empirically visible. The findings also contribute to theory by challenging assumptions of a spontaneous internalization and providing a methodological bridge between collective discourse and individual learning trajectories.

Collaborative learning has been widely recognized as a powerful approach to fostering critical thinking, shared knowledge construction and collaboration skills. Decades of research have illuminated how students build intersubjectivity through group cognition, socially shared regulation, and collective knowledge building (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 20 21; Schnaubert & Bodemer, 2019, 2022; Stahl, 2006, 2016). Yet much of this literature implicitly assumes that what is achieved at the group level naturally transfers to the individual: that once students participate in joint meaning-making, they will internalize and extend those insights  through the social interactions (Lawrence & Valsiner, 1993;  Rogoff, 1995). However, accumulating evidence shows that this assumption does not always hold, pointing a gap in understanding how collective achievements translate into individual learning. For example, empirical studies consistently show uneven benefits across learners, with some students readily transforming collective ideas into personal understanding, while others remain at the periphery of group discourse, rarely express their ideas aligns with the emerging group knowledge construction (Clarke, 2016; Sedova & Navratilova, 2020). The unevenness is particularly salient when considering how individual participation trajectories unfold. Participation in group activities does not guarantee that all students will integrate, reconcile, or deepen the knowledge that emerges collectively (Cang et al., 2024; Puntambekar et al., 2023). Rather than assuming that group-level engagement guarantees individual uptake, or individual contribution led to group work improvement, we argue that learners must move intentionally between social participation and individual sense-making (Damşa & Ludvigsen, 2016; Järvelä & Hadwin, 2013; Olsen et al., 2021). And this movement is contingent, fragile, and design  dependent, and it is reciprocal in that collaboration both supports and demands conscious individual engagement.  This paper examines how learning moves across levels, between the group and individuals. We introduce the concept of the Transitional Space: the discursive and cognitive zone in which students reconcile group knowledge with their own developing understandings. And we investigate how this Transitional Space  emerges and can be identified in technology-enhanced collaborative science learning, and how individual trajectories vary in traversing it.  To do this, it requires us to investigate the group collaborative patterns and individual’s understanding trajectories. Therefore, the research questions are:

1. What are the two group’s participation trajectories? How does it imply

2. What are the individual student’s trajectories in the group collaboration?

3. How does the alignment or misalignment between individuals’ learning trajectories and group

progress during collaboration indicate the emergence of Transitional Space ? Theoretical perspective Bridging collective and individual learning 165 Collaborative learning theory, particularly sociocultural and knowledge-building traditions, has richly described how shared meaning-making unfolds at the group level (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2021; Stahl, 2006). Mutual regulation, group awareness and group cognition are treated as central mechanisms of productive collaboration (Järvelä & Hadwin, 2013; Schnaubert & Bodemer, 2022). However, this orientation has often privileged collective outcomes, leaving the individual’s uptake of group-generated knowledge less explained. While other studies, especially cognitive perspective grounded, highlight individual’s change by treating the collaboration as context. This approach makes it difficult to explain how specific features of group interaction shapes individual learning mechanism of group collaboration. Neither group-focused nor individual-centered approach fully reveal the learning traverse levels adequately. Instead of treating individual and collective learning as two units that can be analyzed exclusively, they must be brought together (Hakkarainen, 2009; Stahl, 2013). Vygotskian perspectives emphasize that internalization emerges from participation in social interaction (Lawrence & Valsiner, 1993; Rogoff, 1995). Yet internalization of collective level’s ideas is not spontaneous and effortless, especially when new ideas conflict with i ndividual’s existing understanding. Leaners must bridge the space between public discourse and private understanding (Matusov, 1998; Wells, 2015). This transition varies across individuals and contexts, shaped by differences in prior knowledge, interactional positioning, and task design (Järvelä et al., 2015). The challenge is therefore to identify when this transition  between levels emerges and how to scaffold students’ understanding when it emerges  (Damşa, 2014; Olsen et al., 2021). It indicates the need of a conceptual lens that can account for transition between levels, when collective knowledge becomes available or unavailable for individual sense-making. This study propose s the concept of Transitional Space, identifying when transitions between levels occurs and explain how it is enacted. Transitional Space and its operationalization Conceptualization of Transitional Space The Transitional Space is when individuals engage with group ideas generated in collaboration, either integrating, adapting, or resisting them to refine students’ own thinking as students are in the discourse of collaborative work (Figure 1). As a result, individual’s learning can move across levels, from individual to collective or vice versa, in the collaborative activity (Stahl, 2013). In other words, it is a temporary and interaction-dependent zone in collaborative learning where group-level ideas become available for individual uptake  (White, 2018). It is theorized as a construct linking group and individual learning with two key features. First, it is enacted because it is not pre-planned or structured in advance, but emerges dynamically in interaction, specifically when group achieve understanding advancement, or higher order of reasoning that go beyond any single participant’s prior understanding  (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995; Stahl, 2016). As such, a zone where individual might take up new ideas, emerges through the group discourse. But whether that happens depends on their ability to engage, process and internalize what the group has constructed (Isohätälä et al., 2020). Second, it is interactional  because the existence and accessibility of this zone depend on how learners  interact one another, including how they interpret and build on each other’s ideas. The discursive moves, like talk enabling joint sensemaking, and  shared artifacts that externalize and stabilize collective thinking  are all possible ways to interactions (Hakkarainen & Seitamaa-Hakkarainen, 2022; Suthers, 2006). Yet, access to this space is uneven. Some learners actively enter and engage with group ideas while others remain peripheral, missing the opportunity to reconcile their own thinking with the group’s (Sedova & Navratilova, 2020). Figure 1 Transitional Space This points it out that Transitional Space is not guaranteed, but a fragile and contingent phenomenon that arises under specific discursive and social conditions, and usually at the unpredicted way (Borge & Mercier, 2019). Moreover, transitions may occur rapidly and are often not directly observable as discrete events. To trace collaborative trajectories and identify instance of transitions across levels requires us to focus on the patterns that 166 become visible or can be inferred through shifts in participation, discourse and artifacts. For this reason, understanding how it operates requires attention not only to the quality of group discourse, but also to the trajectories of individual participation (Dornfeld et al., 2017). That is, how different learners engage with, contribute to, and integrate ideas (Roschelle, 2012). We operationalize it through two dimensions, knowledge complex levels and participation by using three measurements: ICAP, discursive position  and Knowledge Integration (KI) (Chi & Wylie, 2014; Linn et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2008). Identifying Transitional Space: A trace of learning and participating trajectories First, the ICAP (Chi & Wylie, 2014)  is used to capture differences in cognitive participation,  including passive, active, constructive, and interactive,  when individuals participate in group work. It not only reveals whether students are present in  the discourse, but also the level at which they are processing ideas and generating understanding beyond what is explicitly presented. This dimension goes beyond mere talk, unpacking how students engage with collective reasoning and higher-order thinking may be occurri ng. Second, Discursive Position emphasizes the social and interactional aspect of learners in group talk (Azmitia & Montgomery, 1993; Clarke, 2016). Students vary in how they contribute, from private self-talk, to responding when promoted, to transactive contribution and offering unsolicited talk that co-construct knowledge  (Diez-Palomar et al., 2021; Kumpulainen & Rajala, 2017). By analyzing the language in use, we capture how learners negotiate ideas, respond to peers and shape the group’s reasoning collectively  (Tao & Zhang, 2021; Tong & Chan, 2023). Both aspects capture the participations. Third, Knowledge Integration (KI) traces understanding by examining the content and structure of ideas that learners articulate (Liu et al., 2008). KI identifies how students connect scientific concepts, from fragmented or minimally integrated knowledge to complex, coherent integration. Taken together, it provides a multidimensional view of how transitional space  is enacted. Tracing trajectories of learning and participation within collaborative activities  reveals how students move from shared group reasoning toward their own understanding and highlights emergent of transitions. Thus, it indicates whether and when a space for reconciling ideas from others is needed in collaborative learning.

## Context

8th-grade students from Midwestern U.S. classes  participated in a three-week biology unit on designing community gardens. The unit was taught by the same teacher who got professional training before implementing the unit. In home groups of two to four, students completed 15 activities (Table 1) with teacher’s facilitation. Students started with practicing the use of technological tools, followed by the topic introduction, exploring Why Plants Are Important  and conducting food research, and progressed through experiment on the biotic & abiotic factors, hypothesis theorizing and testing, and analyzing data, and making claims of different plants, starting from the tomato plants and continuing to investigate other plants (e.g., lettuce, watermelon, bell peppers). The activities aimed to develop science skills and deepen understanding of key concepts. The activities were supported by a technology-enhanced learning environment (Tool s column in Table 1). While each activity primarily relied on a specific tool, students could access others as needed. For instance, students conducted experiments using the plant simulation tool (PlantSim) (Sultana, et al., 2024) and engaged in science reasoning (e.g., making hypotheses, synthesizing findings, and drawing inferences) using the Idea Wall and/or the online digital science notebook (DigiNb).The Idea Wall, an interactive science reasoning tool, enables students to externalize and synchronize their ideas, allowing group members to create, view, and manipulate shared notes (Zeng et al., 2024). DigiNb, designed to integrate various curriculum units, includes a Notes function (Notes) for jotting down ideas, which can later be revisited or transferred to the Idea Wall. Both the Idea Wall and PlantSim are embedded within DigiNb, providing a cohesive digital learning environment. Students accessed all tools by logging into their accounts on their devices.

## Participants and data sources

Seven students worked in two groups’ (N1 = 4, N2 = 3) but from two classes consented to be recorded. Group 1 consisted of two male and two female students,  and Group 2 consisted of  three female student s. Three of them are whites and four of them non-whites. They were included in this study because both groups used all (digital) tools throughout the curriculum unit, and with minimum class absence. Their participation in activities was videorecorded, and their device interactions were screen-recorded. Videos were  organized chronologically according to the classroom activities implemented across curriculum unit, totaling 30 transcriptions. Students finished some of the activities in more than one class es (45 mins per class) because of the practical reasons, such as additional time needed for certain groups, or teacher needed to facilities students to do some of other tasks that were regular school activities. Videos were transcribed verbatim. Tool interactions, including (1) the written ideas within the notes, online digital notebook, and  (2) manipulation of notes in Ideal Wall (e.g., move notes), provided supplementary insights into students’ idea externalizations beyond verbal contributions. Transcriptions were organized by turns of talk, contextualized by preceding and following turns to capture interaction flow. Teacher interactions, as facilitative rather thandirective, were excluded because these interactions did not reflect students’ participation in group work.

Coding We used discourse analysis techniques, inductively and deductively (Derry et al., 2010; Jordan & Henderson, 1995), to examine students’ collaborative group work through three key dimensions. First, we adapted the ICAP framework (Chi & Wiley, 2014) to analyze students’ participation in group activities, based on both the conversations in the group and the tool intera ction. Their participation was categorized as passive, active, constructive, or interactive (Table 2), representing increasing degrees of contribution and collaboration. It allows us to assess the extent to which they contributed to the collaboration and engaged with peers.  Passive participation, the lowest level, referred to instances where students merely received information or provided limited, unelaborated responses. Active participation  reflected a higher level, where students performed meaningful actions that supported the group’s progress. Constructive participation indicated an even higher level, when students generated ideas that went beyond or reinterpreted shared information, Interactive participation, the highest level, captured moments when students built upon and evaluated one another’s ideas to jointly develop more sophisticated understanding.

Last, we analyzed knowledge advancement  using the KI  framework (Liu et al., 2008). It  assesses students’ understanding by categorizing their connections between concepts. The highest level is the complex link when three or more concepts  are connected correctly, full-link as next level if two concepts are explicated connected correctly, partial-link as an even lower level of  understanding by identifying potential relationships, and no link is the lowest level when invalid/wrong connections occur (more details in our previous studies). Together, three-dimensions allows  to capture the nuances of students ’ participation and conceptual understanding in collaborative activities. This study aims to examine how learning traverses between group and individual levels in collaborative learning, aiming to make the concept of Transitional Space  empirically observable  and identifiable. While smoothed scatterplot showed visual similarities in group participation patterns, further statistical analysis revealed that collaborative dynamics varied substantially across groups. The way students engaged in discursive participation, showed ICAP levels, and KI was not uniform, even when surface-level patterns appeared similar. This variability reinforces the idea that collaborative learning is a highly contextual process, shaped by group composition, task structure, and discourse, rather than a fixed or prescriptive process (Damşa, 2014; Puntambekar et al., 2023). The converge of  ICAP and KI at higher levels, reflected in increased knowledge complexity  (Linn et al., 2013; Roschelle, 2012), offers insights into when transitions are more likely to occur spontaneously. Al though transitions cannot be directly observed, they can be inferred from pattens in discourse and participation across levels. This also suggests that collective progress is driven by continuous individual contributions, as learners more fluently externalize their thinking to the group.

Beyond these group-level differences, we found strong evidence of divergence between individual and group learning trajectories. Nested model comparisons showed that in both groups, only one student’s trajectory aligned with group-level pattern, while most students significantly deviated. This consistent result suggests that group level advancement and success does not necessarily represent the individual learning of its members. While group activity may provide a collective trajectory toward knowledge integration, individual leaners followed distinct paths, sometimes aligned, but often misaligned with the group’s progression (Dornfeld et al., 2017; White, 2018). This indicates that individual sense-making derived from collaborative work requires additional support in order to enhance the benefits that collaboration provides for each learner. These misalignment further points to the importance of Transitional Space, the temporary and contingent zone that emerges when group-level knowledge advancement and participation outpace individual understanding. In such moments, group discourse may generate higher-order reasoning or improvement that exceed the current grasp of some members  (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995; Stahl, 2016). Transitional Space  is not a pre-structured feature of the learning environment, but an enacted and interactional  phenomenon, brough into being through discourse, shared artifacts and uneven participation (Suthers, 2006; Hakkarainen, 2009; Damşa & Ludvigsen, 2016). Critically, it is fragile. Its emergence depends on the discursive and social conditions that allow individual to revisit, reinterpret and rework group ideas in ways that become personally meaningful. Identifying when and how transitional space arises, therefore, requires examine both group-level discourse and the individual trajectories within it (Cang et al., 2024). This can further assist to improve the quality of collaborative work and individual learning.

This study offers insights on identifying transitions as an empirical phenomenon, rather than a purely theoretical one. Research in sociocultural learning and knowledge-building traditions has implicitly draw upon internalization but lack of the tools for detecting when and how individual uptake from group progress  occurs (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2021; Lawrence & Valsiner, 1993; Stahl & Hakkarainen, 2021). This study provides a concrete approach to trace when collaborative progress may successfully or unsuccessfully translate into individual understanding. In doing so, this study contributes theoretically  by challenging the assumption that internalization is an inevitable or uniform outcome of social interactions. Instead, it foregrounds the unevenness of learning in group settings and identifies the T ransitional Space as a critical arena where learning can be supported. While Transitional Space cannot be guaranteed, understanding its dynamics offers new insights into how learning environment might be designed to support individual sensemaking within collective activity. Limitation and future work Although this study provides insights on identifying transitional space as a contingent  and fragile dimensions of collaborative learning, it did not examine how  it varied by activity  or tools. Future work will expand it with microanalysis to capture how reconciliation unfolds in interaction, and to better understand the role of specific instructional supports and temporal rhythms, such as tool shifts, reflection chance, in enabling Transitional Space. Further. while higher proportions of integrative moves suggest greater opportunities for individual reconciliation, they do not directly confirm that learners internalized or retained group-generated ideas. Longitudinal or transfer measures would help to understand sustained individual uptake.
