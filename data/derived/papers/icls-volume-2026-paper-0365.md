# Frustration, Conflict, and Transformative Learning in a Teacher Makeathon

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster investigates the role of social and emotional experiences in fostering transformative learning during a three-day Makeathon for in-service teachers. Using reflective journal data analyzed through Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA), I examine how internal feelings of frustration and external experiences of conflict relate to reported transformative learning. Results suggest that frustration, rather than conflict, is more closely linked to transformative learning, offering implications for designing professional learning in maker-based learning environments. Theory A persistent challenge in educators' professional development is designing learning environments that promote transformative rather than superficial learning (Mezirow, 2000). While prior research often treats frustration and conflict as obstacles (D'Mello, 2012), their nuanced relationship with learning remains underexplored. In this study, frustration is conceptualized as an internal affective response to perceived blockage or unmet expectations, whereas conflict refers to interpersonal tension around incompatible goals or decisions. The two are analytically distinct yet often empirically intertwined in collaborative professional learning contexts. Thus, this study examines how frustration and conflict affect transformative learning using reflective journals from a 3-day professional educational Makeathon for in-service teacher s. Within adult learning theory, Mezirow (2000) proposes a transformative learning framework characterized by disorienting dilemmas, critical reflection, and perspective transformation. However, the impact of conflict is dual-natured, potentially leading e ither to productive reflection or destructive disengagement (Deutsch, 1973). Critical to this theoretical framework is the interactive nature of frustration and conflict within transformative learning. To investigate this interaction, this study employs Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA), a quantitative ethnographic tool that models structural relationships among constructs (Shaffer, 2017).

Data Data were collected from personal reflective journals written by 35 in-service teachers during a three-day Makeathon in Israel in the summer of 2024. GPT-4 and manual corrections were utilized to translate journals from Hebrew to English and to segment them into sentences (“lines”), resulting in 1286 lines of data. These coded data were then analyzed using Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA), a quantitative ethnographic method that models relationships among co-occurring codes. Units of analysis were individual participants, and connections among codes were summed across stanza windows for each participant.

## Qualitative analysis

Qualitative analysis suggests that participants’ frustration is often linked to transformative learning, for example, when a participant describes an emotional struggle internalized as a learning insight: “I learned that I find it really difficult to commu nicate with people who think in chaos rather than in a straight and logical line.” In contrast, when a participant acknowledges tension without evidence of reflective learning, we see a conflict without transformation: “The challenge I faced today in the group was that we couldn’t reach an agreement on a project”. We also found evidence that frustration acts as a mediator in cases where participants experience conflict, which then leads to frustration, and upon reflection, results in transformative learning: At some point, I felt like we wouldn’t be able to work together as a team [CONFLICT]. It was frustrating, and honestly, I lost all motivation, [FRUSTRATION], but then, I took a break to clear my head and returned with a decision [REFLECTION]: no matter what is decided, I’ll go with the flow —the most important thing is to just start working [TRANSFORMATIVE LEARNING].
