# “Virtuous” academic mothers as course designers: A sociocultural perspective

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The study explores the factors influencing Chinese academic mothers’ course design decisions during the COVID-19 pandemic. A qualitative online survey followed by 10 in-depth interviews was conducted in 2022 with over 150 participants. Thematic analysis was used to analyze the survey and interview transcripts. The results highlighted the significance of spousal support and academic mothers’ motivation to sacrifice personally. The findings call for interventions that support academic mothers as course designers.

## Introduction

Chinese academic women’s work-life struggles in part represent the same plight as in other places but retain characteristics related to their social and cultural context. A Chinese woman’s academic  success must  be underlined by her “perfect family” with academic mothers in the “sacrificed” shadow of being “virtuous” caring figures (Tang, 2001). Expectations around Chinese academic mothers’ “virtuous” feminine qualities facilitate them enhancing  their teaching images, but constrain their research productivity  (Xie, 2019). However, how Chinese academic mothers made their course design decisions remained unclear. Therefore, this research sets out to answer the question: What factor(s) influence Chinese academic mothers’ course design d ecisions during the COVID-19 pandemic ? The aim of researching  during the COVID-19 pandemic  period was to discover how Chinese academic mothers fulfill their course design responsibilities under unprecedented work-life conflicts and extreme tension.

## Conceptual framework

The present study adopts a sociocultural perspectiv e that acknowledges the influence of cultural and h istorical contexts on individuals ’ meaning making and their interactions with the world (Danish & Gre salfi, 2018). Specifically, the study examines the implications of Chinese women academics’ “virtuous” mother images, which are rooted in China ’s Confucian cultural heritage and gender stereotype s, for their course design decisions. The social construction of their “motherly” identity as course designers is a dynamic variable that affects their interactions with the surrounding contexts. It highlights the significance of cultural and gender factors in shaping individual behavior and decision-making in Eastern Chinese academic contexts.

## Method

I conducted a qualitative online survey  modified from Dickson (2018)  via Wenjuanxing  (an online survey platform in China)  with over 150 participants at various universities  i n September 2022 (HERC’s reference number: EA220311). Following the survey, I performed ten in-depth interviews to observe Chinese academic mothers’ course design stories nuancedly. The study used thematic analysis  to analyze the qu alitative survey responses and interview transcripts to unpack their collective stories (Braun & Clarke, 2006).

## Result

Following the coding of survey responses and interv iew transcripts, emerging themes are  Chinese academic mothers’ personal sacrifices with overtime work schemes and their support-seeking strategy from family members. ## Discussion and conclusions

The findings suggest that Chinese academic mothers are motivated to work overtime to fulfill their course design responsibilities despite being constrained by heavy  household burdens rooted in Eastern China’s internal and external affairs aff iliations within the household. The results also indicated that t heir motivation to work as dedicated and capable course designer s is further influenced by the support they can seek.  Danish and Gresalfi

(2018) argued that people act in motivated or unmotivated ways according to the contexts and suggested reforming

practices or contexts to promote engaged and motiva ted behaviors. Therefore, the findings of this research will have implications for calling for equal rights betw een men and women inside and outside Chinese households t o support academic mothers in their course designers’ roles.
