# Playful Activities in Mathematics Learning: Comparing Early-Career and Experienced Teachers

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Playful activities have been shown to facilitate engagement, problem-solving, and social-emotional skills in educational contexts. This pilot study examine d teachers’ attitudes towards playful activities in primary mathematics education, focusing on their frequency of use, perceived effectiveness, and challenges. We were specifically interested in whether early-career teachers differ on those variables from experienced teachers (up to 2 years vs. 11 to 20 years of teaching experience). Findings indicated that experienced teachers reported higher agreement with statements supporting more frequent use of individual, pair, and small-group mathematics games and viewed these activities as more effective for engagement and skill-building than early-career teachers. They also agreed more with statements indicating difficulties finding necessary equipment and unreliability of digital resources. These results suggest that professional development may focus on supporting early-career teachers with implementing playful learning while also increasing experienced teachers' confidence with digital resources.

Playful learning has emerged as a cornerstone in early childhood education, nurturing  holistic development and fosters positive learning attitudes across varied contexts (OECD, 2017).  In fact, playful learning activities range from free play to guided play to direct instruction (e.g., Zosh et al., 2018).  In particular, the idea of guided play combines the joyful and actively engaging nature of play with adult guidance and explicit learning goals (e.g., Weisberg et al., 2016) making it prototypical for playful learning. Grounded in the notion that children learn best through exploration and joyful engagement, playful learning integrate s structured playful experiences  into educational environments, both formal and informal, to stimulate curiosity, creativity, and essential skil ls. Across diverse settings, playful  learning has shown promise in facilitating  literacy and mathematical skills, but also promoting social competence and self-regulation as children navigate rules, take turns, and manage emotions in playful activities (White, 2011; Gashaj, Dapp, Trninic, & Roebers, 2021). By embracing children’s innate curiosity and adaptability, playful learning cultivates lifelong learning transferable across formal and informal educational settings (Matthews, 2008; Garner & Bergen, 2006). In particular, integrating playful learning into mathematics education can increase engagement and reduce anxiety for  challenging topics, offering a  new pathway to facilitating foundational mathematical skills (e.g., Gashaj, Thaqi, Mast, & Roebers, 2023). However, little is known about potential constraints of the actual use of playful activities for teaching mathematics. In the present context, playful activities refer to structured, playful experiences (i.e., guided play) intentionally designed with clear mathematical learning objectives that engage students in exploring to-be-learned concepts while maintaining the interactive and enjoyable character of play (Weisberg et al., 2016). These activities include card games, pencil-and-paper activities, digital games and apps, role play, exploration games, puzzles, board games, physical games, and made-up games and more. The present pilot study explored teachers’ attitudes towards such playful activities in mathematics  learning, focusing on frequency of use, perceived effectiveness, and challenges in mathematics instruction, and whether these varied according to teachers’ experience. In fact, little is known about the extent to which teachers integrate playful activities in mathematics instruction and what challenges they encounte r. To overcome this limitation, collaborative efforts that integrate educators’ real-world experience with research insights to develop usable, context-sensitive playful activities as educational resources have been advocated (e.g., Howard-Jones et al., 2018). In this context, better understanding teachers’ attitudes towards playful learning is a crucial step to identify enablers, but also barriers for its classroom implementation. According to the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB; Ajzen & Madden, 1986)  individuals’ behaviors are influenced by their attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived control. As such, teachers’ intentions to implement playful learning activities may depend on their perceived behavioral control and prior teaching experience.

As a first step to better understand how  playful activities are used in mathematics classrooms, this pilot study evaluates teachers'  attitudes towards such activities, focusing on their frequency of use, perceived effectiveness, and reported challenges using questionnaire  data. In particular, we are interested in  whether 1512 attitudes on above aspects differ by teaching experience. In line with TPB, w e expected that longer teaching experience would be associated with more pronounced openness and readiness for adopting playful activities. This hypothesis is motivated by findings, which suggest that longer  teaching experience is  associated with stronger self-efficacy and control beliefs  (Klassen & C hiu, 2010)—factors known to increase  openness to innovative practices. By identifying  such experiential differences, this study aims to inform professional development programs and resource design to better support  both early-career and experienced teachers in integrating playful activities into their mathematics classes.

## Method

This pilot study included data from 48 teachers recruited through professional educator networks, teacher-focused social media groups, and targeted Facebook advertisements. Participation was voluntary, and no financial incentives were offered. This conveni ence sampling strategy aimed to reach teachers from diverse backgrounds, though self-selection bias (e.g., teachers already interested in playful learning) cannot be ruled out.  We collected data in two rounds and removed potential AI-generated responses (in particular: bot responses, based on response patterns and implausible answer combinations), during cleaning. We compared two groups of teachers based on teaching experience: Early career teachers (N = 24): preservice teachers and newly qualified teachers with up to 2 years of experience (ages 18-34; 10 male, 9 female, 1 non-binary and 4 preferred not to say) and more experienced teachers (N = 24): with 11-20 years of experience (ages 35-54, 2 male, 22 female). Participants taught at Early Years Foundation Stage ages 3-5 and Primary Schools (ages 5-11) in different regions of the UK (including England, Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland). The questionnaire employed was developed based on existing  instruments (e.g., Allsop & Jessel, 2015; Russo et al., 2020; Jesmin & Ley, 2020), covering four key sections: i) background demographic variables (e.g., age, gender, teaching experience), ii) Use of Playful Activities (3 items), iii) Effectiveness of Playful Activities (3 items), and iv) Challenges with Equipment and Behavior Management (3 items). We defined playful activities as structured, game-like learning tasks designed with explicit mathematical learning goals, aligning with guided play principles (Hirsh-Pasek et al., 2020). To increase clarity, all items included respective  examples of playful activities (e.g., games, role play). Questions about a playful activity (e.g., How frequently do you use games as a closing activity during mathematics lessons?) were followed by a 5-point Likert scale to measure the frequency with which teachers applied the activity, ranging from very often (i.e., >4 times / week), often (i.e., 2 to 3 times / week, sometimes (i.e. once / week), rarely (i.e., once / month), and never. Other q uestions included statement s followed by a 5-point Likert scale to measure i) teachers’ level of agreement with the statement, ranging from strongly agree, agree, neither agree nor disagree, disagree to strongly disagree (e.g.,.Playful activities are effective for engaging students in mathematics) or ii) the frequency with which they experienced a specific situation from very often, often, sometimes, rarely, and never (e.g., The IT equipment is unreliable when playing online games). To evaluate differences between less experienced and more experienced teachers, independent samples t-tests were run. When the assumption of homogeneous variances was not met, we used Welch’s t-test. All pvalues were adjusted for Type I error accumulation using the Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) procedure with significance level at α=0.05. Given the pilot nature of this study and limited sample size, all inferential results should be interpreted exploratively and considered as generating hypotheses for future, larger-scale studies. and iv) Challenges with Equipment and Behavior Management (see Table 1 for descriptives and statistical details). The higher reported frequency of using playful activities (including individual, pair, and small-group games) among experienced teachers  may reflect their more pronounced classroom confidence and instructional flexibility, which aligns with propositions of TP B (Ajzen & Madden, 1986). According to TPB, behavior is influenced by attitudes, perceived behavioral control, and intentions. More experienced teachers may have developed stronger self-efficacy (perceived control) and more positive attitudes toward playful learning through experience, increasing the likelihood of actual implementation. Experienced teachers’ more pronounced openness to playful activities may thus suggest they are more confident in adopting these methods. In contrast, early-career teachers may still be building the practical experience necessary to translate theoretical ideas around playful learning (OECD, 2017) into everyday teaching practices, particularly in a highly structured subject like mathematics (Howard-Jones et al., 20 20). However, frequency of use alone does not necessarily reflect a more 1514 positive attitude. It might also be shaped by external constraints, such as school policies, curriculum demands, or access to suitable resources. Conversely, early-career teachers’ lower reported use of playful activities might reflect limited opportunities to integrate playful approaches into  rigid lesson plans or under close supervision. Future research should thus consider contextual variables (e.g., school culture, leadership support) more to disentangle attitudinal from contextual influences. An interesting finding in this context is that more experienced teachers reported using playful activities less frequently as a closing activity compared to less experienced teachers. This might suggest that experienced teachers may not view these activities as a means to loosen up or wind down a lesson but rather view them as integral to structured mathematics instruction. In contrast, less experienced teachers might see playful activities as more suited for informal or transitional periods, potentially reflecting their reliance on familiar routines to manage classroom dynamics. Experienced teachers’ stronger agreement with statements indicating that playful activities are effective for fostering engagement and problem-solving is in line with previous findings showing that play-based learning can support cognitive and social-emotional development (Skene et al., 2022; Wohlfart & Wagner, 2024). Th is may be interpreted to suggest that more experienced teachers may be better able to acknowledge  how playful activities can facilitate acquiring  skills essential for math ematics and beyond, contributing to a more dynamic, interactive classroom. Early-career teachers, by contrast, may benefit from observing these practices in action to better understand how playful activities may be integrated successfully with formal mathematics standards. Interestingly, experienced teachers expressed  stronger agreement with statements indicating struggles with IT equipment, including difficulties in finding necessary equipment and its reliability at their school. This may suggest that while early-career teachers are more accustomed to using technology in their classrooms, more experienced teachers may rely more on physical, tangible resources for playful activities. This is in line with self-efficacy, positive attitudes toward its use, access to sufficient IT resources, and a constructivist approach to learning (e.g., Wohlfart &Wagner, 2024). However, this reliance on non-digital resources might not only reflect more experience with implementing playful activities in the classroom. It may also be comfort with established development. Offering technology-focused continuous professional development  might help reduce barriers and facilitate a smoother integration of digital and physical resources, enhancing confidence across experience levels. An alternative explanation is that using playful activities (including digital ones)  more often  also exposes experienced teachers more often to such barriers. In addition, one may suppose that these teachers are being more informed and discerning about resources required for effective playful learning and may thus set higher expectations for their suitability and quality. In this case, their dissatisfaction might not stem solely from logistical barriers but also from a deeper understanding of what is necessary for implementing playful activities effectively. Taken together, these findings indicate that there is potential for mutual learning: Early-career teachers may benefit from the built-up pedagogical expertise of more experienced colleagues in incorporating playful activities in mathematics classes. In contrast, experienced teachers may strengthen their digital confidence through peer learning and targeted training.  Encouraging collaborative professional learning communities may thus promote more balanced integration of playful learning across experience levels. Further research might investigate how mentorship programs or workshops focused on integrating playful learning in mathematics teaching such as so-called game-jams might bolster early-career teachers’ confidence and implementation of playful activities in their mathematics teaching (Aurava, Meriläinen, & Stenros, 2020; Fowler, Ni, & Preston, 2018). Importantly, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, given the small sample and the exploratory nature of the study. Our decision to include preservice teachers in the sample may also have biased the results because preservice teachers do not typically control their own classroom and instructional approach. As such, the work is necessary to interrogate whether the differences in participant response are practically meaningful: it may be that the statistical difference on a survey do not necessarily translate into practical, observable classroom differences. Future studies are alsoneeded to substantiate the present findings in a larger and more diverse sample of teachers. In short, future studies may not only aim to replicate the present study  but also evaluate how the observed differences in attitudes translate into actual classroom practices and, ultimately,  how targeted professional development can support less experienced teachers in successfully adopting playful activities. In sum, the present results suggest that more experienced teachers tend to have a more favorable  and possibly more informed  view towards integrating playful activities into their classroom practice when teaching mathematics.
