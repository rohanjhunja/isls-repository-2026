# Methods for Analyzing Ideological Sensemaking in Interaction

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Recent research has considered how learning both unfolds in the moment and is influenced by broader social and historical forces. Methodological tools are needed to link micro-level analyses (e.g., interactions) with macro-level forces (e.g., ideologies). In the setting of a longitudinal professional development (PD), we present a framework for contextualizing interaction analysis in the moment with ideological discourse over longer timescales. We illustrate our framework with an example of elementary science teachers’ ideological sensemaking, offering a tool for interpreting interactions to analyze teacher learning.

## Objectives and significance

Central to the work of the Learning Sciences is und erstanding how learning happens. Further methodological tools are needed to link analyses at the micro leve l (e.g., interactions) with macro-level forces (e.g., ideologies) to contextualize learning that happens over longer time scales (months or years, rather than a single class session). This is particularly important when considering teacher learning across extended professional development (PD) programs. We seek to connect these two levels of inquiry  through a framework for analyzing ideological sensemaking grounded in I nteraction Analysis (IA) methods. We argue that our framework makes an important methodological contribution to the field because it provides one possibility for making macro-level influences on learning visible within moments of learning in interaction. Theoretical framing Rich work in Learning Sciences has examined the rol e of ideologies, or socially constructed frameworks  for making sense of how society works (Hall, 1996), in reproducing inequitable learning (e.g., Philip, 201 1; Louie, 2018). This work has highlighted how within local communities, dominant ideologies that result in deficit narratives of learners can be rearticulated to chal lenge inequitable norms (Philip, 2011). This rearti culation process occurs through interaction, making interaction analysis a useful methodological approach (e.g., Jordan & Henderson, 1995). We considered the coordination of talk, bodies, and materials in interaction as a process of cooperative action (Goodwin, 2018). Drawing on Goodwi n’s (2018) construct of substrate, or tools and tal k available as resources for use in interaction, we positioned individual teachers in our analysis as situated within a PD learning community and analyzed their interactions for co-operative action, including the use of substrate as a resource, to better understand how ideologies expressed within o ur PD community were rearticulated (or not) through collaborative sensemaking.

To bring together macro-level ideologies with in-the-moment, fine-grained micro analyses of interaction methodologically, we developed two scales which allowed us to analy ze ideologies in interaction. First, we considered how ideologies at the macro level, which  often operate and influence talk and action withou t our awareness, are made visible locally. Drawing on exi sting literature, we conceptualized ideologies at the macro level as being revealed through narratives told at the meso level which are interpreted through stances at the micro level. We argue that ideologies are made visible in interactions through individual  turns of talk that reveal narratives grounded in taken-for-granted, commonsense understandings about the world. For example, ideologies of science as White property position science as belonging to White adult males, resulting in a dominant ideology that leads to the marginalization of children from minor itized communities in STEM education (Mensah & Jackson, 2018). Reinforcing this dominant ideology,  a common narrative based in this ideology  in U.S. science education is that young children are unable to enga ge meaningfully in science learning. This narrative is  made visible through meso-level practices such as science standards that requ ire young children to focus first on “observation, description, and categorization” befo re actively engaging in science inquiry and sensema king (Kuhn, 2016, p. 393). At the interactional level, narratives are retold or refused through stances, or the verbalized sensemaking of individuals in interaction based on their positionality (Philip, 2011). As participants articulate stances in interaction, they (re)articulate their understandings about how the world works, reproducing or refusing meso-level narratives and aligning with or resisting macro-level ideologies. Figure 1 Grain Sizes of Ideological Constructs To trace how macro-level ideologies influenced stance-taking in micro-level moments of interaction, we considered the role of interactional substrate, specifically how prior interactions within our community became part of the substrate for the community’s later interactions. As Goodwin (2018) described with the evolution of tools like the ax, co-operative action and resources available in substrate are powerful explanatory mechanisms for human interaction across a range of timescales. In our context, we considered substrate at three timescales based on our PD design, which consisted of an initi al summer PD and subsequent academic-year PD meetings (Figure 2). In-the-moment substrate includes interactional resources available for reuse and transformation. In our PD community, in-the-moment substrate was established and used by interactional groups within a single interaction. Substrate at the recent time scale included resources and tools established for use within our PD community in earlier shared PD experiences, prior to a focal interaction. While much of our recent substrate was shared by all participants (e.g., resources and experiences established as part of substrate in our whole-group summer PD), other recent substrate was specific to a given interactional group (e.g., a conversation in a previous video club activity held during an earlier PD meeting). Thus, sometimes members of the community needed to be reoriented to recent substrate established elsewhere within the PD community. Similarly, we define recent as an elastic construct that expanded over time based on the PD community’s trajectory. What was in-the-moment substrate for one interactional group in one PD meeting then became a part of the recent substrate for the whole group in subsequent PD activities. Finally, we conceptualized substrate at the historical time scale as established broadly by societies. At this scale, substrate consisted of historical sets of ideas, tools, and understandings taken as commonsense, including ideologies and narratives. We suggest that dominant ideologies and deficit narratives reproduced within a society become sedimented and layered in historical substrate. These sedimented ideologies often surface in discourse but may not be visible to the interactional group because they reflect commonplace assumptions about the way things are (Philip, 2011).

## Context and analytic approach

The data presented below were part of a larger stud y supporting elementary science teachers in a long-term PD community. PD participation included a summer PD series (5 days) followed by four academic-year PD meetings. During each academic-year meeting, participants brought video of their own practice to share. In our focal moment, a kindergarten teacher, Marie, shared video from her class with two colleagues: Jack and Sarah. Heather and Ashlyn (Author  2) were members of the research team and facilitate d the video club session. We selected Marie’s clip for analysis as it highlighted a tensi on between a constraining, deficit view of what you ng students are capable as represented through state standards and a more disruptive view of students taken from phenomenonbased approaches explored in the community’s summer PD. We analyzed the moment using traditional IA approaches (e.g., Jordan & Henderson, 1995), focusing on substrate and co-operative action as it unfolded in sequential turns  of talk (Goodwin, 2018). We attended to how participants’ stances connected to narratives and ideologies at the meso and macro levels as evidence of historical substrate. To trace recent substrate within our PD community, we reviewed summer PD video to understand how substrate established during summer PD served as a resource for participants during the academic year. Illustrating ideological rearticulation in interaction We demonstrate how sedimented historical ideas about young children’s science learning surfaced in one PD interaction. However, by drawing on disruptive resources available in recent substrate, community members were able to engage in ideological sensemaking toward a rearticulation of this dominant ideology. Our focal moment took place during the first PD meeting of the acade mic year. Participants in the interaction included Marie, the kindergarten teacher presenting the video, Jack and  Sarah, who both taug ht 3rd grade, and Heather and Ashlyn, members of the research team who served as facilita tors. Marie shared a video where her students were using Plus-Plus blocks (similar to LEGO bricks) to answer the question: "Can we take something apart and put it b ack together in a new way?" The moment began with Heather asking for context on  how the state standard for the lesson was unpacked by the district. Marie responded: "I feel like it's just so basic for kindergarten, of just getting them to have that basic understanding that things c an come apart and you can put it back together…it's  pretty boring to be honest." As Marie engaged in the interaction, her body language suggested discomfort: she grimaced as she responded to Heather's question, clasping he r hands and pressing her lips tightly together as she finished. Her closed body language in response to how the standard was unpacked echoed the discomfort of her statements that positioned the standard as "so basic for kinde rgarten", and that as a result, "it's pretty boring  to be honest". Through her utterances and body language, Marie's initial moment of stance-taking positioned the state standards as "basic" with the resulting work of learning as “ boring.” This initial stance stood in contrast to t he more antideficit views of kindergartners’ abilities to engage deeply in scientific sensemaking that were later co-operatively assembled.

Through the course of the interaction, the group dr ew on substrate across time scales as part of their sensemaking around Marie’s initial concern. Heather, as the facilitator, validated Marie's concern, bu t she also created space for nuancing Marie's initial stance-taking (being constrained by the standards) by nami ng the challenge as something that first came up during summer PD. Heather positioned Marie’s observation as "one of the challenges…with…the kindergarten standards", sp ecifically that the PD community’s interpretation o f the standard as focusing on "classifying and organizing " was in tension with the phenomenon-focused, active sensemaking that had been centered in the summer PD. Heather drew on recent substrate by explicitly referencing how this same tension had appeared as the teachers planned a unit on solids and liquids during summer PD ("we talked about this this summer, right?"). Marie agre ed with the approaches toward scienc e teaching identified by Heather, but still seemed unsure of how to apply those approache s within her view of the more "basic" physical science standards given by the state. Heather direc ted the group's attention to Marie’s video clip to explore the tension, inviting the group to consider other ways to approach the standard. In this way, Heather held  space for alternative stances to be examined.

Jack stopped the video and explicitly shifted Marie's initial framing of the lesson as "boring” through his stance taking, which reframed the students’ work as “a really deep question for a kindergartner” with “connections to what they’re going to learn later in third and f ourth grade.” Heather made space for this shift by inviting Jack to “say more.” Jack then positioned Marie’s lesson as creating opportunities for kindergartners  to think deeply about foundational science concepts. Jack drew on b oth in-the-moment and recent substrate as resources in the group's ideological sensemaking as he transformed t he way the group was conce ptualizing kindergarteners’ sensemaking as foundational for future science learning, connec ting how states of matter and the water cycle, concepts explored in later grades,  are related to the idea of taking something apart a nd putting it back together. Jack contributed to collaborative sensemaking that hel ped contest the teachers’ and researchers’ historic ally dominant interpretations of the state standards that positioned science learning as memorization of objective facts. Marie accepted and built on  Jack’s stance shifting. She  explicitly named the substrate of her original stance toward students' engagement in the lesson: " I'm like, this is boring". Then, she revised her in itial stance, relabeling the activity as "actual meaningful play"  and said Jack’s stance was "awesome". Onc e the group reframed students’ sensemaking as meaningful, Heather drew again on recent substrate as a resource, inviting the group to think about "some sort of anchoring phenom enon to frame this [lesson]" as they did in summer PD planning. Heather’s move further supported the grou p’s effort to shift their initial views of the standards toward a phenomenon-focused approach. Together, the group offered additional ways to support students' sensemaking. Marie proposed a phenomenon related to skiing. Jack suggested using open-ended questions to allow students to generate multiple solutions to Mari e's ski phenomenon. Ashlyn proposed Transformers as a phenomenon that connected with students' interests. Heather recomme nded restructuring the activity to allow students t o work in pairs, framing "play" as an asset that supports sci entific thinking. In thi s way, the group reimagined instruction by recognizing students' contributions and abilitie s as assets for sensemaking. These comments are par ticularly significant because they focus on actual children, which contrasts with the focus on teachers and curr iculum that dominated much of the conversation.

The interaction concluded with convergence around y oung children's abilities to engage in meaningful science sensemaking as Heather commented that “these are cool ideas”. Through reinterpreting the state standard, the group pushed back on deficit narratives of youn g children’s capacities and actively resisted domin ant ideologies about young children and their relationship to science. By intentionally echoing and transforming Marie G.'s initial stance ("it's boring"), Jack's co-operative conclusion ("I don't  think it's boring") emphasized the collaborative rearticulation that occurred within the group, positioning the kindergartners' engagement in science as "meaningful" and foundational for their future work in science, math, and engineering.

## Discussion and implications

Our illustrative example highlights how ideologies were rearticulated from Marie's initial stance of "this is boring" to Jack's co-operative reconstruction of Marie's stance, "I don't think this is boring." Over the course of the interaction, the group collectively rearticulated t heir understanding of the standard to resist defici t narratives of what young children are capable of in science.  We recognize that most interactions do not  provide such clearly marked evidence of ideological rearticulation. Daniel et al. (2023) offers a contrasting example that demonstrates how ideological interaction within our PD community  was often ambiguous. Disrupting deficit narratives takes work and time and ideological sensemaking toward rearticulation is an ongoing process. Creating more equitable learning spaces for students  requires researchers and teacher educators to attend to the relationship between ideologies and teachers’ actions and interactions. We propose that our methodological approach of link ing ideologies and interactions can help attend to these complex issues within the field of teacher learning.
