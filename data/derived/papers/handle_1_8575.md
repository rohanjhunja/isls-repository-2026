# The aRithmetic Discourse Profile as a Tool for Mapping Students’ Subjectifying and Mathematizing Discourse

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We present a new tool for mapping students’ arithmetic discourse, named the aRithmetic Discourse Profile (RDP). The tool was developed based on the commognitive theory (Sfard, 2008). Its main goal is mapping students’ mastery of arithmetic routines on a continuum between ritual (procedure based) and explorative (goal-based) participation. In addition, the tool maps the subjectifying statements of students (statements constructing their identity in action) to assess the affective aspects of the student’s performance. We exemplify the tool on 3 tasks performed by 12 7th grade students. The findings show various possible relations between subjectifying and ritual/explorative performance.

## Introduction

By the time students finish elementary school, they are supposed to have mastered the arithmetic discourse – the discourse on whole numbers, fractions, and the four  operations that are used to manipulate these mathe matical objects. Yet the learning of arithmetic often fails  at certain points, and students arrive with insuff icient skills to middle school and the demands of algebra, which is built on arithmetic. Moreover, students often develop, during the process of getting initiated into the mathematical discourse (through the gate of arithmetic) identities of failure. They come to endorse narratives about themselves such as “I am no good with numbers” or “I don’t understand fractions” (Heyd-Metzuyanim, 2015). These identity narratives constrain their participation in mathema tical activities and may often lead to disengagement, thus harming not just the learning of arithmetic but the learning of more advanced topics too.

Currently, there are little tools that can assess, holistically, the mastery that students have achiev ed of the arithmetic discourse. By “holistically”, we mean th at the tools relate both to the mathematical aspect s of the discourse (often termed the “cognitive” elements) and the identity-related aspects of this discourse (often termed “affective”). Tests that assess mathematical disabilities or difficulties (e.g. Chinn, 2020; Dowker, 2019) are useful for determining which specific mathematical skills students may be lacking, yet they do not relate to the affective aspects of their participation. In contrast, tests that assess anxiety or attitudes towards mathematics (e.g. Hopko, 2003), do not relate to the mathematical skills of the student. Moreover, most tests of mathematical “knowledge” are built on paper and pencil techniques and focus on the end-result: whether the student’s answer to a task is correct or not. Yet much information about the proc esses of participation, procedures employed and ide ntity narratives produced along the way are lost this way. For this, a more processual approach for assessing students’ mastery is needed – one which is based on examining their talk, not just their writing. In this work, we propose a novel tool, named the aRithmetic Discourse Profile, whose aim is to assess the extent to which a student mastered the arithmetic discourse, as well as their subjectifications – the identity stories that they produce while engaged in this discourse. Our aim in this paper is to introduce the tool, demonstrate its application and examine the results that can be obtained from applying it to a set of 12 students whose discourse was examined with it.

## Theoretical Background

The theoretical framework employed for this study is the commognitive one (Sfard, 2008). This theory is used for several reasons: first, it has established a useful explanation of students’ learning of mathematics as progression from ritual to explorative participation (Lavie et al., 2019; Sfard & Lavie, 2005). Ritual participati on is characterized by rigid rule-following and reliance on external authority of experts. For example, imit ating the teacher who is saying “fifteen” and writing “15” by writing the numerals 1 next to 5 without being able to name the “1” as a “ten”. In accordance with the view of learning as becoming a participant in a community of discourse, Sfard and Lavie (2005) show how such participation is an unavoidable stage of learning, since at the initial phases of getting introduced to a certain discourse, learners are so unfamiliar with the objects on which this discourse is about, that they can only thoughtfully imitate others. Gradually, as they come to objectify the signs and symbols introduced to them, for example, as they come to see “15” as an object made up of certain parts (1 ten, 5 units), they become more explorative in their production of narratives about these objects. That is, they employ routines to manipulate these objects in flexible ways, and based on their own authority, or agency. Most importantly, their ICLS2022 Proceedings 171 © ISLS participation is goal oriented, that is, they employ certain procedures with the goal of the task in mind, rather than being satisfied with following the steps of an imitated procedure. Previous works within the commognitive framework showed that the location of a student on the ritual-explorative continuum may be tightly related to their identity narratives – namely, those endorsable stories a stu dent tells about herself (and others tell about her) that are reifying (talk about attributes of a person rather than about her actions) and significant to the storyteller (Sfard & Prusak, 2005). The relation between ritual partic ipation and identity could be seen, for example, in the case of  a student who described herself as “good in math” but “not good in fractions” and who indeed performed relativ ely exploratively in the discourse on whole numbers  yet ritually in tasks that included rational numbers (Heyd-Metzuyanim, 2015). This student managed to achieve high grades in middle school due to diligent and hard work of following teacher-authored rules in algebra. Yet towards high-school, her ritual participation in the mathematical discourse widened so much that she failed her exams and ended up in a low achieving group. This case illust rated the need for early assessment and profiling o f students “ritual pockets” of mathematical performance, even those students who achieve good grades, so that such “pockets” of ritual participation do not widen and lead to the development of a general identity of failure. The conclusions from this reported study, however, were based only on a single case-study. There is thus need of more rigorous examination of the relation between students’ mathe matizing and their concomitant subjectifications, n amely those utterances that they communicate about themse lves. Other studies of subjectifying activity (e.g.  HeydMetzuyanim & Sfard, 2012) have also pointed to the importance of examining implicit subjectifying actions, namely those actions that are not expressed explicitly (such as “I’m not sure”) but non-verbally or indirectly (e.g. by making a statement and waiting for the teacher to approve, ending the utterance with a questioning intonation, etc.). These implicit subjectifications are often even more potent in forming a story of a student as incompetent than the stories told explicitly out loud.

The idea to use a discursive lens to profile students’ discourse has been employed and tested almost two decades ago, in one of the earliest commognitive works, by Ben-Yehuda (Ben-Yehuda, 2003; Ben-Yehuda et al., 2005). In her dissertation, Ben-Yehuda examined the discourse of 14 7th grade students, attempting to capture the unique characteristics of discourse of students with low achievements. This work showed the rich utility of the discursive framework and established the initial format of the  tool, namely a “think aloud” interview where the s tudent is given a set of arithmetic tasks aligned with the st andard arithmetic curriculum. In addition, it showe d evidence that students experiencing difficulties often did not talk about mathematical objects as existing of themselves but as symbols to be syntactically manipulated on the paper using memorized and meaningless procedures. However, since the theory of learning as progressing from ri tual to explorative participation did not yet exist  at that time, the characteristics found in Ben-Yehuda’s study were still rather scattered. Moreover, the work did not result in a systematic coding scheme for analyzing students’ discourse and did not enable placing a certain students’ profile on a continuum (or several axes of continua) between ritual and explorative performance. A much more recent work (Lavie et al., 2019) provided the missing conceptualization of operationalizing exactly what it means to participate ritually, or enact a ritual routine. This work followed the development of the numerical discourse of a 2.5 years old boy, over two years. I t defined a routine as a pair of task (a situation that requires a certain action) and a procedure (a sequence of steps for achieving the goal). Lavie and colleagues showed through extraordinary detail how the young boy’s routine of  answering the task “where there is more?” de-ritualized according to the following characteristics: (a) it became more flexible, that is, more procedures were employed, alternately, to achieve the same task; (b) it became bonded, namely, sub-procedures became linked to each other in a way that the output of a step served as the input of the next step; (c) it became more agentive in the sense that the boy initiated the procedures and sub-procedures on his own, rather than looking for guidance from the adult who interacted with him; (d) the focus of the child, in enacting the routine, turned from the procedure to the goal of the task, where the importance lay in what it is that is to be achieved through the procedure In the current work, we add to these characteristics some additional one s, taken from other works that examined ritual part icipation and were more tailored to the discourse of older students: objectification (discussed earlier) and mediation (important when seeking to see to what extent the task is performed with the aid of a teacher). Finally, since we are dealing with issues of mastery of a school-discourse we are also interested whether the procedures employed by the students and the narratives derived from these procedures are canonical or not (or “mathematically correct”). With relation to the above characteristics of ritua l/explorative participation, and seeking to underst and the relations between them and subjectifying utterances, we ask in this paper:

1. What is the profile of students’ participation o n an axis between ritual and exploration, as obtain ed from the

2. To what extent does the ritual/explorative participation of students, as captured by the RDP tool, cohere with

## Method

Our analytical tool was developed as a secondary da ta analysis, on data collected by the first author (HeydMetzuyanim, 2011). The data consists of arithmetic interviews conducted with 12 7th graders. The inter views were conducted as part of a larger study where the author taught the 12 students in groups over a peri od of 5 months, in an out of school “course”. The course wa s broadly defined as “mathematics enrichment” and t he original study was aimed at examining interactions between emotional and cognitive aspects of learning. The students were placed in three groups (each of 4): L ow achieving, middle-high achieving, and excelling.  The excelling group consisted of 4 boys who came from an accelerated mathematics and science classroom (to which entrance was based on very high grades in elementary school mathematics and other subjects). Placement in the low and middle-high achieving group was based on reports coming from the school and the students’ parents. The arithmetic discourse interviews were originally  conducted to serve as a form of baseline, to exami ne the progress of the students during the course. However, they were never systematically analyzed. All the interviews were conducted by the first author, in a one-on-one setting. Students were asked to think out loud as they solved the problems (Young, 2005). When they were not succ essful and asked for help, the interviewer attempte d to scaffold the task, yet mostly she refrained from giving feedback on the correctness of the results. Interviews were videotaped with two cameras, one pointing at the student’s face and one on his writing. Written artifa cts were collected too.

The interview protocol was based on Ben-Yehuda’s study (Ben-Yehuda, 2003) and consisted of 24 tasks (some of them very short) that had potential to illuminat e various aspects of students’ arithmetic discourse. For the present study we chose three tasks for analysis (th e full protocol appears in (Heyd-Metzuyanim, 2011)). These tasks were (1) A word problem which asked students to calculate how much would cost a shirt and a pair of pants if two shirts cost 200 Shekels (local currency) and 3 pair of pants cost 300 Shekels; (2) the task  Multiply in your head; and (3) calculate !

The final three characteristics were coded in a bin ary fashion, getting either a score of (1, 0) for e xplorative, or (0,1) for ritual performance. Canonical procedures were coded as explorative if all the steps in the procedure aligned with standard mathematical procedures. Canonical narratives were coded as explorative if the end result of a procedure (e.g. the end result for the task “two thirds of 9” was “six”) was canonical. Mediation was coded as (1, 0) if the procedure was fully initiated and enacted by the student, and (0, 1) if some parts of it were mediated by the interviewer.

Subjectifying was coded in the following way: state ments that included direct reference to the self and one’s actions or mental states (but not just descriptions of mathematical actions), were coded as “explicit subjectifying”. These were coded as negative (-) if they included a negative statement about oneself (such as “I don’t know” or “I don’t think I cando this”) and (+) if they were positive (“I cando this” or “this is fun”). Notice that “this is fun” was coded as explicit subjectification since the as sumption was that a reference to oneself is implied  in the statement (“this is fun for me”). Non-verbal emotional expressions and statements  that communicated lack of confidence (such as smiling embarrassedly or asking  “is this right?”) were coded as implicit negative subjectifications. Although lack of negative subjectifications (impl icit or explicit) could be interpreted as implicitly subjectifying confidence, we did not code for positive implicit subjectifications, since it was impossible to discern between “neutral” affective communicatio n and “confidence”. There were thus only three code s for subjectifying: two for negative (explicit and implicit) and one for explicit positive. Reliability was assured through the following procedures: Two coders developed the coding criteria (the 1st and 2nd author), and then trained the third author on 20% of the data. Following that, the third author blind-coded an additional 20% of the data achieving sufficient agr eement. Next, the third author coded the full set o f 5 tasks, while the two other authors monitored reliability by double coding (blindly) 25% of each task. The overall Kappa of these blind codings was 0.85.

## Discussion

Our goal in this research was to develop a holistic tool for mapping students’ arithmetic discourse along the ritualexplorative axis, as well as to map their subjectif ying (identity construction) discourse.  We asked w hat the application of this tool to the discourse of 12 students, solving 3 tasks, would reveal. We also wished to examine to what extent the ritual or explorative performanc e of students is in coherence with their subjectifi cations – namely their evaluations of themselves as they work  on the tasks. Our findings revealed that the stude nts’ ritual/explorative performance was highly varied and it was generally consistent with their placement in the different achievement groups. Low achieving students generally performed more ritually than their high achieving peers. These findings add credibility to the theory of learning as advancing from ritual to explorative participation, as it shows that students who have mastered the arithmetic discourse (and are thus probably achieving highly on school mathematics tasks) perform mostly explorativ ely on the given tasks, while low achieving student s have not mastered the discourse and thus perform ritually.

In relation to the question of coherence between subjectifications and performance, our findings showed that the students’ subjectifications generally agreed with students’ ritual/explorative performance, with ritual enactments of routines accompanied more by negative subjectifi cations than explorative enactments. There was also coherence between the identity of the student as low, middle/high achieving or excelling (as seen in the placement of students in groups) and their subjectifications: the lower achieving students subjectified more negatively than the high-achieving students. However, there were al so interesting inconsistencies. One was where stude nts subjectified negatively, but their performance was relatively explorative. The other (rarer) occurrences were when students evaluated their performance positively but their performance was highly ritual. These findings strengthen the validity and utility of the RDP tool. The validity can be derived from the agreement between the scores on ritual/explorative performanc e on the three tasks and the general achievement and mathematical identities of the students. The utilit y of the tool can be found in the nuanced view of s tudents’ performance, which, for example, can reveal certain  students’ over-tendency tonegatively evaluate the ir performance, as well as certain tasks (such as addi ng fractions) that may be susceptible to over-confi dence (students’ subjectifying positively despite their ritual performance). Futur e work should examine if other tasks significantly change the picture obtained by the analysis of these tasks. Thus, this work is limited in the ability to generalize from the findings to the general arithmetic discourse of students. It is also limited by the small number of participants, a  regular limitation in highly work-intensive method s such as discourse analysis. Finally, the RDP mostly contain s standard calculation tasks, not complex problems that involve modeling or literacy, nor does it include n on-arithmetic parts of the mathematics curriculum s uch as geometry or statistics (data analysis). Thus, one should be careful in deriving from its results concl usions about the general mathematics discourse of students.

Nevertheless, the RDP has multiple avenues for future development and utility. First, the main utility of this tool is in examining interventions aimed at increasing explorative participation. Current paper-and-pencil tools are not sufficiently discriminative to examine such minute developments. Second, the RDP can function as a diagnostical tool for examining “pockets of ritual participation” that need to be attended to. As reviewed earlier, such “pockets” may widen over time and lead to generalized failure, despite students’ effort and aspirations (Heyd-Metzuyanim, 2015). Finally, learning to use the tool and analyz e students’ discourse with it, may teach teachers t o turn their attention towards processes of ritual and explorative participation. Although it is highly improbable that a teacher could expend the effort of analyzing each of their students with this tool, some experience with it may still go a long way. As shown in professional development effo rts in the past (e.g. the CGI professional developm ent program, (Franke et al., 1998)), when teachers beco me familiar with a certain theoretical lens that he lps them better understand their students’ learning processes, this can, by itself, change their teaching practices to be more attuned to their students’ needs.
