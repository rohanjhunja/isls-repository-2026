# Unlocking Young Minds: How K-12 Students Define Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This case study explores 437 K-12 students’ ontological descriptions of AI before and after they completed AI curricula in ELA classes. In this study, we explored students’ conceptual understandings through a text-mining approach. Initial findings indicate that before the intervention, students primarily offered examples of AI functions. However, after engaging with the curriculum modules, their descriptions became more comprehensive, including discussions on AI limitations and the significance of datasets for task completion.

## Introduction

The rapid growth of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in our daily lives has become increasingly pervasive, from virtual assistants to chatbots. As AI has advanced, it has become critical to support K-12 students’ understanding of AI concepts. Numerous efforts have been devoted to int roducing AI concepts to K-12 students (Wong et al., 2020; Ng et al., 2021). Yet, less is known about what the se students already know about AI concepts and how formal AI training can shape their conceptual understandin gs. Exploring students’ knowledge of the terms migh t affect pedagogy, curriculum design, and assessment strateg ies. Thus, this study aims to provide insights into  K12 students’ knowledge of AI and ML before and after t hey received formal training in their classrooms th rough a text-mining approach. Specifically, we aim to answer: Wh at is K-12 students’ level of familiarity with the terms of Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning, and what are the primary sources of their knowledge o ver the past three years? and How do K-12 students define the term AI before and after com pleting AI curriculum activities?

## Conceptual framework

As a conceptual framework, we followed an ontologic al approach (Gaifman, 1975) that aims to identify h ow a concept is constructed. This approach is specifical ly used to map out concepts by identifying its cons tituent components, the relationships among the se components as well as the descriptions and asset s that concurrently exits and interact. In the context of AI and ML education, the ontological approach can provide an understanding of how K-12 students describe the concepts of AI. This approach has been followed in different studies to explore teachers’ perspectives. As an example, Lai (2022) explored teachers’ perspectives of computational thinking and assessment regarding an ontology approach. This con ceptual framework was used to identify how teachers perceived CT as a multifaced construct. In this stu dy, an ontological approach was followed to reveal K-12 students’ conceptual understandings and multi-perspective representation of AI.

## Methods

This study took place in the context of K-12 ELA classrooms including English Honors, American Literature and Composition, ELA for 8th grades, and ELA for 9th grades. Seven teachers from five different public schools (i.e., five high schools and two middle  schools) attended a four-day AI PD workshop to engage with the StoryQ curriculum. In the workshop, teachers completed fiv e AI curriculum modules (i.e., “Exploring a Model”, “Reasoning and Representation”, “Features”, “Machin e Learning”, and “AI for Your Future”). After atten ding the workshop, each teacher implemented the StoryQ curriculum in their classrooms for approximately seven days. In total, 437 K-12 students (i.e., 217 middle schools, 220 high sch ools) from diverse backgrounds (i.e., 52.5% White, 19.2% Black or African American, 7.1% Asian,  10.4% mixed race, other 7% and 3.0% preferred not to answer) experienced the curriculum modules.

In this study, we specifically aimed to explore stu dents’ familiarity with the terms AI and ML and the ir ontological perspectives of AI. Therefore, we analyzed specific questions from pre-and-post surveys. Before and after engaging with the curriculum modules, students were asked to describe AI in their own words in the surveys. We analyzed their responses to these open-ended questions by employing text-mining approaches in the following order: data processing (i.e., removal of stop words, word stemming), a nd text summarization (i.e., term frequencies, word clouds). The text-mining approach provides an inductive perspective w ithout predefined classifications or initial hypotheses (Yu et al., 2011).

## Findings

Before and after the curriculum intervention, we investigated students’ familiarity with the terms AI and ML. We also explored the sources of their knowledge before  engaging in the curriculum activities. We found th at among 217 8th graders, 15.38% reported  knowing a lot about AI, 58% knew a little, 20.33% h ad heard of it but didn’t know what it means, and 7% had never heard of it, w hile only 2% knew a lot about ML, 17% knew a little, 28% had heard of it but didn’t know what it means, and 54.14% had never heard of it. Similarly, 22% of 11t h graders reported knowing a lot about AI, 63% knew a little,  and 8.73% had never heard of it. In contrast, for ML, 16% knew a lot, 26% knew a little, and 31% had never heard of it, highlighting a greater familiarity with AI compared to ML across grade levels. Moreover, our analysis of students’ familiarity with AI and ML also revealed that most students learned about AI through social media (19%), movies/TV shows (16%), friends/family (14%), and  TV reports/documentaries (11%), with only 5% citing extracurricular activities. These sources were consistent across grade levels. Similarly, students primarily learned  about ML through social media, including platforms  like YouTube and TikTok, with a small number mentioning exposure in high school classes such as Physics, Mathematics, and Computer Science, particularly among 11th graders. After exploring students’ familiarity with the term s, we explored how they defined these terms before and after engaging with the curriculum activities. Term frequency analysis and word clouds were follow ed to explore how the students described the terms. Durin g the text summarization process, we excluded direc t terms (i.e., “Artificial Intelligence”, and “AI”) from the students’ descriptions to reveal the most frequent words. Before the curriculum intervention, students frequently as sociated AI with words like “human” (n=124), “compu ter” (n=93), “robot” (n=72), “program” (n=69), and “technology” (n=63), often comparing AI’s capabilities to human abilities. After the intervention, the most frequen t words shifted to “human” (n=175), “computer” (n=1 37), “machine” (n=106), “learn” (n=70), and “program” (n=51) w hile describing the AI. After engaging with the curriculum activities, students used these terms to  provide more formal and thoughtful definitions, fo cusing on AI's goals, limitations, and examples. For example,  Destiny (i.e.,11th grade, never heard of AI before  the curriculum intervention) described AI as “the simulation of human intelligence processes by machines, especially computer systems. Specific applications of AI inclu de expert systems, natural language processing, spe ech recognition, and machine vision.” In her descriptio n, she described the term as a “simulation of human intelligence”. Instead of making a comparison to define the term, she brought up the goal of AI. Also, she enriched her descript ion by giving examples. This indicated a shift in s tudents’ understanding of AI from superficial comparisons to deeper, more comprehensive perspectives.

## Conclusion

This study demonstrated the gaps in the K-12 students’ initial understanding of AI and ML, influenced largely by informal sources such as social media and movies/TV shows. We found that students’ ontological perspectives of the term AI evolved from superficial associations l ike “human” and “robot” to thorough definitions tha t emphasized learning processes, goals, and applicati ons of AI. This underscores the importance of forma l AI education to prepare students for a future increasi ngly shaped by AI and ML, highlighting the need for broader integration of these topics into school curricula.
