# Developing Relational Agency for Addressing Racialization of School Discipline in the U.S

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, we adapt Edwards's (2005; 2007) concept of relational agency, situating it within the context of racialized schooling in the U.S. We posit that the ability to recognize and leverage others' support in addressing a complex problem necessitates the ability to acknowledge the historical legacies of systemic racial violence ingrained in everyday school practices. To support our theoretical argument, we present a case from Indigenous Learning Lab (ILL), an inclusive task force established to design a culturally responsive behavioral support system addressing racial disproportionality in school discipline in a predominantly white high school serving American Indian students. Specifically, we emphasize the relational agency of Mr. Archer, the school principal, as he worked together with ILL members, particularly American Indian members.

The purpose of this paper is to explore how a diver se group of local stakeholders developed relational  agency (Edwards, 2005; 2007) while collaborating on the In digenous Learning Lab (ILL) project. The project ai med to create a culturally responsive school  behavioral support system addressing racial disprop ortionality in school disciplinary practices at Newhope High School (NHS)  (Pseudonym) in rural Wisconsin in the U.S., servin g American Indian students. The ILL was an inclusive problem-solving task force where members collectively address local systemic social injustice issues (Bal et al., 2018). The ILL was comprised of members from diverse roles (e.g., parents, students, community members, educators, administrators) and racial/ethnic groups. This diverse group collaborated, recognized, and utilized each o ther’s socio-spatial-historical perspectives and lived experiences as legitimate sources of knowledge. Thi s collaborative approach allowed members to collect ively unpack the complex problem of racial disproportiona lity in school discipline and proposed solutions ta ilored to their localized contexts.

This paper specifically examines the relational age ncy of one member, Mr. Kevin Archer (Pseudonym), the principal at NHS, as he learned and developed h is agency through interactions and engagements with  other ILL members. Investigating Mr. Archer’s relational agency is critical because the creation of school d iscipline policies/system is typically perceived as the domain of school technocrats, including principals and other members of administration at the school or district level. Administrators, like Mr. Archer, possess the privilege and authority to make decisions regarding the school system. Hence, it is crucial to understand how individuals with power and privilege utilize these attributes to learn from ot her stakeholders, particularly parents, community m embers, and historically marginalized students to collectively create systemic changes. This paper is theoretically anchored in the framework of relational agency (Edwards, 2005; 2007), which emphasizes how a group of professionals across boundaries of practice work together to solve a complex problem. In the context of the U.S education system, embedded within a history of discrimination and oppression, we argue that the framework of relational agency needs expan sion to capture the historical-social-spatial-political nuances that diverse groups of people bring to the table as  they collaborate. The overarching question guiding  this study is: How did the school principal develop relational agency throughout ILL? Racialization of school disciplinary practices The racialization of discipline in U.S. schools rem ains a persistent civil rights issue, with Black and American Indian/Alaska Native students experiencing the high est percentages of out-of-school suspensions in public K-12 schools (Leung-Gagné et al., 2022). Overrepresentation of historically marginalized students in school discipline is not merely an individual issue of students’ inca pability to follow school norms. It is also not sim ply attributed to educators’ biases in singling out certain studen ts based on their racial groups. We argue that raci al disproportionality is a manifestation of how discipline systems—including norms and the roles of administrators and educators themselves—are embedded within whiteness and are permeated by day-to-day interactions within and outside of the school environment. The racialization of school discipline is fundamentally a form of violence against Black and Brown students, justified through  disciplinary measures. The long-term impacts include the negative stigma associated with the criminalization  of Black and Brown bodies and the loss of instruct ional time (Losen & Martinez, 2020), among other consequences.

For American Indian students and families, historic al practices, such as massacres, forced relocations away from native territories, the devastating impacts of boarding schools (Lewis et al., 2021), forced assimilations, and numerous broken federal treaties  mark the dispossession of indigeneity. In the conte mporary context, even without the presence of boarding schools, the settler-colonial education system continues to function as a tool for the dispossession of American Indian sovereignty (L omawaima & McCarty, 2006). In the school year 2017-18 alone, American Indian/Alaskan Native students were suspended more often than the national average, with a rate of 7% compared to the overall average of 5% (Leung-Gagné et al., 2022). The current settler-colonial education system persistently marginalizes American Indian students through disciplinary practices. Local policy reform from the ground-up Given the nature and complexity of racial dispropor tionality embedded in the structure of oppression a nd discrimination, it is imperative for local stakehol ders to work together in addressing this complex issue. Top-down policy initiatives miss the complex nuance of how historically marginalized students and families experience racial disproportionality on a daily bas is. School administrators’ ontological and epistemo logical understanding, as well as their approach to address racial disproportionality might differ from those of the students and families from historically marginalized backgrounds. Therefore, boundary-crossing efforts that bring multiple stakeholders with various roles and responsibilities to work together to collaboratively address a complex problem is imperative (Akkerman & Baker, 2011; Ko et al., 2021; Edwards, 2020). Despite multiple policy reforms, local school polic ies that co-design school discipline systems with — rather than for—historically marginalized students and families rem ain rare (Penuel, 2019). Although efforts, in the form of Multi-Tiered Support Systems (MTSS), have been made to co-design with students and families, transformative changes are still lacking. A similar  injustice occurs in other racially contested educa tion systems such as special education, where historically margi nalized students are also o verrepresented in disability categories. The lack of co-design with historically marginalized students and families is far from an act of distributed expertise between school and family, it ignores and further exacerbates the racialized system of special education that perceives historically marginalized families as incapable of co-designing policies that impact their lives.

## Theoretical framework

This paper is grounded in the theoretical concept o f relational agency (Edwards, 2005; 2007). Relation al agency is action with others through which to recognize others as resources for collaborative problem-solving and crafting new tools and practices (Edwards, 2005, 2007). Relational agency transcends mere collaborative efforts. Instead, it signifies a capability to identify, actively seek and offer sup ports as well as effectively employ the support of others as valuable assets to transform an existing system (Edwards, 2005; 2007). In CHAT terms, relational agency is capacity to work with others to expand the object that one is working on and trying to transform by recognisin g and accessing the resources that others bring to bear as they interpret and respond to the object. It is a capacity which involves recognising that another person may be a resource a nd that work needs to be done to elicit, recognise and negotiate the use of that resource in  order to align oneself in joint action on the object. It offers an enhanced version of pe rsonal agency and as a capacity it can be learnt. (Edwards, 2005, p. 172) From the cultural-historical activity theory (CHAT) viewpoint, racial  disproportionality in school discipline is a manifestation of historically accumulated and ongoi ng systemic contradictions that are embedded in set tler colonialism, institutional racism and ableism, and structural inequities. This systemic injustice is i ntersectional and extends beyond a single activity system.

Building on the original concept of relational agen cy, in this paper, we offer and expansion Edward’s concept to the context of racialized school discipl inary practices in the U.S. We acknowledge the capa city to recognize and utilize other stakeholder’s knowledge  as an imperative in boundary crossing. However, in  the context of racialized and politicized education system in the U.S., those characteristics requires further adaptation. We argue that the capacity to “recognize” other peo ple as resources necessitates the ability to acknowledge the historical legacies of educational debt and systemic racial violence persisting within  everyday school practices, including disciplinary practices, both in the past and the present (Kramarczuk Voulgarides, 2023; Ladson-Billings, 2006). Stakeholders' ability to recognize  historical legacies of systemic oppression in scho ol disciplinary practices will manifest in their capac ity to draw upon historical accounts and interpreta tions of systemic oppression and racism when collaboratively addressing complex problems like racial disproportionality in school discipline. This, in turn, informs stakeh olders—especially those trained to operate within bureaucr atic technicalities, such as school administrators— in their interpretation and approaches when making sense of racial disproportionality in their localized socio-spatial-historical contexts. Furthermore, the capacity to “recognize” entails acknowledging that historically marginalized students and families, including American Indian and Black students and their families, have endured and perse vered in the face of systemic oppression (Brayboy, 2005). Therefore, they possess legitimate knowledge to co-create solutions to the perennial issue. By recogni zing the intricate socio-spatial-historical contexts contributing to racially dispara te disciplinary outcomes for historically marginalized students, it opens the possibility for diverse s takeholders to actively seek out various perspectiv es, particularly those of historically marginalized students and their families. Second, we argue that in relational agency, the cap acity to utilize other stakeholders' perspectives, knowledge, and empirical experiences, especially those from historically marginalized backgrounds, requires the ability to place historically marginalized  students, families, and community members at the fo refront of local school policy reforms as experts in their own exper iences (i.e., the racialized experience of school d iscipline). In boundary crossing work, each stakeholder enters the  collaboration with diverse ways of looking at the problem, in this case, racial disproportionality. Because th eir motives differ, each member naturally has a uni que understanding of racial disproportionality and cont ributions to finding solutions to the problem. In s ummary, we argue that the capacity to work together requires s takeholders, particularly those with privilege and power to determine the school system, to recognize the education system as embedded within a larger system of oppression and discrimination and to center the voice and valu es of historically marginalized students and famili es in the local level reform efforts.

## Data collection and analysis

Mr. Archer, who is the main focus  of the boundary-crossing actor in this paper, showing the developme nt of relational agency, identified himself as a white male. At the time of the ILL project, Mr. Archer had been working at NHS for over 10 years in various roles. He held the role of principal just before this project began. We utilized ethnographic data collected during the design phase of the ILL that took place from the 2019/20 academic year as the data for this paper. The ILL was comprised of 1 4 school stakeholders embodying multiple r oles and demographic backgrounds (e.g., three parents/commun ity members, five educators, three students, and th ree administrators). ILL members met monthly for a dura tion of one hour and 30 minutes. ILL sessions were guided by a cycle of systemic change (Engeström, 2008) (See figure 1). For this paper, our primary data consists of video transcripts of the recorded meetings. The analytic approach of this study followed abductive process, characterized by iterative movement between theory and data for the purpose of theory building (Tavory & Timmermans, 2014). Initially, we applied Edwards’ original concept of relational agency to make sense of the data. Upon t horough examination, we observed an expanded form of agency  among ILL members. Subsequently, we revisited the theoretical framework and adapted Edwards' model to align with this racialized and politicized  educational context, and as commonly found in the U.S. Utilizin g this expanded framework, detailed in the theoreti cal framework section, we reanalyzed the data and discuss our findings in this paper. During the data analysis process, we were not able to conduct member checking with principal Archer.

## Context

Within NHS, American Indian students were disproportionately represented in school disciplinary actions. While they accounted for about 20% of the student population, they received 64.3% of in-school suspensions and 52.2% of out-of-school suspensions (Wisc onsin Department of Public Instruction, 2019). Raci al disproportionality in NHS has its roots in the social-geographical-historical-political contexts of Newhope, a place known as the “Deep North.” The city of Newhope was the epicenter of anti-Indian movements and anti-spearfishing protests that took place in Wisconsin during the 1980s and early 1990s (Nesper, 2002). Despite state and federal court affirmations of the Ojibwe treaty rights, which granted Tribal p eople the right to harvest fish in their ceded  lands, the community has a memory and lived experience of racism, exclusion, prejudice, and hostility between Indigenous and White residents. The settler colonial tensions are still visible in Newhope today through multiple community conflicts.

## Findings

Specifically, Mr. Archer evolved from viewing school discipline and behavioral achievement as neutral information to adopting a perspective rooted in the socio-spatial historical understanding of discipline within the framework of settler colonial practices. ## Discussion

In this paper, we expanded Edwards’s original concept of relational agency and argued that in boundary-crossing efforts in the context of racialized and politicized U.S. schooling, relational agency needs to includ e one's ability to draw upon historical accounts and interpretations of systemic oppression and racism when working together to unpack the root causes of racial disproportionality  in their localized contexts. This is the first ste p towards designing the new culturally responsive behavioral support system. The case of Mr. Archer’s relational agency provides an example of how relational agency is pos sible when members, particularly those in power and privilege, were able to acknowledge the socio-spatial-historical contexts of racial disproportionalit y and center the perspectives of those who have been historicall y marginalized and utilized those perspectives for collective learning and growth.

This paper can advance existing literature on relat ional agency, particularly within a co-design of an educational system and reform that requires collabo rative boundary-crossing work. While relational agency is a familiar concept in the field of learning science, the framework proposed here can significantly contr ibute to addressing educational challenges within politicize d and racialized contexts. This expanded framework holds value for researchers, practitioners, and decision-makers alike who are interested in conducting school reform that centers the knowledge and lived experiences of hist orically marginalized members of the school and com munity to address complex educational problems at the local level.
