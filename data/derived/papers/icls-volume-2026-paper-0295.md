# A Tale of Two Rivers: Pedagogical Implications Learned Through the Co- Design of an Environmental Engineering Curriculum

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As the impacts of climate change intensify, it is critical to support high school students in developing Critical Science Agency (CSA) and Systems Thinking (ST) to leverage science learning to address environmental concerns in their local communities and beyond. This study is part of a project involving a collaboration between university researchers, informal science educators, and high school students and teachers to codesign a place-and community-based environmental engineering curriculum. We explore pedagogical implications surfaced through the analysis of teacher and student codesign experiences and reflections that focus on community-embedded and relational environmental learning. Findings highlight the importance of physical presence to support critical and informed socio-ecological understanding of place.

High schools in the United States (U.S.) have historically implemented science teaching that treats white and middle-class cultural practices as the norm (Schenkel et al., 2019). Such practices emphasize mastering the knowledge and understanding of Western science, while positioning other types of scientific knowledge as inferior (Bang & Medin, 2014). Thus, there is a need for more justice-centered approaches to science teaching and learning that integrate more diverse ways of knowing, being and doing (Mora les-Doyle., 2017, 2024; Clark et al., 2024) particularly in communities that serve students from non-Western backgrounds. Increasingly, there are varied implementations of science education that seek to integrate justice-centered practices and push for the integration of more experiential knowledge and practice (Schenkel et al., 2019); one such approach is community-based science (CBS).

CBS seeks to create learning experiences that center community ways of knowing and use science as a tool to support community change. CBS is defined as science instruction that is created in collaboration with local communities to support local, place-based, and meaningful learning experiences (Gyles, 2024). Research has shown that CBS teaching that promotes the development of students’ critical-science agency (CSA) integrates canonical science content with students’ own funds of knowledge to work toward more justice-centered community action through science learning (Gyles, 2024). This study centers around a design-based implementation research project (Fishman & Penuel, 2018) co-designing a CBS high school environmental engineering unit that integrates systems thinking (ST; Yoon et al.,

2018) and CSA (Schenkel et al., 2019) to address challenges in a local river, the SAR. In this paper, we explore

the pedagogical implications learned from high school teachers and students during co-design sessions and followup interviews around integrating CSA and ST into a CBS curriculum. For this paper, we focus on the pedagogical implication of “community-embedded and relational learning” and, more specifically, zoom in on how students’ physical presence at the river impacts their understanding of the SAR community. Based on our conjecture that students’ physical presence at the river site would be critical to their development of CSA and ST, we analyze how teachers and students validated and extended our understanding of the pedagogical implications  of one’s physical presence at the SAR to support environmental engineering learning. As such, we explore the following research question: What pedagogical implications emerged from teachers’ and students’ evolving understandings of CSA and ST in CBS, developed through participation in co-design? Theoretical framing This study is guided by two theoretical frameworks: CSA and ST. CSA involves youth developing their expertise in both science and their community context and using that expertise to take justice-oriented action to solve 1967 community problems (McNeil & Vaughn, 2012; Schenkel et al., 2019; Vossoughi & Shea, 2019). We use CSA to highlight how students can build agency through the development of scientific skills to improve their worlds. ST refers to one's ability to identify structures, recognize patterns of relationships and interconnections between structures, and synthesize these patterns into a unified view of the whole (Senge, 1990). The goal of ST is to prepare engineers to understand multidisciplinary systems to tackle la rge-scale engineering problems (Koral-Kadova et al. 2018). We use ST to emphasize how students understand their place in socio-ecological systems, and the dynamic interrelationships between the engineered and biological worlds.

## Context and participants

Project CoAST  is a collaborative initiative aimed at co-designing a place-based, community-informed environmental engineering curriculum for high schools focused on a local river ecosystem, the SAR. Through codesign, we work to integrate ST and CSA into curricular design to support students in examining environmental challenges facing the SAR and proposing environmental solutions that center community needs. To support curricular design, we engaged students and teachers in activities similar to those we might include in the curriculum, receiving feedback and suggestions for design and pedagogical approaches. Co-design participants included three teachers and seven students from three local Title 1 and minority-serving high schools, seven education researchers, and two environmental science educators from the local county department of education. The co-design process included 11 sessions where teachers and students participated in activities, including: (a) building understanding and knowledge of the SAR, (b) taking field trips to the SAR and interviewing community members, (c) identifying and assessing SAR environmental data and challenges, and (d) brainstorming and testing potential unit activities. Co-design sessions were facilitated by the researchers and environmental science educators and took a variety of formats including modeling activiti es, co-constructing themes, and meta-cognitive discussions around relevant concepts (such as CSA and ST). Exit interview protocol To understand participant experiences in the co-design process, researchers conducted exit interviews. Separate protocols were created for teachers and students with 10 and 11 questions respectively. Students were asked to explain a model they drew of the river at the conclusion of the program, as well as general questions around their relationship with the SAR and how it connects to their identity, participation in co-design and tensions that rose throughout, and how roles, relationships, and session structure impacted their experience and understandings of CSA and ST. The teacher protocol included general questions about their co-design experience and understandings of their participation in co-design, tensions that rose throughout, how they think co-design activities promoted CSA and ST, and their overall thoughts about participating in the program.

## Data sources and analytic method

Data included transcripts of 10 audio-recorded 1-hour exit interviews with the student and teacher participants after the co-design sessions, audio-recordings of the 11, three-hour co-design sessions, and 11 sets of field notes. Interviews were the primary  data source for this paper and findings were triangulated with audio-recorded codesign sessions and field notes. The first phase of analysis involved analytical memo writing (Saldaña, 2016) based on co-design discussions. The second phase involved conducting open coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1990) to understand what teachers and students determined as important practices for developing CSA and ST. Finally, we applied deductive codes from Bang and Medin (2014) to understand how they conceptualized CSA and ST and how that informed pedagogical implications of what should be included in the curriculum. Implication #1: Visiting the SAR helps students to observe river ecosystems and build personal connections Teachers and students consistently situated the importance of the river to their local community, which they defined as composed of both biotic and abiotic factors; they also highlighted the importance of visiting the river to develop more nuanced understandings of its ecosystems. This proved to be vital to students’ development of ST, as building these connections supported their understanding of the river’s role in sustaining life, as well as their understandings of how the different components of the river work together, such as how the engineered features support the possibility of people living near the river. Ricardo [student]: “My favorite was probably the field trip to the actual river itself because I was able to see it firsthand, get a more in depth perception of the river... Even though it's a lot shorter, I was able to walk, see people using it for recreational things.” In his interview, Ricardo stated one of the field trips to the SAR was essential to supporting his conceptualization of the multiple uses of the river —in this case, for recreational activities. As all of the student participants lived near a part of the SA R that did not support recreational activities, including Ricardo, their exploration of other areas helped to expand their conceptual boundaries as to the multiple uses of the river. Observing people engaging in recreational activities along the river supp orted Ricardo in developing a more critical understanding of community uses of the river in ways he initially did not know. José [teacher]: “... the field trips that we did were really helpful to see how students are kind of seeing the information in person, how they're kind of processing it, so that … I know how to … address it within the classroom, how to kind of direct thinking, or help guide thinking.” While the benefits of field trips on student learning and development can seem obvious, in this excerpt, José highlights how teachers being present along with students at the SAR supports their learning by understanding how their students are interacting with the physical space. As José mentions, this experience allowed him to see his students in relation to the space and to understand how that would impact in-class learning. This opportunity provides him the means to take this “real world” experience and t ie it back to his regular instruction. It also provided him with the opportunity to understand what aspects of the field trip stood out to students which could impact how and what he teaches when the actual curriculum implementation comes to fruition. Implication 2: Exploring contrasting river regions prompts students’ thinking about diversity in river ecosystems As part of the co-design sessions, the team took two field trips to different parts of the SAR, one to an area of the river that had a “natural” flowing ecosystem, and another, which represented the area of the SAR that most students had a prior relationship with, which is primarily concrete and rarely has flowing water. Teachers and students underscored the importance of multiple site visits in supporting understandings of the SAR’s different ecological systems and critical exploration as to why such differences exist. This implication in particular fostered 1969 students’ development of ST by supporting their ability to recognize patterns and explore interconnections between structures. It also supported students’ CSA because it taught them about what a “just” river system is like by demonstrating that higher-income communities had access to a “real” river, whereas lower-income neighborhoods had access to the concrete section that had no water in it. Carol [teacher]: “I think a lot of the kids in the concrete river area don't see a real river this close, and they're like, what? And it was interesting to see their reactions to it, yeah. Like, how can the same river be so different in two different places?” Carol, a longtime resident around the SAR and teacher, highlighted how unique of an experience it was for the students to see a different part of the river, and how it supported their scientific questioning to think more critically—both socially and ecologically —about what may be causing such drastic differences in the two river ecosystems. Carol highlights in this comment the importance of visiting contrasting river regions as some students may otherwise never see the parts of the river with the more “natural” ecosystem, limiting their understanding of the multiple ecosystems in which make up the SAR.

Analise [student]: “I do think that maybe there's a way for them to be smart about the decisions to where everyone can have the same amount of river water consistently. And not just you go from one area that has lots of water, you see a lot of it flowing. There's fish. They're jumping out of the water, probably even playing. It just looks lively. And then you go to another part where it just looks dead, like a barren, abandoned Zombie Town river.” Analise was the student that had the least experience with the river prior to joining the codesign team, while also living near a part of the SAR —the one we visited during the first field trip —that is more “barren” and “Zombie Town”-like. Analise’s perspec tive demonstrates a broadening understanding of how the SAR is made up of different ecosystems that are a result of humandecision-making. Her use of “there’s a way for them to be smart about the decision to where everyone can have the same amount” demonstrates a critical  understanding of how human communities and social structures play a significant part in determining socio-ecological systems, and the roles of humans in affecting SAR ecosystem structures.

## Significance

Despite research demonstrating that students from historically marginalized backgrounds are able to meaningfully contribute to science learning when education is anchored in their lived experiences and community knowledge, Western teaching practices frequently implemented in U.S. classrooms continue to exclude their brilliance in praxis (Calabrese Barton et al, 2020; Schenkel et al., 2019). Findings from our sessions structured to co-design a justice-centered CSA and ST-oriented CBS curriculum, as well as p ost-design interviews, explored the pedagogical implications around the significance of community-embedded and relational learning at exploration sites, in this case the SAR, through an environmental engineering curriculum. More specifically, these findings extend our understanding of how physical presence at learning sites can foster the development of students’ CSA and ST by building personal connections to river ecosystems and prompting critical thinking around the diversity of river ecosystems. For exam ple, site visits supported students’ critical thinking about river ecosystems and connections between humandecision making, ecosystem diversity, and engineering design (ST) and what justiceoriented actions could improve the river environment and benefit their neighborhoods (CSA). Research has shown that codesign can be a powerful methodological approach for researchers and participants alike to not only make sense of scientific knowledge, but also gain an understanding of design and pedagogical practice (Penuel, 2022). To our knowledge, there have been no studies that have utilized codesign as a space to explore how curriculum can be designed to concurrently develop students’ CSA and ST, specifically within a CBS environmental engineering curriculum. Our work demonstrates how co-design offers an opportunity to leverage participants' lived and experiential knowledge to expand upon traditional pedagogical practices and design curricular activities that support students’ critical thinking and agency in learning.
