# Machine Epistemology as a Lens to AI Ethics Education: Forging Early Adolescents’ Epistemic Insights through Constructionist Learning in Upper Primary and Junior Schools

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
K-12 AI ethics education often focuses on topics including bias or privacy but rarely addresses how AI systems generate decisions in ways that create ethical challenges. This gap limits students’ ability to grasp the underlying sources of these ethical issues. Drawing on machine epistemology, which conceptualizes AI as an epistemic agent with distinctive ways of knowing and reasoning, this design-based research investigates how constructionist learning can foster students’ epistemic and ethical insights about AI. The study was implemented during a two-week summer program with thirty-four upper primary and junior secondary students in Hong Kong, integrating hands-on AI activities with guided reflection. Data from classroom interactions, interviews, and student artifacts were analyzed using inductive thematic coding and a deductive framework based on levels of epistemic insight. Preliminary findings show a developmental progression from surface-level descriptions of AI behavior toward emerging conceptual and critical reasoning, alongside persistent misconceptions about AI’s reasoning processes. Goals of the research This dissertation aims to design and investigate a constructionist learning environment that supports early adolescents’ epistemic insights about AI. Specifically, it seeks to: • Develop and refine a constructionist pedagogical framework that integrates hands-on AI activities with guided reflection to support students’ epistemic and ethical explorations of AI. • Examine how students’ epistemic insights develop as they engage with an AI ethics curriculum. • Identify recurring misconceptions and reasoning challenges that arise during the learning process.

The rise of AI brings innovation alongside ethical concerns related to bias, transparency, and autonomy (Adams et al., 2023). Addressing these issues requires understanding how AI knows—how systems generate, justify, and sometimes fail in producing knowledge. Yet, most K-12 AI ethics interventions focus on what AI does rather than on the epistemic roots of ethical challenges (Wiese et al., 2025). Machine epistemology offers a productive lens for deepening students’ reasoning by treating AI systems as epistemic agents that generate knowledge through statistical inference, pattern recognition, and probabilistic reasoning. This perspective helps students understand why AI systems may be biased, brittle, or opaque (Barelli et al., 2024). Drawing on epistemic insight (Billingsley et al., 2018) as a theoretical anchor, this study examines learners’ abilities to reflect on the nature and limits of AI knowledge, and how these epistemic processes connect to real-world consequences. To address this gap in K-12 AI ethics education, we leverage constructionism (Papert, 1980). While constructionist approaches support engagement, scholars highlight that careful scaffolding is needed to foreground AI’s epistemic mechanisms and connect technical processes to ethical reasoning (Lin et al., 2025). Against this backdrop, this study examines how early adolescents develop epistemic insight within a constructionist AI ethics curriculum and identifies the challenges they encounter. This study employed a design-based research (DBR) approach to iteratively design and investigate a constructionist learning environment that supports early adolescents’ epistemic insights about AI (Barab & Squire, 2004). The eight-session summer camp involved 34 upper-primary and junior secondary students in Hong Kong and integrated constructionist activities—such as training machine learning (ML) models with Google Teachable Machine (GTM) and creating poems with generative AI—paired with guided reflection. An embedded qualitative case study of 11 focal students captured diverse engagement and achievement profiles. Data sources included video-recorded lessons, fieldnotes, student artifacts, semi-structured interviews, a nd teacher debriefings. Analysis combined inductive thematic coding with deductive coding of epistemic levels (descriptive, conceptual, critical/integrative) to trace emergent dimensions of students’ epistemic reflections and assess the varying degrees of sophistication in their reasoning. All procedures followed ethical protocols with informed consent.

## Preliminary findings

Student development in epistemic insight students focused on surface features of AI behavior and often relied on literal or anthropomorphic explanations. At the conceptual level, they recognized AI’s reliance on patterns, data quality, and human input, and began identifying structural limits in machine reasoning. At the critical/integrative level, some students coordinated multiple epistemic considerations—such as ML’s probabilistic reasoning, paradigm differences, and ethical consequences—to evaluate how and why AI produces particular outcomes. Across levels, students’ reasoning clustered into four dimensions: the nature of AI knowledge, human involvement in AI knowing, limits of AI reasoning, and comparative reasoning across AI paradigms. For example, in a banana-classification task using GTM, descriptive reasoning centered on visible actions (“we give AI pictures so it can learn”), conceptual reasoning involved data-pattern explanations and recognition of probability, and critical/integrative reasoning connected misclassification to broader ethical issues such as fairness and bias. Learning challenges and difficulties Despite growth, students showed persistent challenges. Some exhibited cross-level struggles, recognizing the limits of simple data labelling yet defaulting to fixed positions without weighing context or trade-offs. Others demonstrated dimensional struggles, conflating ML and generative AI as generic “pattern-matching” systems or treating prompting as equivalent to training. These flattened distinctions constrained their ethical reasoning and signaled the need for explicit scaffolds that foreground paradigm differences and epistemic uncertainty. Expected contributions Theoretically, this study advances an epistemic perspective on AI ethics by positioning epistemic insight as a core lens for understanding how young learners interpret AI’s knowledge sources, reasoning processes, and limitations. It also offers fine-grained evidence of how such insight develops across AI paradigms. Practically, it generates design principles for constructionist AI learning environments that foreground epistemic mechanisms—rather than technical procedures alone—by leveraging students’ naïve ideas as productive resources and integrating hands-on making with structured reflection. These contributions support the design of equitable, human-centered AI literacy practices that strengthen learners’ epistemic agency and ethical reasoning when engaging with issues such as opacity, authorship, and human–AI co-agency.
