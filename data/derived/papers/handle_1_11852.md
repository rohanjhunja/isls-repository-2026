# Deepening our Epistemic Scripts: CSCL at the Intersection of Learning, Collaboration, and Information Sciences

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The field of Computer Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) research has always needed to refine and deepen its conceptual core in order to regulate the centrifugal and centripetal forces inherent in its multidisciplinarity. Whilst the concept of shared meaning making has been influential as a focus for CSCL, we argue firstly that three main changes over the past decades—collaborative learning becoming mainstream, bespoke systems giving way to platform infrastructures and changes in the scale of collaboration—necessitate rethinking the focus and boundaries of the domain. Secondly, we argue that CSCL needs to be re-situated with respect to adjacent fields, such as learning, information and collaboration sciences. Finally, we propose the utility of focussing on the forms of theorizing within CSCL, drawing on the concept of “epistemic scripts” that give greater precedence to theorizing the role of technology.

## Introduction

CSCL was from its beginnings a  multidisciplinary field of research.  Fueled by the broadening availability of Internet tools, research on group/cooperative/colla borative learning in educational and social psychol ogy came together with computer and cognitive sciences to pr oduce powerful new theories, models and tools. The centrifugal and centripetal forces that  operate in  any multidisciplinary field have been held in balance due to CSCL’s strong conceptual core, defined by Koschmann (2002) as “a field centrally concerned with meaning and practices of meaning making in the context of joint activ ity and the ways in which these practices are media ted through designed artifacts ”. However, the purpose and the extent of multidisciplinarity need to be revisited regularly because neither CSCL nor its adjacent disciplines have remained static. We identify three changes over the past decades that might change what we consider the best configuration of adjacent and auxiliary disciplines for productive CSCL research. In this short paper, we focus less on  theoretical constructs and theories we exchange—if not co-develop—with other disciplines and more on the forms of theorizing we share with them; on what are termed epistemic scripts in some corners of the philosophy and sociology of science (e.g, Boxenbaum & Rouleau, 2011; Grover & Lyytinen, 2015). In a nutshell, as CSCL changes  and matures, we see  it connecting beyond educational and social psychology  to additional  collaboration sciences  (such as CSCW, collaborative design, etc.) and beyond Computer Science to a broader range of information sciences. This closer relation with other substantive research areas also provides us with opportunities to widen our epistemic scripts. After outlining recent changes in the CSCL ecosyste m, we discuss  a possible reconfiguration of the domain in relation to information, learning and col laboration sciences, then the possible contribution  of defining the field’s epistemic scripts to defining research perspectives. Changes in the CSCL ecosystem CSCL emerged out of the growth of Internet technolo gies, so-called progressive pedagogies that emphasised student autonomy and the beginnings of the ‘situate d’ and ‘distributed’ cognition critiques of individualistic information processing cognitive science. Over the past few decades, at least three main changes occurred in and around CSCL.

Firstly, collaborative pedagogies became mainstream in many countries. It is no longer necessary to take pains to argue for the general interest of instigat ing learning situations where collective activities  are organised involving learners and teachers, often with technol ogy mediation. This means that research can focus o n institutional organisation, de sign and understanding the processes involved, often drawing on advanced formal techniques for analysing group behaviours using existing platforms. This motivates us to suggest that an adjacent discipline should be  Organization Science and more generally what we label Collaboration Sciences — a field that does not yet exist but corresponds to an inter esting possibility. Collaboration sciences would include disciplines such as social psychology, specifically  the subfield of small-group research  (or group dynamics, Forsyth, 2010) and its variants in organisational psychology: (work) team research (Mathieu et al., 2018),  group and team communication  (Beck et al., 2021), and group and organisational learning  (Argote & Levine, 2020). CSCL also historically shares roots with CSCW  — given that professional development and training ar e shared objects of study, — also a form of organisational research. On the foundational side, cognitive and neuro-science research that is concerned with social learning (Tamir & Thornton, 2018) should also be mentioned. Secondly, technology for collaboration has become i nfrastructure (see Ludvigsen & Steier, 2019). Of course, bespoke online software for collaboration is still being developed, as it was from the beginnings of broadly available Internet and CSCL in the 1990s; but such software rarely makes its way out of th e research lab and the duration of funded projects. There are exceptions o f course, of which an emblematic example is the CSILE/KnowledgeForum® (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014). But the main effect for CSCL research, of the now prevalent use of platforms such as Moodle, is to pu t the emphasis on designing the organisation of gro ups of students and teachers, with their complex sequences  of tasks, rather than on the characteristics of th e software itself. This trend makes us suggest that Information Systems (IS) research is more closely r elated to CSCL than Computer Science. This reflects the evolution from bespoke collaboration tools to collaboration infrastructure. IS research focuses on the use of information technology to solve business and organisational problems (Paul et al., 2021). It emphasises how people, organisations, and processes interact with technology, employing theories and methods from behavioural, social, and design sciences, incl uding qualitative ones. Another important area has been and remains to be Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) research (Meen at al, 20 23). While in the past years little of bespoke CSCL technologies got developed  —Zoom, Teams, Slack etc provide rich means of online collaboration—we may see much more of artisan educational software being developed again, on the foundation of AI as infrastructure. AI makes it much easier to create technology-rich learning environments. Thirdly, and this emerges from the above, collaboration has changed scale. The foundational situation of collaborative learning is, historically, that of the small group (or even the dyad), where students work together in a largely synchronous manner in the classroom. And what might be termed the classical definition of collaboration was predicated on that situation (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995; Dillenbourg, 1999; Baker, 2015), in that it involves mostly synchronous agency towards a shared representation of a problem, over a limited timescale. Today,  the prototypical case is rather that of the large colle ctive, often blending online with face-to-face, synchronous with asynchronous work, over larger timescales. This is another driver for CSCL to interface more e xtensively with collaboration and information sciences (including HCI, see Figure 1). Figure 1 CSCL's Adjacent Sciences.

CSCL not only ‘imports’ from cogent areas of research but also contributes to them. CSCL makes important contributions to the use of technology in education  by developing the research basis for supporting me aning making and knowledge building in small groups and i n learning collectives. Its contributions take the  form of theories that explain and artifacts (e.g., designs and software applications) that support meaning mak ing and knowledge building. We propose that intersubjective  meaning making is still what makes CSCL interestin g (Suthers, 2006) because it is still(!) different from the  prevalent view of collaborative learning: individual learning merely stimulated and resourced by a social context. Its relevance has even grown as intersubjectivity has been lifted from its individualist-constructivist roots to the level of socio-cultural learning (Cole, 1998). Also, the idea that knowledge is an accomplished and apt practice rather than a substance or representation has been further advanced by inferentialism (Brandom, 2001), a philosophy of language and meani ng that is increasingly takin g hold in education as well (e.g., Derry, 2017). Thus, combined with Knowledge Building theory (Scardama lia & Bereiter, 2014) which tells us how students can learn to go beyond existing culturally provided knowledge, CSCL has indeed a powerful conceptual core.

Theorising in CSCL: Deepening our epistemic scripts Like any multidisciplinarity field of research, CSCL faces challe nges in managing its boundaries and its focus. For instance, in their review and analysis of CSCL theories, Gerry Stahl and Kai Hakkarainen  (2021) noted a "... confusing variety of incommensurate, competing theories..."  with roots in "... constructivist, socio-cultural, social-psychological, and information processing frameworks." (p. 26). There are so many constructs and methods to consider just from educational research alone, a nd so many technologies and technological artefacts  ("apps") are being developed that can be used in and for collaboration. Are we adding to the distraction and fragmentation when we suggest that CSCL should engage with even more disciplines? The conceptual variety and the multiple frameworks used in CSCL can be seen not as a weakness but as a strength for an interdisciplinary field. We a lways had researchers in the community who look ed at the same phenomenon through different lenses. That is not to say that it is not a challenge to br ing these perspectives together on the conceptual and the methodological level. It is interesting to study how other fields achieve this, or not, as the case may be. Even if other disciplines do not have a solution (yet), we should avoid making the same mistakes. Particularly relevant and i nformative in this respect i s research in Information Systems research (e.g., Avison, 2014; Grover & Lyytinen, 2015; Chatterjee et al., 2024). This is because IS research uses social science methodology as do many CSCL studies, and also because information technology is the least theorized aspect of CSCL, compared to the attention that theories of learning and collaboration have received. We only have space to discuss one study in more detail. Groover & Lyytinen (2015) phrase their analysis of knowledge production in IS research in terms of epistemic scripts. The notion of an epistemic script originates in organizational theory, where it refers to habituated ways of organizational thinking—deeply rooted, preferred, and legitimate methods of producing knowledge (Boxe nbaum & Rouleau, 2011). In academia, these scripts “...embody the (implicit) cognitive templates that underpin an academic community’s collective understanding of how valid research knowledge is to be produced —in particular, how theory and empirics become inter related during the process” (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015, p. 272). Through their analysis they find that in IS research, theory formation is done frequently and that the dominating script i s one of suggesting and testing mid-range theory. This script, by the estimate of the authors based on analysis of a sample, accounted around 2015 for over 70% of the published research. Their characterization of mid-range theory is worth quoting in full: The term mid-range theory for us simply denotes research models that result from borrowing abstract reference theories  by transferring and specializing them within an IS context. During this process, highly stylized theoretical constructs from the reference theory typically get instantiated directly into the IS context witho ut significant modification and extension (...). The key contribution of IS scholars in this process is to combine reference theory with some atheoretic, generic accounts of technology, re presented as an investment or a perception or technology that is assumed to reside in the background. (p. 272). Grover and Lyytinen also note that the reference theory —for example, the Technology Acceptance Model—is often uncritically accepted, and new data are usually interpreted as reinforcing it rather than requiring modification or novel theory. There are also opportunity costs: If many researchers in a field are busy working on a s mall set of reference theories using the mid-range script, the likelihood of new insights being uncovered is reduced. As root causes (and as consequences, as the re are feedback loops) for this state of affairs they authors identify (i) inadequate treatment of the IT artefact (in the ext reme, the technical artefact is solely nominal: con tained in the setting or not) and (ii) inadequate freedom in relating theory and data. To our minds, this analysis serves as a warning for CSCL research: While a powerful script, it is one with limited value in understanding how information technology affects work  and cooperation. At the moment, we do not see an equally powerful (and limiting) script in CSCL at work. Instead, there are several scripts, taking the form of enactments of the methodological traditions identif ied by Suthers (2006): iterative design (what is now mostly called Design-based Research), experimental research, and descriptive research (what we might now call descriptive-interpretive research). Regarding research techniques and instrumentation, additional epistemic scripts have emerged around learning analytics and bio/neuro-signal recording. (While we list methods here, epistemic scripts are not just another word for met hods but refer to empirical patterns of enactments of methods, including publication.) But in CSCL, too, information technology often remains undertheorized and our dominant epistemic scripts —our preferred ways to develop new knowledge —sometimes suffer from an overly  tight coupling of theory with data.

What can be done to avoid falling into such traps? As a general heuristic, a field of research should explain phenomena, not data. CSCL research needs to  explain phenomena such as intersubjective meaning making, knowledge building and social regulation, in the context of groups using collaboration technology for the purpose of learning. As a design science, its role should be to design digital artifacts that support collaborative learning and to explain why these artifacts work, for whom, for whi ch purposes, and in which situations. Given the socio-technical and socio-cultural nature of CSCL phenomena and designs, the explanato ry task requires developing deep theories that extend across multiple temporal and spatial scales: evolutionary time,  history of cultures/languages/technologies, group  processes, body and brain  processes, and mechanisms at the level of neurons (see also Nathan & Alibali, 2010, on temporal scales in the Learning Sciences). CSCL is by necessity multi-disciplinary. The challenge consists  in turning  theory and method pluralism into explanatory pluralism: developing synergistic rather than redundant explanations  (Abney et al., 2014). If two approaches contribute the same or highly correlated information about a phenomenon, they should be treated as competing alternatives and should be adjudicated using the criteria of evidentiary support, parsimony, and utility. Productive explanatory pluralism builds on a loose coupling of different approaches and theories, but a coupling nevertheless. Studying the relationships between the theories mobilized to explain a phenomenon should yield new insights. In most cases, the productive configu ration of multiple theories to explain a phenomenon  — and there will always be many options for these configurations — will result in a hierarchical ordering into layers. This epistemic layering can be considere d mirroring the ontological layering of complex biological, social, and artificial systems (Bhaskar, 1975). Let’s take meaning-making as an example. If we take the meaning of a concept

— e.g., force in physics — to be a set of norms governing inferential moves and be part of a wide network of

other concepts (acceleration, velocity, energy, etc., all likewise conceived), then ‘having learned’ this concept amounts to being responsive to these norms and havi ng the capacity for a particular type of social act ivity, the ‘game’ of giving and asking for reasons (Brandom, 2001; Causton, 2019). To explain how  Year 6 students working together in a small group using a chat tool that supports a dialogue game around questions, answers, and reasons 'make meaning' could involve a number of  theories: (a) a didactic theory of what concepts ne ed be included and what constitutes normatively correct i nferential moves for the age group; (b) a linguisti c theory of the ways these inferences and discourse moves can be expressed in the English language  (at that age); (c) a psychological theory of reasoning and (d) of coordinating and regulating reasoning in a group; (e) a design theory for how the chat tool supports discourse moves. Thi s is just one possible theory configuration. In gen eral, configurations are responsive to research questions. If a research question pertained to emotion regulation in group members, then additional theory (and instrumentation to capture relevant data) would be required. The layering of the theories in the configuration could follow a  top-down logic — the inferential layer is constrained by the linguistic layer, for instance — and a bottom-up logic: the chat tool affords certain discourse moves and scaffolds certain inferences.

It would be advantageous to have a framework for configuring theories. Explanatory pluralism does not mean that ‘anything goes’. A framework for levels of explanations that has b een very influential in Cognitive Science is the one developed by David Marr (1982) f or the study of perception. He suggested three levels of explanation: computational, algorithmic, and implementation. Applying this framework, developed in the context of explaining an agent-level phenomenon (vision) as an information processing system, to explaining meanin g making might seem as a far stretch. But it is important to keep in mind that these are level s of explanation, not a substantive theory of levels of vision or any other phenomenon. Applying Marr ’s framework to configuring a number of theories to explain meaning making (etc.) only says that whatever the configuration is, it should attend to the three epistemic levels (computational, algorithmic and implementational). These levels are different because they address separate aspects of understanding: the  overall goal, the specific processes to achieve it, and the material means to carry it out. They come together by providing a complete explanation of a system: understanding what needs to be done, how it’s done, and how it’s physically realized. The same pattern for explanation is applied recursively to the sub-tasks that arise from the over-arching task, such as maintaining group cohesion, k eeping task focus, or regulating group dynamics.

## Conclusion

The purpose of CSCL, as a field of research, is to develop theo ries of technology-mediated collective learning and to develop designs for artifacts that support t he learning processes. To continue to be successful it will need to deepen its conceptual core (theories of intersubjective meaning making and knowledge building) and add value to multi-disciplinary research on educational technology. It needs to position itself as the discipline that provides the ‘glue’ between the many frameworks and theories involved i n researching technology-mediated and technology-supported collaborative learning and that helps to inform design by research knowledge.
