# Computer-Supported Regulation of Learning for Primary School Students in a Collaborative STEAM Module

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In the context of computer-supported collaborative learning, primary school students' (N = 21) use of self-regulated learning (SRL) and socially shared regulation of learning (SSRL) strategies was supported with group-level digital learning diaries during a fifteen-week-long STEAM learning module. The aim was to explore used SRL and SSRL strategies and their relation to motivation in terms of interest. This study provides detailed information about the regulation processes of primary school students during a collaborative STEAM module.

## Introduction

When learning is collaborative, students need to regulate it at both at individual (i.e., self-regulated learning, SRL) and group levels (i.e., socially shared regulation of learning, SSRL) (Hadwin et al., 2011). SRL refers to an active process in which students plan, monitor and reflect  on their learning. In SSRL, students take control of the task together by negotiating shared goals, plans, and st rategies for regulating cognition,  metacognition, motivation, emotions, and the environment (Panadero, 2017). The regulation of learning is situation-specific and motivational factors such as students’ interest in the learning topic have a reciprocal relationship with it (Perry  et al., 2018). Furthermore, such interest may be affected by collaborative learning situations (Shubina et al., 2023). SRL and SSRL strategies can be learned, but student s often need external support until they are able t o regulate their learning independently (Perry et al., 2018). Prior research has evidenced the potential of technology to support SRL and SSRL in collaborative learning, but the focus has been mainly on higher education, while this potential remains understudied for younger students  (Sharma et al., 2024). Additionally, previous studies have indicated that the early years of school are crucia l for students’ learning regulation skills (Dignath  & Büttner, 2008), which can be trained with different process-oriented practices (Perry et al., 2018). STEAM (Science, Technology, Engineering, Arts, and Mathematics) is a specific type of process-oriented learning that provides opportunities for s tudents to practice taking responsibility for their learning by setting goals, monitoring progress, and reflecting on the whole learning process (English & Kitsantas,  2013; Michalsky & Cohen, 2021). Given its complexity and nature, STEAM is often implemented in a collaborative setting (Herro & Quigley 2016), where students need to manage both SRL and SSRL. The aim of this study is to examine the use of SRL and SSRL strategies by primary school students and the relation  they have with students’ interest during a fifteen-week-long collaborative STEAM module. The specific research questions are as follows:

1. What kind of SRL and SSRL strategies do primary school s tudents use during a computer-supported

collaborative STEAM module?

2. How does primary school students’ interest in STEAM vary in relation to SRL and SSRL strategies used

in the group during the STEAM module?

Present study The present study was conducted in Finland. The participants we re 21 primary school students (11 boys and 10 girls; 10 –12 years old) and their teacher.  The teacher’s role was to structure students' work and to provide assistance when needed. The study was implemented within an optional STEAM module, with one session (90 min) per week for 15 weeks. The language of instruction was Finnish. In the STEAM module, students were asked to explore  in groups what changes could be made to the local environment to make it a better place for children and adolescents. Students worked in the same groups (3– 4 students per group) throughout the module, which allowed them to plan, design, implement and evaluat e the process together.

The study design is shown in Figure 1. The school uses a STEAM path as  pedagogical framework to support STEAM pedagogy. The STEAM path includes seven steps: 1) instruction, 2) goal setting, 3) activating background knowledge, 4) ideation, 5) planning, 6) implementation and 7) sharing. Students' collaborative work was audio-recorded in each lesson (21 hours per group, a tota l of 126 hours). To get more insights into students ’ collaboration the STEAM teacher was interviewed after each session (15 min each, a total of 4.5 hours). STEAM interest was measured to get insights of students’ individual motivational approaches during the collaborative work (Shubina et al., 2023). Interest  in STEAM was captured with six Likert scale questi ons by considering cognitive, emotional and value-related aspects of the interest structure (Krapp & Prenzel, 2011). ## The Study Design

During the module, students used  the Qridi (https://www.qridi.com/) digital learning environment  as a structured digital learning diary. SRL and SSRL strategies (Hadwin et al., 2011; Pintrich, 2000) were scripted in the diary following the STEAM path. Students were guided to accomplish a task in the diary related to a specific step, as illustrated in Figure 2 for the implementation step. The diaries from each group were collected as log data. Figure 2 Students Working Collaboratively on the Implementation Step of the Diary. The data analysis integrates quantitative and quali tative protocols. First,  the evaluation of interest in STEAM is categorized as low, moderately low, moderately high  or high at the three different points of measurement. Second, using the groups’ documentation in the diaries, we explore how different groups implemented task-specific regulation processes. Third, we analyze the audio recordings by (a) adapting previously developed SSRL coding schemes (Haataja et al., 2022; Järvelä et al., 2016) to investigate the use of S RL and SSRL strategies, and (b) with sentiment analysis (Zhou & Ye, 2020) to explore in-depth the interaction processes and emotional intensity that facilitate group-level regulation during the STEAM module. The preliminary results suggest that groups differed on the documentation of the diaries and on the SRL and SSRL strategie s used (e.g., help-seeking, time management, reflection)  during the module. Furthermore, interest in STEAM varied both between and within individuals during the module. This study provides important detailed information about the interaction and regulation processes of primary school students during a fifteen-week collaborative STEAM module. As a practical implication, this study gives teachers valuable information about the role of regulation in complex and open-ended problem solving in the context of STEAM, and a practical example of how SRL and SSRL can be supported with technology.
