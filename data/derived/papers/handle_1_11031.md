# Syndrome versus Phenomenon: A Conceptualization of Imposter Phenomenon

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores Black female graduate students’ experiences with identity development as scholars through secondary analysis of ethnographic data. Drawing on Cultural Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) and Critical Race Theory (CRT), I explore how cultural influences shaped internalized ideas of imposter phenomenon. Findings show reasons for manifestations of feelings of imposition. We hope that conceptualizing the phenomenon more clearly will decrease feelings of imposter phenomenon in Black female graduate students.

## Introduction

Mena (2020) highlights the importance of linguistic  accuracy in educational spaces by illustrating how  language ascribes positionality of origins of feelings of in adequacy. Taking up Mena’s charge for accuracy, I disrupt conceptualizations of imposter-ism as a syndrome as an individualistic, internal problem. Instead, I accept Mena’s understanding of imposter phenomenon (IP) as shaped  by external influences that apply negative forces upon people of color to produce feelings of imposition in predominately white spaces in education. IP includes difficulty internalizing success due to experiencing messages of falseness, inauthenticity, or phoniness, regardl ess and/or despite evidence of ability and/or achievement (Clance & Im es, 1978). Despite any external evidence to the contrary (e.g., academic achievements, advanced deg rees, professional accomplishments), individuals wi th IP believe that they have deceived others regarding their intellect to prevent others from discovering their supposed lack of competence (Lee et al., 2020). Many communities experience IP, but the unique expe riences of Black scholars is investigated here.

Framework Cultural Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) conceptualizes the self as an important agentive “dimension” within a profoundly social and relational view of human li fe (Stetsenko & Arievitch, 2004). CHAT as a lens  1) helps resolve some developmental dualisms in humandevelo pment concerning nature versus nurture by exposing the intra-transaction between the two during development; 2) reveals the location of specific interactions of th e self between social and individual development; and 3) i dentifies the self as a complete and complex interw oven, interacting entity. CHAT is beneficial in conceptua lizing how lack of belonging affects self-identity and how external social constructions precipitate perceptio ns and behav iors related to self-identity. CRT acknowledge s barriers created through the justice system to upho ld power structures, specifically white European po wer structures in the Unites States. Historically, thes e external barriers of exclusion in education have included laws enacted to exclude African slaves  from learning to read or write, “Jim Crow” segregat ion laws, and currently enacted Anti-Woke laws. All of these legal movements in the U.S. perpetuated exclusion of Black female students in White academic spaces. Thus, critical observation of Black academic women’s experience allows for highlights of institutional dynamics of oppression to naturally emerge within oppressed homogenous groups (Matsuda et al., 2018). Taken together, CRT and CHAT embrace essential need s to unpack cultural artifacts and address identity and positionality within social systems. Therefore,  I aim to amplify historically silenced voices of Black female scholars in academic spaces.

## Method

Previous ethnographic data were drawn upon in this analysis. Eight participants come from two previously case studies (Craddock et al., 2011). Of these, six  Black female graduate students’ lived experiences as learners in predominately white institutions (PWI’s) shared experiences with IP as graduate students.

## Findings

Participants experienced feelings of inadequacy for varying reasons. Several expressed lack of resources (i.e. time, experience, education, GRE scores) for feelings of inadequacy. One felt inadequate because she assumed that she was the only person in her cohort to be the first i n her family to pursue a doctoral degree. Despite c ompletion of previous degrees, students still did not feel intel ligent enough to be in a graduate program. ## Discussion

This exploration of accurate language is deemed necessary because these students often equate their self-worth to their identity as an academic (Lee et al., 2020). Left unresolved, these feelings can manifest into lifelong struggles with self-esteem issues and IP in academic spaces. Sense of belonging and self-esteem development are interrelated to imposter phenomenon as a foundational human need for understanding self. This need sustains the ability to securely categorize the self (Naser et al., 2022). By accurately acknowledging the unique struggles faced by Black female graduate students in educational spaces, institutio ns can work toward providing more inclusive spaces that empower Black female students to overcome IP.
