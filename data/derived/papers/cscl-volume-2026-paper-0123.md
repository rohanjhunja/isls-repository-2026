# Investigating support for collaboration on the AI Frontier: A Qualitative Study of Middle -Grades Computer Science Classrooms Informed by the MOSAIC Protocol

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates the relationship between middle school student collaborative behaviors and their activities before and after receiving support while collaborating. Regression analysis of data from the MOSAIC protocol was used to conduct maximum variation sampling for a qualitative case studies to explore how student activity immediately before receiving support predicts whether they receive support focused on collaboration and if they receive this collaborative support or not predicts student activity immediately after they receive support. The characteristics of both the collaboration support provided and the nature of the collaborative tasks play crucial roles in shaping these interactions. These findings highlight the complexity of fostering effective student collaboration and underscore the need for tailored support strategies that consider teachers’ pedagogical moves and the specific features of collaborative tasks.

Collaborative learning can foster essential skills (Bower & Richards, 2006; Yoder et al., 2020), positively impact student’s well-being (Johnson & Johnson, 2009), and promote equitable learning (Werner et al., 2004). However, productive collaboration, even with scripting, can be challenging and both students and teachers may need additional support for working as a group, rather than individually (Hakami, Hernández-Leo, & Amarasinghe, 2024; Fernández -Díaz et al., 2024). Simply grouping students does not automatically result in productive collaboration (Kreijins, et al., 2003). While studies have investigated support and scaffolding interactions in the classroom, most research has focused on scaffolding for individual learning (vande Pol et al., 2010), and less is known about the variety of support types in Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) classrooms. Theoretical framing Recently, researchers have started using AI-driven technologies to support collaboration, though the focus is usually on students’ cognitive engagement (Ouyang & Zhang, 2024), and less on their collaboration. Additionally, understanding instructor facilita tion without technological tools is essential for designing effective support systems (Holstein & Olsen, 2023). We situate our work in the context of the AI frontier to provide empirical evidence to support AI tool development’s towards purposeful partnering with AI. This includes a) theoretical alignment, and b) deployment after harm-causing design issues are resolved, as can happen when AI tools obscure decision making, enable exploitation, ignore safeguards, create dependence, do not have humans in the loop, do not align with educator goals and do not attend to trust (Williamson, Molnar & Boninger, 2024; Bastani et al. 2024; Slama, Toutziaridi & Reich, 2024). Knowing what support exists for collaboration and how it is both impacted by and impacts students  will guide human-centered design decisions to support teachers to have more capacity to attend to the much-needed human (and AI augmented) supports for students during collaboration. Successful collaboration necessitates students to work together in groups to establish common ground (Roschell & Teasley, 1995), share and build on each other’s ideas (Suthers et al., 2010; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006), negotiate ideas and coordinate the activity with each other (Barron & Roschelle, 2009), building knowledge in the process, to solve problems together. The teacher is a limited resource, especially in CSCL classrooms where they need to simultaneously facilitate and support learning at multiple levels (individual, group, and class) and coordinate across activities that often include technology, and within t he constraints of time, curricula, etc. (Dillenbourg, 2013).

This study is situated within a larger study of a research-practice partnership between a large research University and educators from two rural and suburbandistricts in the Western United States. The data from this study comes from four middle school classrooms with a total of 302 consenting students, where the teachers were implementing a collaborative Computer Science curriculum as a part of the teachers’ participation in a multi-year professional 689 learning community. This study applies the MOSAIC protocol (Bush Tayne, Dey and Hoang, 2026; Hoang, Bush and Dey, 2024) to identify and analyze support moments, which are moments when individuals outside the observed group (e.g., teachers, other students, etc.), provide support in the form of guidance or answering questions the group may have. We identified 7 instrumental case moments based on review of the 618 support moments and their coding using the MOSAIC protocol.

Using maximum variation sampling of these seven moments, we identified two examples (from two different classrooms) for case studies to describe and explore two distinct situations, one exploratory case study came from a moment that the quantitative analys is identified was uncommon but potentially quite effective, the second case is instrumental, from a moment that the quantitative analysis identified as common and less supportive of students (Stake, 1995). In the first case, we present an exploratory case study to inform future design directions. In the second case study, we present an instrumental case to better understand common issues with this type of collaboration support).

Example 1: Collaboration support modeled collaboration and is catalyzed by curriculum design features, promotes student collaboration The first example is during the  jigsaw activity, where all the group members developed expertise in different programmable sensors and then came back  together to teach one another about their sensors. This example was chosen as an exploratory case study to better understand a situation that the quantitative analysis identified was uncommon. In this exploratory case, students were not collaborating before receiving support, their support focused on collaboration, and then they engaged in collaboration after the support moment. In this example, the teacher prompted students to consider using multiple sensors and modeled how to share and integrate ideas. She also validated their contributions, indicating that their ideas were valued. These actions appear to have prompted students to begin discussing their ideas with one another, suggesting that the teacher’s guidance and examples directly supported the initiation of collaborative behaviors. Example 2: Collaboration support too surface level, no change in collaboration In the second example, students were working on the card sort activity (described in Table 1). This moment was selected to exemplify the situation that the quantitative analysis showed was common: collaboration activity was unchanged by collaborative support, with constant characteristics before and after collaboration support. IIn this example, without additional guidance or modeling, students may not have understood what deeper collaborative behaviors entailed. Although the support was coded as collaboration-focused (as the teacher encouraged students to talk to one another), students received little instruction on what to discuss, share, or how to build on each other’s ideas. As a result, they may have continued their prior behavior without engaging in more meaningful collaboration.

Qualitative analysis reveals nuance to MOSAIC protocol analysis of small group collaboration. Two comparative instrumental case studies  analyzed the ways that the support provided relates to prior collaboration and other relevant features of the learning environment. W e found that collaboration support that 1) directly modeled collaboration practices, 2) included the asking of open questions, and 3) was in the context of tasks that afforded interdependence were features of support that resulted in increased student collaboration. These findings highlight the complexity of fostering effective student collaboration and underscore the need for tailored support strategies that consider teachers’ pedagogical moves and the specific features of collaborative tasks. This research contributes to the understanding of how to better attend to the human components in support to enhance collaborative learning outcomes, while AI tools work to offload teachers’ cognitive demand and automate the less-human elements of supporting youth in the classroom.
