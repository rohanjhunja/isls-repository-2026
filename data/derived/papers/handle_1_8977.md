# Agents, Models, and Ethics: Importance of Interdisciplinary Explorations in AI Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Artificial Intelligence (AI) is proliferating in both visible and invisible ways across our society. It is imperative for our new generation to gain a fundamental understanding of how intelligence is created, applied, and also its potential to perpetuate biases and unfairness. Because of the nature of AI, it is important that we take an interdisciplinary approach in order to ground the systems and models in real and meaningful contexts for learner exploration. This symposium explores these approaches with a multi-disciplinary group of researchers presenting empirical studies on designing and studying AI learning environments. These studies provide unique insights towards design recommendations, challenges, and opportunities in the rapidly emerging area of study in K-12 education.

Symposium Overview Artificial Intelligence (AI) has become essential i n daily technology usage, decision-making, and the STEM workforce (Long & Magerko, 2020). More traditional methods in scientific research and STEM occupations are being replaced with AI-based modeling and problem s olving. The need to prepare the future workforce fo r advances in AI, and its potential to perpetuate bia s and unfairness, has spurred the integration of AI  into K-12 learning settings (Carney et al., 2020; Druga et al., 2017; Forsyth et al., 2021; Touretzky et al., 2019). An emerging theme and emphasis on K-12 AI education is promoting its interdisciplinarity. The public tends to believe that AI-related work is all about programming. Thus, teachers may avoid introducing AI concepts in non-CS (computer science) classes, and students might view AI narrowly as an advanced CS topic (Lon g & Magerko, 2020). An emphasis on AI's interdisciplinary applications can help broaden access to AI (Akram et al., 2021). The interdisciplinary view would help students draw connections between application areas of interest and CS, broadening the range of students to whom such AI learning environments can appeal. Despite the many calls to promote AI's interdisciplinarity (e.g., Kusters et al., 2020), there remains a knowledge gap of how to develop meaningful experiences for students and teachers in K-12 settings. Our contributors are from different disciplines and  backgrounds (e.g., learning sciences, literacy education, science education, computer science, and human-computer interaction) and explore interdisciplinary K-12 AI education from different perspectives. Spec ifically, Zhang et al. investigated teacher learnin g in integrating AI concepts across disciplines; Walsh et al. explored using AI ethics as an interdisciplinary topic for classroom teaching; Akram and Yoder draw on the per spective of computational thinking to show students ’ learning in AI modeling of science phenomena;  Jian g et al. examined students’ data modeling practices  that require an interdisciplinary understanding of AI; Payne et al. illustrated unique affordances of embodied learning ICLS2022 Proceedings 1763 © ISLS through dancing in helping students to reason about  capabilities and ethics of AI systems. Together, t hese contributions shed light on strategies in promoting  AI's interdisciplinarity, teacher learning in inte grating AI in different disciplines, and how students explore AI models and technologies (e.g., what students engage with, what their interactions are, and how do these interactions relate to learning of AI and other disciplines). This symposium will be a landmark in the development of our understanding of learning in the rapidly emerging area of study in K-12 AI education.

We will structure a panel discussion following thes e steps: co-chairs introducing the contributors, contributors briefly presenting their research in a sequence, contributors engaging in a panel discussion facilitated by the co-chairs (discussion topics will be prepare d by co-chairs and also selected from attendees), c o-chairs summarizing the discussed topics and presenting future directions. Individual Contributions

1. Integrating AI Education Activities into Middle and High School Classrooms

Helen Zhang, Irene Lee, Katherine Moore, Yihong Cheng, & Beatriz Perret The rapid expansion of AI is having unprecedented i ndustrial and social impact and necessitates educat ing a generation of AI literate citizens who are familiar with AI technologies and can work with AI tools in various job tasks in the future. Yet, AI education is in its infancy and early findings on AI Literacy are just beginning to shed light on which topics are appropriate to teach at various grade bands and how learners gain an understanding of AI concepts and processes (e.g., Lee et al., 2021). Further despite the fact that AI itself is an interdisciplinary field, many students believe that only computer scientists  or programmers work with AI. Such stereotyped view s prevent them from recognizing the interdisciplinary  nature of AI or their own capability of interactin g with AI tools outside of computer science contexts. More re search is needed to explore how AI education activi ties can raise students’ awareness and foster their understanding of the interdisciplinarity of AI. This exploratory study aims to address this issue b y investigating whether/how AI activities can be infused into classrooms across domains and how the integrated AI learning experiences can impact stude nts, particularly those who have been historically underrepresented in STEM and CS education. This study employs a multiple case study approach (Stake, 2013) and re ports four cases of teachers who embedded AI into t heir teaching of the core subject area. The cases were s elected from educators who participated in a year-l ong PD program that prepared them for implementation of an  AI education curriculum called Developing AI Liter acy (DAILy). The PD program consisted of (1) an online Book Club where educators read an AI book, learned the DAILy activities as a learner, and discussed their experiences and thoughts of AI and the DAILy activities; (2) a practicum where educators co-taught the DAILy curriculum in summer camps to middle school students; and (3) monthly webinars to share classroom implementation plans and experience during the school year. We uti lized purposive sampling (Tongco, 2007) to select the four cases to represent the extremes on a continuum (R ubin & Rubin, 2011) and to allow for maximal variation sampling within the PD program. The selection criteria included subject area taught by the teachers (e.g., science,  library, technology, math, and communication), tea ching structure (co-teaching or teaching individually), and the age group of students (middle or high school  students). All the teachers taught in schools with a high perc entage of underrepresented students (i.e., at least  80% of the students were from low-income families and/or racial and ethnic minority groups). Multiple sources of data were collected such as interviews with teachers, observations of classroom instruction, and student work. Preliminary findings demonstrate that teachers in various disciplines are able to find places to integrate AI lessons within the school day. A closer examination revealed differences in teachers’ integration: in one case the AI activities were interwoven with the science content whereas in the other cases AI was taught as  a standalone curricular component that meets the overarching goals of the course (e.g., developing students' information or technology literacy). For instance, in a science  class, Ms. P developed an activity that asks stude nts to use Google’s Teachable Machine to train supervised models to distinguish between eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells. Students curated a dataset of pictures of both type s of cells, labelled the pictures to train the mode ls, and troubleshot and tested the AI model. Their understa nding of the science and AI concepts was found to b e reinforced through this activity. In the communication/debate class, Mr. D focused on utilizing the AI-generated Deepfakes and misinformation that appear in social media as a motivational hook to engage students. His students learned to use generative AI tools to create stories and art and debated/discussed who should own the copyright of the generated art. Such activities raised studen ts’ awareness of AI in media and sharpened their 21 st century skills of debating, communicating, and critical thi nking. Another finding is that co-teaching is a pot entially powerful approach to demonstrate the connections between AI and other fields. In one case, three teachers (who ICLS2022 Proceedings 1764 © ISLS teach math, technology, and library respectively) chose to co-teach the DAILy activities so that each teacher could contribute examples of how AI is connected to the different fields. These findings contribute to the AI education field by providing concrete examples of teaching AI across disciplines to help students realize the interdisciplinarity of AI. They also inform the design of PD programs by revealing guidelines on how to support teachers in reorganizing and revamping the learning experiences to make AI a valued and integral part of the schooling for all learners.

2. Adapting and Reinventing Interdisciplinary AI Ethics Curriculum: Co-design with

Computer Science, Cybersecurity and English Language Arts Teachers Benjamin Walsh, Aaron Guggenheim, Bridget Dalton, Stacey Forsyth, & Tom Yeh The Imagine AI Project team is in its 3rd year of d esigning, implementing and analyzing Artificial Intelligence Ethics curriculum for middle and high school studen ts. With AI’s potential to solve significant social  problems (Abebe et al., 2020), while simultaneously playing a role  in the widespread violation of human rights (Unite d Nations Human Rights Council, 2021), AI and AI ethics are urgent topics that belong in schools (Touretzky et al., 2019). Our team of Computer Science, STEM and Liter acy educators and researchers is dedicated to helpi ng youth become ethical consumers and creators of AI.

We view AI Ethics as an inherently interdisciplinary topic, which can be effectively taught by integrating CS, Ethics and Literacy instruction. We have design ed and studied project-based instructional modules that combine original short stories with youth protagonists at the center of AI ethical dilemmas with non-fiction media, and immersive CS tool and simulation experiences (A lverman & Wilson, 2011). Together these modules hel p students develop a technical and intuitive understanding of how AI systems work, how these systems are affecting individuals and communities in positive and negative ways, and ask them to consider what it might take to design more ethical and empathetic AI. Our modules address  topics such as algorithmic bias, the ethics of sel f-driving cars, recommendation algorithms and social media, a nd AI art and music. Each module culminates in a di gital design task (such as creating a digital comic, an e mpathetic chatbot, or a creative computing artifact) that asks students to express their own stance on an AI ethics dilemma. We tested these modules in online summer camps and in two 9th grade ELA classes and found that stu dents developed better understandings of how AI sys tems work, and more nuanced views on AI ethics issues (Forsyth et al., 2021). We are currently partnering with CS, Cybersecurity,  and ELA teachers to bring AI Ethics projects to their classrooms. We believe the best project-based  units are customized to fit the needs of a specifi c group of students, and that teachers are vital co-design partners in making this possible (Penuel et al., 2007). An essential component of project-based learning is striving to provide students with authentic learning experiences (Polman, 2012). Our work has focused closely on ensuring that projects are authentic to the academic disciplines, and that students experience projects as authentic to their own interest, experiences and communities.

Using the Imagine AI modules that we tested in year 2 as a starting point, we have worked with teachers to adapt, extend and reinvent our curriculum to fit  their classroom contexts. For example, based on recommendations by a 9th grade Cybersecurity teache r partner, we made a number of small shifts to high light security concerns related to self-driving cars, fac ial recognition systems and recommendation algorith ms, and added digital design tasks to challenge students with more extensive coding experience, including a new ethical app design challenge as a culminating product. Similarly, a 9th grade ELA teacher took the lead in integrating our materials into a project which examines how AI can reproduce and amplify social inequality. This proje ct incorporates a graphic novel version of the classic text “Frankenstein,” and asks students to create evidence-rich multimodal texts (Dalton, 2012) to answer questions  such as, “Can AI be monstrous?” This ELA teacher’s reinvention of the modules has maintained an interdisciplinary scope appropriate for AI Ethics, while also firmly situating the project within the teacher’s primary discipline and academic standards. Teachers have also played a vital role in helping t o ensure that students will experience the AI ethic s projects as authentic to themselves and their commu nities. For example, each partner teacher has share d very specific reasons why they believe particular examples of algorithmic bias are likely to resonate with their students more than others. Furthermore, teachers’ co-design and enactment has been essential in helping us live  up to Universal Design for Learning principles (Meyers et al., 2014), which help ensure that projects are accessible and challenging for all students. This includes scaffol ded design challenges that are appropriate for stud ents with extensive programming experience, and also those who will be writing code for the first time, and providing audio versions of AI ethics stories to support students’ learning and language needs. The various curriculum adaptations and reinventions  that are emerging from these partnerships are informing our larger development and dissemination efforts. As we prepare to publish more of our AI Et hics stories and modules (https://www.colorado.edu/proje ct/imagine-ai/resources), we are including alternat e texts, ICLS2022 Proceedings 1765 © ISLS activities and design tools as well as strategies for customizing our curriculum so that teachers across a range of subject areas classes and contexts can implement pr ojects that will better serve the interests and nee ds of their own unique groups of students and their academic di sciplines. Preliminary data suggests that this approach can help the topic of AI ethics resonate with students in and across multiple subject areas classes.

3. Enhancing Computational Thinking Skills in Middle-school Students’ Computational

Modeling Bita Akram & Spencer Yoder Computational Thinking (CT) skills are becoming the new form of literacy. As a result, efforts for integrating CT in the K-12 curriculum have become the subject of i ncreasing focus (Sengupta et al., 2015). One partic ularly effective approach is integrating CT skills within the mainstream K-12 science curriculum through computational modeling. Many researchers have argued that learnin g scientific concepts in the context of computation al modeling facilitates and strengthens learning across both domains (Weintrop et al., 2016; Sengupta et al., 2015). In this work, we discuss activities for integrating  one of the domineering aspects of CT, Artificial I ntelligence, with a middle-school science curriculum (Akram et al., 2021) in the form of a computational modeling perspective and propose future directions for improving these activities. Our activity design focused on two CT frameworks proposed by Sengupta et al. (2015) and Weintrop et al. (2016). Sengupta et al. (2015) argue that CT co nsists of practices that are essential to computing, computer science, and modeling scientific and mathematical c oncepts. These practices include problem representa tion, abstraction, decomposition, simulation, verificatio n, and prediction. Following this framework, Clark and Sengupta (2015) proposed a computational modeling f ramework that incorporates three scientific modelin g practices: model building, ramification, and deploy ment. This modeling framework shares similarities w ith Weintrop et al. 's (2016) CT framework that introdu ces four primary practices for CT: data practices, modeling and simulation practices, computational problem-solving practices, and systems thinking practices. Specifically, we are developing a block-based simul ation environment that models the spread of epidemic diseases through a contact-tracing app. Epidemic diseases and their effects on the ecosystem are among the core disciplinary ideas mentioned in the NGSS (Lead States, 2013). In these activities, middle school students engaged with the model building and model ramificat ion of Clark and Sengupta’s framework while practic ing problem representation, abstraction, decomposition, simulation, and verification. We will extend our activities to incorporate model deployment and, thus, validation and prediction by providing students with empirical  data about the spread of epidemic diseases. Through our proposed activities, students will be engaged in an iterative computational modeling approach where they build a model based on evidence and observations, observe interactions among different variables in their mod el, and evaluate its effectiveness by comparing the  results generated from their models against empirical data. From this comparison, they identify the source of mismatch and revise their model to accommodate for that. This work will spur much-needed research on designing effective block-based programming environments that help stud ents to build a comprehensive view of computational modeling, specifically AI modeling, for science-centered applications.

4. High School Students’ Data Modeling Practices: From Modeling Unstructured Data

to Evaluating Automated Decisions Shiyan Jiang, William Finzer, Jie Chao, & Carolyn P. Rosé Efforts to incorporate data literacy and analytics throughout K-16 education are on the rise (Lee & Wi lkerson, 2018). In this paper, we argue that a neglected area of data literacy is dealing with unstructured data as well as the restructuring of structured data to prepare for effective modeling. The unstructured data this paper focuses on is text and building machine learning (i.e., a subfield of AI) models with texts from real-world datasets. This study aims to fill the gap of current research on understanding student modeling unstructured data and evalu ating automated decisions from these models.

Through interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) of 28-hours screen recordings of high school students’ AI modeling activities, we identified ten  data modeling practices: drawing on experiences to  create features, making hypotheses about patterns, analyzi ng confusion matrix, identifying patterns in traini ng data, noticing gap between hypotheses and patterns, pursu ing feature surprises, generating “thoughtful” test ing data, reasoning about model decision making, describing models in social contexts, and engineering features. Specifically, drawing on experiences to create features refers to the practice of creating features based on students’ understanding of the way humans describe positive and negative experiences. For example, students created a feature, “delicious”, as from their exper iences, humans would use the word “delicious” to de scribe ICLS2022 Proceedings 1766 © ISLS positive experiences. In this practice, students leveraged their own experiences and knowledge to create different kinds of features, such as generating a feature “service” while describing how important services were for one’s family to select restaurants.  Making hypotheses about patterns  represents the practice of explaining obvious patterns before exploring AI models, such as “if a review has feature ‘love’, most likely it’s a posit ive review.” In the practice of analyzing confusion matrix, students examined correctly and incorrectly class ified reviews systematically.  Through systematically analyzing reviews, students engaged in the practice of identifying patterns in training data. After identifying patterns, they noticed that some patterns were different from their hypotheses in the practice of noticing gap between hypotheses and patterns. For example, they considered “like” as a good feature for positive reviews (i.e., if a review has feature “like”, most likely it’s a positive review), but the model showed that almost half the reviews having feature“ like” were negative reviews. When reading the negat ive reviews, they found that “like” could be used in ne gative reviews such as “don’t like” and “taste like  soap.” To further explore models, students picked features th at showed unexpected patterns in the practice of pursuing surprising features, such as exploring why feature “die” had a big weight for positive reviews. When testing the model with self-generated reviews, students wrote reviews that the model could classify correctly after realizing that the model only used single words and could not understand contexts when classifying reviews. We described this practice as generating “thoughtful” testing data. In the practices of reasoning about model decision making, describing models in social contexts, and engineering features, students explained multiple features driving decision making, discussed humans and social contexts behind data and data modeling (e.g., who would benefit from the model and who would be excluded), and iteratively revised the feature space for better model accuracy respectively.

Our analysis demonstrated that students engaged in  a variety of data modeling practices when modeling texts and analyzing model decision making. These practices require an interdisciplinary understanding of AI (e.g., mathematical understanding for analyzing confusion matrix, literacy learning for making hypotheses abo ut patterns, and computational thinning for engineerin g features). Future studies could explore how stude nts leveraged personal experience and knowledge from different disciplines to build models with unstructured data through investigating patterns of data modeling practices.

5. Lived Experiences with Machine Learning and Dance

William Payne, Francisco Castro-Norwood, Kathleen McDermott, Yoav Bergner, & Kayla DesPortes In this symposium, we will demonstrate how open-ended creative activities connecting computational models and dance led to meaningful and personally relevant dis cussions around the capabilities and ethics of AI s ystems. Researchers have shown that artistic computing (e.g., through dance) can create engaging and authentic learning experiences that embody learners’ identities, experiences, and communities (Lindberg et al., 2020). Furthermore, the incorporation of physical activity may be an en joyable and powerful method for introducing learner s to machine learning (ML) (Payne et al., 2021). We brie fly describe the danceON technology that enabled th e intersection between modeling and dance then share two vignettes from a recent study in which a diverse group of 6 high school girls (2 Black, 2 Hispanic, 2 Asia n) used danceON for 15 weeks, through a paid learni ng opportunity advertised as an internship. danceON is an embodied creative coding environment that em beds ML models for pose detection enabling learners to create movement-responsive animations that are drawn and updated in real time on top of a pre-recorded video or webcam feed of solo dance. Learners write code to affix virtual animations to detected body keypoints and 2D coordinates using the keywords what, where, when, and how (Figure 1). Within this medium, simple animations can take on multi-layered meaning —e.g., learners used circles, superimposed onto dan ce videos, to represent a sun and moon and color change of dark to bright to signify hope. Vignette 1, My body or my shirt?: Direct engagement with ML is central to danceON’s technology and curriculum, and because participants choreographed a new dance nearly every week, they experienced ML system behavior across conditions, e.g., changes in clothing, lighting, camera position, etc. The first creative assignment, “Emoji Challenge,” tasked participants in creating two positions, inspired by Merce Cunningham’s solo “50 Looks” (1979), and coding a when-condition to draw a matching emoji over their face. Many videos yielded inaccurate and missing pose data. We discussed potential causes, and participants identified moments where their positions hid parts of their bodies, e.g., “ if my hand was here one second and then another sec ond it was like behind my head, it would probably think it was stil l out there and not behind my head,” (Camille, pseudonym). One participant, Elise, demonstrated through her clothing how machine perceptions of bodies differ from human perceptions (Figure 1b): “it like saw I had a person on my shirt and accidentally went on to that shoulder instead of the shoulder of my body. ” Participants not only discussed limitations of po se detection, but learned to work within its boundaries altering their code and chore ography, and, as presented in Vignette 2, leveraged  their knowledge when introduced to other ML applications.

Vignette 2, Classifying me and “everyone else”: In a wide-ranging discussion in Week 8, participa nts connected their understanding of AI to personal experiences and publicized instances of bias. danceON integrates body-position classifiers made in Teachable Machine, a free web-application for rapidly training ML mo dels (Carney et al., 2020). They trained models to ident ify two poses and read articles about misuse of fac ial recognition and bias towards white voices in AI hom e assistants (Hill, 2020).  They discussed the caus es of AI bias from the lack of developer diversity, “ products are right now mostly made by white people and geared towards white people” (Janna), to the human role in training systems, “although it is computer learning we're the ones who put it in place” (Gwen). Caitlyn reflected on her family’s Google home assistant, stating “[her parents’] English isn't like, it's not perfect, so not like m ine, or like standard American, so the Google had l ike a harder time to understand.” Directly connecting pose detection and facial recognition systems, Gwen argued, “we widen the range of what can be classified as a human face  so it's like you have to take into consideration a ll features and all face colors and for your poses, like all heights.” Following the discussion of bias, participants imagined a danceON performance that responds to AI bias. They noted that pose detection performs more accurately for slow movements, which Camille connected to classical dan ce styles that are “ expensive and only available to like a certain demographic,” in contrast with fast movements in hip-hop dance. Camille proposed a performance with “two dancers, side by side” with “code that like glitches for the fast dancer” to represent the AI bias against that dancer’s movements.

While the internship was exploratory and wide-ranging, moments captured in vignettes lead us to believe that future activities may capitalize on authentic encounters with AI with danceON. For example, we co uld use when conditions to illustrate key differences between rule-based AI and ML in which learners complete the Emoji Challenge with hand-written rules and Teachable Mac hine classifiers and compare/contrast the results. Additionally, learners could also work with each other’s’ code and classifiers and identify instances in which their systems work less well for others and then implement improvements. ## Discussion and Implications

As artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learnin g (ML) integrate across our society, we bring toget her a collective set of researchers to understand how we teach about these models and their implementations. Individuals must be equipped to understand the ways in which AI and ML are being operationalized, reco gnize issues within its implementations, and understand the consequences (benefits and drawbacks) of their use across contexts. The instantiation of these models is inhe rently interdisciplinary and thus we have construct ed a set of empirical contributions exploring how AI education can integrate across various disciplines in K-12 ed ucation. Drawing on perspectives of our researchers conducting work across the learning sciences, computing education, literacy education, science education, and human-computer interaction, we pose a set of essential ques tions and discussion topics for the symposium authors and participants in the learning sciences community. Promoting Disciplinary Knowledge and Practices Interdisciplinary educational experiences are important for their ability to relate more honestly to t he real world and to promote new practices for learning that develop as we connect across previously siloed content. Through the contributions we will stimulate a discussion ar ound: How we might best support leveraging discipli nary knowledge to develop new knowledge and skills around AI? How can learners develop knowledge about AI that helps learners develop other disciplinary knowledge ? How can we create learning experiences that promo te reflections, experiences, and work with AI that are  representative and embedded in real-world contexts  and scenarios? How can the practices across different disciplinary domains with AI create new practices with AI?

## Integrating Learners’ Perspectives and Experiences

Learners bring a set of knowledge and experiences with AI to the learning environment through direct interaction with agents and models as well as secondary engagement through news and media. We will explore the questions: How can we use interdisciplinary contexts to promot e learners’ integration and reflection of their cur rent perspectives, experiences, and knowledge surrounding AI? How can learners' lived experiences connect to their work theorizing, developing, and critiquing agents and models? Critical Explorations of Ethics and Biases The ways in which AI models are created and impleme nted have social and ethical implications. Learners must develop critical practices with developing and inte rrogating AI. Through the various contributions we will examine: How do different disciplinary practices pr ovide entry to critical reflection and iteration of  AI agents, models, and their implementations? How do disciplinary practices foster different ways to experience and reflect on biases within AI agents, models, and their implementations? How can we construct new practices within our learners that forefront ethics as part of how they engage in development and implementation of AI?
