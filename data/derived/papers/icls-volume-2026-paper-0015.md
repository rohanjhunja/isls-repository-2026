# Mediating Mentoring: Examining Digital Accountability within new Teacher Mentoring Dialogue

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, we examine how digital tools of accountability shape the content of conversations and interactional patterns between mentors and novices. To do so, we analyze a case study of a new teacher and mentor dyad in an equity-oriented mentoring prog ram. We investigate how teachers’ interactions are mediated by digital tools that report out data on their mentoring interactions, with attention to how this digital mediation shapes the depth with which teachers engage in critical inquiry of student thinking. Our findings illustrate that the dyad’s deeper engagement with student thinking occurred when they were not interacting with digital tools of accountability. This paper contributes to an understanding of the ways digital datafication processes can man ifest in embodied teacher learning interactions, shaping the extent to which equity-oriented approaches to mentoring new teachers are realized in practice.

Regarded as key sites for new teacher support, induction and mentoring programs are linked to retention, change in practices, and improved student performance (Long et al., 2012). Most U.S. states require induction programs and often structure mentoring as  a key component of these programs. Although there is no universally accepted definition of mentoring, Feiman-Nemser (2001) introduced the idea of educative  mentoring, as mentoring that moves beyond emotional support and technical advice (Little, 1990), centered on a vision of teaching and understanding of teacher learning; inquiry-and-theory-driven engagement with problems of practice; and centering student thinking.

In this paper, we engage with a program aspiring to take up critical educative mentoring. Drawing from equity and justice-oriented mentoring and coaching scholarship (Land, 2018; Marshall & Buenrostro, 2021; Stoetzel & Taylor-Marshall, 2022), we understand critical educative mentoring to deliberately situate inquiry within interrogation of power across levels of society (e.g., social identities, interpersonal dynamics, institutional structures). Through this, critical educative mentoring might support novice teachers’ discretionary, creative, and relational moves purposefully oriented toward justice (Philip, 2019). However, the critical educative mentoring this program aspires to inevitably faces multiple constraints. Novice teachers continually face “immediate needs.” Mentors navigate competing purposes of induction, including pressures of accountability within policy and program designs, often absent attention to equity (Santibañez et al., 2022). Program resources may also reflect or be drawn toward competing aims due to these pressures.

Program resources, such as tools, are often central to mentoring programs. Many mentor teachers are inexperienced in mentoring other adults and require support to develop their effectiveness (Clarke et al., 2014; Gareis & Grant, 2014). Recognizing this need, research has highlighted the potential of using tools to support more educative dialogue between mentors and novices, encouraging mentors to engage in more substantive, situative inquiry with novices rather than simply providing emotional support (Nesj e & Lesjeberg, 2022). The nature of tools ranges from video viewing/analysis protocols to reflective journaling prompts and portfolios that document goals and progress.

While varied, mentoring tools are increasingly digitized, sometimes for ease of distribution, while at other times they are shared with induction program administration to document learning and meet standards of program accountability. We apply a critical lens to the tool and accountability structure that mediates educators’ shape interactional patterns and substance of mentoring conversations. Given these aims, we ask: how does a mentoring pair’s embodied interactions with one another and with digital tools of accountability mediate the content of their discussions?

We explore this question through a case study of a new teacher-mentor focal pair within an equityoriented comprehensive induction program. Ultimately, we find differences in the content and depth of mentoring conversations, which appear related to whether  and how interaction patterns are mediated by digital tools that report out data on the mentoring pairs’ interactions for accountability and evaluation purposes. We argue that digital mediation is not neutral in the way it shapes social interactions betwee n mentors and novices. The digitization of mentoring tools may encourage discussion of more superficial teaching behaviors that can be quantified and reported as digital data, rather than engagement with more educative dialogue about students. 71 Embodied teacher learning As we examine interactions in a mentoring dyad, we draw on embodiment research to understand teachers' situated interactions as both discursive and embodied. Such research draws attention to how gesture organizes action (Goodwin, 2000) and how people orient themselves as listeners and co-participants within a shared endeavor (Rogoff et al., 2003), and how learning unfolds through the coordination of bodies, talk, and tools within activity (Erickson, 2012). Building on research on joint attention (Siposova & Carpenter, 2019), we attend to whether and how the dyad achieves shared attention, a relational, bidirectional form of engagement toward a common object. Video footage of a mentoring pair’s interaction across a school year allows us to surface microgeneti c evidence of teacher learning (Walkoe & Luna, 2020) as we observe how the coordination of educators’ gaze, gesture, body positioning, and talk shift over time and in relation to mediating tools and shapes the content and depth of their inquiry. Ultimately, we mapped how these embodied actions appear in relation to the digital mentoring tools mediating a given conversation, and how these interactions shape the content of what the dyad discusses verbally. Digital mediation We are guided by a sociocultural lens that allows us to see teacher learning as occurring within social interaction. We understand that teachers’ social interactions in mentoring relationships are mediated by tools that are culturally patterned by the situ ated school activity systems they are used within (Engeström, 1987; Vygotsky, 1978; Grossman et al., 1999; Yamagata-Lynch, 2007). One way tools are culturally patterned is through the type of media in which they are packaged (McLuhan, 1964). Whether tools are accessed on computers, phones, or on paper shapes the embodied interactions that teachers and mentors have around the tool. This is both because of the ways different types of screens can shape the physicality of interaction, and also because digitizat ion leads to data traces from digital systems being shared with others, such as school administrators and technology sales representatives, who were not present during the embodied mentoring interaction (Kavanagh et al., 2025). Tools of accountability In analyzing the content of mentoring dialogue, we keep an eye towards whether conversations focus on technocratic ends versus more educative co-construction. The last several decades have spurred a growth in accountability and technocratic interventions within fields of teacher learning (Anderson & Cohen, 2015), reflecting what some refer to as a deprofessionalization of teacher learning. Within technocratic approaches, teachers’ professional knowledge and discretionary moves are undervalued, and instead, teachers are tasked with carrying out others’ directives (Horsford et al., 2019). While these approaches may mobilize language of equity, they often do so by reducing concepts to an externally imposed checklist (Ladson-Billings, 1995; Sleeter, 2012). In coaching and mentoring, technocratic approaches might emphasize the documentation of specific practices rather than engaging in locally-grounded inquiry of student thinking. One way norms of accountability are carried out within technocratic interventions is through the use of tools. Designed with particular aims, tools carry historical roots (Pea, 1993; Vossoughi, 2014). Even within programs committed to transformative aims, uncritical reliance on taken-for-granted tools might inadvertently redirect interactions toward dominant ideologies. Attending to the ways educators orient themselves to tools and how this mediates the content of their discussions allows us to see how micr o-interactions with tools may reproduce historical patterns of teacher deprofessionalization. Site Data was collected as part of a larger Research-Practice partnership, in collaboration with the district’s new teacher induction, mentoring, and development program. The program provided comprehensive induction for new teachers and mentor/coach development. It explicitly centered equity in its mission, aiming to support organizational transformation through the equity-centered development of all teachers and mentors/coaches, and as a means of support for the retention of minoritized teachers. This paper primarily draws on observations of teacher-mentor meetings (n = 20), semi-structured interviews (n = 6), and artifacts collected by the first author. For this paper, we employ a single-case study approach (Yin, 2014) with a white first-year English Language Arts secondary teacher, and her mentor, a white mid-career mentor-coach within the same school. This 72 approach affords a focus on moment-to-moment interactions and patterns within their conversations across the year. While this close focus may not be generalizable, this white-womandyad was considered an ideal match by research-based program guidelines, and reflects dominant teaching demographics across the U.S. Thus, it may be a particularly illustrative “ideal” case that may generate meaningful insights. Digital mentoring tool Individualized Development Plans for Induction (IDPI) comprise a key mentoring program document. Within the program, IDPIs were entirely digital, housed on Google Docs. IDPI centered around a teacher-identified area of growth and self-evaluation. Subsections provided space to document various learning activities in support of this goal, as well as mid-and end-of-year self-evaluations on the same goal. IDPI documents were shared and evaluated by someone outside of the mentor-teacher dyad, tasked with determining if teachers “passed” the induction requirements. In meetings, the IDPI frequently mediated the content and flow of their conversations.

## Data analysis

The first author reduced the data into content logs and transcripts, then reviewed all data and wrote analytic memos, identifying themes of tool use, recurring dilemmas of practice, and mentor facilitation. In partnership with the second author, a focus on the mediating presence of computer-based tools emerged. Following this, the first author screened the dataset for meetings that visually captured both participants and their orientation toward the computer. The first author then returned to content logs for the entire data corpus and coded for a) ELA content, including logistics and standards, and b) student thinking and experience, including specific practices, student challenges, and student work. From this systematic coding, two meetings were selected for contrasting illustrative potential: one with routine attention to content and (absence of) student thinking, and one with notably deeper engagement, allowing us to examine how interactional patterns differed. We engaged in initial exploratory coding, co-developing a schema organized around topics of discussion and three dominant orientations: interpersonal gaze (eye contact with one another), tool gaze (orientation toward a computer screen), and spatial gaze (o rientation toward students’ empty desks/classroom). From within these two meetings, we then selected two extended episodes, 5 minutes and 11 minutes long respectively, that captured the focal points of comparison. Drawing from Horn (2010), we define an episode as a unit of talk focused on a single topic. When teachers shifted topics, we considered it a new episode. The second episode is longer, and we broke it into sub-episodes, or engagement marked by when they stopped speaking and turned toward their computers or returned to engage with one another.

Once we identified these episodes, we watched them collaboratively. Guided by Erickson’s (2012) description of procedures for inductively analyzing interactional video data, we iteratively described and analyzed what we were seeing. We first watched an epi sode together all the way through without stopping, taking field notes as we watched with attention to moments when significant shifts in either the speaker’s gestural repertoire or the listener's focus or body orientation occurred. We then watched each episode a second time, pausing at these moments of significant shift that we had noted during our first watch. After completing this second watch, we collaboratively constructed a timeline, where we highlighted the major interactional events happening across each episode.

We then continued to iteratively watch each episode in order to generate a detailed transcription that brought the verbal and non-verbal interactions happening in parallel more closely into analytic view (Jordan and Henderson, 1996). To visualize the non-v erbal interactions, we took screenshots every two seconds, and aligned these screenshots with teachers’ verbal utterances (Atkison and Heritage, 1984). 1 We then turned this into a transcript scroll (Gutiérrez, 2020). As we iteratively watched, we added row s to our scroll that described the mentor’s and teacher’s moment-by-moment non-verbal interactions. Once we had completed transcript scrolls for each episode, our final step of analysis was to collaboratively write a memo about ways the teachers’ interactions with both one another and with digital tools of accountability differed across these two clips, where the content and depth of engagement in critical educative mentoring practices substantively varied. Here, the objective is student-focused (creating a student learning opportunity instead of documentation for accountability), and the pursuit of this is shared as both educators engage in the co-creation. Relatedly, with less focus on the digital tools, their shared attention and orientation toward one another entail several interactive dimensions of connection, including leaning in (turn 3.1, 4.7), nodding (turn 3.2, 3.3, 3.7, 4.2, 4.6, 4.10), and increased interactive gesturing (turn 3.3, 3.5, 4.1, 4.10) Second, this episode is characterized by a heightened collective focus on student experience and learning opportunities. Finally, this episode captures the dyad engaged in co-explorative possibilities. In addition to both educators using language related to student experience and learning opportunities, both educators engage in a shared language of wonder. Moving away from a technocratic approach in which specific practices exist to be taken up and implemented, the mentor offers suggestions, not answers. For example, she starts the episode with a suggestive tone, “yo::u could:: go:: do sort of a low stakes round” (turn 3.1), stretching out her words, suggesting an adaptive approach to planning. The words “could” and “might” continue throughout both educators’ speech (mentor turn 3.1, 3.3, 4.1, 4.3; teacher turn 3.8), alongside “wonder” (teacher, turn 4.2) and “maybe” (mentor, turn 3.1, 4.1). We conducted an interaction analysis of the pair’s embodied interactions across contrasting episodes, one in which documentation through a digital mentoring tool played a central role and another in which it was absent. Our appears to drive the discourse towards a more technical focus. In contrast, in the second episode, the educators oriented themselves more toward one another and generated greater engagement with student sensemaking. Building on the existing literature on teacher learning in induction and mentoring (Feiman-Nemser, 2001; Fletcher & Mullen, 2012), we argue that digital tools for accountability in mentoring are not neutral mediators of teacher learning. Instead, these tools actively shape the content of interactional conversations in ways that may inadvertently undermine equity goals.

Our analysis extends work on datafication (Pangrazio et al., 2022; vandijck, 2014), by showing how its processes–reduction, abstraction, and individualization–unfold through embodied interaction. In the first episode, we traced reduction as inquiry narrowed from the substance of student work to completion counts; abstraction as the educators’ gaze turned toward the computer document and away from the classroom where teaching and learning occurs; and individualization as students were sorted into performance levels rather than understood within classroom community dynamics. In the second episode, the disruption of these processes was equally embodied: inquiry expanded through co-exploration punctuated by physical invitations to contribute (pause, gaze, nods) and students were recontextualized as imagined presences in the classroom space.

Our findings illuminate how, even if absent in program intent, accountability-driven approaches to mentoring can emerge through tools hosted on digital platforms that turn mentoring interactions into data and report that data to others for evaluation purposes. As such, they can contribute to historical patterns of positioning teachers as deprofessionalized implementers rather thandiscretionary, professional intellectuals. Future partnership-based research might work to re-mediate mentoring program design (Gutiérrez et al., 2009), attending to how the timing, format, and digital infrastructure of mentoring tools shape the interactional space available for responsive, equity-oriented inquiry.
