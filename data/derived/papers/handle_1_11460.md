# A School’s Agentic Infrastructuring for Scalable STEM Innovation Guided by the MLMS Connected Learning Framework

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Technology enhanced curriculum, and pedagogical innovations take place in schools, which are sociotechnical systems. Achieving scalable educational innovation necessitates infrastructuring changes as learning outcomes at multiple levels. Thus, research practice partnerships (RPP) need to provide guidance beyond the design and implementation of learning at the classroom level. Scaffolding schools in their organizational level self-directed learning journey is crucial for achieving their educational innovation goals. This is a practitioner-led study that reflects on a school’s 16-month RPP journey in a joint-school STEM innovation network. The RPP network adopted the Multilevel Multiscale (MLMS) framework as a design principle for guiding schools in their infrastructuring efforts. The school’s intentional infrastructuring re-design to support cross-disciplinary collaboration and peer learning led to enhanced student agency, teacher creativity, and leadership agility. The findings also highlight the feedback and feed-forward roles of the MLMS framework in facilitating scaling during the innovation mobilization process.

## Introduction

Teacher learning is undoubtedly crucial for curricu lum and pedagogical innovation, as teachers are key  change agents in classrooms. However, teacher learning support that focus on  classroom-level design at the individual level is not sufficient. The scalability of an innovation is a dynamic construct with five interconnected dimensions: depth, spread, shift (of innovation ownership), sus tainability, and evolution (Clark & Dede, 2009). Fa cilitating changes in these five dimensions require changes and learning in the school’s organizational conditions. Schools are entities within the broader educational and soc iocultural ecology. Any change will propagate across multiple levels of the ecological system (Daly, 2020). Teach er learning and practice are influenced not only by  their own school contexts but also by the communities of practice they are part of (Kelly, 2006). Research Practice Partnerships (RPPs, Coburn and Penuel, 2016) are systematic collaborations between researchers and practitioners, often adopted for implementing educational innovations. Engagement in integrated STEM innovation  RPP projects can support teachers ’ professional learning (Lo, 2021).  However, m ost RPPs reported in the literature  focus solely on designing curricula, pedagogy, lear ning resources, or tools for student learning. Learning sciences research has  highlighted the need for infrastructuring —intentional efforts to continuously modify the social infrastructure to achieve innovation success (Penuel, 2019; Chen, 2024). However, guiding infrastructuring is challenging not only because researchers, as external agents, generally lack an emic understanding of the unique organizational contexts  of their school partners. This process cannot be dictated by external agents and must be driven by school-based autonomous decisions, preferably guided by ap propriate design principles. Law & Ko  (2023) suggest that  a Multilevel Multiscale (MLMS) framework for connec ted learning could serve as a design principle to guide innovation teams in schools participating in an RPP innovation network to design their own school-based infrastructuring efforts  for scalable educational innovation. More research is needed to understand the role of MLMS as a design principle. This paper re ports on a practice-oriented study initiated by a team of practitioner-researchers whose school took part in a STEM-focused RPP innovation network. The three practitioner-authors, comprising a vice principal, an assistant vice-principal (head of science), and a STEM teacher, have led the school-based innovation efforts since the school joined the RPP in summer 2023. The y have wi tnessed remarkable progress in terms of their students’ agentic engagement in collaborative inquiry, and the evident connections between the MLMSguided infrastructuring efforts and the changes observed.

## Conceptual framework

Architecture for Learning (AfL), proposed by Stein and Coburn (2008), conceptualizes how an organizati on’s structure and functioning facilitate learning inter actions. It emphasizes learning through participation in communities of practice (Wenger, 1998; Wenger & Sny der, 2000). Core components of AfL include organizational structures, interaction mechanisms, and reification artifacts (Stein and Coburn, 2008).  Shared activities in both formal and informal settings offer interaction mechanisms that help negotiate meaning. Formal organizational structures facilitate decision-making resulting from interactions while artifacts consolidate shared understanding. Law et al. (2015) added technology as a fourth component to support participation and reification. Infrastructuring (Penuel, 2019), which brings about changes in  AfL, may result from external conditions or intentional efforts within the institution. The latter are often generative strategic leadership actions (Karasti et al.,

2018) that foster situated learning for teachers, thereby promoting a culture of continuous learning among teachers

and encouraging them to engage in learning through practice. The MLMS connected learning framework (Law & Ko, 2023) extends the concept of infrastructuring by emphasizing the interconnected nature of learning within ecosystems and the importance of cross-level alignment, e.g., linking leadership decisions to classroom pra ctices and participation in interschool sharing for  teachers and students. It provides design guidelines to promote alignment across levels (student, teacher, school, community, system) and among peer units at each level. MLMS-guided infrastructuring emphasizes innovations that  are iteratively adapted across scales while maintaining  coherence. An RPP adopting the MLMS framework would require school level infrastructuring efforts to re-design AfL components that facilitate cross-level and cross-unit (e.g., cross-subject, cross administrative unit)  interactions. The MLMS-guided infrastructuring underscores the importance of timely creation/modification of AfL components  to achieve scalability in different dimensions (Clarke and Dede, 2009).

## Research context

The three practitioner-researcher authors are teachers at School X, a progressive, co-educational school offering classes from grades 1 to 12. Advocating “STEM for All” and “STEM for Diversity”, the school provides regular STEM lessons for grades 1 to 9, covering diverse topics. Even before joining the RPP in summer 2023, the school had been promoting STEM education. In the 2022-23 school year, the school joined a STEM support program offered by an NGO. A project team comprising an assistant vice-principal and three STEM teachers was formed to lead the initiative. However, the school did not perceive significant progress re sulting from the effort  due to challenges such as fragmented teacher collaboration and limited cross-disciplinary support. In summer 2023, the school decided to join the university-led RPP project network with 27 other schools. It provides both school-and network-based support for creating and implementing interdisciplinary STEM curriculum units, adopting an inquiry-oriented, self-directed learning pedagogical approach. There was alignment between the school’s vision and the goals of the RP P. The goal of the school was to develop new project-based STEM curricula so that all grade 9 students would have an opportunity to learn through doing a project under one of seven themes: 3D Drawing and Printing, Laser Engraving and Cuttin g, App Design, Robotics, VR Scene Design, Smart Planting, or a Self-Proposed Topic. The goal for the student was to create a product th at can help users to improve their quality of life and enhance their own well-being.

## Research questions and study design

This study was initiated by the partitioner-researcher authors participating in the above-mentioned RPP who wish to take stock of the school’s infrastructuring efforts and their relationships with the progress made in achieving the school’s STEM curriculum goals. The specific  inquiry question was: What were the changes in AfL components for different levels of stakeholders dur ing the  16 months of  implementation from July 2023, the reasons for and consequences of the changes?

A case study design was adopted (Yin, 2003). To address the research question, various data sources were used to understand different aspects of the case. School documents (e.g., five-year development plans, annual STEM plans) and biyearly interviews conducted by univers ity researchers were used to analyze changes in AfL components (i.e. organizational structures, interac tion mechanisms, artifacts, and technology) at different levels across time, consequences and reasons behind. Teaching and learning artifacts, teacher debriefing  and student focus groups were used to identify changes at teacher and student levels as a result.

## Findings

The initial AfL design and consequences Organizational structure. At the beginning of the first year, the school established a project team that comprises personnel at different levels of the school organiz ation. The project team had 8 members: the principal, vice-principal (academic), assistant vice-principal (head of science), head of technology, three STEM teachers, and a STEM teaching assistant. The project team was convened when there were impor tant issues to discuss or deliberate. In addition, there was a co-design team comprising the head of science and the three STEM teachers who took responsibility for designing the grade 9 STEM lessons. It was found that the multilevel leadership team played an important empowering role in supporting the design team with great flexibility for pedagogical explorations. Prior to joining the RPP network, teachers had limited capacity in adjusting learning conditions for students. The involvement of school leaders such as the viceprincipal in the project team enabled teachers to i mplement necessary changes with leaders’ ad ministrative support. For example, adjustments in timetable were facilitated by the vice-principal so that extra mass lectures could be conducted where students were given information on how to write project proposals. Teachers could reorganize classes for students to work in small groups  and provide tailored feedback. Teachers were able to implement nontraditional assessment methods so that  learning artifacts generated —such as project proposals, reports, presentations, and peer evaluations—could be used to enhance the design of formal assessments. Interaction mechanisms.  There were RPP monthly meetings, and co-design sessions with a RPP consultant. The teachers’ professional development in the RPP project was very action-oriented that teacher learning was tied to the innovations they wish to co-construct. Hence, the formal interaction mechanisms via the project meeting and co-design team meetings were crucial in  supporting teachers’ professional practice change. Teachers’ participation in these meetings was enabl ed by school leaders: t he vice-principal made administrative adjustments with the timetable team to arrange for STEM teachers to attend RPP meetings. The principal and vice-principal also supported teachers in participating in other cross-unit interactions so that teachers not only could share their innovation experiences in  the RPP but also other events, such as the Learning  and Teaching Expo, an annual educational event in Hong Kong.

RPP network level interactions consequently provided learning opportunities to fuel the co-design team’s work. For example, the RPP introduced assessment rubrics to address the evaluation of self-directed learning (SDL) at various stages (Hew et al., 2016). Teachers then were guided to de sign their own rubrics for assessing various kinds of student assignments, such as proje ct proposals and project logbooks, making the asses sment systematic at different stages of SDL. These rubrics also align the marking standards among different teachers. There were some “within-unit” interactions (Law & Ko, 2023) facilitated by school leaders to spread the curriculum innovation among teachers as a result. For example, the school arranged for the project team to share their curriculum innovation with the entire school. This and other informal interactions led two senior Physics and Biology teachers, though not formally part of the S TEM project, to guide students on bridge resonance and nutrient comparisons between supermarket and homegr own hydroponic vegetables —topics beyond the STEM teachers’ expertise.

Artefacts and technology. Artefacts such as school self-evaluation presentations and departmental annual reports served to communicate project progress to t he school board to facilitate monitoring and garner  top-level support. Other artefacts such as curriculum resources and as sessment rubrics scaffolded professional communication and shared practices. The teachers also found the technology platform, Learning Design Studio® (LDS), provided by the RPP valuable in scaffolding their design during the co-design process. Consequences after the first year.  The school  level infrastructuring changed learning conditions for teachers and eventually for  students. Teachers re-designed learning conditions  to include students’ proposal presentations for feedback and to incorporate asses sment rubrics for peer-and self-evaluation. Students demonstrated self-directed learning by articulating improvements and revising based on feedback. “When we do it on our own, it is very hard to find what we miss …. So, like after listening to our classmates’ and the teacher’s feedback, it will  be easier for us to improve our project.” (Student A, the first student focus group) Students also articulated their growth in 21 st century skills, such as collaborative skills. “I also learn that cooperation is important. We bas ically cannot finish all the stuff on our own. I really have great impression of it because 3D printing is actually very challenging. So, you must need someone to help you or assist you or do i t together.” (Student B, the second student focus group).

The positive responses from students made teachers felt excited about the positive outcomes for learners of diverse abilities through the STEM innovation. As teacher C expressed in the year-end project review: “For relatively talented and experienced students, … are willing to challenge themselves by working on more difficult topics. They learn advanced knowledge, and it is impressive that they can understand this level of material, as it is alr eady at a university level. On the other hand, students who may be less motivated in STEM can still choose topics of interest and learn basic STEM skills. This represents a step forward for them, de monstrating their ability to engage in self-directed learning and design thinking.

School X redesigning the AfL to feed-forward innovations and reasons During the first-year end project review with the RPP university team mem bers, teachers expressed that the multilevel project team structure and the many inte raction mechanisms to be very helpful in facilitati ng project success in the first year. However, challenges and further opportunities were also identified. MLMS has guided school level infrastructuring to change AfL components to feed-forward the schools’ further innovations in the second year, as follows.

1. Strengthening the organizational structure. The workload of the STEM teachers in coaching a diverse range

of student group projects is heavy. A teacher mentioned, “...frankly speaking, the workload of this (STEM) lesson is substantial” during the year-end review. To relieve stress, school leaders added a STEM teac her with a strong engineering background to the project team. With this new team member, the co-design team teachers decided to incorporate AI elements into their STEM lessons in the second year, such as facilitating students in using ChatGPT to brainstorm during the goal-setting phase and to develop criteria for success tailored to their projects.

2. School-based curriculum redistribution  and refinement. Teachers noticed that some student groups had

difficulties in setting their group project goals. To make the STEM project learning time more focused  on this, the teaching of digital literacy topics, including proper APA citat ion, will be conducted in ICT lessons as agreed by the Head of Technology and the other grade 9 ICT  teachers. The Vice Principal further suggested usi ng the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (Unite d Nations, 2015) as a scaffold to help students set  project goals, which also aligns with the school’s vision of fostering a global outlook in students. Such refinements in the curriculum represented an evolution of STEM learning design. Recognizing the outstanding benefits of the STEM innovation project, the head of science stated in the year-start teacher interview that the SDL pedagogical innovation would be spread to other grade levels: “We will attempt to incorporate an SDL assessment f ramework into the science curriculum to evaluate whether students demonstrate their perform ance across various SDL stages within a Scientific Investigation, such as their abilities in goal setting...”

3. Re-designing AfL to extend students’ interactions with p eers in and outside of the school. Recognizing the

benefits of expanding the audience for grade 9 stud ents to share their learning, the vice principal pr oposed organizing a Science Exhibition during the post-examination period, enabling all groups to present their projects to Grade 8 studen ts. A STEM teacher supported this idea, suggesting, “We can ask the grade 8 students to vote for the best project. Meanwhile, they  will also be able to preview what they will learn a nd do in the following academic year.” Furthermore, after the meeting, another teacher sug gested inviting the previous 2023-24 cohort of grade 9 students to assist groups working on similar research topics during the current school year.

4. Readiness to explore using a new Learning Managemen t System (LMS) for better e-learning support. The

RPP has been providing an Interactive Learning and Assessment Platform  (iLAP) as the LMS for all schools  as it has more pedagogical features aligned with the LDS platform that the schools adopted to support their learning design work. The teachers were reluctant to change to iLAP in the first year of joining the RPP. To further enhance student e-learning in STEM, the STEM teachers decided to try out iLAP in the second year.

## Discussion

It is remarkable how fluent the school leaders and teachers were in appropriating the concept of AfL and in their intentional adoption of the MLMS framework in navigating challe nges, consolidating experiences, and scaling SDL practices in STEM curriculum innovation. This self-reflective investigation shows that AfL is never st atic as the school ecosystem is always in a state of flu x and in-situ infrastructuring is needed (Chen, 2024). School leaders should prioritize strategies for infrastructuring their schools’ AfL dynamically to enhance the learning environment to ensure that teachers have sufficient time and space for professional development and co-planning with their colleagues. Further, school level infrastructuring to scale educational innovations is possible but it takes intentional efforts involving stakeholders at different levels. Importantly, professional  autonomy empowers teachers to proactively engage in creative exploration. The MLMS framework facilitates interactions that promote agile, timely adjustments to ensure success in pushing beyond traditional boundaries.  Non-STEM RPP projects can also adopt the MLMS framework to support intentiona l AfL re-design for scalable innovation s that respect school’s autonomy within an ecological context.  Further research is needed to understand the school level factors that facilitate or impede such intentional infrastructuring for successful scaling of educational innovations.
