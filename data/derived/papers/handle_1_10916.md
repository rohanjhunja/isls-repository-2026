# Is Block-Based Programming “Real Programming”?

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores high school students' perceptions of "real programming" in the context of block-based programming environments, specifically focusing on VEXcode and Scratch. The research found students held diverging views on the authenticity of block-based programming. These findings present a more nuance view on students’ perceptions of block-based programming, showing that attribution of authenticity extends beyond block-based interface itself to also include the environment its situated in and the programming tasks and capabilities it supports.

## Introduction

Block-based programming  environments, such as Scratch,  have been proven to be an effective method for introducing novice students to computer science programming. However, there is a wide range of paradigms and genres of block-based programming environments  (Lin & Weintrop, 2021)  and the capabilities of block-based tools and the types of programs that can be written  with them is rapidly expanding. A growing number of blockbased programming environments have been developed in recent years to support physical computing and robotics, such as MakeCode Microbit and VEXcode. Some of these tools support both physical and virtual robots, such as MakeCode Microbit and VEXcode which highlights how block-based tools can be used to create a programs for a growing number of contexts.

This poster presents a case study drawn from a larger research project, focusing on learners' perceptions of authenticity in the context of block-based programming. The aim  of this work  is to assist educators and practitioners in understanding what constitutes "re al programming" for learners  and how this could inform teaching and designing of the tool.

## Background

Research has shown that block-based programming is an effective way to introduce learners to programming (Weintrop & Wilensky, 2019). However, Weintrop and Wilensky (Weintrop & Wilensky, 2015) have also found that students identify certain drawbacks to block-based programming when compared to conventional text-based approaches. These drawbacks include a perceived lac k of authenticity and limited power. For example, s tudents have expressed views such as "Java is actual code, while [block-based programming] is something nobody will let you code in." Other researchers (Bau et al., 20 17) have identified a similar issue, noting that season ed programmers might consider block-based programming to have little relevance to "real code."

## Method

This case study is part of a larger research projec t focused on transitioning students from block-based to text-based programming. In the study, a group of high sc hool students (N=8) from a mid-western city in the U.S. received four weeks of instruction using a block-based programming environment called VEXcode. VEXcode serves as a one-way transitional environment that allows users to c ontrol virtual robotics in a simulated world. The students were enrolled in a semester-long computer science programming co urse. The study includes six white males, one multi-racial male, and one non-binary individual of another racial background. After the initial four weeks of instruction, the students were invited to participate in a semi-structured interview. One of the interview questions was, "Do you think VEXcode is 'real programming'?" For students who had prior experience with Scratch or other block-based programming environments, the question was ex panded to include, "Do you think Scratch or other block-based programming environments are 'real programming'?" It is important to note that students were free to define "real programming" in their own terms during the interview.

## Findings

Seven of the eight students interviewed responded that VEXcode qualifies as "real programming." One student argued that the ability to control virtual robotics  constitutes "real programming."  “ One makes it [to be “real programming”] because you can control how the robot moves. You cando that on [VEXcode].” Another student said, "Because you're still putting in the work to figure out what to do in the first place, [and] how to fix what you did wrong. You know, it [block-based programming] takes a little less effort, maybe, but it's still real programming to me." The responses changed when the interview question s hifted to whether they considered Scratch to be "real programming." Students who had used Scratch b efore generally did not view it as "real programmin g," dismissing it as a tool designed for kids to create mini video games. Two students expressed the following views: "No, Scratch is not real programming. It is something made for kids to create mini video games," and "No, I think it's [Scratch is] more like just for kids who want to make a little video game. It is for beginners who want to make video games but don't have a clue how to do that. " However, they viewed VEXcode as "real programming" due to its focus on robotics.

Conversely, one student with prior experience in Ar duino programming contested this perspective. "So, you can use it [Ardunio], I guess, for example, for  LED lights and a water flow sensor. And they're al l from different companies, and I had to find the code lib raries for those, " he said. According to this student, "real programming" should allow for the integration of va rious libraries and third-party sensors—something he found lacking in block-based programming platforms.

## Discussion

This research yields intriguing findings that add nuance to the literature on student percep tions of authenticity in block-based programming tools. Contrary to previous resea rch, which stated that students generally do not consider block-based programming to be "real programming" (Weintro p & Wilensky, 2015), the students in this case study viewed VEXcode—a block-based environment that controls a virtual robot —as "real programming." However, they did not hold the same view for Scratch or other block-based programming environments. One potential way to interpret this finding is that  students rely heavily on surface features of environments to determine their authenticity. Scrat ch, being brightly colored and including cartoon-inspired sprites and backgrounds suggests a childish environ ment, even though the platform can interface with n umerous robots and physical computing devices. The presenta tion of Scratch differs from VEXcode, and other tools such as AppInventor (Chadha & Turbak, 2014) and CoBlox (Weintrop et al., 2018), which have a different, and older, target audience. Interesting open questions remain about the roots of these perceptions of authenticit y and ways to change them. Would having students use Scratch t o control a robot be sufficient to shift their pers pectives on block-based programming altogether? Inversely, could usin g VEXcode help learners see Scratch as more authentic?

## Conclusion

This poster presents a small-scale case study examining students' perceptions of  whether block-based programming environments like VEXcode and Scratch qualify as "real programming." Students  in the study generally viewed VEXcode as "real programming" due to its capability to cont rol a robot. In contrast, they did not consider Scratch to be "real programming," dism issing it as a tool designed primarily for children  to create mini video games. By highlighting these differing p erceptions, this work contributes  to the ongoing discussion about what constitutes "real programming" in the minds of different students.
