# Future Trends in Digital Learning for Enabling Holistic Student Development: Review of Educational Policy

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
There is a push to adopt educational practices fostering holistic student development in K-12 due to current uncertain times. To understand what these new practices entail, we take a global policy perspective and identify trends that will likely shape the future of education focused on holistic student development. We identified eight trends for future-focused education and discuss how these can be mapped to holistic student development. These trends represent three interconnected dimensions of education – Why (goals for education), How (the design and organization of education), and What (the content of education). We identified 20 sub-trends that reflect the multifaceted nature of the trends. Teacher capabilities required to facilitate the implementation of these trends were described as well. These provide a robust base for (re)imagining how to prepare K-12 students (and teachers) as change-agents instead of only a workforce.

## Introduction

Holistic student development (HSD) has emerged as the need of the hour due to current uncertain times. This perspective emphasizes that educational systems and  resources should cater to students as whole indivi duals (body, mind, and spirit) within a larger society. D ifferences exist while interpreting the meaning of engaging in HSD. Proponents of Social and Emotional Learning (SEL)  suggest one viable path of engaging in whole child development (e.g., CASEL, 2015). CASEL (2015) focus es on five competencies-self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, self-awareness and responsible decision making. However, CASEL lacks explicit emphasis on moral, spiritual, and physical development dimensions of  HSD. Positive education offers another relevant lens for HSD aiming to promote flourishing amongst whole school communities (e.g., Norrish et al., 2013). It emphasizes that school education should focus on meaningful persona l growth, fulfilment of students’ potential, and well-being. More recently, Gordon (2022) provides an all-encompassing framework for holistic student development that builds on SEL and positive  education. This framework provides a holistic approach to education by considering students’ i ntellectual, emotional, social, moral, spiritual, c ultural, and physical development (Gordon, 2022). Thus, we use this in our research. In this conceptual paper, we take a global policy perspective to identify key trends that are likely to shape the future of education (and research) focusing on holistic student development.  We argue that such an effort makes a conceptual con tribution towards ongoing learning sciences research by creating HSD pathways within the community and encourag ing more research emphasis on preparing school students and teachers to be future-ready. Our research questions are: (RQ1) What are the main challenges and opportunities for educators’ profess ional learning for holistic student development? (RQ2) What are the specific capabilities educators need to develop instruction for future-focused holistic student development?

## Theoretical framework

Holistic development positions learning as a multifaceted process spanning multiple dimensions that include SEL and positive education, namely – Intellectual, emotional, social, moral, spiritual, cultural, and physical development (Gordon, 2022). The intellectual dimension relates to learning processes that focus on the cognitive domain. Bloom’s taxonomy (Bloom, 1956; Krathwohl, 2002) provides a comprehensive overview of these learning processes, driving numerous research into this dimension  (e.g., Crompton et al., 2019). For holistic development, this dimension is seen as integrated w ith the other dimensions (Gordon, 2022). The emotional dimension focuses on developing students’ self-esteem, self-awareness, and optimism (Gordon, 2022). Students learn how to recognize and manage their emotions, o ften utilizing self-regulation and coping strategies (Skinner & Saxton, 2019). The social dimension pertains to the skills for social engagem ent and self-control. It includes aspects of joint attention and articulation (Fischer et al., 2018), being empathetic, and taking perspectives (Roberts & Lyons, 2019). Education focused on the social dim ension acknowledges the importance of engaging in dialogue, collaborative problem-solving, exchange of ideas and feelings, and confli ct resolution skills (Gordon, 2022). Moral (and ethical) dimension at the K-12 level pertains to students’ ability to resolve m oral and ethical dilemmas in an empathetic manner (Triplett, 2017). Researchers have used techniques such as moral imagination (i.e., imagine alternatives before deciding) and moral sensitivity (i.e., consider effects of our decisions on others) (Pass & Willingham, 2009). The spiritual dimension pertains to connecting with students’ inner resources, values, and cultivating a sense that people are interconnec ted and interdependent (Gordon, 2022). Mindfulness – awareness of the present moment in non-judgmental ways – is related to this dimension  and influences school students’ anxiety, stress, and fatigue (Kielty et al., 2017; Meiklejohn et al., 2012). Cultural dimension pertains to developing students’ aesthetics and artistic skills. Multisensory learning and collaborations between multiple partners – schools, cultural organizations, and artists – have been found to improve students’ learning outco mes and promote their holistic development (Hanna, 2008; Bowen & Kisida, 2017). Physical development pertains to knowledge about one’s health and healthy habits. Ph ysical education that is fun, inclusive, and promot es healthy decision-making benefits students’ overall development (Belc astro & Ramsaroop-Hansen, 2017). Trends that shape the future of education need to address these dimensions  at various levels. In addition, teacher capacity building for fostering holistic student development  needs significant attention, paying special consid eration to their changing roles, professional preparedness, and mindsets (Gordon, 2022). Recent global policy ini tiatives have started taking steps to address this need of holistic student development (e.g., OECD, 2024; UNESCO, 2021; EC, 2024). These initiatives highlight key trends that are likely to shape the future of education while addressing various dimensions of holistic student development.

## Method

To understand trends that are likely to shape the future of education for holistic student development, we searched for the keywords “future” AND “education”  in the databases of  global policy organizations, namely, European Commission, UNESCO, OECD, UN, World Economic Forum, Association for the Development of Education in Africa, Amideast, Education Development Trust, East Asian Educational  Association, and the Commonwealth. These databases were identified based on their extent of global and regi onal footprints. Documents were considered if they met all  the following criteria: (a) f uture-focused and described how societal, economical, technological, environmental factors are expected t o influence education, (b) focus on a larger geographical area (e.g. sub-Sahara or Europe), (c) f ocus on primary education and/ or lower and/ or upp er secondary education levels, (d) written in the English language, and (e) published between 2019-2024 (to identify recent trends). The coding of the policy documents followed a stepwise method, identifying sub-trends, grouping these into trends, and categorizing trends into levels of why, how, and what. Sub-trends were identified through inductive coding of a sub-set of 21 documents that took broad perspectives on  how various factors would shape the future of education. Most of these documents were written by the European Commission, the OECD and UNESCO. First, relevant excerpts from the policy documents were coded using ATLAS.ti and then related codes were merged into sub-trends. Next, descriptions of these trends and sub-trends were iteratively developed based on the  quotations. Codes and descriptions were discussed within the re search team and refined iteratively until agreement was reached. Second, sub-trends were categorized into larger trends and these trends were included in the framework if they were mentioned by at least  three of the previously mentioned organizations. Th ird, identified trends were further grouped at three levels, foregrounding the goals for education (“the why”), the design and organization of education (“the how”), and the content of education  (“the what”). While trends are grouped on differen t levels, they are not disconnected and influence each other.

Next, two systematic reviews and meta-analysis were identified per trend (where available) that described the capabilities teachers need to design or enact education considering these trends. The databases of the journals Research of Educational Research and E ducational Research Review were searched for releva nt publications in the past five years to identify recent meta-analysis. These journals were chosen as they provide a generalized yet robust picture of the field. If not available there, reviews (within the past five years) were identified through Google Scholar. One discussion article from a special issue on student well-being has been included. The systematic reviews were inductively coded on various levels (i.e. knowledge, skills, beliefs, competences). The findings present an initial framework of trends for future-focused holistic student development and teacher capabilities that will be further refined and validated through expert panels.

sustainable development Preliminary mapping of the trends and sub-trends with holistic student development dimensions The trends and sub-trends together with the various teacher capability presented above (Table 1) relate to various dimensions of HSD.  Here we present  our preliminary ideas on possible prominent mappings with HSD dimensions. For instance, the trends of ‘Education for well-being’ and ‘Education to navigate uncertainty’ (trends dimension ‘Why’) can be mapped to all the HSD dimensions-spiritual, cultural, moral, emotional, social, and physical development. Education focused on fostering s tudents’ well-being and capacity for navigating uncertainty is likely to have positive influence on students’ awareness on how to adopt effective coping strategies to manage their emotions  (emotional dimension) and engage in mindfulness practices to reduce stress (spiritual dimension). Such an education has the potential to foster learning that enables them to view themselves as part of a larger ecosystem (spiritual  dimension). It can also promote engagement in collaborative problem-solving and practicing effective conflict resolution skills (social dimension) in an empathetic manner (moral dimension), while navigating uncertainties inherent to real-world community-based projects (cultural dimension). These engagements influence students’ health and their habits (physical development). The trends corresponding to the dimension ‘How’  emphasize how to organize education. Thus, if education is organized in the ways suggested by the se trends and sub-trends then it can positively influence a subset of the  HSD dimensions. For instance, fostering diversity in classrooms and promoting belonging in interconnected ways with students from different backgrounds can positi vely cultivate a sense of interconnectedness (spiritual dimension) and invite creative expressions during learning processes (cultural dimension). Personalized and flexible education along with responsible use of AI caters to students understanding of the domain (intellectual dimension), confidence in using digital tools (emotional dimension), and understanding the different perspectives and influence of such digital technologies on others (social and moral dimensions). The trends corresponding to the dimension ‘What’ pertain to the content of education and can also relate to most of the  HSD dimensions. For example, a n ecologically oriented education can stimulate students’ knowledge about climate change (intellectual dimens ion), optimism about the future (emotional dimension), and emphasize the interconnectedness amongst people both as a value (spiritual dimension) as well as opportunities for engaging in collaborative problem-solving and conflict resolution (social dimension). Collective agency and active citizenship can, among others, invite joint artistic expressions across multiple partners (cultural dimension).

## Conclusion

The findings from this trend analysis have implications for (re)designing curricula at the global level. Since these findings are based on policy documents that cover m ost of the regions of the world, we believe that th ese trends will be meaningful to educ ators and decision-makers from various regions. We expect that the tre nds (and thus the mappings to HSD) may be interpreted and prioritised in different ways by different regions. However, despite these differences, these trends and sub-trends provide a robust base for (re)imagining how to prepare students (and teachers) as change-agents instead of only a workforce. Further, the ca tegories of trends and sub-trends can provide a guiding framework for education researche rs and enable focused research into designing scaff olds, interventions, and teacher professional development  programs with the goal of fostering holistic student development.
