# When Failing Generates Math Anxiety Instead of Productivity

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Experiences of failure and confusion can be intentionally purposed for learning tasks. In the Productive Failure instructional design, students attempt to solve problems before they are formally instructed on the relevant content. However, confusion and the perception of failing can elicit negative emotions in learners, even more so in students who already feel anxious about failure. Although research has demonstrated that failure can lead to math anxiety, and vice versa, the role of math anxiety in instructional designs that leverage moments of failure has not been investigated. This negative emotional cycle may negatively impact some students’ learning and interest. We argue that future research would benefit from considering potential tradeoffs between enhanced learning and increased anxiety. We ground our arguments in an exploratory mixed-methods design that combines behavioral data with a task-based interview.

Affect and Math Anxiety in Math Education The various elements of affect, attitudes, and stud ent beliefs—such as those concerning one’s understanding of math, or beliefs concerning success and failure—are increasingly acknowledged to play an important, yet poorly understood, role in math learning. For example, students’ affective disposition can be a more critical predictor of entry into STEM subjects than enrollment and grades (Maltese & Tai, 2011). When experiencing a negative emotion, we may focus on our negative sensations. Psychologically, this is done to find the means of eliminating the negati ve feeling. Thus, it is not surprising that there i s an effect of emotions on learning, and studies have shown that l earning is negatively influenced by unpleasant emot ions, unless the tasks are simple (attention allocation e lsewhere; see Hascher, 2010). Regardless of their v alence, emotions can also be beneficial for learning, such as happiness (Kim & Pekrun, 2014), confusion (D’Mello et al., 2014), and even frustration (Liu et al., 2013). On the other hand, boredom and anxiety are associated with reduced learning (Pekrun, Elliot & Maier, 2009), while frustration can be destructive (Kort, Reilly & Picard, 2001). While uncertainty has been shown to be beneficial for learning, it may also be detrimental (Lamnina & Chase 2019). In short, instructional designs that intentionally generate uncertainty could contribute to either:

(1) negative emotions about knowledge (Sinha 2021): a focus on the uncertainty of the problem, or

(2) negative achievement emotions (Pekrun & Linnenbrink-Garcia, 2014): a focus on personal failure.

Thus, fostering uncertainty could lead to (1) curiosity to learn what the student is (now aware to be) lacking—but may also lead to (2) self-preoccupying thoughts that interfere with learning. The latter may even feed into vicious cycles of anxiety and failure (Pekrun, Elliot & Mai er, 2009). Some affective aspects in math learning appear to be rooted so deeply that they are difficult to diminish by instruction (Gomez-Chacon, 2000). Because there is more to a learning situation than the local affect, we also must attend to the global affect—the lingering presence of experiences that made the learner the person they are (Gomez-Chacon, 2000). We argue that it is important to also attend to the more global affects, such as math anxiety, when designing for uncertainty. Math anxiety, which develops through v arious educational and non-educational experiences throughout the learner’s life, has been described a s a feeling of panic, helplessness, paralysis, and mental disorganization that arises when one is solving a mathematical problem or managing numbers (Ashcraft, Kirk & Hopko, 2000). In interviews with math anxious indiv iduals (Sloan, 2010), the most reported causes of m ath anxiety were: negative experiences with math classes, teaching practices in school, memorization of procedural knowledge, math avoidance—low math achievement, test anxiety—failure, lack of confidence. Thus, pre-existing math difficulties cause or aggravate math anxiety (Núñez-Peña & Suárez- Pelliconi, 2014), and poor math performance can lead to math anxiety. Taken togethe r, math anxiety is not merely a local affect but is  better understood as a global affect that influences learn ers’ behavior and cognition. This global affect oug ht to be considered when designing instruction that generates instances of failure and uncertainty, because this approach could generate or reinforce a student’s already existing negative attitudes, beliefs, and feelings towards math. Productive Failure and Affect Productive failure (PF; Kapur, 2016; Kapur & Bielac zyc, 2012) is a subset of Problem Solving followed by Instruction designs (PS-I; Loibl, Roll, & Rummel, 2 017), that broadly follow the paradigm of preparati on for ICLS2022 Proceedings 1465 © ISLS future learning (Schwartz & Bransford, 1998). In PF, the problem-solving phase is intentionally designed as an initial generation and exploration phase, which off ers students the opportunity to activate existing a nd intuitive knowledge. Since the problems are based on concepts  that have not been formally taught to the students, the problem-solving phase typically generates feelings of uncertainty and moments of failure (e.g., incorr ect solutions). In a subsequent instruction phase, the desired concepts are conveyed to the students. PF designs, like impasse driven learning (Van Lehn, 1988), lead students to an impasse in their learning that forces a transition into “repair” strategies, such as seeking help, to overcome the impasse. Piag et (1964) formulated in his theory of cognitive development t hat we experience a state of imbalance when encount ering information that requires us to develop new schema or modify existing schema. This uncomfortable state  of imbalance drives us to the assimilation of new schema or the adaptation of old schema. Without accommodation, we are left with old schemas. For some, this might be “I am not good in math; I don’t know how to do it”. Present Study Only a few studies investigated the role of affect in PF (e.g., Lamnina & Chase, 2019; Sinha, 2021). T o our knowledge, we are the first to investigate math anxiety specifically in a PF design. Math anxiety affects all stages of cognitive math processing (e.g., Ashcraft, Kirk, & Hopko, 1998). Therefore, we hypothesize that students with higher levels of math anxiety will benefit comparatively less from the PF design on learning outcomes.

## Participants

Sixty-two students (age range = 18-24) were randoml y assigned to one of the conditions (PS-I or “Direc t Instruction followed by Problem solving-I-PS”). Two students dropped out due to their high level of math anxiety. Those two students took longest on the task and voc alized their perceived difficulties and feelings to wards the situation. The first author took notes of these com ments and conversations and translated them to Engl ish. All participants were naïve to the goals of the experiment and provided written informed consent. The study has been approved by the local Ethics Committee following the Declaration of Helsinki.

## Procedure and Materials

A classical PF design (Kapur, 2014) was used along with questionnaires about different cognitive and affective mechanisms such as knowledge gap awareness, state c uriosity, cognitive load, learning goal orientation, and attitudes towards mistakes (Sinha & Kapur, 2021). I n addition to that and relevant to the present stud y, math anxiety was measured with the Abbreviated Math Anxiety Scale (AMAS; Hopko et al., 2003). The participa nts responded using a 5-point Likert-scale, ranging from 1 (low anxiety) to 5 (high anxiety).

## Qualitative results and discussion

The quantitative results do not capture the experience of the excluded participant Belinda (name changed; highest AMAS score in the sample: 43). The inclusion of interview notes illustrates the challenges faced by math-anxious individuals when dealing with designs that intentionally introduce feelings of uncertainty and failure. ## Conclusion and Future Directions

While PF designs tend to produce positive effects on student understanding, they also generate moments of failure and feelings of uncertainty. This is not a criticism per se: after all, learning math is difficult. However, the concern is that certain learners might experience increased  math anxiety and more negative opinions regarding their abilities and knowledge. Even if learning can be in creased with such designs, do we not also court the  risk of losing potential STEM students? As Maltese and Tai (2011) suggests, it may be possible to simultaneous ly increase students’ math achievement yet decrease their interest in majoring in STEM fields. Further research should be done to determine whethe r the present results can be generalized. Future experimental studies should contrast math anxious a nd non-math anxious students in a PF design (i.e., a 2x2 design, PS-I vs I-PS, math-anxious vs non-anxious) to investigate if math anxiety leads to lower posttest scores. Nonetheless, the present results offer support for our central argument that math anxiety plays a sign ificant and thus-far unexplored role in instructional designs that generate moments of uncertainty and failure. Course instructors and teachers should be aware of these findings, such that appropriate help and support can be provided to. In an ideal setting, students can experience curiosity and benefit from such designs, yet “ideal” is not something we can intentionally design for.
