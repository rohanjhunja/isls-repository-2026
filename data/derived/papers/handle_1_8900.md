# The Construction of Mathematical Authority in a First Grade Classroom

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study uses positioning theory to understand how students come to construct their own and others’ mathematical authority. The study took place in the context of a classroom teaching experiment with 22 first-grade students in a demographically and academically diverse school. We coded the interactions of the eight students who had the most whole class airtime. We also determined airtime for these participants, which involved total talking time during whole class discussions, total utterances spoken during whole class discussions, and the number of times they were talked to. Findings show how one student’s airtime contributed to her establishing undue authority and in turn having an incorrect idea endorsed by another student. Findings also reveal some of the interactions that contribute to authority construction over time.

In this study, we explore the following research qu estion: In what ways do students come to construct their own and others’ mathematical authority? Mathematical authority is "the degree to which students are given opportunities to be involved in decision making and  whether they have a say in establishing priorities  in task completion, method, or pace of learning.” It is spe cific to math, or “who’s in charge” of making mathe matical contributions (Gresalfi & Cobb, 2006, p. 51). The q uestion we pose stemmed from our observation of an interaction among a group of first grade students i n Lesson 12 out of a total of 16 from an 8-week cla ssroom teaching experiment (CTE).

The interaction occurred during Lesson 12 when a gr oup of students were discussing how to represent two sisters’ ages (Janice and Keisha), using variables. In the situation being discussed, Keisha is three years older than Janice. One student, Leo, proposed that Janice ’s age could be represented with j and Keisha’s with k, indicating that these are the first letters of thei r names. Another student, Nyah, then proposed j for Janice’s age and m for Keisha’s age, using the order of the letters of the alphabet (e.g., a = 1; b = 2, etc.) and counting on three more from j. This approach has been documented elsewhere (e.g.,  MacGregor & Stacey, 1997) as a strategy students use when representing variable quantities. A third student, Zyla, then proposed representing Keisha’s age as j + 3. A fourth student, Tia, disagreed with Zyla and instead proposed l for Keisha, counting three but including j in her counting (i.e., j, k, l). About a minute later, another student, Azaiah, stated that he agreed with Tia and that “I think it’s l.” When asked why, he referred to a moment earlier in the same lesson, when: “Tia was the right one because she put three on there (indicating that Tia  had written “+3” on the shared classroom poster) a nd that was—and Leoneeded help.” Tia’s mathematical author ity and Azaiah's assessment of her idea based on hi s experience of her past expertise is at the crux of this paper. To investigate this dynamic, we use positioning theory (Anderson, 2009) and build on authors who have used  this framing to explore related questions about au thority (e.g., Langer-Osuna, 2016; Langer-Osuna et al., 2018), along with Engle et al.’s (2014) influence framework.

## Methods

This paper draws data from a larger study carried out in a demographically and academically diverse public school in the Northeast region of the United States. The participants included 22 first grade students who participated in a CTE. The CTE involved eight weeks of classroom instruction organized into two 4-week cycles (with two 40-minute lessons taught per week). Each lesson was video recorded with two cameras and transcribed. The tasks designed for the CTE were based on field-tested tasks developed in previous research by the project researchers. The overarching goal was to engage the  students in algebraic thinking, or to support them  in ICLS2022 Proceedings 941 © ISLS generalizing, representing, justifying, and reasoni ng with mathematical relationships (Blanton et al.,  2011; Blanton et al., 2015). Students were encouraged to share their ideas and to discuss among themselves the merits of their ideas, to agree or disagree, and share their reasoning. Our goal was to have a distributed sense of authority among all participants in the classroom, and, as te acher-researchers, to share with students both the sources of ideas proposed as well as the decision making aroun d whose ideas should be taken up. Lessons typically  began with a whole class (WC) discussion before students broke into small groups (3-5 students) to work on mathematics tasks at their table, and then wrapped up with another WC conversation. All CTE lessons were videotaped and all written student work was collected. All classroom lessons, from Lesson 1 to Lesson 12, were transcribed verbatim. L esson 12 includes the interaction in which Tia’s in correct idea was accepted. We are not including Lessons 13-16 in this paper because our study was focused on investigating how, from Lesson 1 to Lesson 12, mathematical authority was constructed. Additionally, while we coded all participants’ utterances, we report on eight of the 22 students because these students had the most airtime and in turn had more opportunities to contribute in  ways that shaped their positions. If we included d ata for the remaining students, the frequencies and percentages for each code for these students would be significantly low. Moreover, the five students who were at the center of the Lesson 12 interaction that motivated us to focus on the construction of authority are a subset of the eight  focus students. We further selected from the datas et and only include the interactions that occurred in a WC sett ing because the eight students were present in this  setting, whereas these students were separated when working in small groups and cameras did not capture all small group conversations.

We developed our coding scheme by a line-by-line review of the Lesson 12 transcript. We selected this lesson because of the interaction in which Azaiah a ccepted Tia’s incorrect idea. In our line-by-line r eview and using the constant comparative method (Glaser & Strauss, 1967), we identified lines in which ideas were proposed and how they were responded to because these moments appeared to be linked to how participants were positioned. We define an idea as a thought or suggestion, which  can be articulated orally or in writing. Ideas may  vary depending on the task and situation at hand and are  not necessarily stable or unitary (see Brizuela et  al., 2015). Some utterances include one or more ideas, while some utterances do not. Proposing an idea is a bid, or an opportunity for s omeone else to respond to your idea. The response typically comes from the teacher and is either neutral, positive (idea was accepted), or negative (idea was rejected). Sometimes the teacher’s or student’s tone led us to believe their response was neutral, positive, or negative, not the words they said. For example, “okay,” said enthusiastically could indicate an idea is accepted whereas “okay” said flatly could indicate a neutral response. In addition to coding transcript lines, we kept track of the total airtime for each one of the students in each lesson, number  of utterances spoken by each individual, and the n umber of times an individual is talked to. We use Langer-Osuna’s (2015) definition for an utterance: “what was stated by a speaker when the speaker held the floor, regardless of whether the utterance was interrupted or was a completed turn. In the case of an interrupted utterance, if t he speaker gained the floor again to complete the t urn, the two turns were each coded as separate utterances” (p. 59). We found the totals for each of these codes acr oss the 12 lessons for the eight focus students. We then ranked students based on the totals for each code. For example, we counted the number of times an individual proposed an idea across the 12 lessons, and then ranked that student in relation to the other eight students. If a student proposed the most ideas they were ranked as the hig hest for proposed ideas. Since some codes were associated with a negative responses (e.g., an idea is rejected), we reversed the ranking system when necessary. For example, if a student had the fewest instances of an idea being rejected, they were ranked the highest for ideas rejected. To  ensure the reliability coders overlapped on 20% of  the data. We calculated values for both percent agreement and  Fleiss’s Kappa to ensure agreement beyond chance agreement. The percent agreement was over 90%, and the resulting Kappa range was 0.58 – 0.71 for all videos.

## Conclusion

ICLS2022 Proceedings 943 © ISLS By looking at both the frequencies of airtime, utterances, and ideas, as well the ways in which ideas were responded to as a percentage of proposed ideas, we can see that Tia stands out in terms of amount of a irtime, utterances, and ideas proposed. However, there were other students who stood out proportionally more than Tia in the ways in which their ideas were responded to. The incorrect information that Tia provided in the lesson 12 exchange is noteworthy because from her and Azaiah’s perspective it was logically sound. Tia was arguing that since Keisha is three years olde r than her sister, and her sister’s age is represented with j, that Keisha’s age should be represented with l. She is actually correcting another student who suggests m for Keisha’s age. To Azaiah, Tia’s argument is int uitive. Relatedly, Lane and Harris (2015), who worked with three to eight year olds, found that children are more likely to accept intuitive claims. Thus, we conclude that the content of that particular claim may have played a role in why it was accepted as valid and in turn resulted in granting Tia undue authority. Understanding the construction of authority has imp lications far beyond the first-grade mathematics classroom in which our study occurred. Supporting students in developing a positive perspective on mathematics and their mathematical abilities is deeply intertwined with understanding mathematical authority in a classroom. As we think about how to cultivate positive mathema tical mindsets among our students, in which they vi ew themselves as sources of mathematics knowledge, we must better understand students’ perceptions of themselves and others as sources of mathematical knowledge.
