# Learning with the Seasons: Lands and Waters-Based Temporality in Learning and Mobility

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The natural world is an active learning environment and provides a discursive context in which humans make meaning with one another. One way to experience collective meaning making in this learning environment is through group forest walks by walking, storying, and mapping lands and waters which Marin et al. (2020) refer to as ‘learning on-the-move.’ Water specifically creates a visual and sonic landscape where humans shift their attention and movement in response to it. This micro-ethnographic study uses video-based interaction analysis to understand the moment-to-moment unfolding of learning by paying attention to the semiotic layering of talk and movement while on forest walks during monsoon, winter, and summer seasons. The objective of this study is to explore how water shapes learning processes and learning environments as participants attend to relations with water through shifts in their movements, bodies, and conversation in relation to water while on forest walks. Broader implications of this study include using Indigenous ways of knowing to resist erasure and respond to increasing privatization of traditional lands and waters in the Global South. Project background Water mediates and organizes human relationships to the natural world, teaching us to attune to the sights, sounds, and seasonal rhythms of nature. It is also an active subject in organizing human attention and mobility, creating opportunities for humans to follow its sound in search for it, developing sensorimotor skills to swim, float, or traverse it. Knowing about water, however, is different from water-based ways of knowing. Indigenous communities have long relied on water-based ways of knowing about the world, developing kinship with waters and water life, and adapting to change as relationships to water change through the seasons. Alongside these longstanding practices are also issues of climate-change, environmental (in)justice, and increasing privatization of the lands and waters that Indigenous peoples call their home. I do not imagine the natural world to be a pristine, uncontested concept, but recognize that it is saturated with ongoing political struggle, colonial pasts and presents, and are sites where Indigenous futures are made. This dissertation examines how water-based ways of knowing organize human attention, coordination, and mobility, and contribute to resilient Indigenous communities, helping communities adapt to the seasonal rhythms of nature, especially in the context of ongoing dispossession of traditional lands and privatization of forests for industrialization. Participants of this ethnographic study are multigenerational members of the Siddi and Halakki communities, two Indigenous communities that share space in the Uttara Kannada region of southern India where public forests are becoming increasingly privatized and local paper and cardboard processing mills are polluting rivers and waterways. Amidst this violence to their environment, this study asks how learning with water can be a liberatory pedagogical tool, helping communities re-member relationships with lands and waters that are attempted to be dismembered by neoliberal development. This dissertation examines how water-based ways of knowing organize human attention, coordination, and mobility, and contribute to resilient Indigenous communities, helping communities adapt to the seasonal rhythms of nature, especially in the context of ongoing dispossession of traditional lands and privatization of forests for industrialization. Participants of this ethnographic study are multigenerational members of the Siddi and Halakki communities, two Indigenous communities that share space in the Uttara Kannada region of southern India where public forests are becoming increasingly privatized and local paper and cardboard processing mills are polluting rivers and waterways. Amidst this violence to their environment, this study asks how learning with water can be a liberatory pedagogical tool, helping communities re-member relationships with lands and waters that are attempted to be dismembered by neoliberal development. Goals of the research Mapping, storying, and walking with land and water are longstanding Indigenous activities, shaping understandings of water through sensory experience and the production of land-and water-based artifacts. Forest walks are a focal activity in this study during which Siddi and Halakki members walk through land, interact with the sights and sounds of water, and build meaning by conversing with one another, centering land in their restorying. These forest walks bring together multigenerational participation in cultural and linguistic practices, connecting the often-separated worlds of adults and children (Rogoff, 2003). As such, a variety of ages are represented in Siddi and Halakki group forest walks.

Specific research questions during these activities include:

1. How does water organize attention, storying, and mobility for learning?

2. How do Siddi and Halakki members on forest walks learn to “read” water (Bruce et al., 2023) while on-the-move? What does water-based literacy and reading look like?

3. How do lands and waters guide human attention in multiple and simultaneous ways (Rogoff & Correa-Chavez, 2005) while on forest walks?

M ethodology The goal of my project is to methodologically unsettle normative time-space ontologies and human-nature divides (Bang & Marin, 2015) in the learning sciences. This requires attending to onto-epistemic heterogeneity (Warren et al., 2020) and recognizing Indigenous ways of knowing (Smith, 2012). Research methods are powerful when they can foreground ways of knowing and making-meaning about the world that are often rendered invisible. This dissertation uses participant-generated video-based ethnography using interaction analysis traditions (Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Derry et al., 2010), viewing humans and water as co-participants in an activity. Although any person may choose to document their forest walk, this study focuses on subjects age 7 or above. This is to ensure that each participant has adequate ability to walk, operate a camera, and converse with other subjects. Walking through land presents opportunities for water to organize human attention and guide movement across multiple sensory dimensions. “Talk, gesture, and movement form a layering of semiotic resources that provide opportunities for the transformation of action and learning” (Goodwin, 2013; Melander, 2012). Forest walks offer an opportunity to observe how people relate to one another, make relations with the natural world, move through the world with their bodies, and organize attention while jointly participating in an activity (Marin & Bang, 2018; Marin, 2020). I do this over several seasons to understand how individuals change through time while practices endure through time (Esmonde, 2016). The seasons and seasonality are Indigenous, land-based forms of temporality, allowing us to understand humandevelopment along non-linear temporal scales. Together, forest walks as a research methodology offer us a glimpse into the way that relationality, place, and movement shape humandevelopment over several seasons.

## Data corpus and preliminary findings

My fieldwork is taking place over a period of 9 months, split into three rounds of data collection and organized by the seasons of the Uttara Kannada region. During each round, I have collected video data of forest walks and have generated weekly fieldnotes from daily jottings, comprising one complete ‘set’ of data. As I have already begun my fieldwork, I am completing the forest round of data collection from Nov 2025 to Feb 2026, comprising the winter season with eight sets of data. My second round of data collection will be the summer season from April 2026 to June 2026 where I anticipate collecting eight sets of data. The final season will be the monsoon from July 2026 to September 2026 where I anticipate collecting eight more sets of data, totaling 24 sets of data for saturation.

Videos from forest walks are content-logged and transcribed three-dimensionally (Mondada, 2016; Rymes, 2015) to include human action, speech, and the sonic landscape with sights and sounds of the natural world such as water, birds, wind, and trees. Birds chirping, the wind blowing, leaves ruffling, and water flowing are all ways that nature provides a discursive context for meaning making (Ingold, 2007, 2010; Ingold & Vergunst, 2008). Codes generated from transcript analysis were raised into categories and themes using a grounded theory approach (Charmaz, 2001).

A summary of important findings thus far includes: (1) Water plays a central role in organizing sensemaking as participants used water-forms such as streams and lakes to make sense of other life such as deer frequenting a place. Furthermore, water was used to make sense of social structures such as caste and social history of place as participants inquired about the unequal access to water and water-infrastructure for activities such as farming, which is predominantly controlled by elite/upper-caste landowners. (2) Water organizes movement as participants make bodily movements in relation to the sound of water while on forest walks. Expected contributions Walking and storying the land on repeated forest walks offer new opportunities for encounter and making relations with land and water (Marin & Bang, 2018) and demonstrate how water shapes learning environments by directing the attention and movement of people through land. However, such opportunities for place and sense-making cannot exist in isolation from histories of oppression and violence such as private property, race, and in South Asian contexts, caste. Designing forest walks as a learning activity by attending to place stories creates “an opening to grappling with the ethical potentialities of plural more-than-human worldings where both the human and more-than-human shape and are shaped by political, economic, and cultural forces.’ (Kirskey & Helmreich, 2010; p. 545; Nxumalo, 2016, p. 644). My work with the Siddi and Halakki communities not only includes questions about how the natural world organizes the attention and movement of learners on forest walks, but how knowledge about the history of the land and its people can make these walks a politically liberatory act. In this way, place is not a neutral landscape or a blank slate on which relationships are made but an event (Massey, 2005) that has many stories in the making.
