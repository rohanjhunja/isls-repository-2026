# How Story Interpretation + Contextualization Created Possibilities for Collaborative Inquiry with Young Children in Families

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We partner with young children + caregiver + university team members to conduct co-observation of family learning. We examine how interpretation and contextualization of families’ stories served to invite uni team members into collaborative inquiry about phenomena of interest to children. We ask, what does it look like to engage in family storying in support of collaborative inquiry with young children and caregivers as members of a research team? We analyze an instance of storying leading to inquiry to examine how understanding collaborative inquiry informs prior representations of inquiry as a members’ phenomenon. We reflect on the data and co-observation methods as particularly valuable for young children (often represented in pejorative ways) and COVID-cautious families (an emergent, marginalized community).

## Introduction

Families are a privileged site for learning because of the extensive time spent together (Goodwin, 2007) especially before children start schooling. We partner with fa milies to develop emergent syncretic methods drawin g on multiple research traditions and families’ practices (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016) to de velop ecological valid representations of learning contextualized by famil ies’ understandings of their cultural practices. Ou r research team consists of children (ages 3-5yrs), caregivers, and university (uni) members. Th e present analysis seeks to understand how members of our team collaborated to interpret and contextualize stories in ways that led to shared inquiry. This collaborative process supported resea rch teams (child + caregiver + uni) to engage in co llaborative inquiry as a members’ phenomenon (Keifert & Stevens, 2019). We ask: What does it look like to engage in family storying in support of collaborative inquiry with young children and caregivers as members of a research team? Framework Families are an important site of intergenerational learning, being, and doing (Vossoughi & Booker, 2020). Within families, children develop their repertoires of pra ctice even as they engage in and (re)shape family c ulture (e.g., Keifert, 2021). Yet recent  COVID-era representations of families position them as si tes of learning-loss (e.g., Callimachi, 2021). While deficit narratives harm al l families, they are particularly harmful to famili es from marginalized communities who are already framed by deficit narratives. Furthermore, there is an emergi ng community of COVID-cautious people in the U.S. who continue to engage in social distancing, masking, and other preventative measures. How researchers come t o understand the lived experience of these children  and families will be critical for how this group of humans is positioned within society in decades to come. We set out to examine children’s lived experience (Rogoff et al., 2018) in ways that center families’ existing storying practices —documenting, reflecting, theorizing (Ochs et al., 1 992). In the present analysis, we focus on interpreting—explain or tell the meaning of something—and contextualizing—place something within histories of activity—as necessary components of storying. Stories also often arose as a means of contextualizing ongoing activity. These stories provide context in interaction while also providing insight into families histories, presents, and futures (Keifert, 2021). In the data excerpt we share, we moved with one family from sto rying to inquiry. We take up a definition of inquiry as a me mbers’ phenomenon (IMP): “moments during which participants orient to a phenomenon as a shared puzzle and explore that phenomenon by drawing on sensemaking resources that they deem relevant to the point of t heir own satisfaction” (Keifert & Stevens, 2019, p.  265). However, unlike the original development of IMP, ou r inquiry was not merely observed by uni researcher s, but collaborative constructed by children, caregiver, a nd uni members of the research team. Thus, we name this coinquiry. We have conceptualized interpretation, con textualization, and co-inquiry to prepare the reader to understand how these practices might inform innovative approaches to centering family + uni practice as a context for the study of learning in families.

## Methods

Our research-partnerships with families support developing anti-deficit narratives about intergenerational learning, being, and doing (Vossoughi & Booker, 2020) in COVID-cautious families. Partnering with children to “tell their story on their terms” (Gutiérrez et al., 2019, p. 9) supports ethically valid research att ending to the distribution of research materials, resources, and activities to produce ecologically valid representa tions of learning embedded in participants’ understanding of  their family culture. We, the Learning, Being and Doing (LBD) team, are developing co-observational methods to study existing practices i n collaboration with participants.

We recruited via social media offering opportunitie s for self-identified COVID-cautious families (children 3-5yrs, at least one caregiver 18+), to engage in col laborative research about learning in families. Interested families completed a preliminary surv ey that allowed the uni team members to select fami lies with differences in racial/ethnic, disability, education, and socioeconomic experience. After consenting, c hild + caregiver + uni team members began zoom playdate se ssions directed by children (e.g., watching lego pl ay, showing toys, drawing, playing a game), followed show-and-tell sessions (e.g., children asked uni team members to prepare stories, makings such as cardboard anima ls or campsites), and finally co-examination of video and image records. D ata come from the Wonderland family (pseudonyms cho sen by families) who had participated in the study over 16 months at the time of submissi on. Participants in Wonderland: Bunny (4-5yrs), Carrot (3-4yrs), and Flamingo (Mom). Data are video-recorded Zoom sessions, Zoom auto-generated transcripts, live logs (content logs generated during playdates and visibl e to all team members in the chat), and artifacts (e.g., art, photos, videos) created by team members. In our analysis, we looked for moments that families initiated storying that led to co-inquiry. We present one sequence. After selecting t his episode, we (re)viewed all data (video, transcripts, Live Logs) and engaged in Interaction Analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995)—sequential analysis of turns to develop an understanding of how interpretation and contextualization arose in family co-storying leading to possibilities for whole team inquiry.

## Discussion

The above analysis illustrates how spaces of [uni | family | hybrid ] shaped collaborative inquiry. We emphasize this hybrid space because it illustrates (a) how fa milies are creating the contexts for shared understandings of stories/experience that allow  for (b) collaborative inquiry within the hybrid spa ce. Shared understandings were created through families sharing stories. Telling stories served to contextualize prior family experience and related ideas (e.g., bear behaviors, danger, human-bear relations). Families shared their experiences to the uni-team, while opening up possibilities for collaborative exploration of related ideas. Through this, Littles’ and Uni’s experiences were pulled into a shared context, creating a hybrid space of understanding shaped by the whole research team. Our collaborative inquiry was many things: joyful, imaginative, and perhaps most of all, child-centric. Children led orienting to phenomena as something wo rthy of exploration (e.g., Bunny asking about bears). Like previous work on inquiry as a members’ phenomenon w ith children, affective dimensions of our inquiry r anged from silly to playful to concerned even within a si ngle sequence of inquiry. Co-inquiry was made possible by families storying and it was actively shaped by the  experiences and related ideas  shared in storying. Yet inquiry in these moments included our uni team members as equal collaborators, unlike prior illustrations of IMP. Having examples of children’s inquiry within this hybrid space changed the nature of available data. We (as uni learning scientists) were not fly-on-the wall observers watching “participants” (childre n and caregivers) engage in inquiry. We were collaborators as members of a child + caregiver + uni research team studying learning together. We propose that this new form of endogene ity—collaboratively constructed within hybrid practices  of family + uni research teams —continues to represent storying and inquiry  “from within” through collaboratively establishing “what inquiry is about from the perspe ctive of those engaged in inquiry” both through sha red interaction and in our shared reflection (analysis)  on interactions (Keifert & Stevens, 2019, p. 243).  Our inquiry is not something uni team members manage, but rather something we as a team co-construct and reflect upon. These perspectives have been affirmed by families. Bunny and Carrot expressed an understanding of our time together as opportunities to show valued thing s. Flamingo characterized our work together not as “experts studying families” but instead “all hands on deck” as a collaborative effort to understand “how learni ng is intertwined with everything you do as a family.” Th e Wonderland+Uni team positioned our work as attend ing seriously to children’s ideas and learning together as a collaborative effort. Within our hybrid space of co-inquiry, the interests of Littles were drawn on by adults in  ways that allowed uni-experiences and family-experiences together to shape the development of activity and co-constructed understandings. This work is of utmost importance in research, part icularly for COVID-cautious families with children experiencing different developmental pathways in relation to school due to precautions (e.g., delayed school start). Furthermore, there remains a need to avoid common p ejorative representations of young children in LS r esearch and the broader fields of education and child-focused studies, particularly for very young children (Keifert, 2021). Co-observation is one method for addressing these issues in collaborative, ethical relations.
