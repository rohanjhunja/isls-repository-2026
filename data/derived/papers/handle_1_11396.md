# A Healing Research Interface: Coming Together to Create Indigenous Learning Experiences Through Community-Driven Design Research

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Scholars who employ humanizing research methodologies are also tasked with the labor of developing infrastructure and resources to support the mutually beneficial and reciprocal partnerships required. In response, we share our process of building a “research interface” that invites participation from community partners and centers community needs. We present our year-long partnership-building process guided by the research question: what are effective practices in conducting community-driven design research among partnerships with researchers, designers, educators, and Tribal Elders? Through a critical ethnographic approach, we identified practices that served as guides in our partnership-building process. Insights from our findings contribute to current understandings for how community-driven design research practices support humanizing and healing approaches to research.

Objectives As scholars and researchers work toward humanizing and healing research methodologies (e.g., Archibald  et al., 2019; Paris & Winn, 2013; Wilson, 2008), partnershi p-and community-based approaches to research have continued to flourish (e.g., Bang et al, 2016; Conr ad, 2023). From a Western research approach, this m ovement toward community-driven research requires a deliberate shift away fr om what institutions demand toward what communities need. Scholars who employ humanizing re search methodologies are also tasked with the labor  of developing infrastructure and resources to support the mutually beneficial and reciprocal partnerships  required (Brant et al., 2023). One persistent challenge in carrying out partnership-and community-based approaches is that the majority of these efforts operate from the assu mption that science, and thus, research, are acultu ral, which presents deep epistemological, ontological, axiological tensions (Bang & Medin, 2010; Butler and Menzies, 2007; Johnson, 2018). Not only does this ignore the cultural, historical, and political roots of research, but it also further marginalizes historically minoritized groups, such as Indigenous peoples, who take an ecological approach to research and recognize it as inextricable from culture (Aikenhead, 1997; Bang & Medin, 2010; Butler & Menzies, 2007). As researchers seek to work with communities, the work itself makes visible these si gnificant tensions, especially as much of this work is often carried ou t among or in partnership with academic institution s that privilege Western ways of knowing and being, which are not values shared by the very communities with whom scholars seek to partner (Datta, 2018). Hence, this  systemic disorientation demands a “interface” (Ryd er et al.,

2020) and spotlights a  need to align existing research practices with the epistemologies (knowing), ontologies

(doing), and axiologies (being) of the communities with whom scholars seek to partner (Hayward et al.,  2021; Ryder et al., 2020).

In this paper, we share our process of building a new “research interface” (Ryder et al., 2020) that invites participation from Tribal Elders, community members, and educators. We are guided by the research ques tion: what are effective practices in conducting communit y-driven design research among partnerships with researchers, designers, educators, and Tribal Elders? We specifically focus on the practices we built together to provide a foundation for doing research that centers the particular Knowledge Holder’s or community’s goals and desires. As a multicultural team of scholars and co mmunity members, we draw on a year of data that doc uments our coming together and creating together. Along th is journey, we engaged the rhythm of a critical eth nography to understand how we might conduct r esearch in ways that center the needs of the commun ities with whom we collaborate. Through ongoing collective reflections, we identified three practices (developing a share d identity, employing transparent research principles, and bein g and creating together) that served as guides to s upport our coming together to create learning experiences for community and classroom education. Insights from ou r findings make contributions to the growing scholarship on partnership-and community-based research through identifying responsive and responsible practices to support this work.

## Theoretical framework: Community research methodologies

Scholars persistently argue that community-centered research methods are critical approaches to enact community values and to serve the communities with whom we pa rtner (e.g., Bang et al, 2016; Conrad, 2023; Coburn  & Penuel, 2016; Drawson et al., 2017). We are heavily shaped by this scholarship and wisdom that came before us, especially community-based design research (CBDR; Bang et al., 2016) and  critical indigenous research methodologies (CIRM; Brayboy et al., 2012). Specifi cally, CBDR is “design efforts that work from within the “ongoingness” of communities” (Bang et al., 2016, p. 11) and is characterized axiological innovations that occur as a result of three interrelated design commitment s: critical historicity, intergenerational learning, and transforming institutional relations (Bang et al., 2016). CI RM realizes research as “emancipatory project roote d in relationships and is driven explicitly by commun ity interests” (Brayboy et al., 2012, p. 448). This methodological orientation recognizes “knowledge is sacred and to be entrusted with it carries great responsibility, thus adding a seriousness to subsequent decisions r esearchers make in terms of how and when to ask for information and how and when to share the knowledge  with which they have been entrust ed” (Brayboy et al., 2012, p. 448). As we seek to honor tribal sovereign ty and self-determination through our enactment of our community-driven approach, we draw on the practice of disruption (Co nrad, 2023, p. 260) of colonial research infrastructures that shape what is possible in our work. Part of this di sruption requires “weaving a methodology for ‘research at the interface’” (Ryder et al., 202 0. P. 7). This approach indicates the effort and la bor required to build an interface, including infrastructure and re sources, that weaves together Western and Indigenou s ways of knowing, doing, and being in ways that support partnerships.

## Methods/data

Our methodological orientation recognizes the histo rical, cultural, and political nature of partnering  with Indigenous communities. Specifically, our community-driven design research process is heavily shaped by TribalCrit Theory’s (Brayboy, 2005) three key princ iples for partnering with tribal communities: respe ct and reciprocity in relationships, self-determination, and sovereignty. As these principles shape our attitudes and efforts as researchers, we constantly ask what is meaningfu l to the communities with whom we work (Hayword et al., 2021). In this paper, we present our learnings from  a yearlong partnership-building process that included three university researchers, a team of two Title VI Educators, a team of two Tribal Elders & Educators, a team of two K-12 Educators, and a cultural technologist. Specific ally, we trace our partnership building process, wh ich included four in-person team meetings, three one-hour team check-ins on Zoom, and ongoing communication (emails, phone calls, text messages) between meetin gs. In-person team meetings ranged in length from two full working days (one meeting) to full day meetings (two meetings) to half-day meetings (one meeting). Our meetings are shaped by the rhythm of Gathering (Litts, 2023), which is a design process that supports va lue-centered, transparent, and generative shared work.

We employed a critical ethnographic approach (Madis on, 2011), which establishes an ethical responsibility to contribute to “life sustaining kn owledges and resources of restorative justice” (Mad ison, 2011). In particular, we captured our process through a ra nge of qualitative data documenting our process inc luding: graphical recordings that visually captured our collective meaning-making process, meeting notes that were collaboratively developed during and after our meetings, design work that we created together, and regular critical reflective discussions that serve as points of sensemaking and analysis. W e utilized a collaborative and reflexive meaning-making analytic approach to identify insights by it eratively triangulating (Creswell & Poth, 2018) interpretations and claims across partners, perspectives, and documentation. As we engaged this cyclical analytic process, we identified three practices that grounded our community-driven approach.

## Findings

Through coming together, being together, and creating together, we identified three practices that served as guides in our partnership-building process. It is important for us to also note that this process is situationally created and uniquely shaped by the communities with whom we work. We share practices here that support our own enactment of the values of community-centered work and we recognize that these are fundamentally shaped by the particular time and place of our work.

Developing a shared identity: What do we want to invite people to? Over the course of the first year, especially in the first six months of coming together, we focused our Gatherings on identifying shared goals, values, and enactments of those values (see Table 1). It also made visible two shared  goals to guide our work: (1) Sustain culture and s trengthen kinship with Native youth, their families, and thei r communities, and (2) Create learning experiences shared by Indigenous peoples/communities that promote kinship, educate the public, and inform policy. Employing transparent research principles: How do we work? A core practice in our community-driven process, particularly for the university res earchers, continues to be for us to employ transparent research principles. Since before our initial Gathering, the university researchers on the team have explored ways to make our research transparent and accessible to all members of our team. An ongoing illustration of transparency in our research proces s is evident in the graphical recordings shared abo ve. We use these visualizations as one tool to reflect back to the broader team how we are understanding the shared work we are doing. In our in-person meetings, we keep space to reflexively consi der these representations and consider how they capture our shared sense making. We unders tand these as a collective resistance t o hierarchical educational partnerships. Another illustration of t ransparency in our research process is adopting par ticular principles around the use of technology (Table 2), because introducing technology can often create distance and/or blackbox certain features of the research and design process (Morgan et al., 2024). As we continue to come together, we have begun to r ecognize the healing nature of being with others who share goals and values, especially for those of us in env ironments where we are typically surrounded by resi stance to the work we are doing. Even more, creating with those enacting the values we claim to share (see Figure 2). Much of what characterizes Western research methodologie s further disenfranchises and causes harm to Indige nous communities through explicit and implicit dismissal s of cultural sover eignty (Coffey & Tsosie, 2001), technological sovereignty (Winter & Boudreau, 2018), and rhetorical sovereignty (Lyons, 2000). Through engaging in a community-driven process, we move toward recognizing the sovereignty that has existed since time immemorial. This move toward honoring sovereignty i n research partnerships, which are spaces that have historically caused trauma and harm, instead allow for a process of healing relationships with others, communities, culture, technology, and story.

To date, we have created three learning experiences  across the three communities participating in our larger collaborative. Each of these learning experiences (a three-day curricular unit in classrooms, a month-long summer camp with Native youth, and a multi-week, hybrid art workshop with a Tribal community) are enactments of our shared values. As we move toward creating together and commit to enacting our values, we have found he aling individually and collectively in how we show up for each other. In a sense, we have noticed a reciprocity between creating together and healing, because as we create, we enact our shared values. It is in the enactment that we find healing.

## Significance

We join ongoing efforts to center community needs in research through exploring what we call community-driven design research. This paper both describes this approach to resear ch and provides important nuance through our coming together, being together, and creating together. The three practices we share in this paper are meant to be illustrative tools to guide researchers and c ommunities in how to come together to work in commu nity-driven rhythm. The insights we share not only contribute m ethodologically to partnership-and community-based approaches, but also contribute insights to designi ng with communities, particularly Indigenous commun ities, more broadly.
