# “Research is Like a Wild Animal”: What Do Student-Generated Metaphors Reveal about Their Understanding of Research?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines high school students’ epistemic cognition about research in connection with their research experiences. We analyzed the metaphors about research generated by a class of 12th graders, who participated in community-based research with professional organizations. Students generated metaphors to reflect on the nature of research using the prompt “research is like... because..." Qualitative analysis of student-generated metaphors revealed their epistemic notions of research as an ever-deepening process with uncertainty. Students’ metaphorical embodiments of research make abstract epistemic notions tangible, shaping their ongoing engagement in research work.

## Introduction

Living in a rapidly changing world facing complex challenges, students must learn to work as knowledge builders and producers who candeal with an open world of information to solve challenging problems (Pendleton-Jullian & Brown, 2018; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014). The ability of knowledge processing and creation is even more essential in the age of  generative AI that transforms the ecology of inform ation. It is imperative to  cultivate a productive skillset and mindset of research among all students. Students need to be literate about what research is (and is not)  and how research is advanced, used and evaluated by various social actors to meet various needs. Therefore, we propose the concept of “research lite racy” as an educational goal in K-12 and beyond. We define research literacy as epistemic, social, and cultural practice s by which an individual or community comes to understand, critique, conduct,  and communicate research works in various disciplinary and social settings. It involves epistemic understandings of what research means and how it works, social and cognitive engagement in research processes to address knowledge gaps and solve problems in profes sional and personal settings, and discursive practices to communicate research with various audiences and contribute to the larger social discourse. Core research literacy may give students domain-general “ habits of mind,” such as to problematize, explore promising ideas, examine evidence and sources, pers evere through progressive (iterative) problem solvi ng, and approach diverse ideas through dialogues  (Gardner, 2007; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 20 14). More specialized research literacy is needed for students to advance knowledge in a disciplinary field, integrating domain-specific conceptual tools, epistemic processes, and norms. While various programs exist to engage college students in research experiences (Bangera & Brownell, 2014; Eagan et al., 2013; Linn et al., 2015), the field  faces a critical gap in integrating authentic research in K-12 education (Sadler et al., 2010). Particularly, designs of authentic research need to foster students’ epistemic agency for charting the course of research, grappling with uncertainty about what to research, how, and toward what outcomes (Zhang et al., 2018; Manz, 2025). The current study examines students’ understanding of research at a high school that integrates authentic research experiences, focusing on students’ epi stemic cognition about research practices. Epistemic cognition refers to peoples’ beliefs, theories (mental models), and dispositions related to knowledge, knowing, and researching (Barzilai & Chinn, 2018, 2024; Chinn & Sandoval, 2018). Building on Barzilai and Chinn (2024), we propose that students’ epistemic cognition about research may include how people understand the aims and values of research in particular settings, the criteria  (ideals) used to evaluate and benchmark research outputs, and epistemic processes used to design and carry out research and create knowledge products  in light of the criteria. While extensive research has investigated students’ epistemological beliefs about knowledge and learning (Chinn & Sandoval, 2018; Schommer et al., 1997), researchers have rarely examined students’ epistemic cognition about research in K-12 settings. In higher education, several studies have looked at how college/graduate students and faculty understand the nature and methods of research (Åkerlind, 2008; Brew, 2001; Feng et al., 2024; Meyer et al., 2005). Without using the concept of epistemic cognition, t he findings revealed epistemic  understandings of research: solving practical problems; uncovering of what has been hidden through ‘re-search’ or reinterpretation; building theories; validating sources and viewpoints; making an impact on a research field or the society; or leading tonew ways of seeing the world, a problem or oneself. As a unique lens, this study uses metaphor analysis (Wohlmann, 2019) to understand students’ epistemic notions of research, which are often tacit and hard to verbalize. In cognitive linguistics, a metaphor is defined as an analogy that allows a person or community to map one experience in the terminology of another experience so as to understand a complex topic or a new situation  (Moser, 2000). A metaphor involves projecting the features of a source domain (schemata) onto a target domain (Lakoff & Johnson, 1980). Metaphors help people reflect on and communicate complex topics and further influenc e people’s interpretation of experiences and planni ng of actions. Compared to propositional statements, metaphors sup port more holistic representation s of personal understandings and tacit knowledge. From a sociocul tural view, the socially shared use of metaphors helps a community reflect on its social practices, develop mutual understandings, and build  shared experiences  and expectations that organize people’s interaction  (Moser, 2000). Researchers often use a variety of metaphors to describe their work  (Pitcher, 2013), such as wrestling and pushing forward. Young students use metaphors to describe their research process too, referring research as like navigating a journey or digging a rabbit hole (Zhang et al., 2018, 2024). Such metaphors may serve as “metaphorical embodiment” of research experience, which uses visual perceptions, motor actions, and mental image s as source frames to represent abstract conceptual thoughts about research. Metaphorical embodiment helps make abstract conceptual thoughts tangible. When processing a conceptual idea based on a metaphor, the same neural and bodily responses involved in motor control are recruited or activated (Lakoff, 2012). Arguably, students’ metaphorical embodiment of research may play an important role in supporting epistemic cognition and shaping their personal involvement in research-based settings. In this study, we are interested in understanding the various metaphors that students use to describe how research works in connection with their actual research experiences. Our research questions ask: What do studentgenerated metaphors reveal about their epistemic understandin gs of research, such as the aims, processes, and criteria? And how are students’ notions of research related to their research engagement and experiences?

## Participants and classroom contexts

This research took place at T School located in Nor theast U.S. T School is a four-year public high school that enrolls students from different school districts in  the region. This school embraces innovative, authe ntic, and student-centered learning as its vision. In the 12th grade, students participated in a culminating research experience: the Innovative Solutions project. This project was  co-facilitated by three teachers, who specialized in English, arts/business alliance, and special education, resp ectively. Our research team has been co-designing this project with the teachers  (Ding et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024), integrating the principles of Knowledge Building that emphasize epistemic agency for continual idea improvement in a collaborative community (Scardamalia, 2002). In October of each year, a dozen community partners came to meet with studen ts to present authentic challenges or problems that the ir organizations needed to address. Students formed te ams based on their interests in the challenges. Each team worked with a community partner to carry out research and generat e solutions over a tenweek period. Students reflected on metacognitive issues about research as they carried out specific research activities. As the final product, each team wrote a research report and presented their research works and solutions at a public event. The current study  was conducted based on the data collected in third iteration of this design-based research project. A total of 36 students worked with 11 community org anizations on authentic challenges in various social sectors (e.g., semiconductors, public media, entrepreneurship). Data sources and analysis Around the beginning of their research, a classroom discussion was facilitated by Mrs. M to discuss what it means to do research and how students might kick off their research process.  Students first used a reflection sheet to write down their understandings of  research. The prompts for reflection included: (a) What does it mean to d o research? How would you complete the following sent ence:  "Research is like... because..."? (b) What  is your normal process for beginning research? Students then participated in a face-to-face discussion  to share their personal reflections. As the students individually shared their metaphors with the class, a student volunteer wrote down the key phrases on a whiteboard. Mrs. M occasionally asked students to e xplain what they meant by the metaphors.  A researcher observed and audio-recorded the discussion. We analyzed the student-generated metaphors to understand their notions of  research and further drew connections between students’ metaphors and their research  experiences recorded in their reflection sheet and through our subsequent observation s of their research projects. Following procedures of  metaphor analysis (Wohlmann, 2019), two coders first identified metaphors recorded in the students’ reflection sheets. A total of 26 metaphors were identified in student reflection  (e.g., “Research is like a wild animal because it is very unpredictable”). The two coders collaboratively coded each metaphor focusing on the source domains (e.g., wild animal), the target domain (research), and the mapping of the constituent elements that the source and target share and that are carried over (e.g., unpredictable). The constituent elements of the different metaphors  were then reviewed to identify theme-based clusters. Each theme highlights a  core feature of research revealed by the metaphors as related to epistemic aims, criteria, and processes (Barzilai & Chinn, 2024). We critically read and checked the themes against students’ reflection dat a, with a few themes rephrased for clarity and cons istency. Finally, we traced connections between students’ me taphors and their research experiences recorded in the reflection sheet. The metaphor analysis was interpreted in the contex t of whole Innovative Solutions project, which involved systematic tracing of students’ research processes and outcomes. A student interview was conducted at the end of the project to understand their research experiences.

## Findings

Our analysis revealed students’ epistemic conceptions about the nature of research and how it works. Five themes were identified from the metaphors related to the goals, ideals, and processes of research. Theme 1: Aims of research: Transform thinking and expand knowledge Three of the 26 metaphors explicitly focus on the g oal and results of research, which are about transf orming our thinking and expanding the edge of knowledge. S7’s metaphor considers that r esearch is like a key because it “opens up your mind to the unknown.” Along the same line, two other students also used metaphors to highlight that research transforms and grows our thinking, such as by saying “Research is like a mental expansion because your mind's database grows and changes every time y ou do it.” They recognize that the outcome of research not only increases the amount of knowledge but change how people think about things. Theme 2: Ideals of research: Achieve coherence and depth out of messy information Six of the metaphors convey a sense of the ideal end while dealing with an open and messy pool of information. By eventually finding and connecting relevant infor mation, people build a coherent  and deep  understanding. Regarding coherence, three students noted that research is like putting together puzzles or building Legos to find solutions. As S28 said, “Research is like a puzzle, you're given a bunch of random pieces and are expected to put them all together, sometimes you have to backtrack to find information.” The success of research is about fitting the pieces together as a coherent whole, and findin g the missing pieces as needed. S29’s metaphor further highlights depth as a criterion of knowledge: “Research is like piloting a submarine, you usually have the best

Theme 3: Process of research: Progressive inquiry of ever-deeper issues Aligned with the aim of research to achieve deep understand, six of the metaphors recognize research as an everdeeper inquiry process, progressively uncovering new unknowns. S4 said: “Research is like astronomy, once you see a little, you realize how much there is to disc over.”  On a similar note, S21 wrote: “Research is like cave jumping because the more you go down and the more you look the more you discover and the bigger it becomes.” His conceptualization of research was described in motor and visual terms of jumping in, going down, l ooking, and discovering new things while seeing more to research. Several other students also used action-based metaphor to describe the continual, ever-deeper process of research, such as: “Research is like deep cleaning something rather than just wash it off, because you’re forced to dig deeper to every nook n cranny.” Theme 4: Process of research: Persevere through uncertainty and hard work Eight student-generated metaphors bring forth the uncertain natur e of research, as the  process and results of research are both unpredictable. S18’s metaphor conveys a holistic feeling about the  uncertainty: “Research is like a wild animal because it is very unpredictable.” Two other students also talked about the uncertain process by saying that the research process is like a maze or a roller coaster. S5’s metaphor shows how to navigate the uncertain research process strategically and iteratively: “Research is like fishing, you cast out a line, see what you catch and either throw it back in or keep it.” The concrete actions, such as casting, catching, throwing away, and keeping show strategic acts of thinking that resear chers need to take to advance what they know. S14 u sed a metaphor from The Wizard of Oz to describe the uncertain process with a sense of perseverance toward the goal: “Research is like the walking on the yellow brick r oad. It's a long and winding process, and if you pe rsevere and follow it, you will reach the Emerald C ity.” With a sense of hope for the rewarding outcomes, students could persevere through zig-zags and overcome difficulties in the research process. Theme 5: Process of research: Productive skills, appropriate tools Students’ metaphors also emphasize productive uses of research skills and tools that match with the goal  of research. “Research is like hunting because you're looking for the best catch by using the proper tools.”  (S11) Students emphasized the need of research tools for finding information and collecting data. Another student drew a connection between research and his sport experie nce: “Research is like skiing because you have to learn how to do before you can make progress.”  Students highlighted few skills in their reflection  sheets, such as asking questions, assessing the credibility of sources, checking biases, and synthesizing main points. Student research experiences in relation to their notions of research Our analysis further traced connections between stu dents’ metaphors and their research experiences. They commented that their learning at T School, which em braced innovative and authentic learning, allowed t hem to gain rich research-based experience so research is more relatable to them. The ways they approached research are related to their notions of research. For example, S29, who em phasized depth through the submarine metaphor, approached information search as a progressive inquiry process: to find enough information from the initial search, then rephrase the search to find more information. “If I am very interested in a topic and/or am somew hat knowledgeable on it already, I might be enthusiastic to research it so I can learn more.” To deepen the whole picture view of student research notions and experiences, we further present a case study of two students—S7 and S14 —who have been working as a team i n the Innovative Solutions project. As noted earlier, S7 considered that research is like  a key to open the unknows, while S14 used the metaphor from The Wizard of Oz. In the project, S7 and S14 worked with the ir principal as the professional partner to envision the “2.0 version” of their school. They worked through a progressive inquiry process to identify specific issues they needed to address and collected key information needed for their search of potential solutions, so they could navigate “the yellow brick road to reach the Emerald City.” The two students met with the principal to clarify the goal and process of research, discussing critical questions such as: Who are the similar schools like ours out there? What are they doing? Where can we find such informa tion? They further conducted focus group interviews with peer students across grade levels to understand the ir challenges and experiences. Based on the data collected, S7 and S14 met with their principal to share a set of  problems identified and discuss ideas for possible solutions. Their ideas included  (a) peer mentoring across grade levels to help freshmen  adapt to an innovative school environment; (b) providing a flexible schedule on Fridays that would  allow students to pursue their passion and ideas or catch up with schoolwork based on individu al needs; and (c) enhancing personal accountability in team project grading by focusing on “how you did on your team instead of how your team did the work.” Their principal acknowledged these ideas as both innovative and useful and shared the ideas with the teachers to plan for change. In the final interview, S14 thought that their research was successful (reached the “Emerald City”) because it had led to innovative and useful ideas that might impact the future changes at their school.

## Discussion

This study examined high school students’ epistemic cognition about res earch in connection with their research experiences, which were  designed based on the principles of Knowledge Building pedag ogy (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014). The themes emerged from the metaphor analysis revealed students’ notions of research along several dimensions of epistemic cognition (Barzilai & Chinn, 2024). Their notions highlight the aim and outcome of research as about transforming human minds, figuring out unknows, and continually expanding knowledge (Theme 1). Their metaphors convey a sense of criteria (ideals) of knowledge based on coherence and depth, which emerge from continual work with messy information and diverse ideas (Theme 2). They recognize that the process of research is progressive, ever-deepening (Theme 3), uncertain and unpredictable (Theme 4). It requires students to embrace the uncertainty and make persistent efforts to uncover deeper unknows and discern emergent directions as their knowledge evolves. To make the research process reliable and fruitful, students need to develop solid research skills and make wise choices of research tools (Theme 5). The findings contribute to understanding student epistemic cognition about research-based, knowledge-building work, embracing  open-endedness, complexity and  uncertainty as epistemic norms. Current classroom practices, including many inquiry-based activities, often reflect little of the uncertainty that researchers grapple with in authentic research (Manz, 2025).  The students in this study demonstrated salient not ions about the uncertaint y in research and embraced uncertainty as part of their experience. These epis temic beliefs have possibly been enabled by their long-term immersion in an innovative, authentic learning environment with rich opportunities of reflecting on what they do. This study showcases metaphor analysis as a method for investigating student epistemic cognition. Thei r metaphorical embodiments of research make such abst ract epistemic notions tangible and actionable (Lakoff, 2012; Moser, 2000). Our ongoing analysis will trace how their epistemic  understandings of research shape their participation in research work with community partners. With a deeper understanding of student epistemic cognition of research, the field may develop productive visions and models for cultivating research literacy in K12 schools and beyond.  Students not only learn from the knowledge products  of past generations but become members of the knowledge-producing culture, connecting with more knowledgeab le members, learning their skills and tools, sharing their aims and norms of knowledge work.
