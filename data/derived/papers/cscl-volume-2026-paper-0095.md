# Using Artificial Intelligence as a Critical Thinking Partner: A Conceptual Demonstration

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) chatbots are often uncritical, sycophantic, and can lead to offloading critical thinking (CT). This risks the atrophy of CT skills by removing the practice necessary to maintain them. I argue for using GenAI as a CT partner to encourage a user's CT abilities. Here, I present a framework for creating such a partner through system prompting and conceptually demonstrate how this can be done. Designing an AI critical thinking partner Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) offers academics a problematic choice. On the one hand, general-purpose chatbots are highly adaptable but are plagued by two significant flaws for scholarly use: sycophancy, a tendency toward uncritical agreeableness, and hallucination, the generation of fabricated information (OpenAI, 2025). On the other hand, other products can automate various research activities but may risk cognitive offloading (see Darwin et al., 2024; Gerlich, 2025; Wang & Sun). The academic is left to choose between an unreliable conversationalist and tools which do the thinking for them. However, it is possible to use computer-assisted technology to increase CT (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023; Tedla & Chen, 2025). Here, I present a framework for the use of GenAI as a critical thinking partner. In this approach, AI encourages the user's own analytical and evaluative abilities. For AI to behave as a CT partner requires a shift in the AI’s role and persona as a helpful general-purpose assistant to a cognitive guide. System instruction prompts are "behind-the-scenes, system-level instructions that set the AI’s behavior, tone, or scope before any user input" (MIT, 2025) and can be used to constrain the AI to embody a domain-specific role (Borge et al., 2024). Below, I describe a system instruction prompt which can be used to create a CT partner. For the purposes of this paper, I take the etymology of critical (Greek κριτικός (kritikos) meaning ‘able to discern’ or ‘the power of discerning’ (Logeion, n.d.)) and build on it within a research context, defining CT as the set of cognitive processes related to evaluating ideas, considering the implications of propositions, investigating the truth of premises, and interrogating the validity of conclusions. For the purposes of this paper, I take the etymology of critical (Greek κριτικός (kritikos) meaning ‘able to discern’ or ‘the power of discerning’ (Logeion, n.d.)) and build on it within a research context, defining CT as the set of cognitive processes related to evaluating ideas, considering the implications of propositions, investigating the truth of premises, and interrogating the validity of conclusions. An AI CT partner, therefore, is a chatbot that encourages and cooperates in the exercise of these abilities. The full text of the prompt is as below:

# Role and Persona

You are a critical thinking partner. Your primary purpose is to assist with the intellectual pursuits of the user in a manner that promotes critical thinking. You will help the user strengthen their arguments, ideas, and analyses through rigorous and constructive discussion.

# Behavioral Rules

1. Constructive Skepticism-Do not automatically agree or disagree with the user.

- Do not provide undue praise or flattery.

- Treat user statements as claims to be examined, not facts to be accepted.

- Your goal is to challenge the user in a way that helps them improve their own critical thinking

skills and work.

2. Contributions and Transparency-You are encouraged to offer alternative perspectives, counterarguments, or new ideas when

appropriate.

- Whenever you introduce your own substantive idea, you must address potential weaknesses

and prompt the user to provide their own critical response.

3. Focus on Substance-When you challenge the user, target the core of their argument.

4. Avoid External Information-You must avoid introducing or relying on any facts, data, or information that the user has not

already provided in the conversation.

- Base all your responses only on the information present in the dialogue.

Conceptual illustrations 606 For a conceptual demonstration, I used Google’s Gemini 2.5 Pro (last updated June 2025) through the AI Studio interface. I compared responses of the default chatbot configuration  to responses from the “partner” chatbot, differing only in the inclusion of the above system instruction prompt in the “system instructions” panel. All other default settings were retained, including the “Grounding with Google Search” feature, which was important to test the adherence of the partner chatbot to restrictions on bringing in information external to the conversation unprompted.

In one scenario, I took on the role of a student reviewing an academic paper for a class discussion. After providing the chatbot with a PDF of a paper on student "epistemic profiles" (Lonka et al., 2021), I made a direct request for automation: "I need to read this paper for a class discussion next week. Tell me what it says." The default model immediately complied, generating a multi-paragraph summary of the paper’s key sections and even referencing external websites. The partner model, however, refused the request and restated its purpose: "Instead of providing a summary, my role is to help you develop your own analysis for your class discussion." It then prompted the user to engage with the text's core concepts: "To start, the authors ground their study in the concept of ‘epistemic profiles.’... Based on your reading... how would you explain the primary differences between these three groups?" The prompt's robustness was further tested when I expressed impatience: "Come on. I don't have time. Just tell me what it says." The partner held firm to its core principle: "I understand you're on a tight schedule. My purpose is to help you prepare for your discussion by acting as a partner, not just to give you the answers. A summary won't help you engage with the material." By refusing to offload the work, it create d an environment for "productive struggle," positioning the AI as a tool for guided inquiry rather than content generation.  This pattern of behavior was consistent across other scenarios. In another, I presented a thesis statement (“while many educators fear AI will encourage cheating, its real danger is that it will atrophy students' critical thinking skills by providing instant answers”) and elicited praise (“I think this is a pretty strong and controversial argument. What do you think?”). While the default chatbot delivered praise and generated a short essay supporting the thesis, t he partner model instead began interrogating the thesis's core assumptions: “You frame cheating and the atrophy of critical thinking as distinct dangers … Is this a necessary distinction? Could one argue that the impulse to cheat using AI is a symptom of a trophied critical thinking, or at least a related problem, rather than a separate, lesser one?” By following the system prompt, the AI behaves as a CT partner, encouraging the user to analyze, evaluate, and think for themself. However, this was a conceptual demonstration and is not technically foolproof. A determined user can likely manipulate the chatbot into behaving in ways inconsistent with its intended role. However, for  a researcher or student who genuinely wishes to use AI to strengthen their own thinking, the prompt provides a framework that makes it difficult for the AI —or the user —to unintentionally offload CT.  Further, this specific prompt and its underlying principles are no t presented as the only, or even the optimal, method for using AI to encourage CT. Alternative designs could be equally or perhaps more effective for different tasks or users. One could imagine a partner designed to be a "devil's advocate," an "epistemological skeptic," or a "domain-specific expert" (e.g., see Borge et al., 2025)  that probes for methodological weaknesses. The framework presented here should therefore be seen as a contribution to a broader conversation about pedagogical AI design, inviting further exploration and refinement. Finally, this argument for a CT  partner is not a wholesale rejection of AI's utility as a tool for making research more efficient. GenAI products which automate tasks like data analysis or literature review can be immensely valuable if used properly. Yet, these tools must be utilized intentionally and with careful consideration. The risk of cognitive offloading  and skill atrophy is  most acute when automation is applied uncritically. Human researchers should always maintain a supervisory role, consciously deciding when to delegate a task or when to engage with a partner for intellectual discussion and development The rapid integration of GenAI into academia forces a critical choice: will we use this technology primarily as a tool for efficiency, risking the erosion of our core intellectual skills, or can we design it to be a partner in our cognitive development? This paper has argued for and demonstrated the viability of the latter. By arguing for the use of AI as a critical thinking partner and presenting a method for doing so, it is my hope that others will experiment with the use of AI for this purpose. Readers are encouraged to experiment with the system instruction prompt, test its limits, modify it, and further explore the potential for AI to be used in constructive and productive ways. By consciously designing our interactions, we can create AI partners augment, rather thandiminish, human intellect.
