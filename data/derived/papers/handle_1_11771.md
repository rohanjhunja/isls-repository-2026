# “I'm Glad This Focuses on Minority Communities!” Exploring the Experiences and Perceptions of Undergraduate STEM Majors

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores the experience and perceptions of undergraduate STEM majors in the STEM voices mentorship program grounded in the theoretical framework of communities of practice (CoP). This research investigates how undergraduate STEM mentors perceive their growth and development within the STEM Voices program, designed to engage African American middle school youth in STEM as a tool for social justice. Using a qualitative research design, the study collected data through semi-structured interviews with nine undergraduate STEM majors who participated in the program in 2023-24 school year. Thematic analysis revealed that relationships were central to their experiences, with significant connections to youth engagement, personal growth, community service, and a sense of belonging. Building relationships was viewed as fundamental to participants' experiences and development within the program.

## Introduction

Increasing representation for Black students in STE M has become a national priority in the United Stat es (Oseguera et al., 2020). In STEM spaces, it is rare for Black students to see individuals who share their racial and cultural backgrounds, leading to feelings of isolat ion and disconnection (Allen et al., 2022). This la ck of representation can hinder their ability to feel a sense of belonging and persist in STEM disciplines (Adams et al., 2021). According to the National Science Foundation’s  NCSES data (2016 –2017), African Americans earned only 8.27% of bachelor’s degrees in STEM in 2016, a nd just 6.55% of those with bachelor’s degrees were employed in STEM occupations.  Despite the increasing demand for a diverse STEM wo rkforce, Black students remain significantly underrepresented in STEM fields at both undergraduate and graduate levels and within STEM careers (Corneille et al., 2020). This lack of representation emphasizes the need for  interventions that improve their engagement in STEM at the undergraduate and graduate levels and encourage them to pursue STEM careers (Adams et al., 2021).

The underrepresentation of Black individuals in STE M reflects systemic barriers such as institutional racism, stereotype threats, and limited access to r esources (Adiredja, 2019). Studies reveal that part icipation in CoPs tailored for Black students significantly enha nces their STEM identity and persistence (Wendt, 20 23; Oseguera et al., 2020; Adiredja, 2019). A study by Majka et al. (2023) demonstrated that an interdisciplinary CoP can establish a strong community of practice among first-year STEM students. This approach promoted psychosocial gains such as increased belonging and science self-efficacy and positively influenced students' intention to remain in STEM (Majka et al., 2023). By fostering inclusive and supportive CoPs, educational institutions can create environments and interventi ons where all students have the opportunity to thri ve and succeed in STEM.

One example of such an intervention is the STEM Voices (pseudonym) program, an after-school STEM club for African American middle school students and a mentorship program for African American STEM undergraduates. This program’s main goal is to prov ide a supportive space where Black STEM undergraduates can see themselves represented and connect with oth ers who share their racial and cultural backgrounds. The program aims to empower African  American youth through STEM and foster mentorship a nd community engagement, enabling undergraduate mentors to inspi re the younger ones while strengthening their STEM identities. By focusing on how Black youth can use STEM to create change in their communities, STEM Voices creates an inclusive environment that promotes a se nse of belonging and connection among participants.  The selection of African American students majoring in STEM is based on research demonstrating the importa nce of reflective identity, wh ich is seeing people who look like them engaging su ccessfully in STEM and connecting their cultural values to STEM interests (Graham, 20 09). Though STEM Voices is a structured program, it constitutes a CoP as it involves a shared domain (mentorship and STEM), a community (undergraduate mentors), and a practice (weekly engagements and reflections)  through which participants develop  collective knowledge and mutual engagement.

Despite the existing literature on CoPs and underre presentation in STEM, little is known about how undergraduate Black STEM majors perceive their developmental journeys within intentionally designed programs that center community and cultural identity. Therefore, this study explores Black undergraduate STEM majors' experiences and perceived development in the STEM V oices program. Specifically,  how participation in the program influenced their relationships, sense of belonging, and personal growth. The research question that guides this study is: How do undergraduate STEM majors describe their experiences in the STEM Voices program?

## Theoretical framework and literature review

This study is grounded in the theoretical framework  of Communities of Practice (CoP) (Lave &Wenger, 19 91). CoP is a social learning theory that draws on the dynamic social nature of human beings as a fundamental source of learning, growth, and betterment (Wenger, 1998).  Although communities and groups of like-minded individuals have practiced the idea for hundreds of  years, it has only been formally integrated into m odern academia and organizational activities in recent de cades. Lave and Wenger (1991) introduced the concep t of community of practice as a method to understand lea rning, particularly learning in the workplace. A co mmunity of practice may be defined as a network of people who work on similar processes or disciplines and come together to develop and share their knowledge in that field to benefit themselves and their organization(s)(Lave &Wenger, 1991). A community of practice, according to Uriarte (2008),  “consists of members exchanging knowledge, and in the process, they build relationships and develop a sense of belonging and mutual commitment” (p. 55). Wenger et al. (2002) described three fundamental elements of a community of practice: (i) a domain of knowledge, which defines a set of issues; (ii) a community of people  who care about this domain; and (iii) the shared p ractice that they develop to be effective in their domain. Thus, a community of practice is a platform for sharing knowledge, where a group of people come together and share eac h other’s experiences on a particular subject or a common interest.

In the context of STEM, CoPs offer a space to foste r learning and belonging among underrepresented gro ups, particularly Black students and professionals (Adiredja, 2019). Integrating race and identity into CoPs highlights the importance of inclusive practi ces, especially in underrepresented fields (Johnson, 2024). Adiredja (2019) argues that synthesizing CoPs with frameworks like Community Cultural Wealth (CCW) ensures that students of color leverage cultural assets such as familial, na vigational, and resis tant capital to navigate exclusionary academic spaces. Following Adiredja (2019), this study incorporates Community Cultural Wealth, especially familial, resistant and social capital  (Yosso, 2005, as cited in Adiredja, 2019), as lenses to examine how undergraduates navigated and contributed to the men torship space.  CCW is a framework that highlights the resources, assets, skills and values of marginalize d communities (Adiredja, 2019). This is vital for e mpowering marginalized groups in STEM through equitabl e access and addressing disparities resulting from marginalizing and minoritizing practices (Richard & Gray, 2018).

## Research design

This study used a qualitative research design (Saldaña, 2011) to explore undergraduate STEM majors' experiences and perceived development in the STEM Voices progra m. We used this qualitative approach to understand the participant's relational experiences, sense of belonging, and community engagement. We employed a hybrid analytic approach by combining thematic coding with Epistemic Network An alysis (ENA) to capture the depth and structure of participants’ reflections. This al lowed us to have a thematic unde rstanding and structural visualization of the data.

## Data collection

We collected data through semi-structured interviews to enable our research to exp lore individual experiences and allow emergent questions throughout (Glesne, 20 15). We conducted the interviews, either in person or through Zoom, depending on the participant’s prefer ence, at the end of the Fall 2023 semester, which w as at the end of the first semester of their part icipation. Each interview lasted about thirty to si xty minutes. The interview questions focused on the undergraduate STEM majors and their experience in the STEM Voices program.

Epistemic network analysis (ENA) Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) is a technique use d to model the structure of connections in data (Bo wman et al., 2021; Shaffer, 2017). In our study, we applied ENA (Shaffer et al., 2016; Shaffer & Ruis, 2017) to 565 lines of data using the ENA Web Tool (version 1.7.0) (Marquart et al., 2018) with each line representing a turn of talk in the interview transcripts.

The ENA algorithm uses a moving window to construct  a network model for each line (or turn of talk) in the data, showing how codes in the current line are connected to codes from the previous three line s (Ruis et al., 2019; Siebert-Evenstone et al., 2017). As the window slides down the segmented interview d ataset, it counts the co-occurrences of codes within a window size of four lines. Codes that co-occur within the bottom line of the window are counted, and codes that co-occur between the bottom line and the previous three lines are counted as well. These summations are then represented in a no de-link network visualization in which the nodes are t he codes, and the links are weighted. The codes that co-occur more often are represented by thicker and bolder links. ENA also constructs a mathematical space in which t he nodes (codes) are fixed for every person’s network that is compared. To create this space, the  ENA model normalizes the matrix of co-occurrences for all units of analysis before applying dimensional reduc tion. We used singular value decomposition for the dimensional reduction, which produces orthogonal di mensions that maximize the variance explained by ea ch dimension (see Bowman et al., 2021 and Shaffer et a l., 2016 for more mathematical details). In this st udy, we displayed the first two dimensions, which collectiv ely accounted for 38% of the variance in data on th e X-axis and 27% on the Y-axis. In addition to the network representation, ENA also displays the centroid of the network for each person. This is a helpful representation for viewing multiple people’s discourse data at one time in a twodimensional mathematical space. The centroid of the  network is correlated with the transformed data po ints, or scores, from the singular value decomposition to determine if the mathematical space and the position of the nodes are reliably interpretable. This correlation is called a goodness of fit measure in ENA. Our model showed a strong goodness of fit, with co-registration correlations of 0.99 (Pearson) and 0.9 9 (Spearman) for the first dimension and 0.98 (Pearson) and 0.98 (Spearman) for the second dimension. Relationships were at the center of all these codes, showing strong connections with al l other categories. This suggests that building rel ationships was fundamental to participants' experiences and development within the program. For example, a psychology major, Marcus, mentioned how being part of STEM Voices made him more open-minded through teaching and building bonds with the  youths and his fellow undergrads. The more robust connection between these two codes indicates that the basis of the relationship the participan ts formed was through engaging with their mentees, the middle school students in the STEM Voices program. ## Discussion

This study explores undergraduate STEM majors' expe riences and perceived development in the STEM Voice s program. The findings revealed that building and ma intaining relationships were central to participant s' experiences, with significant connections to Youth Engagement, Personal Growth, Community Service, and Sense of Belonging. This centrality aligns with Lave & Wenger's (1991) assertion that a community of practice is built on a shared domain, community, and practice, emphas izing that learning and growth  are inherently social and occur through community participation.

Participants emphasized how the program’s focus on African American youth and minority representation made them feel included and valued. This reflects Adiredja's (2019) argument that CoPs tailored to communities of color empower students by leverag ing cultural wealth and creating inclusive environm ents. The sense of belonging reported by participants als o aligns with Johnson's (2024) work, highlighting t he importance of representation in STEM spaces for fostering persistence. Participants like Emma and Maya shared how the presence of peers with similar racial and c ultural backgrounds allowed them to be their authen tic selves and feel comfortable in STEM environments. This sense of belonging, in turn, strengthened their commitment to mentoring and community engagement. The strong conn ection between Relationships and Sense of Belonging highlights the importance of shared identities in f ostering a supportive and inclusive community. This  finding supports Richard and Gray's (2018) argument that addressing systemic barriers and promoting inclusive practices are crucial for empowering underrepresented groups in STEM. The undergraduate STEM majors also reported growth in confidence, leadership, and interpersonal skills through the relationships they formed, highlighting  the role of social interactions in fostering perso nal and professional development (Wenger, 1998). This findi ng is consistent with Majka et al. (2023), who foun d that participation in CoPs can lead to psychosocial gain s. The shared cultural backgrounds and focus on emp owering minority communities created a counter space, as Ri chard and Gray (2018) described, where participants could build relationships free from the pressures of marg inalization and the need to conform. This safe spac e allowed for authentic interactions and deeper connections, ultimately fostering personal growth. Mentorship was the medium through which participants experienced personal growth. The relationships formed with middle school mentees encouraged participants to develop leadership, communication, and interpersonal skills. Maya’s reflection on her tran sformation into a more responsible and nurturing leader demonstrates how CoPs facilitate the development of new competencies and expand capacities within a supportive community, as theorized by Wenger (1998).  While our findings align with the core tenets of Com munities of Practice, such as mutual engagement and shared prac tice (Wenger, 1998), we also noted ways the STEM Voices program pushed beyond the traditional CoP framework. In particular, the mentors often drew on resistant capital (Yosso, 2005, as cited in Adiredja, 2019) to addres s systemic issues like environmental injustice  and use their STEM knowledge as a tool for advocacy. This suggest s that culturally responsive CoPs can be more than just learning communities. They  can also be spaces for activism, where participants  act as changemakers in their communities (Richard & Gray, 2018).

The program’s focus on community engagement further underscores the importance of integrating CoPs with frameworks like Community Cultural Wealth (CCW) (Yosso, 2005, as cited in Adiredja, 2019). Participants actively utilized various forms of CCW, including f amilial, social, and resistant capital, to navigate  their roles within the STEM Voices program. Familial capital wa s seen as participants highlighted their responsibi lity to mentor and inspire African American youth, drawing from the values of reflective identity (Graham, 200 9) and intergenerational support. Social capital was evide nt in how participants built networks with peers and mentees, drawing on shared experiences and collective knowle dge to foster a sense of belonging and empowerment. Resistant capital was seen as participants using their involv ement in STEM Voices to challenge environmental injustice in their community and using STEM as a to ol for social justice. Participants expressed that they developed a sense of responsibility and appreciatio n for contributing to their communities. Unlike tra ditional STEM environments, which often fail to recognize th ese cultural assets, the STEM Voices program recogn ized participants’ C CW as a strength, allowing participants to connect the ir cultural values with STEM-related activities. Our findings show that culturally respo nsive STEM spaces can leverage C ommunity Cultural Wealth to promote personal development and community impac t, and this offers a new perspective on how CoPs ca n be tailored to support Bla ck students in STEM. Therefore, by leveraging CCW w ithin CoPs, we can create safe environments that actively integrate cultural values, community engagement, and STEM identity development. Overall, the findings of this study highlight the c ritical role of relationships in the experiences and development of undergraduate STEM majors in the STE M Voices program. Specifically, relationships served as the foundation for participants to build trus t and co-create an environment that helped them feel a sense  of inclusion and authenticity in STEM. By centering th e program on shared racial and cultural identities,  the participants felt seen, valued, and understood in w ays that traditional STEM spaces do not often provi de. The relationships they formed created a sense of solida rity and community that alleviated the isolation th ey often experienced in the STEM field due to a lack of repr esentation. The program’s culturally responsive app roach encouraged participants to draw on their CCW to connect their cultural values with their STEM experiences. These findings emphasize the importance of cultural ly responsive and inclusive CoPs in STEM. It also emphasized the need for educational institutions and STEM programs to prioritize representation, cultu rally affirming mentorship, and community engagement to s upport the retention and success of underrepresente d groups by integrating inclusive practices and share d identities into CoPs. By integrating inclusive pr actices and leveraging the cultural wealth of diverse students,  CoPs can create supportive environments that empow er marginalized groups to thrive and persist in STEM d isciplines, advancing equity, inclusion, and succes s across STEM fields.
