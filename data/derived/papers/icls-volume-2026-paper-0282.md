# Exploring the Affordances and Challenges of Participatory Co-Analysis of Clinical Interviews

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Clinical interviews have long served as a method to learn about individual’s thinking and reasoning in mathematics and science education. Yet, analysis of interviews has often relied solely on researcher interpretations. In this methodological paper, we s hare an alternate approach in which we engaged in co-analysis with research participants. We focus on co-analysis sessions conducted with one preservice teacher (the third author) following her engagement in a task-based clinical interview about s patial rotation. We examine how the co-analysis provided additional insights into the participant’s spatial reasoning and the challenges that arose for both the researchers and participant. Our findings suggest the potential of co-analysis for providing greater understanding of participants’ use of gesture and language.

Our findings suggest the potential of coTask-based clinical interviews have long been used to investigate how learners reason about mathematics and science problems (diSessa, 2007; Ginsburg, 1997). They are especially valuable for studying processes that may not be visible in written products al one. Yet, the analysis of interview data often remains researcher-led: Participants contribute responses during the interview, but they rarely participate in interpreting those responses afterward. Limiting participant perspectives positions the researcher  as the primary meaning-maker, therefore marginalizing the participant’s own interpretive agency (Bishop, 1991). Participatory research traditions challenge models in which participants are treated mainly as sources of data rather than collaborators in knowledge production (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Philip et al., 2018). However, participatory approaches have been less frequently taken up in research using task-based clinical interviews (diSessa, 2007). Applied to clinical interviews, participatory co-analysis, in particular, invites participants to revisit, reflect upon, and co-construct interpretations with the researcher (Bang et al., 2012). Such a shift is methodological as well as ethical: I t changes who is authorized to interpret data and can reveal more meaningful and relevant findings  that are not accessible through researcher inference alone (Sahakyan, 2023).

In this methodological paper, we explore how incorporating co-analysis into task-based clinical interviews can enrich researchers’ understanding of one participant’s embodied and language-mediated spatial reasoning. We also reflect on the methodological challenges that arise when participants are invited to take on an analytic role. We ask: 1) How did co-analysis enrich researchers’ understandings of the participant’s spatial reasoning? 2) What challenges did the researchers and the participant face during the co-analysis? We focus on spatial reasoning, and in particular rotations, because spatial reasoning provides a rich context in which to study embodiment and language use, as learners often rely on both bodily enactments and linguistic explanations to make sense of spatial transformations (Alibali, 2005; McGarvey et al., 2015). This study is situated within Author 1’s Master’s thesis project investigating the reasoning strategies preservice teachers (PSTs) used when solving  2D rotation  tasks, where we considered reasoning strategies to be gesture when enacting reasoning strategies.  Author 1 is a Master’s student with 3 years of teaching experience, Author 2 is Author 1’s thesis advisor, and Author 3 is the research participant who participated in the task-based interview and co-analysis. Authors 1 and 2 approached this study as co-researchers with Author 3. While we acknowledge our influence in framing the tasks and discussions, our positionality and past research/teaching experiences sensitized us to nuances in reasoning that might be ove rlooked by academic research and motivated our attention to participatory analysis as a method for foregrounding the participant’s perspective. For the sake of clarity, we refer to the university researchers as “researchers” and the participant as “participant.”

## Theoretical perspectives

Our study is situated within socio-cultural theories of learning, which suggest that reasoning is situated, embodied, and mediated by language and tools (Vygotsky, 1978; Lave & Wenger, 1991). These frameworks emphasize that cognition is shaped by interaction, artifacts, and the cultural context in which learning occurs. Within this perspective, embodied cognition and language use are core mechanisms through which reasoning happens 1897 (Nemirovsky & Ferrara, 2009). Embodied cognition highlights that gesture and bodily movement are not merely illustrations of completed thought but can be part of reasoning itself (Alibali & Nathan, 2011; Goldin-Meadow, 2003). In spatial tasks, learners may trace motion, compare orientations, or simulate a transformation with their hands. Apparent gesture-speech mismatches can therefore be analytically important because they may signal emerging ideas or shifts in frame of reference (Alibali & Goldin-Meadow, 1993). We also focus on how everyday wording, metaphors, and dynamic verbs helped the participant stabilize and communicate spatial relations. Language therefore worked alongside gesture as one semiotic resource within a broader multimodal process. Spatial reasoning refers broadly to the capacity to represent, interpret, and transform spatial information in problem-solving contexts (Mix et al., 2016). In STEM education, spatial reasoning has been increasingly recognized as a critical component of learning and problem-solving (Young et al., 2018).  Students draw upon spatial reasoning when they interpret geometric figures, measure shapes and solids, or read and construct data representations (Mix & Cheng, 2012; Uttal et al., 2013). In contrast to s patial skills—which  refer to narrower, task-specific abilities, such as mental rotation, spatial visualization, or spatial orientation —s patial reasoning encompasses the higher-order integration of these skills within problem-solving situations (Uttal & Cohen, 2012). More broadly, w e frame spatial reasoning as a type of mathematical reasoning (Ball & Bass, 2003): A way of thinking that involves making sense of and explaining qualities of space. Within this study we focus on 2D mental rotation as one type of spatial reasoning. 2D mental rotation is the ability to rotate objects mentally to determine equivalence, matching, or fit (Fujita et al.,  2020). Although mental rotation has been studied widely, most studies focus on accuracy of individuals’ responses rather than on how individuals reason about rotations (e.g., Harris, 2023). In this methodological paper, w e thus aimed to understand how co-analysis would reveal how the participant reasoned about rotation tasks, with particular attention to their embodied and linguistic resources.

This paper draws on a broader participatory case study involving four preservice teachers (Yin, 2009). We focus on Author 3, a multilingual fourth-year Bachelor of Education student at a large public university in Eastern Canada. Before the study, she had completed two courses on mathematics teaching methods (which included only a brief introduction to rotations) and three practica.

Author 3 participated in a 44-minute semi-structured clinical interview led by Author 1. The interview included four paper-based rotation tasks (Figure 1). In the first three tasks, the participant was first asked what she noticed and then asked, “How can you move this object (the right one) to look exactly like this object (the left one)?” Follow-up questions asked participants  to elaborate on their language (e.g., “What do you mean by…”) and to explain the reasoning behind how they visualized the rotation (e.g., “How did you know where to stop [the rotation] there?” The paper could not be physically manipulated. In Task 4, she was asked which keyhole a key would fit into and why. Participants were invited to use any language; Author 3 chose to respond in English. We then watched each interview task, watching and discussing Tasks 1a, 1b, 2a, and 2b in the first session and the remaining tasks in the second session. In the first co-analysis session, we stopped after each sub-task (e.g., after Task 1a, then Task 1b) to share observations. In the second co-analysis session, we stopped less frequently (after each full task) to ensure that we had sufficient time to complete the analysis. During each of these discussions, we first invited Author 3 to share her observations and then followed up with specific questions about gestures and language that we had noted during Phase 1. In contrast to a stimulated recall interview, we also shared our own observations related to the analytic foci, allowing for a dialogic and collective interpretation of the data. In Phase 3, Author 1 used the co-analysis to develop an initial coding scheme to capture reasoning strategies. Authors 1 and 2 then engaged in multiple rounds of coding samples of data for all participants to further develop the coding scheme. Finally, in Phase 4, Author 1 used the finalized coding scheme to re-code the data and then revisited the notes from the co-analysis to provide confirming or disconfirming evidence. For the purpose of this paper, following the co-analysis, we compared our notes of our initial interpretations from Phase 1 with the audio-recorded notes  from participatory co-analysis in Phase 2. We identified differences in interpretations in gestures, language use, and reasoning strategies. Post co-analysis, each author also documented challenges they experienced during the co-analysis. Co-analysis enriched the researchers’ understanding of the participant’s reasoning in four ways. First, co-analysis changed how we interpreted key gestures. For example, in Task 1a, Author 3 used a claw-like hand gesture (Figure 2A) while explaining how to move the right triangle to match the left triangle (Task 1a). In Phase 1, we interpreted this as tracking particular vertices. During co-analysis, however, Author 3 explained that the gesture represented the movement of the triangle as a whole. Before co-analysis, we had noticed the motion but not the importance of using one hand as a visual referent for the endpoint. Co-analysis therefore changed our later coding of the strategy from tracing a point to tracing the whole-object.

As another example, in the same task, Author 3 also used both flat hands simultaneously to show how she would rotate the triangle (Figure 2B). She placed one hand on top of the first triangle and the second on the second triangle. She explained during co-analysis that each hand stood in for the respective triangle. She rotated the right hand counterclockwise 90-degrees to show how the triangle  should be moving to match the first one. Prior to co-analysis, we did not recognize the significance of “substituting” her left hand for the left triangle to be able visualize the endpoint. As Author 3 explained, “as I was rotating [unintelligible], I will know that both my hands look the same so should be the triangles.” These reinterpretations again altered the researchers’ understanding of her spatial reasoning strategies. Initially, Authors 1 and 2 would have classified Author 3 as drawing upon the reasoning strategy of tracing her right hand to stand in for  the moving shape, not recognizing the significance of the left hand. In contrast, following co-analysis, the researchers identified a second reasoning strategy of using one hand (in this case, the left) as a visual comparative referent of the final orientation. Second, co-analysis provided essential context for understanding the meaning behind Author 3’s language. The participant used metaphors  such as “stand upright” and “on the table” to describe orientation. During co-analysis, she explained that these expressions helped her map abstract spatial relations onto familiar bodily and physical experiences. She also linked one task to memories of the “Mr. Maker” television show, which involved hands-on construction and visual manipulation. These explanations showed that her language was not incidental; it was part of how she anchored the task in prior experience and imagined action. Third, co-analysis clarified an apparent gesture-speech mismatch. In one segment, the participant said the triangle moved “counterclockwise” while moving her hand clockwise. From a researcher-only perspective, A) B) 1899 this might have been coded as inconsistency or error. During co-analysis, she explained that she was speaking from the figure’s point of view rather than from that of an outside observer. What appeared contradictory was therefore a shift in frame of refere nce. This clarification was especially important because it showed how easily multimodal data can be overinterpreted when participants are not invited into the analytic process. Finally, the cosupport the participant’s own reasoning. She explained that some gestures were meant to make her thinking visible to the researchers, whereas others helped her “see” or “feel” the rotation for herself. This shifted our attention from what forms appeared in the video to what functions they served in the moment. Co-analysis also generated methodological challenges. Early in the process, the researchers realized that broad analytic prompts were being interpreted by the participant as requests for summary rather than for analysis. More concrete questions, such as what she was paying attention to when making a movement, yielded more useful reflection. The participant reported uncertainty about whether she should treat the figures as printed images or as manipulable objects. She also reflected on the role of note-taking during co-analysis. Initially, she did not take notes, but as the session progressed, she begandoing so to track moments she wished to revisit or elaborate on. Recording timestamps allowed her to take analytical ownership of the process, returning to specific instances with greater intentionality. For example, she wrote timestamps to be able to revisit during discussion. This practice turned co-analysis into a two-way analytic dialogue: Rather than responding passively to researcher prompts, she developed her own strategies for organizing and articulating insights.  A final challenge she faced was deciding whether to stop after watching the recording of every sub-task and analyze it before moving on to the next, or to watch the interview in longer segments. In the first analysis session, the video was broken down into smaller segments whereas during the second session, we viewed larger segments. However, her notes piled up and led her to lose track of some of the details that she wanted to mention that had occurred during the interview. Having tried both methods, she felt that breaking down the analysis into smaller segments was more manageable.

Our findings revealed that interpretation is not neutral, and researcher-led analysis alone risks imposing external meanings on multimodal data. One of the most significant outcomes of this process was a deepened awareness of how meaning is negotiated between researchers and participants, not only through the data itself, but also through the very structure of the analytic process.  The participant’s challenges highlighted how ambiguities in the interview tasks could carry over into co-analysis. In particular, she noted how she understood that she was rotating the object with the paper instead of just the object. This difference in understanding highlights the importance of task framing and representational mode in spatial reasoning co-analysis. The participants’ interpretations of what counts as the “object” of thought can subtly shift the nature of the reasoning itself and how it is then analyzed. A related insight relates to shared understanding of analytic practices. At times, the participant’s interpretation of what it meant to “analyze” diverged from ours. This highlighted the need for a more explicit coconstruction of the analytic process itself, what we might call a shared analytic grammar/language.  Research in teacher education has shown the importance of developing shared frames (common interpretive lenses, Goffman,

1974) when teachers engage in collective learning around problems of teaching practice (e.g., Horn & Little,

2010). Similarly, a shared analytic grammar/language may provide a shared frame for collective analysis among researchers and participants. Future studies could incorporate an early “meta-method” session to establish a common understanding of what counts as analysis, reflection, and explanation within the study’s context.

The study highlights additional design considerations for future work. Co-analysis sessions should also build in manageable pauses, participant note-taking, and opportunities to return to specific timestamps. These supports can make participation more analytic and less reactive. O ur approach was shaped by practical and conceptual limitations. Time was a significant factor. Co-analysis is an inherently slow and dialogic process that demands sustained engagement from both researchers and participants. Although it would be ideal to involve the participants in subsequent phases of analysis, this would demand a lot of their time and may have reduced participant recruitment. In addition, as with many retrospective analytic methods, participants’ insights were constrained by what could be recalled or reinterpreted after the moment of reasoning. Co-analysis could not fully reconstruct the immediacy of embodied and cognitive processes that occurred during the original task.  Still, the than treating interpretation as the sole responsibility of researchers, co-analysis redistributes interpretive authority and aligns interview analysis more closely with sociocultural views of learning and knowledge production.
