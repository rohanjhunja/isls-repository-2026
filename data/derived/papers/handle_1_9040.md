## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Countering oppressive practices in digital spaces is essential to prevent control and exploitation. Youth need to be empowered to reflect on their use of such spaces. In this poster, I present a design for an alternate reality game that engages learners in participatory research and storytelling in a virtual world to develop data tactics to repurpose data practices.

## Introduction

In understanding how technology can become oppressive for youth, I focus on the inequalities present in digital spaces. With the growing use of personal digital de vices and mass platforms, there is an unprecedented  rate of data generation, often collected without the individual’s knowledge. Without the knowledge to counter oppressive practices in digital spaces, youth are often traced, tracked, and policed through the use of their per sonal data leading to a state of automated inequality (Eubanks, 2018). Using Pangrazio and Selwyn’s (2019) framework of personal data literacies, I seek to explore how sto rytelling and games in virtual worlds can support t he development of data tactics to counter or repurpose  current data practices within a youth participator y action research (YPAR) framework where the boundaries between fiction and research are purposefully blurred. The use of stories to engage youth in making sense of digital spaces can be drawn from indigenous practices regarding place-making. Abenaki author Lisa Brooks used awikhigawôgan, an ongoing and collective writing activity, to reconstruct the community (2008). According to Basso (1996), building “place-worlds” is a deeply human activity that does not require any storytelling skills but a sense of curiosity. Through the use of letters, scrolls, maps, portraits, and photographs, Brooks (2008) was able to engage in making sense of the different spatial experiences of the community. In this design, I imagine awikhigawôgan as virtual worlds created by learners to make sense of their experiences with data in digital spaces. Based on Freire’s (1992) notion of the “consciousness of the possible,” I seek to understand how learners can engage in a collective experience of designing possible futures through virtual worlds that could reverberate to inspire change in their communities. To engage in storytelling, I designed an alternate reality game (ARG); a game that takes place in the real world by intertwining fictional elements. This game was inspired by Ask Anansi, an ARG developed by Antero Garcia (2017) where youth connected with a fictional character who engaged them in a research process through questions and conversations. While Ask Anansi was mostly an in-person experience for students, the research context for my design required the development of a virtual world. This poster describes the design principles that support the game. Design principles In order to engage learners in recontextualizing their relationships with digital spaces, the game introduces, Nayah-Irú, a 16-year-old girl from the future who claims to be from what was once known as Uruguay. After a series of puzzles and cryptic messages, Nayah-Irú reveals that she lives in a future where all forms of digital space have been banned. Nayah-Irú, who is fascinated by the digital spaces in the 21st century, ignores the ban and finds a way to communicate with students through multiple platforms to find out why they were prohibited. Context and purpose: an ARG environment to support a spatial analysis of data practices In July 2021, Nayah-Irú contacted a group of 18 students aged 15-20 from rural Uruguay who attend a secondary education center for learners who have fallen out of formal education. The program provides instruction in core knowledge areas, physical education, vocational workshops, and information technology. The game was played with learners during two weekly periods over twelve weeks during the information technology class. The main character interacted with learners in three ways. Through automated bots that asked questions to students during the week or sent text messages when student solved a puzzle; through e-mails and social media posts which often introduced the weekly challenge; and finally, as a character in the Gather platform, an avatarbased videoconference system which served as the virtual world for the game. I played two roles in this design: I facilitated the meetings with students using my personal identity, and I also engaged in conversations with learners as a fictional character.

The game design was structured in three stag es which provide learners with opportunities to und erstand the digital spaces they inhabit, engage in performative work through storytelling, and eventually transform their communities by extending their performance towards shared knowledge (Garcia, 2020). Making sense of data in space During the first stage of the game, players are required to explore different worlds created by Nayah-Irú in Gather. The activities are designed as scavenger hunts which lead to videos, story fragments, or illustrations that promote reflections and discussions. As they solve each challenge, they uncover more fragments of the story.  One feature of the game in this stage is that players are not allowed to share any personal information since Nayah-Irú believes their communications may be intercepted. Thus, stud ents engaged in discussions using their avatars and usernames without revealing their identities. After six weeks, Nahay-Irú shifts from the game puppeteer and leads players into asking their own questions. In turn, students turn from participants in the story to storytellers. Nayah-Irú requests information about the nature of datafication in their lives which she will use to stand against her regime. To answer these questions, students had to develop their own research questions to understand why digital spaces are banned in the future. Some o f the questions in this first iteration of the game  focused on how data can be tracked, the addictive characteristics of social media, and the impact of digital spaces on mental health.

Mapping new spaces for the community Students were trained to use a wide variety of research methods discussing their ethical implications, the quality of the data they were collecting and how the data w ould serve the purpose of telling their story. Once  students collected data to answer their questions through interviews, observations, and surveys, they were encouraged to design a virtual space to communicate their message. At this stage, participants designed their own sc avenger hunt where they hid their message to Nayah-Irú. Students had to discuss whether they wanted to give Nayah-Irú a grim perspective of digital spaces which could justify the digital ban or incorporate a rich myriad of perspectives to support her decision to resist. The storytelling is discussed by students to determine whether this is a story of hope.

Since students are not capable of sending the virt ual space to Nayah-Irú into the future, she suggests they place a physical poster around their city with a li nk to the virtual space. This activity allowed lear ners to create their own cryptic puzzle to impact their community.  By interacting with the poster, community members could visit the virtual space, solve the mystery, and listen to the videos and messages designed by students. Current progress and future work At the time of this poster, a working prototype of  Nayah-Irú has been piloted with a group of student s and received feedback from both the students and other educators. The second iteration of the game will involve a new cohort of students and incorporate new characters designed by the current participants. Some of the areas to be explored within this desig n are the affordances of virtual worlds for youth t o engage in participatory research through storytelling and how educators can facilitate game-based part icipatory research experiences with youth.
