# Being Fair & Equitable: A Qualitative Study of Science Teachers’ Shifts in Priorities and Expectations During COVID-19

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how secondary science teachers described the shift in their priorities and expectations to be more equitable towards their affected students during the initial COVID-19 lockdown. Ten teachers were recruited via a purposeful convenient sampling from diverse socioeconomic status (SES) schools and interviewed through Zoom. Findings indicate SES-based differences in teachers’ perception of the impact of COVID-19 on students, and their approach to fairness and equity through subsequent shifts in priorities and expectations.

## Introduction

The challenges of the COVID-19 pandemic continue to provide insight into issues of teaching  and learning that are not unique to the pandemic. During the Emergency Remote Teaching (ERT) of March  2020, students' wellbeing was impacted, teachers struggled with online teaching, and the role of the home environment became more important than ever before (Huck & Zhang, 2021). While several quantitative studies have highlighted increased inequities based on socioeconomic status (SES) duri ng the pandemic (Liu, 2023; Parolin & Lee, 2021), l imited qualitative research has explored the reasons behin d these disparities among different SES schools, particularly from the perspective of challenges encountered by teachers. This study aims to understand how the lockdown impacted teachers and exacerbated the inherent inequity in schools in different rural and urban areas of a state in New England in the United States. Using the equity framework lens (Sherman & Poirier, 2007), we utilize two types of equities in our study--horizontal equity (equal treatment of those who are equally situated) and ve rtical equity (different levels of support  for those differently situated). According to Maiztegui‐Oñate and Santibáñez‐Gruber (2008), vertical equity recognizes that students have different starting points, therefore extra-support can be necessary for certain students to reach hori zontal equity. Thus, equity includes but also transcends equality. The goal of this research is to understand how certain secondary science teachers experienced the process of remote teaching and assessment during the COVID-19 lockdown by probing the following t wo research questions–(a) according to the teachers, how were the students imp acted by the COVID-19 lockdown? (b) how did teachers deal with the issue of fairness and equity during the Emergency Remote Teaching?

## Methodology

The participants in this study were 10 secondary sc ience teachers from 10 different school districts in a New England state. Schools were classified into three categories based on the percentage of the student population that was on free or reduced lunch (FRL): high-SES (3 schools, <25% FRL), middle-SES (3 schools, 35-50% FRL), and low-SES (4 schools, >50% FRL). Participants were initially recruited based on conv enience, and additional participants were purposefully recruited from each SES category to even the sample size. For ease of reference, teachers are identified with an alias corresponding  to their school's SES category (e.g., Hank, Hazel,  Heather for high-SES; Mia, Mike, Myra for middle-SES; Liam, Lisa, Luke, Lydia for low-SES). Data was collected in the summer of 2020 using Zoom interviews, when all teachers had concluded their first experience of teaching remotely. The nature of these interviews was semi-structured and conversational, and recordings were first transcribed and then cod ed for themes to answer the research questions. Data analysis followed a deductive thematic analysis approach (Br aun & Clarke, 2006) to categorize codes relevant to  two major themes: Impact on students (Theme 1), and teachers being fair and equitable (Theme 2). The two themes were identified as the most relevant to describe th e shift in teaching and assessment practices of all  participants based on our framework of horizontal and vertical equity. Afterwards, responses were sorted according to SES category, and any characteristic differences betwee n high-SES and low-SES schools were examined through a comparative analysis method.

## Findings

Following an in-depth analysis of the t wo themes–impact on students and being fair and equitable –we noticed several commonalities and some SES-based differences in teacher responses. In Theme 1, regarding the impact on students, most teachers sha red that their students struggled. However, there was a di fference in severity of this struggle between low-SES vs high-SES school teachers’ description. For instance, Hank, a high-SES school teacher shared, “...majority missed the social aspect of school…Quite a few students reported just having tr ouble staying motivated,” which is similar to responses of other high-SES school teachers. In comparison, Luke, a low-SES school teacher, told us, “Like I said, you know, emotional stability…that was our number one concern...kids were stressed.”  He also added, “Access to technology we had, we had problems with that, you know…” which is in line with responses of teachers from lowSES schools who highlighted that their students’ mental health was highly impacted, most of the students had poor study environments at home, and some did not have access to the internet. In T heme 2, concerning fairness and equit y, most teachers said that they reduced or adjusted their expectations and made themselves available for supp ort, to be fairer and more equitable. A high-SES school teacher, Heather, mentioned, “I feel like they [assessments] were fair and equitable in that everybody had all the opportunities to get that pass, as many as they needed…Some gain was expected of all.” Heather was an example of a high-SES teacher who reduced the rigor of assessment and  lowered expectation s from mastery to some understanding. Conversely, low-SES school teachers' responses indicate that they s ignificantly reduced their expectations. Structural issues, such as lack of internet access and inability to easily share written work in rural areas, placed constraints on teachers.  There was no summative testing among any of the low-SES schools. For instance, Lisa, a low-SES teacher shared, “my expectations of where they were at the end had kind of just gone. And it was more of a just, hey, you’re there, you’r e participating, you’re attempting to do  this. That’s all I can ask for, so very different [expectations].” Another teacher, Luke mentioned, “No summative, and that was our policy for the school. Thus, given the circumstances under which low-SES teachers were operating, low-SES teachers reduced their rigor significantly to be equitable and align with school policies.

## Discussion and implications

Findings about variations in teachers’ perspectives on students' well-being based on school-SES are consistent with previous research (Huck & Zhang, 2021) suggesting that as impact of COVID-19 varied within and between schools, new vertical inequity gap s existed for teachers. To be fair and equitable, teachers reduced their expectations, became more flexible, and made themselves available to provide support. Low-SES school teachers experienced a more pronounced vertical inequity due to severe perceived impact on students and structural issues. Thus, low-SES schools reduced their expectations much more (to partial completion and effort) compared to highSES schools (to less content depth and exposure over mastery). Teachers in affluent schools had relatively higher expectations for students' learning outcomes and were relatively closer to reaching horizontal equity. These findings are consistent with the previous studies relating SES and teacher expectations (Alvidrez & Weinstein, 1999; Rubie-Davies et al, 2007). This study bridges a gap in the literature, sugg esting that it might be low-SES schools’ and teachers’ attempts to be fair and equitable, being considerate and understanding of their students’ situations, which leads to the change in their overall expectations. In summary, the socioeconomic inequity built into schools was exacerbated during the COVID-19 pandemic. The unequal  negative impact on students and unequal challenges of teaching amplifi ed vertical inequities. To be fair and equitable, teachers did the best they could by shifting their priorities and expectations to best serve all students.
