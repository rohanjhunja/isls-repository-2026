# Touch to Think: Supporting Curriculum Design with Tangible Models of the Classroom

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Physical models are routine in architecture but rarely used in curriculum design. This study examines how tangible classroom models support teachers in designing inquiry-oriented lessons. This study compares four representation modalities (2D digital, 2D paper, 3D digital, 3D physical) in a curriculum design task, finding that groups using physical models demonstrated the highest engagements. To understand underlying mechanisms, we conducted in-depth design sessions with two experienced teachers. Analysis revealed that physical models serve as "thinking tools" that help teachers visualize and test pedagogical scenarios. Teachers used models to identify spatial constraints, test activity flows, and rapidly prototype different design alternatives. Through tangible manipulation, teachers were able to link pedagogical goals to concrete spatial implementations, making previously implicit decisions explicit and actionable. This study suggests that physical models can transform curriculum design from abstract text-based planning to embodied spatial-pedagogical reasoning.

The concept of "environment as the third teacher," originating from the Reggio Emilia approach, has long emphasized the pedagogical potential of physical learning spaces (Strong-Wilson & Ellis, 2007). While teachers increasingly recognize the importance of leveraging instructional environments, educational practitioners often lack sufficient spatial literacy to understand how physical learning environments can support pedagogical goals, particularly in inquiry-oriented pedagogies where flexible space utilization is crucial (Lim et al., 2012; Alterator & Deed, 2013). Traditional curriculum design tools, predominantly text-based or two-dimensional graphic representations, are inadequate in supporting teachers' spatial reasoning and environmental design. In a pilot study, we engaged instructors in curriculum design using four presentation modalities (2D digital, 2D paper, 3D digital, 3D physical), and found that physical models showed particular promise in supporting teachers' design of inquiry-oriented curriculum by enhancing participant engagement and stimulating exploration of design ideas. This study builds on these early explorations to investigate the following question: How do physical classroom models support teachers in designing inquiry-oriented curriculum?

## Literature review

Research consistently demonstrates that well-designed learning environments enhance student engagement, collaboration, and academic outcomes (Barrett et al., 2015). Byers (2021), drawing on Lackney (2008), Steele (1973), and Leighton (2017), argues that teachers need spatial competency to harness spatial affordances for pedagogical gains. However, research on supporting teachers in designing curriculum for these spaces remains limited, and teachers often encounter difficulties integrating space and technology in active learning classrooms (Cleveland & Fisher, 2014; Leijon et al., 2024). Current curriculum design tools (e.g., text-based frameworks like Understanding by Design (Wiggins & McTighe, 2005), linear templates, or digital platforms like Google Classroom) present curriculum as objectives and sequences divorced from physical context, lacking support for spatial-pedagogical reasoning. This may partially account for why teachers struggle in adopting innovative pedagogies.

Physical models offer unique affordances that digital representations cannot fully replicate. Research in architecture and design reveals that physical models enable "thinking through making," where tactile manipulation drives conceptual development (Pallasmaa, 2009). Models provide immediate spatial feedback, revealing sight lines, traffic flows, and proximity relationships hidden in 2D representations (Yaneva, 2005). This tangibility allows rapid iteration, as designers can quickly test "what-if" scenario s by physically reconfiguring elements (Viswanathan & Linsey, 2013). Furthermore, physical models serve as communication representations to facilitate discussion among designers (Scali, Shillito, & Wright, 2002). These documented benefits in design fields suggest potential value for educational contexts.

Prior research in embodied cognition suggests that thinking can be mediated through bodily interaction with the environment (Wilson, 2002). To explore whether physical manipulation supports spatial-pedagogical reasoning, we first conducted a pilot study comparing four representational modalities.  Based on outcomes of the 1952 pilot study we then conducted a more focused study of two instructors designing curriculum using physical models.

We thus conducted a thematic analysis of the 3D model group and identified three themes: Establishing correspondences (11 instances). Teachers first mapped model pieces to real classroom elements, anchoring subsequent reasoning in concrete spatial affordances. Zone-based scenarioing (14 instances). Teachers used the model to ground instructional narratives in specific areas, making proposed activities concrete and testable against spatial constraints. Spatial reconfiguration (9 instances). Teachers physically rearranged furniture into recognizable layouts to support activities or resolve issues, exposing trade-offs through tangible iteration. In-depth investigation Building on the pilot study's findings that physical models sustained high engagement and interesting discursive moves, we conducted in-depth design sessions with two professors from a graduate program in Education, within a Canadian university. Each professor used a physical model to design an actual session for their current course. We deliberately selected two classroom spaces with contrasting furniture mobility to explore how spatial constraints shape design practices.

In order to gain a deeper understanding of instructors' design processes, we conducted a two-step model grounded design talk; (2) Conduct a thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006), partitioned the MGDT segment by theme, and reported each theme’s share of total MGDT (theme time/total MGDT time). In this way, we can clarify not only which models were used, but but also how teachers used model-anchored reasoning during curriculum design.

Teacher 1: Design through high spatial flexibility Teacher 1 demonstrated the highest MGDT rate (86%) while designing a three-hour class session using a model with highly mobile furniture. By repositioning furniture and reconfiguring the space, he demonstrated a sustained level of model-grounded reasoning that explored the use of movable elements as a means to support his pedagogical approach.

Theme 1: Exploring underused zones as learning  spaces (8 instances, 28% of MGDT). Teacher 1 repeatedly examined areas of the classroom that he had seen as marginal, trying to reframe them as intentional learning spaces. By strategically repositioning furniture, he identified zones that students might perceive as casual conversation areas, explaining, "This part is the most underused space; people see it as casual conversation, but we need to be intentional about this being a learning space. These are on wheels, so I may reposition them." By physically moving sofas and other mobile furniture in the model, he tested how these zones could support specific pedagogical activities, transforming spatial potential into instructional opportunity. Theme 2: Decentralizing the classroom via furniture moves and multi-screens (9 instances, 25% of MGDT). Teacher 1's most sustained design focus was disrupting the traditional front-oriented classroom. This theme focused on the problem that a single screen or podium in a central location inevitably creates a "front" that inhibits teacher mobility and student engagement. As he proposed, "If there were three screens around the room, I wouldn't have a front. With one screen or a podium, it keeps fronting the classroom." The model enabled him to move freely throughout the space without automatically establishing a hierarchical center by distributing screens and rearranging furniture. In this iteration of his design, physical manipulation of model elements informed his exploration of decentralized inquiry-based learning.

Theme 3: Aligning routines and intent with instructional purpose  (2 instances, 10% of MGDT). This theme addressed a critical tension between layout flexibility and student well-being. Teacher 1 recognized that constant room changes could cause student anxiety, so he balanced stable routines with adaptable rearrangements. Further, he emphasized the importance of making instructional reasoning behind spatial choices visible to preservice teachers. As he noted, "When the room looks different every time, it creates anxiety. We should keep routines, and be explicit: tell stu dents why today's setup fits the activity, so they learn the instructional intent behind our design." This meta-level reflection illustrated how the model-prompted thinking not only about spatial arrangement but also about communicating design rationale to students. Teacher 2: Design under spatial constraints Teacher 2 demonstrated a slightly lower MGDT rate (67%), manifesting distinctly different design strategies shaped by spatial constraints. Unlike Teacher 1, who had access to a highly flexible model, Teacher 2 worked 1954 with a classroom model where most furniture was fixed in place. While designing a 90-minute class activity, his interaction with the model illustrated three salient themes that revealed how tangible representations supported instructional design despite, and in response to, limited flexibility or configurability of the classroom space. Theme 1: Customized representation: Objects as content proxies  (6 instances, 10% of MGDT). With major furniture elements fixed, Teacher 2 strategically repurposed surplus model pieces to represent instructional content rather than spatial elements. For instance, he designated an extra desk to represent board games, a spare couch to stand for a mixed-reality game station, and a room divider to represent an interactive screen game activity. By transforming surplus objects into content placeholders, Teacher 2 effectively expanded the representational capacity of the constrained model, demonstrating adaptive thinking in design tool use. Theme 2: Versioning design under time constraints (4 iterations, 45% of MGDT). Teacher 2 engaged in iterative versioning to reconcile the complexity of his multi-station workshop with a 90-minute time limit. He revised the activity flow four times, using the physical placement of objects as a visual mechanism for scheduling and scope control. Each iteration involved adjusting the duration, sequence, or content of learning stations. As he stated, "Ten or fifteen minutes to see/learn... then 10– 15 minutes to discuss... then switch... then design," accompanied by finger-tracing actions on the model to reference stations and timing, calibrating pedagogical ambition against practical limits.

Theme 3: Representing student movement in tight space  (8 instances, 20% of MGDT). To address circulation challenges in the spatially constrained classroom, Teacher 2 used chair models as student proxies to simulate movement patterns. By physically moving chairs from station to station, he tested whether students could efficiently rotate through the workshop's three learning zones within the time allocated. This embodied simulation helped him identify potential bottlenecks and refine the activity sequence: "Maybe the students could move from one station to another station... 15 minutes at a station... then change... pick a second table... we cando three." By transforming abstract logistical concerns such as student flow, timing, and spatial density into tangible, manipulable problems, Teacher 2 was able to test and adjust them in real time. Cross-case analysis There were three practices used by both teachers when engaging with physical models. First, object redefinition: both repurposed model pieces to meet design needs (e.g., using room dividers as screen proxies). Second, scenario simulation: both arranged furniture to ground instructional activities in specific zones, testing feasibility through localized spatial configurations. Third, iterative positioning: both used physical repositioning as design iteration, with each adjustment corresponding to revised thinking. The key difference lay in how furniture mobility shaped design focus. Teacher 1, with a highly configurable room, explored spatial configurations to test pedagogical approaches. Physical furniture represented itself, while content was verbalized. His desig n centered on how different layouts (decentralized arrangements, multi-screens) would affect student engagement. Teacher 2, constrained by low mobility, focused on content representation through objects. He repurposed surplus pieces to stand for instructional activities (board games, VR stations), using the model as a curricular planning tool while leveraging limited movable elements (chairs) to consider student circulation. High mobility afforded configuration-focused thinking; low mobility prompted content-focused thinking, with both teachers adapting the tool's representational capacity to their constraints. This study demonstrates how physical classroom models function as tangible thinking tools that support teachers' spatial-pedagogical reasoning during curriculum design. By physically manipulating model elements, teachers visualized and simulated instructio nal flows, making the complex interplay between pedagogical approaches, spatial configurations, student movement, and content delivery concrete and testable. The hands-on nature of the model appeared to reduce design fixation, enabling iterative "what-if" prototyping that encouraged exploration over premature commitment. Critically, our cross-case findings reveal that furniture mobility shapes design focus: high mobility prompted configuration-focused thinking about spatial pedagogy, while constrained mobil ity redirected attention toward content representation and temporal sequencing. Some limitations of this study include its small sample size, which clearly limits the generalizability of the findings. Future work should examine these patterns among more diverse teachers, subjects, and institutional contexts. Our analysis focused on design sessions rather than implementation outcomes; longitudinal studies could allow tracking whether designs supported by physical models actually improve classroom practice and learning experience.  In spite of these constraints, the operationalization of  MGDT and the identification of furniture mobility as a contextual variable provide a foundation for further investigations. In the future, hybrid physical-digital systems might combine tangible manipulation with computational affordances for simulation and documentation.
