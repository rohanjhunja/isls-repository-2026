# Estimating Teacher Orchestration Load in Co-located and Online CSCL: Multimodal Data Analysis

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teacher orchestration load has been an area of interest in the field of Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) as it reflects the real-time cognitive and emotional burden teachers experience. Orchestrating CSCL activities, especially in differen t learning environments, may impose stress on teachers. This study investigates teacher orchestration load when using a scripting tool, focusing on two learning settings: co-located and online. Multimodal data combined self-reports with physiological measures to capture teacher orchestration load. In this context, electrodermal activity (EDA) was used to capture physiological indicators of teacher orchestration load. EDA peaks corresponded with self-reported stressful moments, serving as an additional indicator for estimating orchestration load. Results revealed higher orchestration load in co-located settings compared to online. Moreover, factors such as the number of students and task type affect teacher orchestration load in both settings. Understanding these factors can support the design of teacher-supporting technologies that may assist in balancing teacher workload.

In the domain of Technology-Enhanced Learning (TEL) and CSCL, orchestration is the real-time management of classroom activities using various technological tools (Dillenbourg et al., 2011; Giano & Patton, 2023). This involves the real-time adjustment and coordination of the technological tools, resources, tasks, and social design used to effectively address the evolving needs of students and adapt to the dynamics of the learning environment. This study focuses on orchestration as the run-time coordination of CSCL activities by teachers. Moreover, real-time orchestration may impose a considerable burden on teachers, including cognitive, decision-making, technological, emotional, and physical dimensions. Understanding the orchestration is crucial as it could i mpact the effectiveness of the learning environment. This brings us to the concept of teacher orchestration load, which is “the effort necessary for the teacher – and other actors – to conduct learning activities'' (Cuendet et al., 2013). Despite its importance, estimating orchestration load remains challenging. Earlier studies often treated it as a “black box” (Amarasinghe et al., 2021a) due to the lack of valid measurement tools. Some used intrusive behaviors, while others focused mainly on co-located settings. More recent work has adopted multimodal data approaches, integrating physiological, behavioral, and self-reported data, to gain richer insights into orchestration processes (Prieto et al., 2015a; Crespi et al., 2022; Hakami et al., 2024). Building on this line of research, the present study uses non-invasive wearable sensors to collect Electrodermal Activity (EDA) data as a physiological indicator of teacher stress and workload. EDA, which measures changes in skin conductance linked to emotional arousal, has been widely used in health, sports, and education research but rarely applied to teachers. To contextualize physiological signals, the study triangulates EDA peaks with qualitative data, teachers’ self-reported stressful moments, observation notes, and dashboard screen recordings, to interpret what triggers orchestration load during CSCL activities. Therefore, the study aims to address a gap in the literature by extending the investigation of Orchestration Load in online learning environments and demonstrating how multimodal analytics can reveal underlying factors contributing to teacher stress and workload. This examination becomes more potent with the growing use of multimodal data analytics in educational technology research (Blikstein & Worsley, 2016; Sharma & Giannakos, 2020). The main research question the study aims to address is:  What can multimodal data reveal about teacher orchestration load in co-located and online CSCL settings? This study employed a mixed-method approach, following a single-subject research design (SSRD) to examine teacher orchestration load in scripted CSCL activities across co-located and online settings. By combining quantitative physiological data with qualitative teacher perceptions, it provided a comprehensive view of 155 orchestration load as both an objective and subjective phenomenon. The SSRD was chosen because orchestration load varies by individual and context, allowing detailed analysis of a single teacher’s experience (a female teacher from a Spanish public university) across 20 CSCL sessions without needing a separate control group. This design enabled in-depth exploration of how orchestration load changes across settings and what factors influence it. Procedures and materials We collected multimodal data while the teacher was monitoring the CSCL activities in both co-located and online settings (N = 20). Objective data collected included electrodermal activity (EDA) from a Shimmer3 GSR+ sensor, also known as Galvanic Skin Response (GSR). This study focuses on EDA peaks as a physiological indicator of increasing the orchestration load in CSCL activities. The sensor was strapped (see Figure 1) to the teacher's non-dominant hand, specifically, the left forearm, and electrodes were placed on the inner side of the left wrist (Dooren et al., 2012). The sensor monitored the teacher non-invasively. Moreover, log data extracted from the orchestration tool’s database was collected to extract more details about the activities. Dashboard screen recordings were collected when the teacher orchestrated the CSCL activities to analyze the teacher's actions using the dashboard. Observation notes and responses to post-activity questionnaires were also collected to collect details on stressful moments. The Ethical approval for this study was obtained from a university ethics review board. The orchestration tool used in this study is a web-based CSCL tool that follows the pyramid CLFP based on scripted collaborative learning activities (name removed for blind review). It enables teachers to create CSCL activities in diverse learning settings (co-located, online, and hybrid). During a CSCL activity, students individually respond to the task provided by the teacher. Then, they  are automatically assigned to groups to rate the other answers from students. Lastly, they collaborate with their group members and agree to write appropriate answers. The tool has a teacher-facing dashboard (see Figure 2) designed for teachers to control and monitor the activity flow in real-time and take pedagogical actions. The dashboard also shows real-time students’ activity participation and contains controls that enable activity adjustment (i.e., increase time, pause activity, skip phase, and end activity when needed). The dashboard has a  flagging feature that allows teachers to flag (mark) student answers and present them in the dashboard interface for discussion (Dieckmann et al., 2022). In addition, the dashboard has three tabs (see Figure 3): Student Task Tab, which displays the task provided to the students, Response Related Tab, which presents the individual answer from each student, the highly voted answer, and the final answer of a group, depending on the phase of the activity, and Participation Related Tab, which presents the participation differences between groups. The dashboard visualizes students’ CSCL activity participation in real-time and is aimed at supporting teachers’ real-time regulation of the activity by showing relevant information (e.g., group participation and collaboration or task progress) and alerting teachers (i.e., using specific alerts triggered by learning analytics indicators) on relevant interventions. ## Data analysis

157 ConsensysPRO software was used to extract EDA data from the sensor. Then, a tool called EDA Explorer was used (Taylor et al., 2015) t o automatically identify EDA peaks. The EDA peaks reflect the increased emotional arousal experienced under stress (Salim et al., 2020). Focusing on EDA peaks as a potential indicator of increasing the orchestration load, as it provides an objective measure of the teacher's physiological response to the demands of managing CSCL activities. Therefore, this study correlates each EDA peak with the stressful moments reported by the teacher.

Consequently, the stressful moments (Appendix A) are analyzed through a qualitative content analysis approach. These stressful moments were grouped into three categories, namely, Technological difficulties (e.g., Dashboard Problems), Actions by students (e.g., Noises from the students or Answers), and Time-related issues (Hakami et al., 2023). Through the analysis of the data, an additional category emerged: Teacher-related, and this category is related to teachers' actions, such as issues in the activity design. The other data (i.e., dashboard screen recordings and observation notes) provided additional details of the context, helping us to identify information that was not captured in self-reported data alone. Therefore, these stressful moments were analyzed in detail using the dashboard screen recordings and the observation notes, linking them with EDA data. Correlation analysis was performed to investigate factors affecting teacher orchestration load. The variables used in the analysis were relevant to the teacher orchestration load in both co-located and online settings. Specifically, a dependent variable extracted from the sensor (EDA peaks), and three independent variables extracted from log data (number of students, activity duration, and task type used in the activity). The tasks used in CSCL activities are categorized based on Bloom's taxonomy (i.e., knowledge, comprehension, analysis, synthesis, and evaluation) (Bloom, 1956). All variables were chosen based on a previous study (Hakami et al., 2024), which identified these variables as factors that affect teacher orchestration load. In the following, we present the main results obtained in this study to answer the research question: What can multimodal data reveal about teacher orchestration load in co-located and online CSCL settings? To address the research question, we conducted two different analyses. First, we identified the stressful moments and triangulated them with EDA peaks. Second, we conducted the correlation analysis between EDA and contextual factors that emerged from the learning design (activity's duration, and task type) and context, such as the number of students who participated in sessions, to investigate their influence on orchestration load in CSCL. The results of these analyses are presented in the following sections.

Stressful moments and EDA analysis In Tables 2 and 3, we present the results related to teacher-reported stressful moments from the self-reported questionnaire, along with EDA signals highlighting the related stressful moments. First, considering the responses received to the self-reported questionnaire, it was found that there were nine stressful moments in the co-located setting and five stressful moments in the online setting. In the co-located setting, teachers reported three stressful moments related to student actions, such as managing classroom disruptions. Two stressful moments related to the technological issues and difficulties using the orchestration tool on a tablet. In contrast, these issues were absent in the online setting; instead, there were two moments related to teacher-related issues, such as configuration problems (forgetting to publish the activity). Lastly, in both settings, the teacher experienced stressful moments related to managing activity time, such as increasing or reducing the time for activities, specifically four moments in the co-located settings and three in the online settings. We detected a total of 402 EDA peaks in the co-located setting and 46 EDA peaks in the online setting. The higher number of peaks in the co-located setting suggests that the teacher experiences more frequent and intense stress in co-located classrooms when  compared to online settings. In this study, EDA peaks are often correlated with self-reported stressful moments, such as managing student answers, handling time constraints, or dealing with classroom noise. Also, the difficulty in pressing some buttons in the dashboard was corroborated by both the EDA data and screen recordings in sessions 5 and 6 (co-located setting). In the Online setting, although EDA peaks were less pronounced (i.e., in sessions 4 and 6), the other sessions still aligned with reported stressful moments. Factors influencing teacher orchestration load The study aimed to investigate how teacher orchestration loads differ in each learning setting (co-located and online). However, due to the differences in the number of students (295 vs. 91), session duration (103 vs. 68 minutes), and the types of tasks us ed, we further investigate whether these factors may influence teacher orchestration load in each setting.  Moreover, a previous study found that these factors (number of students, duration of the activity, and task type) affect teacher orchestration load i n a co-located setting (Hakami et al., 2024). The current study investigates whether these factors influence the online setting. Also, compare their impact between co-located and online settings; thereby, correlation analysis (Table 4) was used to study the effect of the three factors.

By linking EDA data with dashboard screen recording, observation notes, and self-reports, the study identified stress triggers and contextualized moments of high orchestration load. The findings show that multimodal analytics is a valid and comprehensive approach to assessing teacher orchestration load in scripted CSCL contexts. The comparative analysis between co-located and online settings revealed distinct sources of stress: In co-located settings, teachers frequently reported stress related to Actions by students and Technological difficulties categories that were not in the online setting. The physical presence in a classroom needs immediate interventions for disruptions, managing classroom noise, or interacting with students, adding to the load on teachers, especially when controlling CSCL activities. Additionally, the usability of the orchestration tool's dashboard on a tablet in the co-located setting posed challenges. The teacher reported difficulty using the dashboard, which may be due to the tablet’s touch sensitivity or screen size, leading to an added load on the teacher during activity management. A recent study also reported that technical issues affect teacher orchestration load in collaborative hybrid settings (Ortega-Arranz et al., 2024). In contrast, in the online setting, the stressful moments were related to the Teacher-related category, mainly due to configuration issues, such as forgetting to publish the activ ity, which prevented students from starting the task and may cause stress for the teacher. The Time-related issues were a common source of load in both settings. Moreover, the study found that teacher orchestration load was higher in co-located settings. The division of teacher attention between physical and digital spaces might contribute to an increase in teacher stress in the colocated setting (Hakami et al., 2023). Understanding the specific triggers of orchestration load can inform the design of more supportive CSCL tools and dashboards.

In addition, the study also identified three contextual factors, i.e., number of students, activity duration, and task type, that influence orchestration load beyond the learning setting (co-located vs. online). Specifically, the number of students showed a positive relationship with EDA peaks, indicating that larger class sizes increase orchestration load. This supports prior studies showing that more students heighten stress (Huang et al., 2022) and classroom management challenges (Biggs, 1999).  Regarding task type,  our results indicated that task type was found to be a factor affecting the load in both co-located and online settings. Previous research by Amarasinghe et al. (2021b) has shown that certain task types may demand more targeted teacher interventions to achieve learning goals, thereby increasing workload. Finally, there was a difference in the duration factor, which was found to be a significant factor affecting teacher load in co-located settings when compared to online settings. This difference supports that in co-located classrooms, longer activity durations often involve direct interaction with students, managing real-time classroom dynamics, moving around the classroom, or addressing immediate 161 student needs. Thus, the activity's duration could impact the orchestration load in co-located settings (Hakami et al., 2024).

This study built upon the previous studies from (Amarasinghe et al., 2021c; Crespi et al., 2022), which proposed triangulating EDA with self-reported data to assess the teacher orchestration load in CSCL activities. The finding shows that the triangulation of EDA with self-reported data can be a valid approach for estimating teacher orchestration load in both co-located and online settings. This was achieved by analyzing the stressful moments reported by the teacher in the questionnaire and correlating them with EDA peaks extracted from the sensor. Overall, the EDA peaks are often associated with the stressful moments in which the teacher reported increased stress or the load in the CSCL activities. Thus, the findings indicate that EDA was a promising physiological indicator to estimate teacher orchestration load in CSCL activities. Furthermore, the findings provide insight into the differences in teacher orchestration load between the settings. The study highlights the greater orchestration load experienced by teachers in co-located CSCL settings. This study highlights the distinct challenges and stress faced by teachers in various learning environments, providing insights for designing more effective and supportive CSCL tools and dashboards.  In addition, we have also identified some factors (i.e., the number of students, duration of the activity, and task type) that influence teacher orchestration load.

While this study is focused on a single subject, some of the teacher workload observed aligns with previous research. For example, the number of students may impact the teachers' physiological stress (Huang et al., 2022) and increase classroom management challenges (Biggs, 1999). In addition, the technical issues affect teacher orchestration load (Ortega-Arranz et al., 2024). This shows that certain dependencies in orchestration load, such as managing real-time student interactions, handling technological difficulties, teacher-related issues, or managing time, may be consistent in different teaching contexts. The current study provides an example of how, in this specific case, orchestration load dependencies emerged based on student number, task type, and activity duration. As more cases are studied, these insights can inform the development of orchestration tools that support the needs of different learning environments.

The study acknowledges several limitations. It focuses on a single female teacher, which limits generalizability and overlooks possible gender, experience, and familiarity effects on orchestration load. The study also examines only one collaborative learning flow pattern (pyramid CLFP); therefore, findings may not apply to other collaborative designs. Reliance on self-reported questionnaires introduces potential bias; future research should include qualitative methods like interviews or focus groups for deeper insights. Additionally, future work should explore hybrid learning contexts that combine online and face-to-face teaching, as these add complexity to orchestration. Finally, understanding how variables such as class size, activity duration, and task type affect teacher stress can guide interventions to reduce orchestration load.
