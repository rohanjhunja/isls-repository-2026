# Teachers' Experiences of Learning Analytics Support for Learning Design in Higher Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores the role of the Balanced Learning Design Planning tool in supporting higher education teachers' design practices. The tool and concept aim to enhance learning design by focusing on learning outcomes, student workload, and constructive alignment. Using design-based research principles, this study investigates teachers' experiences through stimulated recall interviews and abductive thematic analysis. Findings indicated that the tool could offer support in various aspects of design thinking.

Findings indicated that the tool could offer support in various aspects of design thinking. Design is an essential part of teachers’ work. Teachers’ design work involves the planning and preparation that teachers undertake before the academic session starts (Bennett et al., 2018). This includes deciding on the topics to be covered, determining how to present the content, planning the learning activities for students, and figuring out how to assess student learning. However, teachers’ learning design work extends further by problematizing learning opportunities, incorporating options and challenges for students, predicting student needs and reactions, continuously striving for improvement, and working within contextual limitations and influences to meet learning goals (Bennett et al., 2018). Designing for learning also involves collecting feedback, making adjustments during teaching, enhancing future versions of the course, and tailoring designs to fit different contexts (Bennett et al., 2018.) Recent discussions in learning design research are framing teachers’ design work within the larger context of the design field (Asensio-Pérez et al., 2015; Bennett et al., 2017). A teacher acts essentially as a designer by identifying educational challenges they want to address with their students and creating learning experiences to solve these issues (Lockyer et al., 2016). Bennet et al. (2017) discovered that the design work of university teachers shares characteristics with design thinking found in other disciplinary fields such as architecture and engineering. Design thinking is generally described as a process that combines analysis and creativity, allowing individuals to experiment, develop and test models, collect feedback, and make improvements (Razzouk & Shute, 2012). Micheli et al. (2019) identified 10 attributes of design thinking in a systematic literature review: abductive reasoning, ability to visualize, blending analysis and intuition, creativity and innovation, gestalt view, interdisciplinary collaboration, iteration and experimentation, problem solving, tolerance for ambiguity and failure, and user-centeredness and involvement. Bennett et al. (2017) suggested that substantial research and practical applications are needed to improve design thinking and practice in higher education. We need more information on how design thinking attributes relate to teachers’ learning design practices when utilizing learning analytics in their work.

Divjak et al. (2022) introduced a comprehensive learning design concept and tool Balanced Learning Design Planning (BDP), driven by the requirements recognized by professionals in higher education. The purpose of this concept and tool is to apply current research discoveries and theories to support Balanced Learning Design Planning (Divjak et al., 2022). The concept and the BDP tool provide scaffolding to the process of learning design by placing significant emphasis on learning outcomes and student workload, alignment of study program and course level learning outcomes, ensuring constructive alignment and assessment validity, enhancing learning design through the utilization of learning analytics, and offering adaptability for various educational settings and teaching methods. Recently, the practical assessment of the BDP tool within real-world higher education environments has been identified as an area for future research (Divjak et al., 2022). This preliminary study aims to answer the following question: How is the BDP tool supporting learning design practices according to higher education teachers?

## Procedure

The research is conducted by applying the principles of Design Based Research (Brown, 1992; Collins, 1992). Design Based Research is here viewed as a systematic but flexible method intended to advance educational practices through iterative analysis, design, development, and implementation, based on researcher and practitioner collaboration in real-world settings, and heading to contextually-sensitive design principles and theories (Sawyer, 2022; Wang & Hannafin, 2005). Stimulated recall interviews (Vesterinen et al., 2010) were held for nine higher education teachers, and the transcribed recordings were subjected to abductive thematic during which the interviewer shared the plan prepared by the teacher using the BDP tool, providing genuine stimuli and cues to reflect on the experiences of using the tool. The interviews lasted for about one hour. The teaching experience of the interviewees ranged from newly started to several years (1-20 years). For some, teaching was the main task, while for others, it was only a small part of their job description. The teachers worked in the fields of education, economics, humanities, and technology. This preliminary analysis started by posing the question: how do teachers describe the benefits and challenges of using the BDP tool? Then, expressions (unit of themed into similar groups, and descriptive names were given to these groups, seeking explanations or confirmations from Micheli et al. (2019) attributes of design thinking to support the interpretations. Preliminary results in this study indicate that when using the BDP tool, teachers were experiencing support for collaboration, gestalt view, ability to visualize, creativity, student involvement and iteration (see Table 1). This year’s conference invites researchers to embrace five central aspects of education and educational research:

(1) embracing utopias, (2) agency, (3) imagination, (4) speculation, and (5) futures thinking. Nørgård and Holflod

(2024) position these five aspects in relation to education and technology. Utopias point to an ideal learning

environment, agency focuses on how humans and non-human actors design such an ideal environment, imagination is defined as how humans widen thinking around what may be possible, speculation allows for the numerous possibilities in considering how things can be different, and futures thinking points to “that-whichought-to-be” (Nørgård & Holflod, 2024, p. 2, emphasis in original). This poster investigates how the five central aspects intersect with Learning Sciences and learning environments research to promote five design principles:

(1) including voices in defining and embracing utopias in the development of learning environments;

(2) analyzing the sociomaterial: human and non-human agency within our learning environments;

(3) affording imagination in our learning environments;

(4) adding speculation in the reflection on our learning environments;

(5) considering that-which-ought-to-be and futures thinking in our learning environments.

This poster theorizes the five central aspects in relation to the design of learning environments and aims to engage researchers whose work overlaps with sociomaterial and learning environments frameworks. Design principles Design Principle 1: Including polyvocality in defining and embracing utopias in the development of new learning environments From controlling interaction to conditioning movement (Nespor, 1997), learning spaces are infused with power (Orr, 1993). As such, when embracing utopias, it is critical to ask, whose utopias are included? To consider a broadening voice, Rook et al. (2015) advocate for having a learning theorist and someone embedded in the Learning Sciences in the designs of spaces and environments, but that is not enough. Approaches such as Afrofuturism, have shown promise for providing learners a critical voice in designing spaces towards reframing them more inclusively and representatively (Holbert et al., 2020). As such, researchers should consider including additional voices such as community members and students, but also attend to the possibility that inclusion can lead to exclusionary practices. For instance, bringing in traditionally minoritized populations (e.g., Black girls in STEM), may ignore the multiple intersectionalities that they inhabit as individuals, resulting in artificial generalizations, that exclude their more diverse voices (Constanza-Chock, 2020). Design principle 2: Analyzing the sociomaterial: human and non-human agency within our learning environments Sociomateriality involves analyzing learning as an entanglement of learners and non-human actors in a network, providing emerging theories and new conceptualizations of learning and how they drive methodological developments (Damşa et al., 2023; Fenwick, 2015; Latour, 2005; Barad, 2003). Learning is an emergent activity that involves interactions among human and non-human actors such as tools, resources, and norms of behavior (Tietjen et al., 2023). To consider the sociomaterial, researchers can apply analytical frames to understand how non-human actors afford and contribute to emergent activity, public sensemaking, collaborative benchmarking, and learning residue (Kumar & Tissenbaum, 2022; Rook et al., 2024; Tietjen et al., 2023). Design principle 3: Affording imagination in our learning environments Imagination can be framed as a process of relating to people, institutions, and other forms of power in order to project into the present and future (Khasnabish & Haiven, 2012). From this perspective, imagination is essential to learning and creating new futures for people and communities. Constraining this process are systems of power of oppression (e.g. white supremacy) that limit the kinds of emotions, ideas, and practices that individuals can participate in (McCausland, in press). This constraining of participation hinders the imaginative proleptic thinking needed to produce new kinds of environments, learning, and the futures they make possible (Gutierrez, 2019). To design more imaginative learning environments, we suggest cultivating an improvisational ethos, or “a shared commitment to affirming and validating the existence and experience of others” (Tanner & McCloskey, 2023, p. 6). For preservice teachers, cultivating an improvisational ethos produced emotions and sensemaking that were productive for helping them take risks, question, and play around with new ways of being and teaching (McCausland & McDonald, 2023; 2024). For students, allowing them to creatively explore making for themselves and their communities has resulted in them creating more personally meaningful designs (Bawankule & Tissenbaum, 2023). Affording imagination by finding new ways to have people relate to one another, the material, and ideological systems around them, holds possibilities for making visions of the future a reality. Design principle 4: Adding speculation in the reflection on our learning environments Speculating on the design of learning environments encourages reflection on the perspectives embodied in the design itself (Cleveland, 2009; Monahan, 2002). Methodological approaches, such as interaction geography (Shapiro et al., 2017), provide opportunities for visualizing teaching and learning experiences across space-time. In addition to reflecting on space and interaction, interaction geography can craft opportunities to reflect with learners, teachers, and designers. Such representations of teacher-learner movement, proximity, and pedagogical encounters offer potential to recognize the utility of learning environments, and, significantly, speculate on possible learning futures. In turn, these speculations foster opportunities for educators and learners to design future-oriented learning spaces.

Design principle 5: Considering that-which-ought-to-be and futures thinking in our learning environments Future learning environments such as those identified in Rook et al. (2020) have the potential to foster desired, that-which-ought-to-be learning communities by bringing together individuals and enabling shared affective responses, holding community together, and moving community together. Designers also can apply that-whichought-to-be as a framework. Zuboff’s (2023) concept of Who Knows, Who Decides, and Who Decides Who Decides helps designers engage all when considering that-which-ought-to-be. Even with good intentions, persons that are considering that-which-ought-to-be may exclude marginalized individuals. If marginalized voices are not included as partners throughout the design process, designers only further this exclusion (Costanza-Chock, 2020). When paired with designs for new learning spaces, the five central aspects of education promoted by the ISLS theme enable productive, new design principles that allow for designers and developers of environments to embrace utopias, agency, imagination, speculation, and futures thinking. These five design principles move the conversation beyond including diverse voices to those voices that have been excluded traditionally from the design of spaces—community members and marginalized individuals. In other words, in the conversation of imagined futures, we can look to a future that invites those marginalized to play a central role in the design of spaces. Instead of designing for people or for specific pedagogical practices, we candesign with new voices that promote new ways to learn to address powers of oppression, such as improv. We can also explore new methods to visualize activity that enable us as designers to reflect with learners, teachers, and designers and apply analytical frames to understand the role of non-human actors. Combined, these design principles prove to be useful tools to move our field forward towards a future that-which-ought-to-be.

Knowledge Building is an educational model that empowers students to collaborate effectively in enhancing their understanding of a subject (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2016). Students typically engage in hybrid environments supported by Knowledge Forum (KF) to transcend classroom boundaries. During collaboration, students’ question, inquiry, and refine ideas shared in the KF to address authentic knowledge problems (Gutiérrez-Braojos et al., 2024). Scientific literature provides evidence of the benefits of concurrent and reflective assessment sessions in the knowledge-building process. These sessions help generate a metadiscourse on how to coordinate individual efforts to improve shared knowledge (Tong & Chan, 2023). Researchers and practitioners have collaboratively developed technologies to assess collaborative knowledge building in KF (Zhu & Kim, 2017). While several widely used tools exist, we think that, staying true to KB principles, there is always room for improvement. While communities go through similar stages (de Jong, 2020), they differ in their initial conditions and the ways in which they navigate these stages. Therefore, we think this margin lies in creating software that does not provide predefined dashboards but allows users to choose among them to create a dashboard tailored to the circumstances the educator deems most appropriate for the community. This poster explores whether KBAT supports the progression of discourse within the Knowledge Forum, as well as students’ perceptions of its practical use and how these perceptions can inform future improvements.

## Method

Participants were 42 students enrolled in an educational research program at the University of Granada, Spain. The KB was implemented in a hybrid format, supported by Knowledge Forum (KF) and reflective assessment dashboards generated by the KBAT (Figure 1 & 2). Dashboard data enabled students to reflect on their participation, challenges, and progress, proposing improvements and recognizing shifts toward collaboration and community leadership. On the one hand, to examine whether KBAT fosters discourse progression, student contributions on KF were analyzed across three cycles using a coding scheme. On another hand, data were collected through a Likert scale (four dimensions: awareness, motivation, ethics, technical aspects; adapted from Gutiérrez-Braojos, 2023) and three open-ended questions to identify strengths supporting KB and suggest improvements. environments to support human metacognition and socio-metacognition for experts. However, the study emphasized the need for further exploration in natural settings, i.e., the classroom. Student interaction with chatbots relies heavily on their ability to take intentional actions. This aligns with more general arguments in the CSCL literature. Productive dialogues as part of computational design for learning activities cannot be taken for granted (e.g., Baker et al., 2021). In the educational policy field scholars have started to conceptualize what GAI tensions may include (Warschauer et al., 2023). We argue here that empirically oriented studies are needed based on instructional models tailored to various kinds of teaching and domain-specific needs with GAI in classroom settings. The research questions are: RQ1: How do teachers facilitate middle school students' learning processes when using adapted generative chatbots? RQ2: What are the tensions between using the chatbots as tools for learning about GAI as a tool and learning in a domain?

The project is defined as Design-Based Research (DBR), theoretically grounded in a sociocultural approach to learning (e.g., McKenney & Reeves, 2018). Sociocultural approaches to learning are connected to sociolinguistic stance taking, which can be understood as a social act of discursive positioning within learning processes (Bakhtin, 1986; Du Bois, 2007; Park et al., 2024). Given the need for comprehensive empirical knowledge about the use of GAI in K–12 education, the first phase of the research design is exploratory. Figure 1 LAA Project Timeline, 2024–2025 The data collection includes video recordings of teacher instructions and student interaction, interviews of teachers and students, written lesson plans, and screen recordings of students’ work. To get an overview of the different data types we generated and refined initial themes in the data corpus as parts of the six phases in thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2022). We identified multiple recurring themes, including but not limited to: lesson planning, task structure and content, teacher modelling of GAI interaction, students interactional moves with GAI, and level of user knowledge and experience. In this poster we limit the analysis to the learning activities and the purpose of using the adapted chatbot.

During the initial cases (Winter & Spring 2024), some of the students confirmed that the chatbots were useful as tools for generating ideas and for explaining and shortening AI-generated text. This pattern aligns at the surface level with previously described expert use (Borge et al., 2024). However, when we asked students to demonstrate interactions with the chatbots, we were unable to find many examples beyond two or three sequences of conversational prompts across the different schools.

The analysis indicates an overly high degree of complexity between learning the chatbots as tools and using them when working with school subjects – a set of tensions (Warschauer et al., 2023). The relationship between a generic tool and subject-oriented learning in principle can elicit a tension in teachers-students’ interaction. What is the focus of the activities and what are the conditions (the tool) for the students to accomplish the task at hand? Based on our analysis, we drew a distinction between three types of teaching.

Figure 2:

The Model Describes Tension in Teaching About, With, and Through GAI as Transitional Processes The analysis reveals that the data corpus contained no explicit distinction between teaching about, with, or through GAI during the initial cases. During Fall 2024, complexity was reduced after the teachers were introduced to the model during a workshop. As part of a DBR project, formal interviews were conducted in Fall 2024 with four teachers assessing the model in Figure 2. A recurring theme in the teacher interview data, is that the model was both tangible and relevant and could be used in the development of lesson plans. Based on these initial findings, teachers should make instructional decisions based not just on whether the lesson(s) aims to represent teaching about, with, or through GAI.

Our data analysis indicates that teachers could also consider the core idea of the lesson(s): Does the core idea comprise a focus on GAI or domain-specific content? To reduce the complexity for students, we suggest that teachers should first teach about GAI through tasks that support students’ understanding of the technology. This would clarify the focus of learning processes in lessons, and over time help students develop knowledge and skills for interacting with GAI. The instructional hypothesis proposed above will be examined further in upcoming cases in order to develop a robust empirical grounding for how adapted GAI can be used productively in classrooms.

Based on analysis of the data corpus from classroom studies of generative AI in Norwegian classrooms, we encourage teachers to make instructional decisions when planning their AI-mediated lessons by considering whether they want to focus on teaching about, with, or through chatbots in their lesson.
