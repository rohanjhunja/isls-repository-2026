# Cross-disciplinary mentorship: A First Step Toward Innovative Action Research

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This qualitative study employs an autoethnographic approach to examine the years-long process of transforming a mentor –mentee relationship into a collaborative partnership centered on shared goals for students within a school community. The reflection draw s on the first author’s enduring professional friendship with a current high school principal, who originally served as her mentor assigned by a school district. The study highlights how teachers can cultivate a supportive professional community, encourage one another in pursuing personal and professional goals, and leverage their collective knowledge and experience to support the youth within their school community better. This study focuses on the author’s reflective dialogue drawn from several years of conversations with a colleague and friend, exploring how their shared vision led to an action research project aimed at improving students’ math learning experience in a school characterized by low socioeconomic status.

The study highlights how teachers can cultivate a supportive professional community, encourage one another in pursuing personal and professional goals, and leverage their collective knowledge and experience to support  the youth within their school community better.  This study focuses on the author’s reflective dialogue drawn from several years of conversations with a colleague and friend, exploring how their shared vision led to an action research project aimed at improving students’ math learning experience in a school characterized by low socioeconomic status. Seoyeon (first author) met the Shelli (second author) eight years ago when Seoyeon was a new teacher at a school where Shelli had been teaching for several years and serving as a mentor. What began as a collegial, professional relationship gradually evolved into a strong and enduring partnership grounded in mutual respect and shared professional goals. Over time, this relationship became a vital source of academic and emotional support, as both authors encouraged one another to pursue advanced studies, including doctoral programs, and to take on leadership roles such as the principalship. Both found deep fulfillment in classroom teaching, which made the decision to leave that role complex and challenging. The uncertainty of new professional directions required external affirmation and encouragement, which we found through their collaborative relationship and support. In many ways, our partnership exemplified the significance of professional mentorship evolving into reciprocal support for growth and transformation. This study was motivated by a desire to explore ways to empower teachers and administrators while fostering healthy, supportive relationships that benefit both educators and students. The eight-year mentorship and friendship at the center of this reflection warrant careful examination to understand why this relationship has sustained even after both individuals left the same school, continuing to engage in ongoing conversations about education and student success. Seoyeon had experienced other mentoring relationships throughout her teaching career before meeting Shelli. She had two mentors in the past: one during her student teaching and another when she began working as a first-year teacher. Both mentoring relationships were arranged with experienced teachers in the same discipline, mathematics. Matching a mentor and mentee is often compared with “blind dates; there would be a small probability that the match would be successful, but more attention to the selection phase would raise this probability above chance level” (Chao et al., 1992, p. 634). Several studies have stated  that effective mentorship can be highly beneficial, and scholars have attempted to identify factors that contribute to successful mentor – mentee matching. Although many factors  such as age difference, gender, or cultural background  have been examined, research has not identified a single factor that consistently predicts successful mentoring outcomes. While no universal formula exists for creating successful mentoring relationships (see Kutsyuruba et al., 2019; Rajuan et al., 2010), intentional matching based on factors such as learning style may increase the likelihood of a productive mentoring experience. The research questions guiding this study are as follows: 1) What characteristics of a mentor –mentee partnership contribute to its longevity, mutual benefit, and the professional growth of both participants? 2) How does a reciprocal mentorship influence teaching practices, overall professional development, and the broader school community?

## Conceptual framework

This study, along with a larger project, will ultimately contribute to a grounded theory as to mentoring and collaboration, given the reflective and dialogic nature of autoethnography, duoethnography, and polyethnography. These methodologies rely heavily on reflection and dialogue as both data and process. The present study employs an autoethnographic approach, guided by Schön’s (1983) concept of the reflective practitioner.  According to 2076 Schön, traditional models of expertise assume that an expert “is presumed to know and must claim to do so, regardless of uncertainty” (1983, p. 300). In contrast, a reflective practitioner acknowledges uncertainty as an inherent part of professional practi ce and recognizes that others in the situation may also possess valuable and relevant knowledge. Reflective practitioners view uncertainty and complexity as opportunities for learning—both for themselves and for others. This study illustrates how a researcher and a school principal initiated an action research project and how their shared interests and collaborative inquiry evolved over the years. Through continuous reflection on their dialogue and experiences, they came to understand the transformative power of authentic reflection as a means for professional growth and educational change.

## Context and participants

This study involves two authors, but was primarily drafted by Seoyeon, focusing on her memories and reflective writing pieces documenting each of the past eight years. Before drafting any written reflective piece, Seoyeon revisited email exchanges and text  messages to enhance the accuracy and richness of recall. These materials, however, were used solely as memory aids. Eight reflective written notes were composed; each centered on significant events in Seoyeon’s teaching career or academic career. The focus of these reflections was to examine how the mentor provided support and how informal dialogues between the authors evolved into pivotal moments of professional decision-making.

Data source and collection In addition to the first author’s memories and eight reflective journals, field notes from visits to the high school where the second author currently serves as principal were included as supplementary data. The first school visit took place during a professional development day for tea chers, led by a mathematics educator recognized as a district-level expert in math education.

Seoyeon arrived early, had the opportunity to meet several staff members and teachers  at a high school where Shelli works, and attended the mathematics-focused professional development session, spending approximately two hours with the faculty. Further conversations were held with the school’s curriculum coach and two mathematics teachers, followed by a subsequent phone conversation with Shelli regarding her vision and plans for the academic year. These interactions informed ongoing dialogue and collaboration between the two authors around professional growth and instructional improvement.

## Data analysis plan

Data were analyzed using in vivo and descriptive coding (Saldaña, 2021) as the first cycle of grounded theory coding. The second cycle identified patterns and relationships across reflective writt en notes  and field notes. Following Saldaña’s approach, the constant comparative method was used to compare segments from eight reflective journals, each documenting a year of the authors’ evolving mentorship and friendship. This process revealed broader themes regarding the changing mentor –mentee relationship, professional collaboration, and sources of affirmation and moral support.  Because the reflections spanned multiple years, themes emerged holistically, reflecting the interconnectedness of experiences across time. During analysis, recurring keywords and patterns were highlighted through multiple readings of the texts. As comparisons continued, unexpected patterns were coded and incorporated as analytic insights. Although independent coding was performed by Seoyeon only, the analysis was supported by ongoing analytic dialogue with Shelli, who reviewed draft manuscripts, discussed emerging codes and themes, and confirmed that interpretations reflected shared experiences.  In addition, several follow-up phone conversations took place. These discussions were not treated as additional data. Instead, they served as opportunities for reflective clarification and member checking. The conversations helped recall earlier experiences and allowed Shelli to respond to the developing interpretations. This strengthened the analysis through collaborative reflection. This section presents the themes that emerged, highlighting the benefits we both perceived from our cross-disciplinary mentorship.

Theme 1: Treating a mentee as a fellow expert The mentee typically sought advice, feedback, or approval, with limited space for dialogue or innovation. As a result, there were limited opportunities for collaborative idea development, and the mentoring process 2077 predominantly focused on evaluation rather than cooperation. However, the mentorship described in this study stands out for its strength and endurance, which Seoyeon believed that it stems from mutual respect and the recognition of each individual's expertise in their school subject fields. Seoyeon’s previous mentors before Shelli were experienced mathematics teachers who generously shared pedagogical strategies and evaluated lessons, yet these interactions tended to be one-directional. In contrast, Shelli—an experienced English L anguage Arts (ELA) teacher—encouraged Seoyeon to think creatively and pursue interdisciplinary ideas. This openness allowed Seoyeon to dream or plan about designing lessons that incorporated students’ linguistic and cultural backgrounds and to seek engaging resources beyond the mathematics curriculum. Watchi ng closely how Shelli supported students’ writing development provided insights into differentiating mathematics instruction for diverse learners. The flexibility and responsiveness of ELA instruction inspired Seoyeon to explore ways of making mathematics more accessible and personally meaningful for students. Theme 2: Cross-disciplinary pairing for more ‘informal’ space for sharing ideas Even though Seoyeon’s three mentor teachers were all arranged by the  same school district within a formal mentoring program, mentorship with Shelli felt like informal mentorship. Individuals with informal mentors tend to report more positive outcomes in terms of career-related support and psychological support than those with formal mentors (Chao et al., 1992). It has been widely discussed that effective mentorship is best facilitated when mentors and mentees are located within the same building, allowing f or daily interaction and shared disciplinary mentorship is paired solely based on school subject, significant benefits may be overlooked. One example of this cross-disciplinary impact occurred when Shelli introduced Seoyeon to her school counselor recently, highlighting her innovative approach to teaching Algebra to Spanish-speaking students in their native language, which Shelli  witnessed. While some of the teachers at our  old high school questioned the feasibility of this approach, Seoyeon ’s belief in her students’ linguistic strengths proved successful, fostering collaborative learning between monolingual English-speaking students and Spanish-language-speaking students and providing an opportunity for the students to see their Spanish skills as a huge asset. This shared value of leveraging students’ assets resonated deeply with Shelli, whose school now serves a predominantly Spanishspeaking population. Seoyeon reflected the countless lunch break discussions with Shelli, the current school principal, often centered on how to design mathematics instruction that acknowledges and incorporates students’ linguistic and cultural resources.

The mentor’s openness extended beyond praise; Shelli frequently sought Seoyeon’s perspective on engaging students who disliked mathematics. These conversations often emerged organically—from Seoyeon’s helping Shelli with math-related coursework for her administrative certification to broader reflections on how math could be taught differently. What began as small moments of assistance evolved into sustained conversations about students’ mathematical experiences and effective intervention strategies. Such meaningful dialogue would have been less likely in a traditional, same-discipline mentoring context. Our relationship illustrates how genuine collaboration can flourish when two educators from distinct fields respect one another’s expertise. Theme 3: Seeing strengths and supporting growth Another key benefit of cross-disciplinary mentorship is the way it expands teachers’ understanding of students as whole learners. Seoyeon reflects that this change in perspective regarding high-performing students was very important in her teaching career. She always strives to recognize the additional knowledge and skills that her students may not demonstrate in their math exams, but which they certainly possess. Students who struggle in mathematics may thrive in other subjects, and informal conversations with colleagues from other disciplines help teachers recognize students’ broader strengths. Through frequent dialogue with ELA teachers  including Shelli, Seoyeon developed a more holistic understanding of her students, leading to more compassionate and personalized instruction. Collaborative efforts between teachers from different subjects also send powerful messages to students—that their teachers care, communicate, and are invested in their success. The two authors came from different departments, which meant there was no power struggle or departmental hierarchy affecting their relationship. In many departments, differences in teaching philosophy or seniority can hinder open dialogue, but cross-departmental mentorship allows for greater freedom of expression and innovation. Seoyeon felt that Shelli respected her professional judgment and always trusted her as a competent educator. In return, Seoyeon encouraged Shelli to pursue an interim administrative position, even in the absence of job security because Seoyeon believed that her mentor would empower more other teachers. Likewise, Shelli supported Seoyeon's decision to begin a doctoral program. Our ongoing conversations, even after both transitioned 2078 out of the classroom, show how our relationship evolved into a reciprocal partnership grounded in shared educational values and mutual growth.

Theme 4: Turing casual conversation into a collaborative action research plan Over time, the conversations between the two of us  naturally led to the development of an action research initiative. Individual focuses might differ; Seoyeon focuses on cultivating a culture of reflective practice in teaching (Schön, 1983), while Shelli’s focus is improvements in mathematics performance and enhancing her students’ math learning experiences. However, both are guided by the same overarching goal, which is to strengthen students’ mathematical confidence and success. Our collaboration, includi ng classroom visits, participation in professional development day's meeting, and small chats with other mathematics teachers  at Shelli’s school, shows how trust, friendship, and professional respect can extend beyond a single partnership to influence other teachers and staff in a school community.  During the meeting, they were deeply engaged in professional conversations, discussing the mathematical work they had recently completed collaboratively as a group. The teachers also asked thoughtful questions about Seoyeon’s research interests and expressed genuine curiosity about ideas that might support their students. Reflecting on her visit to Shelli’s school, Seoyeon began to consider the strong sense of community that Shelli consistently cultivates in the workplace. She recalled Shelli’s warm and welcoming approach, as well as the care and support she offered during Seoyeon’s maternity leave. At that time, Shelli visited Seoyeon on behalf of the teachers and staff, ensuring she remained connected to and supported by the school community. These gestures reinforced Seoyeon’s sense of belonging during her time away from the classroom. The action research project that emerged was not externally mandated but developed organically through years of shared reflection and dialogue. While same-discipline mentorships can be valuable, this study suggests that cross-disciplinary mentoring provides a unique space for creativity, mutual respect, and holistic understanding. When mentors and mentees recognize and value one another’s distinct expertise, they foster not only individual professional growth but also a more collaborative and inclusive educ ational community.

## Concluding, remarks, significance and implications

This autoethnographic study lays the groundwork for another  upcoming project that will include the second author’s voice through a duoethnographic approach, focusing on the joint planning of an action research initiative. This subsequent study will serve as a prerequisite for a collaborative autoethnographic approach, involving at least three other teachers who will implement and reflect on the action research plan. This collective inquiry will allow participants to examine their shared experiences and co-construct new understandings of effective teaching practices in their school community while they notice the gaps between espoused theory (what they say they do) and theory-in-practice (Argyris & Schön, 1974).

Since trust plays a role between researchers and educators in education inquiry (Beault, 2016), this study also shows a good example of fostering a strong and trusted professional relationship. Universities that partner with school districts, as well as districts that pair veteran mentor teachers with newly hired teachers, should reconsider long-standing norms that prioritize mentorships within the same discipline. Instead, they might explore pairing experienced, nurturing educators with novice teachers from different subject areas. For any mentorship to be truly mutually beneficial, both voices must be heard and valued. When a mentor who has internalized years of content expertise is paired with a novice teacher, it is expected that the power dynamic becomes one-sided. Crossdisciplinary mentorship offers an opportunity to balance that dynamic and foster professional growth through shared meaningful conversation.

Interdisciplinary collaboration requires openness and trust —it invites risk, but also innovation. Schools must be willing to trust new teachers and create space for them to share their perspectives and ideas. We hope that our stories and experiences will encourage other educators and researchers to reflect on their own mentoring relationships and consider whether all voices are being heard.
