# Enacted Utopias for Transformative Agency, Survivance, and Sovereignty

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The United States of America has striven for the dissolution of Indigenous societies by establishing a colonial society on seized land, quelling sovereignty, and collective agency of Indigenous communities. This has disrupted Indigenous people’s ever-evolving, multilayered cultural-historical identities and future-making activities as transformative agents. Settler colonialism forced Indigenous people to assimilate into an ahistorical existence of individual objects within the settler-native dyad through genocide, resettlement, and education. In this paper, we will present a-four-year long formative intervention study, Indigenous Learning Lab, implemented at a rural high school serving an Anishinaabe tribal nation in the state of Wisconsin. Indigenous Learning Lab facilitated transformative agency among American Indian students, families, educators, and community members and non-Indigenous educators to design and implement the school’s new behavioral support system.

Genocide, resettlement, and education The United States of America (U.S.) was founded on settler colonialism, slavery, and land-based capitalism. U.S. has striven for the dissolution of Indigenous socie ties by establishing a colonial nation (Wolfe, 2006), quelling sovereignty and collective agency of Indigenous com munities. This has disrupted Indigenous people’s ever-evolving, multilayered cultural-historical identities and future-making activities as transformative agents. Settler colonialism forced Indigenous people to assimilate into an ahistorical existence of individual objects within the iron cage of settler-native dyad through genocide,  resettlement, and education. In this paper, we will present a formative intervention study, Indigenous Learning Lab, implemented at Northwoods High School through a coalition of an Anishinaabe tribal n ation, Wisconsin Department of Public Instruction, Wisconsin Indian Education Association, and a university-based research team. The city where the school is located was the epicen ter of Wisconsin’s Anti-Indian movement of the 1990s, the Walleye War. Anishinaabe harvesters were physically attacked or prevented fr om practicing their treaty-guaranteed fishing rights on lands ceded in treatie s. The school had significant disparities in disciplinary actions. Indigenous students who made up 21% of the student population received over 60 % of the suspensions and 100 % of the expulsions in the 2014-2015 academic year (U.S. Department of Education, 2 018). The school leadership implemented Indigenous Learning Lab to f urther their partnership with the community and systematically address disparities  in behavioral outcomes: to move beyond the status quo.  Indigenous Learning Lab aimed at facilitating transformative agency amo ng American Indian students, families, educators, a nd community members and non-Indigenous educators to design the future of their school’s behavioral support system. Eight Indigenous community members (students, paren ts, teac hers, and a member  of the tribal government) worked with six non-Indigenous school staff and a research team includi ng Indigenous and non-Indigenous scholars from a local university.

## Theoretical framework

In this study, we utilize a cultural-historical activity theory-based transformative agency and Freire’s (1971) liberation pedagogy. Transformative agency is a change-oriented process of undertaking actions to repurpos e or expand the object of activity (e.g., from punishmen t to support) and create novel solutions to systemi c contradictions (Engeström & Sannino, 2021). Freire (1993) stated When a word is deprived of its dimension of action,  reflection automatically suffers as well; and the word is changed into idle chatter, into verbalism, into an alienated and alienating "blah." It becomes an empty word, one which cannot denounce  the world,  for denunciation is impossible without a commitment to transform, and t here is no transformation without action (p. 68).

Informed by the work of Spinoza, Hegel, Marx, Engel s, Lenin, and other historical materialist philosophers, CHAT has three key ideas: The centrality of mediation, genetic (historical analysis), and grounding in culturally organized activity (Cole, 2015).

Decolonizing agency in a settler colonialist system The settler colonialism in the U.S. took four insti tutionalized acts: Invasion, genocide, resettlement, and assimilation. U.S. cavalry captain Richard Henry Pr att, who opened the first boarding school in 1879 f or Indigenous children in Carlisle, Pennsylvan ia in a speech summarized the purpose of the “Kill the Indian in him and save the man.” Our historical analysis is informed by the decolonial approach: “Decolonization, as we know, is a historical process:  that is to say it cannot be understood, it cannot become intelligible nor clear to itself except in the exact measure that we candiscern the movements which give it historical form and content” (Fanon, 1963, p. 36).

Decolonization is not accountable to settlers’ expe ctations, interests, safety, and comforts and settler futurity, settler colonialism and its decolonization implicat es and unsettles everyone (Tuck & Yang, 2012). Two guiding principles of our decolonial approach to transformative agency is Indigenous sovereignty  and futurity. Indigenous sovereignty is the ability of Indigenous peoples and nations to un impededly use Indigenous knowledge, languages, and ways of being to guide and shape how the Indigenous nations and people wish to be in this interconnected world.

## Methodology

Indigenous Learning Lab is part of a multi-site formative intervention called Learning Lab (Bal, 2018). Learning Lab is an inclusive knowledge-production and systemic design process at schools. Learning Lab utilized called transformative agency by double stimulation  (TADS) focusing on the development and use of mediating tools, which enable systemic transformation  aligned with decolonizing motives and epistemologi es. Indigenous Learning Lab started in the 2019-2020 academic year to tackle racial disproportionality in school discipline at Northwood High School serving an Anishinaabe tribal nation. Indigenous Learning Lab brought about systemic transformation in the settler-colonial school system  in reciprocal and sustained partnership with the tr ibal government, Wisconsin Indian Education Association,  state’s education agency, the school district, and  a local university. Before the intervention started, it took approximately two years to build a coalition among tribal nation, school district, school administra tors, state’s education agency, and the university.  The systemic transformation in the project was examining the existing system and designing and implementing a new, culturally resp onsive behavioral support system with Indigenous students,  parents, community members, educators along with W hite settler educators. We conceptualized the project as an international projec t. Our interactions with the Indigenous community members and tribal government agencies demonstrated that we respect In digenous sovereignty and futurity.

In Indigenous Learning Lab, administrators, teachers, and American Indian parents and students engaged in collective design praxis —critically examining systemic breakdowns embedded i n the existing system and exerting their transformative agency in designing an equity-oriented new support system Bal, 2016). To facilitate transformative agency, a constellation of volitional actions, agents (subjects) need mediating artifacts that help them to take steps toward changing their circumstances. TADS is a  process by which practitioners intentionally break out of paralyzing circumstances  by transforming them with the help of artifacts (Sannino, 2015). The precondition and starting point of TADS are con flicts of motives (first stimulus) that may be experienced as daily disturbances (e.g., dilemmas, conflicts) and paral yses in activity systems. TADS is set in motion by means of second stimuli, artifacts appropriated or developed by community members, which serve the function of an auxiliary motive (Sannino, 2015).

## Results

The Anishinaabe transformative agency A volitional act is an act carried out by choice. Yet, choice and decision-making are not sufficient for volitional acts: “If an action is carried out without obstacles, it cannot be volitional, even if there is a choice and  a decision is made” (Leont’ev, 2005, p. 80). The Anishinaabe transformative agen cy involves survivance  (e.g., never give up). Survivance is the combination of survival and resistance that moves Indigenous people from passive objects to agents of power, goal, and change (Vizenor, 1994). The Anishinaabe transformative agency can be seen in their steadfast commitments to protect culturally important beings that have sustained them since time immemorial. In the Great Lakes, the Anishinaabe and others live thei r interrelated values of Nibwaakaawin (Wisdom), Zaagi’idiwin (Love), Minaadendamowin (Respect), Aak ode’ewin (Bravery), Gwayakwaadiziwin  (Honesty), Dabaadendiziwin (Humility), and Debwewin (Truth). M anoomin (wild rice) is a central reason for why the Anishinaabe are in the Great Lakes with their creation stories directing them to live where the food grows on the water.

Through engagement with joint, collaborative proble m-solving, members voiced five different types of transformative agency: Resisting, criticizing, explicating, envisioning, a nd committing to concrete action  (Ko et al., 2021). The pattern of members’ transformative agency was aligned with the expansive learning cycle (Engeström, 2015). Learning Lab sessions were organized to facilitate a praxis, a continuous cycle of action and reflection to create a critical dialogue among school communities and transform their world (Freire, 1993). Multiple mediating artifacts such as school’s outcome data, the map of the existing behavioral response plan, and student handbook were mobilized to generate new motives, volitional actions, and ultimately expand transformative agency among members. Through collaborative problem-identification and problem-solving actions, members can become historical actors by expanding their transformative agency (Freire, 2000). Through questioning and empirical and historical an alyses actions, members collectively analyzed the school’s academic and discipline data, which was di saggregated by race and shared their experiential knowledge(s) of settler colonialism and white supre macy. School data including suspension and expulsio n and academic scores served as primary stimuli. Statisti cal data and members’ experiential narratives funct ioned as first stimuli through which members can interpret d isproportionality with a historicized and situated lens of how school discipline systems historically play a role in reinforcing white privilege by excluding Indigenous students. After developing a historicized understanding, members moved to map out the existing school discipline system. Through collective analysis, members identified multiple systemic breakdowns embedded in the current system. In the modeling process, members exercised transfor mative agency to develop an ideal system of support. In the process of constructing the object,  Indigenous practices and epistemologies became mor e visible and consequential. For example, during the discussi ons related to classroom practices and curricula su ch as science, Indigenous participants and Indigenous res earch team members explained Anishinaabe people hav e a kinship relationship with the land (lake, deer, wal leyes). Land is their f irst teachers. Th e mapping out and modeling made visible that 20 % of students at Northwoods think of land as kinship and 80% of the students think of land as a place. Therefore, place-based learning was offered as a culturally responsive science curriculum based on the White-settler epistemologies. Following such critical examinations, Learning Lab members mobilized their expertise, cultural knowledge, and future imaginations to design a new behavioral support system that aims to be inclusive, positive, culturally responsive, and ecologically valid through modeling. Members envisioned multiple solutions to address ra cial disproportionality in school discipline. For example, they envisaged expanded community outreach, such as holding conferences at tribal communities  to restore reciprocal relationships with the tribal community. They also envisioned restorative process es to repair broken relationships between White teachers and stu dents. The new system was conceptualized as a livin g, adaptive artifact responsive to school community’s ever-evolving needs and goals and lo cal dynamics. Administrators, teachers, the head of the Tribal Ed ucation Department, and students participated in th e implementation team. They are currently working on adapting and improving the new support system desig ned through Learning Lab processes to address the unprecedented challenges and opportunities posed by the COVID19 pandemic.

## Conclusion

Decolonization should be based on  Indigenous sovereignty and futurity (Tuck & Yang, 2012). "The future is something to be constructed through trial and error rather than an inexorable vice that determines all our actions." (Freire, 2000, p.54).  In this presentation, we will first historicize hyp er-punishment of Indigenous youth in U.S. schools at cultural-historical and ontological time scales. We then will present how TADS facilitated and nurtured transformative agency for systemic transformation a t a rural high school serving students from an Indi genous nation as well as White settler communit ies to address the racialization of school discipli ne. Our decolonizing approach is based on Indigenous sovereignty and futurity and utilizes cultural-historical analyses and praxis. The Anishinaabe transformative agency is based on s urvivance-the combination of survival and resistance. The present study may contribute to the literature by demonstrating h ow transformative agency can be facilitated at schools serving Indigenous youth through an inclusi ve knowledge-production and future-making activity with a decolonizing approach.

Indigenous youth  cannot s urvive and thrive in an oppressive institutional cu lture that persists and reinforces racism  and white supremacy. Instead, addressing racial disprop ortionality requires developing systemic, adaptive, and insistent solutions with historically marginalized communitie s and practitioners and building justice-oriented, strategic coalitions among multiple commu nities and activity systems that envisions systemic transformation. To this end, there is a ne ed for creating an inclusive problem-solving space in which local stakeholders collaboratively engage in future making with a praxis for critical dialogue on racism and settler colonialism to dismantle the existing punishing, di sabling, and marginalizing education systems and simultaneously re-imagine schools as sites of expansive learning, future making, and joy.
