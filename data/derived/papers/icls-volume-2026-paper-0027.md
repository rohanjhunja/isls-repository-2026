# Advancing Epistemic Justice, Equitable Medical Realism, and Inclusive Standard -setting in Medical Simulation Training

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines standardized patient (SP) simulation in medical education as a cultural pedagogical practice shaped by institutional priorities, epistemic assumptions, and educators’ interventions. Based on 28 interviews with SP educators in the United States and one co-author’s three decades of experience in SP simulation practice, this study critically analyzes how the prevailing model of “medical realism,” framed through standardization, psychometric measurement, and objectivity, constrains the devel opment of inclusive standard-setting practices and equity-oriented approaches to SP simulation pedagogy. Grounded in cultural-historical theories of learning and science and technology studies (STS) literature, the analysis identifies and critically examines epistemic contradictions in SP educators’ work towards advancing diversity, equity, and inclusivity (DEI) in SP simulations. We show how by transforming contradictions into opportunities for inclusive standard setting practices, SP educators reimagine simulation as a site of epistemic plurality and transformative learning.

In medical education, performance-based human simulations offer experiential learning opportunities without the risks of treating and harming actual patients. One widespread model involves standardized patients (SPs), who are laypeople trained to closely portray “real” patient behavior to train and assess students. Since the 1960s, health professions education in the United States has relied increasingly upon developing methods for valid and reliable assessment of students’ clinical competencies (Schuwirth, et al, 2020), including the introduction of SPs. The SP pedagogy has since evolved into a highly structured methodology medical students’ clinical skills assessments and medical licensing exams (Mosher, 2025). The SP simulation emerged in  response to the limitations of a typical standardized test to assess clinical competence, particularly for practicing bedside care, clinical reasoning, and communication with the patient. Therefore, this simulation pedagogy continues to be  grounded in the logics of standardization and objectivity (Timmermans & Berg, 2003; Hanemaayer, 2020). To create valid and standardized simulations, SPs need to be rigorously trained by SP educators to embody the quality assurance judged primarily through psychometric measures (Boulet, et al., 2003). This paper aims to gain new insights and a n understanding of the current practices of using human simulations to advance health equity in medical training in the United States. It  seeks to highlight the role of SP simulation pedagogy and the opportunities already embodied in this pedagogical practice,  both as a tool of standardization and as means of  humanizing health care education. We focus specifically on the experiences of SP educators because they are responsible for training standardized patients to portray patient identities, behaviors, emotions, cultural backgrounds, and lived experiences during simulation encounters with medical students. Through their training practice s, f eedback, and quality assurance processes, they participate in the construction of a simulated patient portrayal that counts a valid, reliable, and rigorous  training tool and measure of assessing medical students’ clinical skills.

In 2021, the COVID-19 pandemic dramatically changed the established practice of high-stakes assessment of medical students’ clinical skills in the United States at the national level that were in place since

2004. Their indefinite suspension prompted medical schools to start innovating their curricula and rethink teaching

and assessment of clinical skills. It opened up space to reconceptualize medical simulation beyond preparing students for high-stakes, licensing exams. The term “standardized patient” itself originated in North Americandue to the use of SP simulations at the U.S. national medical licensing exams. In 2017, The Association of SP Educators (ASPE) began to use the term “simulated patient” interchangeably with “standardized patient” to promote a more inclusive understanding of a variety of roles  these performers play and the varying degrees of behavioral standardization in medical simulations (Beigzadeh et al., 2016; Lewis et al., 2017). The current shifting landscape of clinical skills assessment in United States also creates a unique, timely opportunity to reimagine simulation as more than a technical exercise, grounded in standardized assessment. 187 At the same time, despite its continued dominance in medical education, SP simulation pedagogy has been increasingly critiqued (e.g., Dieckmann et al., 2007; Hopwood et al, 2019; Johnson, 2008). Darwing on cultural-historical theories of learning (e.g., Vygostky, 1962), scholars have proposed an understanding of simulation as a potential space for social transformation (Hopwood, 2017) and as a creative synthesis between simulation and clinical practice instead of being treated as a laboratory exercise (Kneebone et al., 2004). Hopwood

(2017) calls for a shift from simulations that mirror clinical reality through an “as if” logic toward a “what if”

approach, “where cultural-discursive, material-economic, and socio-political arrangements of both real and imagined practices come together, interwo ven with those of responsive emergent pedagogy” (p. 63). These perspectives have been further complemented by ethnographic work that foregrounds the sensory (Harris, 2020), affective (Guarrasi, 2015), embodied (Guarrasi, 2019; Underman, 2020),  and material dimensions (Koski & Ostherr, 2022; Wojcik et al., 2022) of constructing simulation as a social practice. Building on these critiques, this study draws on the lens of epistemic injustice (Fricker, 2007; Goldberg,

2017) to examine dominant standardization practices in SP simulation. As Carel and Kidd (2017) explain,

“[h]ealthcare systems rely on complex structures of epistemic norms and expectations, both implicit and explicit, that create knowledge asymmetries – for instance, privileging the knowledge derived from medical training and theory, rather than potentially rooted in patient experience …” (p. 336). I n simulation training, the concept of epistemic injustice allows considering the consequences of the dominant the model of fidelity and standard-setting that privileges biomedical frameworks for patient care. The standardization practices in the simulation laboratory also construct standards of care based on specific  definitions of what knowledge about the patient is considered epistemically relevant for clinical skills training.  This is particularly relevant regarding patients whose cultural, racial, and gendered identities and differently abled bodies are marginalized in society as well as healthcare. This study explores opportunities for advancing more inclusive standard-setting practices in SP simulation pedagogy to advance health equity through medical education. To do so, we examine the standardization practices in SP simulations that, in our view, rest in an uncritical understanding of “realism,” which assumes that the most effective way to teach and assess students’ clinical skills is by creating simulations as close to the real-world clinical practice as possible (Hayes & Singer, 1989; Dieckmann et al., 2007). We suggest that the underlying conceptual divide between the “simulation” and “reality” must be critically rethought to advance the existing potential of simulation pedagogy as a culturally mediated site of transformative learning. While the concept of fidelity (to clinical reality) is central to simulation design in medical education, in our view, it rests on the problematic assumption that clinical reality is self-evident, stable, and universally shared. This paper challenges the uncritical use of the fidelity to clinical reality as a narrow guiding model of simulation pedagogy. Grounded in literature on simulation education in healthcare, critical learning sciences, and science and technology studies (STS), this study draws on 28 interviews with SP educators and trainers working in medical schools with standardized patients as w ell as one co-author’s over three decades of experience in SP program development, standardization, and assessment in the United States. To address the ISLS 2026 conference theme Partnering with Purpose, our study design exemplifies purposeful research– practice partnership between an experienced SP simulation practitioner and an interdisciplinary social scientist in health professions education. We explore how a practice-research collaboration and dialogue across disciplines can open possibilities towards more equitable, inclusive, and transformative model of medical simulation pedagogy. In doing so, we draw in particular on Anna Stetsenko’s (2008) concept of a “transformative activist stance” (p. 474). Stetsenko critiques the positivist views on learning and humandevelopment that assume objective, measurable reality that is independent of human perception and subjectivity. In medical simulation, this logic treats clinical skills and competencies as stable behavioral units of competence that can be measured (on a rating scale or checklist) and replicated across individuals as well as social and cultural contexts. Psychometric research and quality assurance of SP simulations assessment is an example of this positivist approach (e.g., Myford & Wolfe, 2004). In contrast, Stetsenko advances an integrated sociocultural theory of learning and development that is socially, culturally, historically, and politically mediated, and “inexorably linked to ideals of social justice and emancipation as pursuits of common humanity that substitute for group particularism and the politics of difference…” (p. 474). Such pedagogy positions learners as active agents in transforming both their own individual learning and the collective social world. Building on Stetsenko’s (2008) perspective on learning and humandevelopment, we explore how SP simulation can move beyond a skill acquisition model of learning and a technical exercise toward a vision of learning as collaborative, justice-oriented world-making. As we will show in our analysis, SP educators’ microinterventions that challenge the prevailing model  of medical realism can be understood as intentional efforts to transform SP simulation from within through everyday, culturally grounded, and just ice-oriented teaching practices. We examine how the dominant model of fidelity in medical education, treated as a transparent window into clinical reality, constraints what counts as legitimate knowledge and clinical skills, creating practical 188 contradictions for the equity-oriented reimagining of SP simulation that educators are working to advance. Conceiving the SP simulation not as a mirror of clinical reality but as an epistemic practice that produces its own realities, we discuss how SP educators engage in micro-interventions of what Walter Mignolo (2009) calls “epistemic disobedience.” Specifically, this paper addresses two interrelated research questions: RQ1: How do SP educators describe their everyday practices in simulation la boratories, and what opportunities and barriers  arise when working toward more inclusive and health equity-oriented pedagogy?

RQ2: How do SP educators’ everyday practices challenge institutional standards and epistemologies to help develop more inclusive and health equity-oriented SP simulation pedagogy?

To address these questions, we identify and critically analyze four tensions between the practices of standardization and efforts to promote diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) in the SP simulation: the assumption of cultural neutrality as fairness, the  pursuit of “fidelity-to-standard” model of medical realism, institutional constraints as epistemic priorities, and  SP educators’ simulation design micro-interventions as acts of epistemic agency. We conclude by advocating for reconceptualizing SP simulation as a site of transformative learning to advance health equity.

## Theoretical framework

Standardization in medical education ha s been viewed to uphold an objective approach to simulation design and assessment of student clinical competence skills (e.g., Morgan et al., 2004; Ruiz-Manzanera, 2024). Drawing on science and technology studies (STS) (e.g., Lampland, 2010; Strathern, 2000a; 2000b), we critique this assumed neutrality of standardization and quantification measures. According to Strathern (2000a), institutional transparency isn't a neutral process, but it is “embedded in certain practices (artefacts, technologies) of accountability [and quantification measures such as] “performance indicators are highly selective objectifications of performances” (p. 313). Her critique shows that standardization practices create and appearance of fairness while strengthening institutional authority over what counts as legitimate knowledge. Lampland (2010) shows how quantification in the form of “provisional and false numbers” (p. 378) can function as a tool of institutional control by prioritizing adherence to formal procedures over the accuracy of the numbers themselves. We call the dominant orientation of standardization practices in the simulation laboratory a fidelity-tostandard model of simulation. It is a model of “medical realism” that disciplines what counts as real, legitimate, knowable, and teachable in clinical education. Such fidelity-to-standard model risks producing a closed loop of simulation-based performance, where learners demonstrate mastery of institutional expectations rather than engage with the lived realities of patients and culturally embedded practice. This dominant model of “medical realism” is rarely questioned in simulation literature, and its limitations are seldom made explicit. Our paper aims to reconceptualize model of medical realism to advance a more equitable approach to SP simulation design that is attentive to how medical realism shapes the clinical reality it seeks to represent. Philosopher of science Sánchez-Dorado (2025) argues that models are not simply selective abstractions or simplification of reality. In her view, we need to understand “modelling practices not only as practices of representing but also as practices of worldmaking” (p. 49).  She gives an example of abstract art, showing how abstractions make new relationships visible. This means that when educators or scientists design models, they are not merely reproducing reality as it is but creatively constructing some version of reality. Following the argument of Sánchez-Dorado in the context of SP simulations, the fidelity-to-standard model of realism then, should be understood not as an objective representation of clinical reality, but as an act of creative worldmaking. In the dominant model of medical realism,  we argue, clinical reality is constituted through standardized procedures, quantifiable outcomes, and measurable performance.

By viewing SP simulations through Sánchez -Dorado’s framework, we assert that the notion of medical realism in simulation education, then, should not be understood as fidelity to some true external clinical reality, but as fidelity to an institutionally sanctioned model of what clinical practice is imagined to be. Analyzing the practices of designing, performing, and sustaining such standards, we argue that the defining relationship in SP simulation is not between a preexisting clinical reality and its reproduction in the simulation laboratory. Rather than treating reality and simulation as separate domains, we understand them as operating on the same ontological plane. The real and the simulated both operate in the realm of actuality, participating in the ongoing production of clinical reality.

Here we draw on Gilles Deleuze’s (1990) elaboration of “immanent modes of existence” (p. 269), which challenges hierarchical distinctions between modes of reality such as model versus copy, or clinical reality versus simulation. Instead of grounding one form of being (e.g., simulation) in a referential relationship to another (e.g., 189 clinical reality), Deleuze suggests that no form of being is ontologically superior or more authentic to another. In practical terms, this means that SP simulation is not just a copy of clinical reality but a generative site that produces new meanings and relations. In our model of medical realism, standardized patients become what Massumi (1987) calls “realer than real” (p. 90), not because they faithfully mirror actual patients, but because they produce a version of clinical reality that did not previously exist. This reality is not bound by an ontology of the “real world,” but is manifested through the (immanence of) the simulation process. Finally, our argument is also informed by the intervention-based methodologies in critical learning sciences focused on social change and equity (e.g., Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Nasir et al., 2020) in response to the dominant trends in STEM and health professions education that narrowly define learning in terms of standardized, employable skills (Binkley et al., 2012; National Research Council, 2012). Critical learning sciences scholars (Hand, Penuel, & Gutiérrez, 2012; Stetsenko, 2017; The Politics of Learning Writing Collective, 2017) argue that downplaying the social, cultural, and political dimensions of learning continue to reinforce inequities. In addition, previous research has shown how epistemic agency of marginalized voices can be supported through culturally responsive designs such as counterstorytelling (Dutta et al., 2022), culturally responsive Learning Lab (Bal et al., 2014), curated sites of learning (Stroupe, 2022), and shared epistemic agency (Damşa, et al., 2010). This paper contributes to this line of critique by showing how SP simulation can become a site of transformative learning in medical education.

This study is based on 28 semi-structured interviews with SP educators and trainers from institutions across the United States, recruited through professional listservs and direct outreach to simulation centers. Participation was voluntary. Many SP trainers in our sample had previously worked as SPs  before transitioning into trainer and educator roles, however, prior SP experience was not a prerequisite to be included in the study. In addition, many participants in our sample have an acting background, which is quite typical for SPs and SP trainers/educators in the healthcare simulation field. Participants were asked to reflect on their experiences designing, implementing, and assessing SP simulations, with particular attention to how DEI efforts have been implemented in their programs. Many participants self-selected due to concerns about equity-related efforts in their programs, personal identification with underrepresented groups, or involvement in DEI committees. The sample thus reflects a population of educators who were especially attuned to the equity-related limitations of SP simulation. Interviews lasted about 30 –60 minutes, and were conducted via Zoom, recorded, and transcribed verbatim. We asked a core set of prompts, with follow-up questions tailored based on their responses. We asked about the participants’ definitions of “standardized patient,” SP training methods they use, approaches to fairness and consistency across patient portrayals performed by different SPs, their experiences with formative and summative assessment, and the role of diversity, equity, and inclusion in SP program s in their institutions. Participants were also asked to reflect on SP case development, institutional DEI commitments, and the involvement of diverse patient populations in simulations. A qualitative, interpretive approach to thematic data analysis of the interview transcripts was employed. Four coders (including the first author and three research assistants) independently read the transcripts multiple times and conducted iterative coding. The analysis followed an inductive approach in which codes were generated directly from the data. We identified recurring themes, developed initial codes, and refined them through iterative readings, and later comparison among individual codebooks. New codes were added, and existing codes were modified as additional insights emerged from the data and team discussions. We  paid particular attention to the language SP educators used to describe how diversity, equity, and inclusion are incorporated into the process of standardization (e.g., case development, training, assessment, and debriefing) of SP simulations, identifying opportunities and barriers to inclusive standard-setting practices and equitable healthcare training across different institutions. All study procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board. The data analysis also reflects the combined expertise of the research-practice partnership. The second co-author has been an SP educator since 1992, working simultaneously with national boards medical examiners (NBME) and medical schools. They have directed multiple SP programs nationally and worked with medical licensing boards to create communication assess ment rubrics that shape how doctor –patient interactions are evaluated. Their work focuses on preparing SPs to assess history taking, physical exam techniques, and interpersonal communication skills, giving learners a safe yet rigorous space to practice and grow. They also train SP educators in human patient simulation methodology. In addition to case development and SP training, they oversee quality assurance and delivers rater bias mitigation training to ensure fairness and consistency in assessment. The  first co-author is a social scientist, whose research brings together interdisciplinary communication studies, feminist science and technology studies, and cultural-historical theories of learning in non-canonical educational settings to interrogate dominant medical epistemologies in healthcare training. 190 Our analysis also draws on a long-term collaboration of the first and second author that began in 2013. Over the years, our partnership has often taken the form of experimental, often speculative collaborations — what STS scholar Jane Calvert (2024) describes as “thinking with others” (p. 175) and practices of otherwising” (p. 128). Our ongoing dialogue has enabled critical reflexivity, epistemic hybridity, and mutual transformation of our disciplinary perspectives.

The following analysis identifies and critically examines four central contradictions in SP educators’ work that reveal the epistemological and institutional tensions in  DEI efforts within medical simulation: i.) the assumption of cultural neutrality as fairness; ii.) the pursuit of “fidelity-to-standard” model of medical realism; iii.) invoking the lack of logistical resources when SP pedagogy does not align with institut ional epistemic priorities; and vi.) SP educators’ micro-interventions into simulation  design as acts of epistemic agency. They reveal how current simulation practices both constrain and create opportunities for more inclusive standard setting practices in SP simulation training. These dynamics not only reflect epistemological commitments t hat privilege institutional legibility over relational and culturally responsive approaches to care, but also SP educators’ personal commitments to health equity and social justice. We use these contradictions as analytical entry points to examine the opportunities for transformative change towards more equitable SP simulation design. Drawing on the cultural-historical activity theory (CHAT) at the intersection of medical work and adult learning, Yrjö Engström (2018) emphasizes that the word contradiction should not be conflated with the word problem: “Contradictions are historically ac cumulating structural tensions within and between activity systems. […They] show themselves in disturbances, often taking the shape of discursive manifestations such as dilemmas, conflicts, and double binds” (p. 16). The examples of the activity systems that might come into conflict in healthcare include professional roles, institutional policies, and patient needs. Engström explains that in the dynamically changing healthcare system, expertise cannot be understood as the acquisition of a fixed set of knowledge and skills by junior practitioners. He argues that what constitutes expertise evolves as healthcare professionals confront new contradictions and challenges in their work. Therefore, addressing these contradictions requires the collective redesign of work practices. Similarly, in the context of pharmacy practice and learning, Guarrasi et al. (2025) conceive systemic contradictions not as obstacles to be eliminated, but as generative sites of transformation that mobilize new forms of work practice to emerge. The assumption of cultural neutrality as fairness Despite medical schools’ official commitments to diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) stated in their mission statements, educators described how simulated scenarios that are focused on cultural competence tend to be 191 added to the core curriculum as “special cases,” rather than representing an integral dimension of everyday care. As one SP educator shared: Something that I hear all the time, and it drives me crazy is like, oh, we can't make this patient gay because then it'll be too distracting, and it won't be standardized anymore. I think the same thing is sort of unspoken with race as well, that you have these patients that have very white names, and it just sort of is assumed that they're white, and if you start adding any cultural details, then ‘that's too distracting, that's too complicated.’ I hear that all the time.” SP educators shared their frustrations with the institutional insistence on fairness to students through standardization defined by cultural neutrality. When diversity is treated as “too distracting” or “off-topic” from the clinical core, as simulation educators note in the interviews, the institutional logic equates fairness with uniform performance metrics. As one SP educator reflected: What it comes down to is the leadership needs to reflect on the diversity as well. The things that were being asked [by students] were such great teachable moments for leadership to be educated. But they didn't want to listen. Because if a person in a whee lchair is present in an OSCE, it's not fair to the learner. At least that's what they told to us. I can't put SPs in wheelchairs in OSCEs because that's going to jar a student? I would like to advocate for medical students that they deserve more than that. In doing so, the leadership are reinforcing dominant images of what a “typical” patient looks like while dismissing culturally specific knowledge and social determinants of health as distracting. What is included in a case scenario and what is left out depends upon the perspectives of and sources used by simulation case authors. From the standpoint of narrative medicine, Frank  (2013) reminds us that illness narratives are shaped by cultural templates and cannot be neutral. Similarly, science and technology studies scholars Martha Lampland and Sisan Leigh Star (2009) argue that the application of standards in practice is predicated on the process t hat they call “degrees of delegation” (p. 16). Through this process the standard is transformed from an epistemic unit into material, embodied practice or artifact that is socially and culturally embedded. This transformation reveals how ostensibly neutral standards carry implicit cultural and epistemic values. Institutions use the language of diversity in their miss ion statement, but our findings indicate that they often lack the commitment to producing actual changes. Having a diversity statement or policy is a step towards learning for health equity, but at many medical schools where our participants work, such efforts seem  to fall short of creating meaningful transformative action in everyday practice.

The pursuit of “fidelity-to-standard” model of medical realism Standardization in SP simulation design and assessment is achieved through a careful case design, SP training, and calibration so that all learners encounter the same clinical scenario and are assessed by the same criteria. The type of required standardization depends on the psychometric stakes of the assessment (Hillier et al., 2023). In summative, high-stakes and evaluation-focused assessment contexts such as licensing exams or Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs), SPs are trained to portray cases in scripted and highly consistent way, and assess students based on observable behavior such as ‘uses lay language to explain diagnosis’ (Cleland et al., 2009). In formative, learning-focused assessment, the goal is to give feedback to students, so SPs are given more flexibility in how they respond to students, and the assessment is based on more subjective interpretation by SPs. In both contexts, standardization practices act as an epistemic filter that reinforces an idealized view of clinical reality as defined by standards of best practice (Lewis et al., 2017).  Medical educators maintain that the portrayal of realism in medical simulation is essential to students’ learning of hands-on clinical skills. However, in order to train and evaluate students’ clinical and communication skills in these simulation contexts, educators are required to  employ methods derived from psychometric s. As one of the prominent SP educators Peggy Wallace emphasized (2007), the most valued characteristics of SP simulations is that the patient challenge to each student remains constant with every student. An SP educator in our study described the process of SP training: I'm trying to standardize not only their role portrayal, their emotional portrayal, their pain portrayal, but also the feedback that they're offering.  Naturally, because there's two different people coming into the encounter and each bringing in their own stuff, it's impossible to be 100% standardized. I think if we can get 80% standardized, that's pretty good.  But even so, if 192 you had five people whose behaviors were standardized within a certain bandwidth, you still get a bit of variation because they're human beings. But there are certain things that you cando. SP educators and SPs, who are tasked with carrying out these normative prescriptions as a series of stepby-step procedures, therefore face a predicament: How do they realistically imbue an SP with a sense of human personhood while representing their body in a way that  consistently maintains a standard in their performance across multiple encounters with different students ? As one SP educator reflected, “Standardization is a dream of exam administrators that we endeavor to carry out, but you can’t close us.  I don’t think there is a standardized patient any more than there is a standardized doctor.”  Their statement reveals the tension between institutional demands for standardized performance and the inherently variable, embodied, and relational nature of simulated encounters.

Such fidelity-to-standard model of medical realism creates practical contradictions in SP educators’ work, who struggle to overcome the  contradictions to design a health equity-oriented simulation within  an epistemic framework that privileges psychometric measures and standardization  of SP performance. They have to navigate the tension between highly formalized practices of standardization and their pedagogical commitment to inclusive, culturally responsive standard-setting practices. We argue that the pedagogical objective to train students’ competencies for real clinical practice becomes undermined by its logic of “medical realism” that obscures its own constructedness. The SP simulation functions as a simulacrum (Baudrillard, 1981) or an appearance of a clinical work not as it exists but as it aligns with institutional ideals of standardized and measurable performance of clinical work that can be assessed on a rating scale. According to Baudrillard, the simulacrum does not mirror real ity but creates a semblance of that reality through signs that are disconnected from the real world they are meant to represent. Baudrillard’ argument provides a helpful analogy, since the purpose of the SP simulation is not for students to heal the patient but for a medical school to measure and grade their performance.

Institutional constraints as epistemic priorities SP educators also identified institutional barriers to their efforts for more culturally responsive simulation design, such as a lack of budget, time, and insufficient institutional support. Many participants pointed to a gap between medical schools stated commitments to diversity, inclusivity, and equity (DEI) in their mission statements and the lack of concrete structural support to realize those values in practice. Even when SP educators actively work to implement DEI principles into simulation design, institutions often cite a variety of logistical challenges that prevent hiring SPs that represent communities these schools serve. Because SP roles are typically low paid contract jobs based, constrains such as such as not reimbursing SP performers for taking public transportation (policies that cover only fuel costs while many of them don’t have a car available for their transportation), or limited schedule flexibility makes hiring SP performers from culturally underrepresented groups challenging. One educator illustrated this tension by explaining: The college hired a director of diversity, equity and inclusion a little over a year ago, and my direct manager and the medical director were breathing down my neck asking for numbers. But the reason they were breathing down my neck about the numbers was because the students kept saying, ‘We go to our OSCEs, or we go to our longitudinal classes every week and we see all white SPs and then we go out to the clinics, and we see all black patients.’ So, the students were saying that the SP pool was not representative of the population that they see externally. And so, leadership, kept asking me for numbers and I kept saying the numbers don't matter because the SPs at our university are vendors, not employees. And so even if our pool is 50% black SPs, that doesn't mean that's what the students are going to see. And we couldn't set a vendor up who didn't have a bank account, who didn't have a checking account. And not everyone who comes in [to work as an SP] has a checking account.

Because of the budget restrictions, simulation scenarios are often recycled without being reevaluated for cultural inclusivity. Professionals writing them often do not represent marginalized groups’ perspectives and experiences or the community served, so the cases are written from what our participants described the “white” perspective. While the medical schools often mention logistical and financial constraints, these decisions also reflect institutional values and healthcare training priorities. Resources for creating culturally responsive simulation scenarios seem to be limited, while at the same time, school are allocating large sums into advanced simulation technology. As one educator aptly put it when describing the implementation of DEI values into SP practice at their institution: “It's all cosmetic. In one year, all of these initiatives, they're just going to fizzle out because it's just to show we are doing it.”  In this sense, the barriers educators face extend beyond practical and financial obstacles, and reflect deeper epistemic priorities. Informal SP educators’ interventions as acts of epistemic agency Despite these institutional constraints, many SP educators found ways to make small-scale changes towards equity and inclusion. For example, educators described adding unscripted lines and inserting cultural information that they felt were crucial to the p ortrayal of the patient character and background but were absent from the script. Illustrating the personal stakes behind some of their choices, one of the SP educators explained: For me personally, I am a member of the LGBTQ community, and so it’s been more of my personal focus because I have personal connections with our community. So, I’ve tried to make our standardized patient program also diverse in terms of gender expression and we have transgender SPs. And that's something that's pretty important to me. I have made it my personal mission. We had a case that was an HIV-positive man who was gay. I pointed out that having an HIV positive man who was gay, is reinforcing a bias. And so, I proposed changing the scenario to a heterosexual and either sex to mitigate bias. And that was a way that I was able to influence at least OSCE curricula and case curricula.

We argue that these seemingly minor changes by educators embody what Stetsenko (2008) describes as a “transformative activist stance” (p. 474). In contrast to dominant acquisition-oriented models of learning, Stetsenko envisions learning as “contributing to collaborative practices of humanity; continuing, while simultaneously transforming them” (p. 489). This vision is reflected in the educators’ acts of epistemic agency, as they challenge dominant norms from within to make simulation more inclusive, even i n the absence of institutional recognition.   We conceptualize epistemic agency as enabled or constrained within activity system (Holland et al., 1998).  In the context of SP simulation, these acts of epistemic agency are  purposeful, everyday micro-interventions of SP educators that challenge dominant epistemic framing of simulation scenarios and SP training. By transforming contradictions in their work into opportunities for justice-oriented action, SP educators reimagine simulation as a site of epistemic plurality and transformative learning. As one educator explaine d: “I was proposing things like meeting with the Diversity, Equity inclusion director and meeting with our current pool of non-white SPs and getting their perspectives and asking them for ideas and trying to address institutional barriers.” In doing so, SP educators act as critics of SP simulation, contesting its dominant epistemic model. The efforts of SP educators also point to broader opportunities for growth in SP simulation pedagogy. They advocated for greater community involvement, diversification of the simulated patients’ names and cases pools used, incorporation of real people with disabilities as simulated patients, and creating cases centered on culture, underrepresented groups, and social determinants of health. SP educator initiatives highlight how practitioner-led interventions can generate meaningful institutional learning and  change, and contribute to more inclusive, equity-centered approaches to simulation design. The findings suggest that the transformative potential to advance health equity-focused simulation pedagogy already exists within the simulation practice itself. This potential is carried forward by SP educators who navigate institutional constraints by engaging in small-scale interventions. This paper shows  that SP simulation is a contested social and epistemic space, where versions of clinical reality are selectively reproduced and contested from within the simulation practice itself. In other words, simulation produces clinical models of care that gain meaning and validity through their enactment in simulation training. We have interrogated the dominant, institutionally endorsed model of simulation fidelity and its implications for advancing epistemic justice, and we identify opportunities for culturally responsive and health equity-oriented SP simulation pedagogy. For SP simulation practice, we highlight the importance of epistemic reflexivity in SP simulation training and design, encouraging educators to recognize the normative epistemic assumptions  of cultural neutrality  and fidelity-to-standard medical realism underlying simulation pedagogy, and their implications for  reproducing health inequities. Our analysis points to the limitations and opportunities for reconciling the contradictions created by current standardization practices when SP simulation encounters lived, diverse, and culturally grounded patient care.
