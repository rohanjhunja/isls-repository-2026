# Sowing Futures: Developing Curriculum Materials for Urban Agriculture within a Community- Based Partnership

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this practice-oriented paper, we underscore ICLS’s 2026 theme of “Partnering with Purpose” by describing the process and outcomes of a collaborative project between community members and a large public university. By attending to values of reciprocity, relationships, community expertise, and the power of place, we developed a set of curriculum materials that draw on the expertise of two Black urban farmers in their work to transform educational and agricultural experiences for youth. We outline our collaborative process, project outcomes, and next steps for supporting this and other innovative community partnerships.

Institutions of higher learning and research play a key role in their broader communities. As such, they have an obligation to not only consider their impact on the community, but to engage with community members in ways that are reciprocal, community-driven, and designed to support the well-being of those within and outside of the institution (Tiger, 2024; Tuck, 2009). Place-based initiatives (Guajardo et al., 2015), participatory design projects (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016), and other community-based partnership models offer valuable insights for questioning “assumptions about power, privilege, space, and place in deep and meaningful ways.” (Yamamura & Koth, p. 193). These approaches emphasize the importance of building strong, reciprocal, and trusting relationships; recognizing the wisdom that community members bring; and attending to the power of place. In this practice-oriented paper, we describe the process and outcomes of an inaugural Learning Studio collaboration initiated by community members and supported by a large public university in the US. The goal of our Learning Studio project is to attend to the needs of the community by taking on projects that community members have identified and advocated for, rather than pursuing university goals in the community context. In this project, two co-authors, Black urban farmers who own and operate a local farm, approached the fourth author, a faculty member and director of the university’s center for the learning sciences. The farmers described how their work with young people within and outside of public schools was hampered by inadequate curriculum materials. What materials they did have access to did not effectively address the needs of the community nor meet their goals of supporting young people to see themselves as capable of sustaining their families by growing their own food and recognizing farming as a potential career. Thus, driven by community need, we leveraged the strengths of each author over the course of one summer to develop Sowing Futures, a set of curriculum materials designed to encourage youth interest in food, farming, and agriculture  (Bautista et al., 2025). In what follows, we outline the roles and positionality of each of the authors, the design and implementation process for developing the curriculum materials, the outcomes thus far, and some of the lessons learned throughout the process. Positionality and role of authors The first author is a doctoral candidate and teacher educator whose research focuses on elementary science teacher education and expanding ideas of what counts as science to consider the range of ways children and families engage in science in their everyday lives. She spent five years teaching science in elementary and middle schools, has supported teacher learning in schools as an instructional coach, and serves as a teacher educator in a universitybased teacher education program. Prior to becoming a science teacher, she was a farm apprentice in the Northeast, operated her own micro-farm in central Texas, and spent five years as a nursery and landscape professional. The second and third authors are local urban farmers who are partners in life and partners in business. They run CWO Farms in Detroit, MI, and lovingly grow fresh, organic produce with no pesticides or GMOs. Mark comes from a long line of farmers—his agricultural expertise and passion for supporting the next generation of farmers is unmatched. Candi began farming with her husband and has since become an established member of the wider agricultural community, serving as the Vice President and Promotion and Education Co-Chair of the Wayne County Farm Bureau. The two have established agricultural greenspaces at three high schools and support a local homeschool network to understand where their food comes from, how to sustain their own families through gardening, and to get excited about the potential for farming as a career and way of life. 2132 The fourth author is a faculty member and director of the Eileen Lappin Weiser Center for the Learning Sciences. The center’s mission is to “propel the innate capacity for human learning and to foster relationships that lead to wondering, understanding, becoming, and thriving” (University of Michigan, 2025). She has extensive experience collaborating with community members to design and create learning experiences and study how people learn within them (e.g., Herrenkohl et al., 2019). All authors were involved in developing the materials, with the farmers taking the lead conceptual role. The center director connected the team with university resources, including hiring a learning sciences fellow (the teacher educator) to lead the development of the curriculum materials. The teacher educator, Jessica, wrote the lessons that make up the materials (and this paper) while meeting regular ly with Candi and Mark for conceptual feedback, and with Leslie, the faculty center director, for input on the process and pedagogical decisions. Community-based partnership A key component of the Center for the Learning Sciences’ multipronged vision is providing resources and support to address challenges identified by community members. The intention of this work is to start with the community and leverage university resources to work towards the community members’  vision of success, defined not by institutional or researcher goals, but by the community partners themselves  (Tiger, 2024; Tuck, 2009). In this case, we took up Candi and Mark’s goal of developing agricultural curriculum materials that prioritize a vision of farming that is accessible and sustainable for youth. Once the curriculum materials were finalized, we met together and identified three essential components (and highlights) of our partnership process: prioritizing place, leveraging expertise and relationships, and feeling seen and heard. We describe each of these components below. Prioritizing place Coming from a primarily white institution and attempting a collaborative partnership with Black urban farmers in a majority-Black city, the center director and teacher educator (who both identify as white) worked to immerse themselves in the farmers’ space—by attending community events, volunteer ing on the farm, sharing  meals together, and accepting invitations to be present with the farmers even when these moments did not have a direct impact on the larger project. We realized early on that we needed to engage with the community partners on their home turf to develop meaningful, reciprocal relationships where their mission would be prioritized over university goals (Yamamura & Koth, 2019). We did our best work on the farm, sitting in lawn chairs or the back of a flatbed truck, and replacing our laptops with printouts. Jessica was also able to shadow Candi as she worked with youth in a summer program—an  experience that underscored Candi’s self-described goals for youth and the curriculum materials and provided invaluable insight into how she might enact those materials  with youth. By prioritizing place, we began to shift dynamics of power towards the community partner (Tiger, 2024). Leveraging expertise and relationships Essential to this collaboration was recognizing both community and university partners’ unique expertise while finding ways to support CWO Farm’s goals (Tiger, 2024). This required each of us to listen closely to each other and build relationships based on trust and mutual respect (Yamamura & Koth, 2019).  In taking an asset-oriented approach to this partnership, we were able to leverage the community farmers’  agricultural expertise and their knowledge of the community to inform the conceptual heart and soul  of the curriculum materials. We drew on the teacher educator’s pedagogical expertise and the center director’s access to university resources and years of experience building community partnerships to create the muscles and bones of the materials  that would allow them to take shape and move. It was necessary to leverage all co-designers’ knowledge and skills to develop agricultural curriculum materials  that could be  accessed, adapted, and enacted  by informal and school-based educators (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016).

Feeling seen and heard While preparing for a university-based event to celebrate and share the outcome of this collaborative project, the community farmers shared their own highlights of this process. Candi said that “seeing [Sowing Futures] in print” was validating in that it gave her a chance to see the work she had already been doing formalized and uplifted for other audiences in ways it had not been before. Before the semester started,  she shared the curriculum materials as evidence of her work in  grant applications that will help her expand her reach in the community. Mark emphasized that one of the most important outcomes was  that they were “being heard” by the other members of this partnership. When the first three authors met throughout the summer, Jessica brought hardcopies of lessonsin-progress and asked questions like, ‘How would you use this with youth, if at all?’ Mark often posed essential conceptual questions that shaped key aspects of the curriculum, like the attention to cultural diversity and understanding profitability.  These moments  amplified the community partners’  agriculture and community expertise and made space for them to be heard.

2133 Project outcomes The Sowing Futures  curriculum materials center the community farmers’  vision for developing the next generation of farmers while blending  their agricultural expertise with established literature around youth development (Forum for Youth Investment, 2021). These farmers’ work with youth exists at the boundary of inschool and out-of-school spaces —in afterschool and summer school programs, through city-wide internship initiatives, as community events in school gardens—and the curriculum materials reflect this dynamic. As experts in agriculture and the Detroit community, the farmers’ goals for youth took  precedent over more traditional measures, like state educational standards. Instead, the curriculum materials are organized around five themes that Candi and Mark  identified as essential to support the next generation of farmers: Accessing Land, Feeding the Soil, Sowing and Growing, Garden to Table, and Careers in Agriculture. Learning design principles We worked to determine what agricultural themes would provide the structure for the curriculum materials while also making decisions about the learning design principles that would inform the content within the materials. Because of the unique context that the farmers work in and the range of places and spaces where they engage with youth and other community members, we orient ed the work around four principles for youth learning and development: that learning be personalized, empowering, culturally-affirming, and transformative (Forum for Youth Investment, 2021). This created opportunities for learning that could move across boundaries, connecting school and community spaces in ways that recognize youth as individuals who navigate a multitude of contexts every day. Just as expertise does not sit squarely in the bounds of the university, learning does not start and stop at the classroom door (Lee et al., 2003; Yamamura & Koth, 2019). Aligned with these principles, we used critical sociocultural pedagogy as a theoretical framework to  guide the design of the curriculum materials (Vossoughi & Gutiérrez, 2017). This style of pedagogy attends to questions of power and emphasizes supporting youth to develop the tools necessary to affect change and work towards social transformation. This theoretical framework aligned with the principles of youth learning and collectively informed the design of the materials. Figure 1 shows snapshots of different lessons. The full curriculum is available at myumi.ch/marsal-sowingfutures. Figure 1 Excerpts from Different Lessons within the Curriculum Materials From Sowing and Growing  From Garden to Table  From Careers in Agriculture Connected to our goals for personalized and empowered learning, we designed the materials to recognize different forms of expertise while developing lessons that aimed to position youth as experts. For example, in Feeding the Soil, there is a lesson that highlights Indigenous soil testing practices in Africa to show different ways farmers use their senses to develop essential knowledge of the land (Penniman, 2018). After reading an excerpt from Leah Penniman’s (2018) Farming While Black, the facilitator is directed to “Engage youth in a discussion to think about the value of different kinds of expertise and knowledge. ” Discussion prompts included questions like “Does the author depict these people as experts? How do you know? ” and “What are you an expert in? How did you develop that knowledge ?” (Bautista et al., 2025). Another lesson in the Garden to Table  unit has youth engage with oral history excerpts from Arab Americans describing their experiences with and relationships to food, farming, and their communities in the US and abroad (Digital Special Collections, 2025). The subsequent lesson asks youth to develop their own oral history related to food, gardening, and culture by interviewing a family 2134 or community member. A major goal of this unit is to empower youth to recognize their own expertise and the value in collecting and sharing stories from their communities. We also worked to ensure that a range of sociocultural perspectives, especially those from historically marginalized groups, were highlighted in culturally-affirming ways. For example, the first unit around Accessing Land focuses on stories of Black farmers in and around the local Detroit community. One lesson asks youth to explore case studies of Black farmers as they navigate different challenges to accessing land  (Morrone et al., 2025). It was essential that we went beyond identifying obstacles and found ways to also highlight these farmers’ successes. The Sowing and Growing unit focuses on the Three Sisters, an Indigenous agricultural practice in Central and North America where squash, beans, and corn are grown together as mutually beneficial and supportive ‘sisters’ (Kimmerer, 2013). One lesson in this unit asks youth to interrogate how authors of different and perspective while working to affirm and uplift the different cultures that are represented. Finally, to support transformative learning, we included opportunities to interrogate power and privilege through the lens of urban agriculture. The first lesson in the Accessing Land  unit asks youth to consider a quote by Malcolm X (1963/2024) before engaging in a discussion around the relationship between land, freedom, and farming: “Revolution is based on land. Land is the basis of all independence. Land is the basis of freedom, justice, and equality.” Where this first lesson urges youth to consider the relationship between farming and freedom, the final lessons in the Careers in Agriculture  unit have youth develop and pitch a plan for their own greenspace. Throughout e ach unit there are also  direct, hands-on opportunities for agricultural skill development, like soil testing, seed sowing, pickle making, and business planning. Collectively, and aligned with the farmers’ goals, these units seek to provide an outline for formal and informal educators interested in supporting youth to learn how to sustain their families and transform their communities through agriculture. Use-inspired curriculum materials A major priority for the university partners was ensuring that any product would be directly usable by the community members it was intended for (Tiger, 2024). Thus, t he Sowing Futures  curriculum materials were designed to be accessible by those who might not have traditional teaching experience —community members with agricultural expertise like the farmers, homeschool networks, and/or other outside-of-school programs. This was also a priority for Candi and Mark as much of their work with youth exists in a gray area that often transgresses school, home, and community boundaries. While the farmers typically worked with high-school aged youth, they also partnered with a homeschool network that supported children of all ages and frequently participated in community events geared towards adults. Thus, the beginning of each of the five curricular units includes learning objectives, an overview of the unit’s lessons, key terms and literacy connections, agricultural background information, connections to the learning design principles, and potential modifications for younger children and community members. Each lesson also includes a facilitation guide and youth-facing resources, like handouts and reading materials intended to support their engagement and sensemaking. Lessons learned and next steps We are proud of the outcomes of this project, yet we recognize that in many ways the work is just beginning. With the start of the school year upon us as we write this, Candi and Mark have not yet used the materials with youth, so we don’t know what works well and what needs revision. We have heard from other community members, however, that Sowing Futures is already being shared widely across a local homeschool network. We have many questions left to explore: What will different users find effective and what will they find cumbersome? How will these materials be adapted by families and educators? How will youth experience these materials? As the curriculum materials begin to be tested  by diverse users, we are considering other ways  to support their use and revision. For example, there were not opportunities for Jessica to model or co-teach lessons with Candi and Mark as part of the development and feedback process.  What might it look like to engage in this  work while making sure the community partners are empowered in the process? We hope to explore questions like these and continue our collaborative work to document Candi and Mark’s experiences with the curriculum in the future. Aligned with this year’s conference theme, we recognize this collaboration as one where we worked to intentionally partner with purpose. At every level, we sought to privilege a pluralism of expertise, engage in relationships with reciprocity, and to exist in many spaces physically and metaphorically (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Torres, 2005).  We hope that in sharing this work, we can be in dialogue with the learning sciences community to continue to reimagine what reciprocal and transformative community-based partnerships might look like. We are grateful for the relationships we built within and across these spaces, from school gardens and greenhouses to the university.
