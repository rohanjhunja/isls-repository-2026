# Game-Based Simulation for Ethics Understanding

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Though games have been proposed as potentially useful tools for research ethics education, designs have tended to focus on content-oriented understanding, ethics reasoning, and compliance. We explore a game in which contexts and pressures that tend to complicate typical research ethics decision-making were simulated. Preliminary findings suggest that the simulation was better than a traditional, video-based training in helping students realize the socially situated factors influencing ethical research decisions.

## Introduction

Governments and education leaders have called for im proving how STEM research ethics is taught (Howard et al., 2019), so that undergraduates and graduate students may be better prepared for the ethical dilemmas they will face as they move into post-graduate work. Teaching research ethics in a way that leads to future ethical practices is complicated, however, and across disciplines, the effectiveness of ethics courses has been called into question (Bucciarelli, 2008; Eckles et al., 2005; Waples et al., 2009). While games have been proposed as a potentially useful approach for improving ethics education generally (Schrier, 2015)  and research ethics education in particular (Briggle et al., 2016), they have tended to follow educational aims of awareness and compliance (Hu et al., 2023; Zvereva et al., 2023; but see Shen et al., 2021) and have underestimated systemic contributions to social practices (Aubert Bonn & Pinxten, 2019) and the inh erent embeddedness of decision making in complex social contexts and activitie s (London, 2021).  A more comprehensive approach to research ethics education requires not only attending to ethical action in terms of adherence to disciplinary or professional standards of practice, but also considering the way that actions and decisions made in situ require a combination of subject matter expertise, theories of ethics, and consideration for the real-world limitations under which ethical decisions are  typically made (time limits, incomplete information, etc.; London, 2001). In this project, we explore a game-based approach to teaching research ethics that lev erages the simulation affordances of games, providing players with an experience of conducting research in a fict ional but quasi-realistic laboratory setting in which they must navigate typical pressures of publication  and collaboration. Our approach departs from  prior models where  verisimilitude is expected to lead to students ’ content-based understanding. Instead, we explore how ethically complex and ambiguous  material should be incorporated into game-based learning materials in ways that lead to productive post-game understandings, discussions, and subsequent learning. In prior research, we found that players’ interpretation of the game depended on the ir knowledge of research practices, with graduate students  who had some research experience  more readily identifying and being concerned with the contextual pressures within the research simulation as compared to undergraduates who had no prior research experience, and who tended to focus on more superficial eleme nts in the game (Gaydos & Nardo, 2022). In the present study, we focused on understanding 1) h ow students with no research background make sense of the game, and 2) how understanding from game play compares to understanding developed through a typical ethics training. By investigating how game play supports students’ sense-making of the simulated research experience and ethical quandaries therein, we aimed to better characterize the pedagogical role the game might play in a larger curriculum, especially to inform our design of post-game reflection and discussions that would support students’ ethical understanding and development. Write or Wrong: Games as mediators Write or Wrong (WoW) is a 2-d game that simulates four years of graduate student research, taking place primarily at a computer in a fictional student’s lab. The game is focused on ethical decis ion making associated with publishing research and presents players with key d ecisions being made around activities like whether to spend time reading background material (i.e., journals, b ooks), taking courses to improve their writing, res ting, or directly writing papers to publish. WoW’s design is based on two key ideas: 1) that rea l-world ethics problems are often wicked, resisting a clear “correct” answer and sometimes requiring re-solution (Nardo & Gaydos, 2021; Sicart, 2013), and 2) that games can support learni ng through experiences that need not simulate subje ct-matter content, rather can provoke transformational learni ng by presenting players with experiences that don’ t fit with their prior understanding of the world (English, 2013). Players are guided through the decisions that are germane to research publications. As the game progresses, players are increasingly confronted with social and institutional pressures that graduate student researchers might feel as a part of their work. In this study, we compared the WoW experience to a typical research e thics education approach, the elearning course on research ethics provided by the Japan Society for the Promotion of Science (JSPS), a mandatory module for all recipients of national grants. Specifically, the purpose of this research was to explore how the game versus the traditional approach resulted in different changes in students’ thinking regarding authorship ethics, including AI use, research obligations, and social expectations.

## Methods and analysis

Participants were 15 undergraduate students (7 played WoW) recruited from a small private university in Japan. Participants were divided into conditions on a rolling enrollment basis  and based on student availability. At the start of both condition sessions, participants were given 5 minutes to answer the question “When deciding whether to use AI to help your writing, what are important things to consider in order to do so ethically? How  are these related to the ethics of the decision?” Then, participants either played WoW or worked on the JSPS course for 60 minutes with an optional five-minute bathroom break after thirty minutes. After completi ng WoW or the JSPS course, participants were given 10-minutes to discuss and answer three group discussion questions: “(GDQ1) What do different “authorities” of graduate students and researchers (e.g., bosses, la ws, job requirements, etc.) expect? (GDQ2); What makes research authorship “fairness” difficult  to achieve? (GDQ3); What “responsibilities” do researchers have to consider in order to make ethical decisions? ” These discussions were audio and video recorded and transcribed. Finally, participants were asked to answer the same question about AI ethics that they had been asked at the start of the study. The entire experiment lasted around 90 minutes. Written responses to the individual pre  and post questions were descriptively coded by two researchers separately (Saldaña, 2009). Each response was treated as a unit and multiple codes were allowed. Two researchers then reviewed each of the descriptive codes  in order to reach a consensus about what codes to a pply to each response. Consensus-derived descriptive codes were grouped thematically. Group discussion transcripts were coded in the same  way as individual responses – descriptively first, with researchers coding separately, followed by discussion and consensus. At times, group written responses were unclear, as what was written was often a summary of the group’s discussion. For these, researchers referred to the transcript of the group discussion to better understand the meani ng. Given the brevity of group responses  across the three questions, descriptive codes were pooled and then organized into themes.

## Results

Individual differences in students’ responses to qu estions about AI writing ethics were not observed t o be meaningfully different and are not reported here for brevity. Descriptive codes for group  responses were organized into t wo themes: 1) “Individual oriented” describing positive qualities of an individual  (e.g., commitment and determination) and good practices of a researcher (avoiding plagiarism, being honest); and 2) “Setting oriented” referring to setting influenced work challenges (e.g., expectations from bosses, collaboration issues, the nature of the work as ambiguous). Across all responses to the three questions, students’ answers in the game condition were “setting-oriented,” noting the social pressures signaled by the high-output research lab context presented in the game (e.g., pressure from their advisor to publish, the need to maintain a work/life balanc e). In contrast, students’ responses in the JSPS condition responses were almost entirely individually oriented, raising issues such as compliance with rules (e.g., avoiding gift authorship, appropriate data handling) or research ideals (e.g., honesty).

## Discussion and conclusion

Group responses to the questions about  authority, fairness, and responsibilities suggest that the simulation of social pressure in a graduate publishing environment helped orient students’ understanding of the ethical decisionmaking around academic authorship practices  away from focusing on individual action and toward the contexts of decisions. As the game was not designed to be used on its own, rather was inte nded to be embedded within a graduate seminar  on ethical research practices, further work is needed to determine how to best l everage the educational aspects of students’ orientations and to support discussions regarding contextual pressures influencing ethical research publication decisions.  Though preliminary, these findings are promising for supporting a simulation-based approach in the stresses of graduate school can be used to provide an experience that shapes subsequent ethical discussions.
