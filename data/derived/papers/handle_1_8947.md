# Playful Learning Following Deviations: A Mixture of Tinkering

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This work expands on process-based accounts of the intersection between play, learning, and failure. We examine middle school and college players navigating Baba Is You, a puzzle-based video game, based on prior scholarship in debugging and problem-solving. We document how players notice deviations, generate causal explanations, and revise their approach when problem-solving. Using qualitative interaction analyses and quantitative sequential analyses, this work shows how players approach the puzzle through persistent tinkering guided by generating causal explanations of game mechanics and providing revision rationales. In addition to nuancing the field’s understanding of the relationship between failure and learning, the study has implications for theorizing about and designing for learning through play and problem-solving.

## Introduction and literature

Play is a perennial childhood activity and an enduring quality of adult life, considered by many to rise above the status of a privilege and toward recognition as a h uman right (Souto-Manning, 2017). In the learning s ciences, play is recognized as a common and generative conte xt for learning (Gee, 2005). Numerous mechanisms ha ve been proposed to describe how play supports learning. In the context of a puzzle-based video game named Baba Is You, we examine one of these mechanisms in this paper:  how play-based learning productively facilitates responses to impasses, breakdowns, incompatibilities, or failures. These terms are heterogeneously defined in the literature, but are primarily concerned with moments (however brief) in which actions lead to outcomes that are out of sync with a player’s goals (Clifford, 1984). Scholars have developed many arguments to account for how failure during play supports learning, including collaborative discourse about the game mechanics (Anderson et al., 2018; DeLiema et al., 2019), just-in-time feedback (Gee, 2005; Williams-Pierce, 2019), play-based framings of failure that curb the otherwise aversive experience of struggling (Juul, 2013), and flow during the play activity (Kiili et al., 2012). A central challenge for the field is to build on these accounts to document process-based views of what players are doing following moments of failure during play to generate learning. Building on DeLiema, Bye, & Marupudi (2021), the backbone of our approac h is scholarship that has over decades consistently recognized three components of debugging (Katz & Anderson, 1987): (a) noticing a deviation between the programmer’s preference/goal and the outcome when running their code; (b) forming a causal explanation of why that deviation arose; and (c) formulating and implementing a plan to revise the code and resolve the discrepancy. Deviations are not neutral, objective characteristics of goal-oriented play. Particular situations stand out to players as deviations from intention/expectation based on the player’s goals and their awareness of some form of misalignment between those goals and their approach. In the problem-solving literature, this type of problem finding is non-trivial (Hayes, 1989). Following moments of deviation, there is a robust human tendency to search for a causal explanation (Weiner, 2018). In the debugging literature, causa l explanations for why a program is failing drive how the programmer fixes their code (Ko & Myers, 2005). Lastly, when moving through a go aldirected play activity, players revise their approach to resolve deviations. Without havin g a ready-at-hand fix, players engage with problem-solving, in that players do not have “a prescribed set of actions to reach the goal,” and have to consider multiple parts and possible solution paths (Salehi et al., 2020, p. 157).

## Study goals and context

We center the following exploratory research question: How do players (middle school and college students) of a puzzle-based video game notice and respond to deviations during play in ways that foster their learning of game mechanics? The play context for this study is an in dependent video game, Baba Is You (see a screenshot in Transcript 1). Players use a keyboard to move Baba, a small sheep-like creature. The game’s core conceit is that players see the game’s rules within the playable game field (e.g., the phrase WALL IS STOP is out in the open), enabling the player to interact with particular wor ds in the rule phrase. Moving a word, and thus brea king (or creating) a rule, changes the physics on that level (e.g., a previously impenetrable wall is now permeable) as the ICLS2022 Proceedings 1421 © ISLS player tries to find a winnable arrangement. The pl ayer must add the WIN attribute to an object (e.g.,  FLAG IS WIN) and then touch it, but the player does not know at the outset what object will allow them to win. We selected Baba Is You because the player’s interactions with explicit rules makes their problem-solving process particularly visible. Furthermore, the game requires no previous content knowledge, while having a unique structure  with a consistent rhythm of new rules, which make it accessible but challenging for a diverse body of players, and hence calls upon authentic problem-solving practices previously identified in the literature (Salehi et al., 2020).

## Method

Middle school students (n=13) and college students (n=12) with no Baba Is You experience participated in the study. Participants played the game on recorded Zoo m sessions with a researcher for an hour once a wee k for three weeks. We used a think aloud protocol for the  sessions and the researcher did not intervene unle ss the participant needed help. For data selection, we examined data across all players, focused on Baba Is You levels in which players were (a) progressing after learning core game mechanics; (b) grappling with new mechanics; and

(c) solving the level typically in under 5 minutes.  We chose two levels – Float and Changeless – that met these

criteria. We transcribed the video data from these two play sessions for all participants following co nversation analysis conventions and including multimodal detai l. Our research team worked over several months on interaction analyses (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) of other transcribed Baba Is You levels. Over time, we iteratively refined our problem-solving framework and codebook. Our 5-member researcher team then broke into (rotating) teams of 2, each team member coded a transcript while watching the primary data, team members met to compare their interpretations, and then worked toward a con sensus. Each team kept a record of interrater discr epancies. Discrepancies were then discussed with the full team and used to update and formalize our coding scheme, before we re-coded the data and reached a new consensus. Below, we present interaction analyses (Jordan & Henderson, 1995), and sequential analyses (Faraone & Dorfman, 1987) to investigate players’ problem-solving processes. ## Discussion

We aim in this work to extend a long-running inquir y in the learning sciences into how players respond  to deviations during play in ways that deepen their exploration of the game world. First, we note that it is somewhat misleading to say that players only respond to deviations; they first have to notice them. This process of noticing deviations is active work in its own right, and ign ites the player’s exploration of new game mechanics. Second, and in alignment with prior scholarship on the productivity of tinkering (e.g., Berland et al., 2013), we found that players tended to operate within a baseline pattern of alternation between noticing a deviation and revising their approach without outwardly stating what had gone wrong and how their revision might work; they were seemingly ‘playing with’ the game mechanics to understand the puzzle at hand. Third, the vast majority of players eventually branched out to generate causal explanations of their deviations and/or provide rationales for why their revisions might work, clear indicators of their sense-making about the rules of the game world. Fourth, we found that efforts to explain the game’s mechanics by generating causa l conjectures and revision rationales primarily tra iled moments of noticing deviations.
