# Neurodiversity in Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning: A Literature Review

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
R ecent evidence has shown a significant prevalence of neurodivergence in the general population, suggesting that many learners, including students and research participants, are neurodivergent. This situation entails potential concerns for both research results interpretations and ethical considerations. In this context, we explore d the intersection of neurodiversity and the CSCL through a review of its international journal and handbook, finding a minimal use of neurodiversity-related terminology, despite indirect references toneurodivergent traits. The study identified 58 publications with potential reference to the neurodivergent population by filtering key terms—such as equity, inclusion, and low ability— that align with neurodivergent experiences, though often without explicit acknowledgment. We conclude that there is a significant gap in explicit consideration of neurodivergent learners within CSCL scholarship and argue that the CSCL community may have inadvertently included neurodivergent participants.

In this context, we explore d the intersection of neurodiversity and the CSCL through a review of its international journal and handbook, finding a minimal use of neurodiversity-related terminology, despite indirect references toneurodivergent traits. Insights on neurodiversity have permeated through education research and practice, with respect toneurodivergent people. Broadly speaking, the term neurodiversity refers to natural variation of differences in human brain functions and behaviours, while neurodivergent refers to those whose natural variances impede their social daily lives or their ability to participate in classroom learning. The neurodevelopmental conditions, such as Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder, Autism Spectrum Disorder, and other learning disabilities are considered under the umbrella of neurodivergent variations affecting cognitive, affectual, and sensory function (Bertilsdotter Rosqvist et al., 2020). Some studies estimate that between 15% and 20% of the student population is included in the neurodiversity label (Hamilton & Petty, 2023; Statistics Canada, 2023). Such a high prevalence of neurodiverse students represents both an ethical concern and an opportunity for the C omputer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) community. Studies within CSCL might have inadvertently involved neurodivergent participants, with potential implications for  the conclusions drawn from research findings. The research team consists of two doctoral students, including the lead author who identifies as neurodivergent, and an Associate Professor who is  committed to advancing neuro-inclusion and awareness in CSCL. We decided to review the CSCL literature, from the international journal (ijCSCL) and the international handbook (iHCSCL), regarding the potential involvement of neurodivergent population. While we recognize that ijCSCL and iHCSCL show more prevalence towards typical learners, we decided to investigate potential evidence of a neurodivergent population involved within CSCL  scholarship. Advocating for the consideration of the neurodivergent population by the CSCL community, w e report the results of a literature review exploring how ijCSCL and iHCSCL may have involved neurodivergent individuals  and provide recommendations for considering neurodiversity in educational research and practice pertaining to CSCL. We searched publications from the ijCSCL dated 2006-2024 (n=457) and iHCSCL (n=35). We filtered through the titles and abstracts using terms such as ‘neurodiversity,’ ‘neurodivergent,’ ‘special education, ’ and ‘accessibility’ to identify references to the neurodivergent movement. These search terms yielded no results, and we decided to conduct a systematic review using terms often associated with the neurodivergent population. We followed a multiplier approach (Page et al., 2021) for systematic reviews regarding selecting and filtering relevant articles. For inclusion criteria, we focused on all the publications up to December 2024 in ijCSCL and iHCSCL.We refined our sources from ijCSCL database by removing duplicates (n=395), corrections (n=387), erratums (n=385), and book reviews (n=384) before screening articles that met our selection criteria. The terms we used for searching  documents related to the neurodivergent population derived from the literature and our experience in the classroom and educational institutions: “ bias,” “collaboration,” “equity,” “intervention,” “low,” “regulation,” “risk,” and “visual.” We identified 63 publications using at least one of these terms in the title or the abstract. Then, we review ed the publications for relevance to our interest, selecting 58 documents for the review.

The most noteworthy years were 2006 (n=9), 2008 (n=7) and 2011 (n=12) as they had larger numbers of articles published that contained, defined and contextualized terms aligning with the neurodivergent movement. It was in 2008 when, for the first time, the terms 'inclusion’ and ‘bias’ offered contextual understanding and illuminated barriers that align with the neurodiversity movement. In 2011, we found publications including the term ‘equity’ and offering a contextual understanding and illuminated barriers that align with group participation. Representation of terms The most noteworthy terms were ‘ collaboration’ (n=58), ‘low’ (n=12), and ‘equity’ (n=5). It was probable that ‘collaboration’ would have the highest term representation due to the focus of CSCL. ‘C ollaboration’ has the highest term representation when other terms such as ‘equity’ and ‘inclusion’ are the lowest term representation. This is because key components of collaboration are equity and inclusion; therefore, it would have been hypothesized that equity, inclusion, and collaboration would have had similar term representation. The term ‘low’ was filtered generally as many researchers described participants or their level of understanding in different contents. For example, researchers wrote ‘lower order thinking skills’ (Wegerif, 2006), ‘low agreement ratings’ (Buder & Bodemer, 2008), ‘low prior knowledge’ (Ke, 2008), ‘low achievers’ (Molenaar et al., 2011), ‘low ability’ (Molenaar et al., 2011; Walker et al., 2011), and ‘low-level argumentation’ (Pontual Falcão & Price, 2011) however it was not until 2011 that Cress et al. (2011) wrote ‘intellectual disability.’ Using and contextualizing ‘intellectual disability’ aligns more with the neurodivergent movement as ‘low-ability’ or ‘low-achievers’ can relate to multiple levels of learners. Equity was first offered contextual understanding in 2011.  Schwarz et al. (2018) recognized the difference between equity and equality in their research. They researched the emergence of conceptual learning in a collaborative setting, elaborating on critical moments in group learning and teachers' identification of critical moments to scaffold students to the emergence of conceptual learning (Schwarz et al., 2018). This distinction between equity and equality is important to meet the individual needs of student wellbeing and learning goals. However, the term ‘equity’ was not represented again in the selected and filtered articles after this publication until the iHCSCL published a chapter interrogating the role of equity, diversity and inclusion in CSCL (Gomez, et al., 2021).  In this investigation, Gomez et al. (2021) define Equity, Diversity and Inclusion (EDI) within the context of CSCL and outline the challenges of scalability and identify three critical issues: language, differentiation, and identity.

Our discussion on the terminology within the ijCSCL and iHCSCL relates to the rise of the neurodiversity movement that has broadened over time. Neurodiversity is not a diagnosis but an identity that encompasses a variety of neurodevelopmental and more recently, mental health conditions and seeks to move the conversation away from medical ‘disorders’ and instead focusing on how an individual perceives the world (Bertilsdotter Rosqvist et al., 2020).  This context is important as the language changes as our understanding of neurodevelopmental conditions increases and research expands, we need to look through early research in ijCSCL and iHCSCL with the lens of behaviours, characteristics, that may represent neurodivergent traits. As we considered terms that might assist us in finding connections to supporting neurodivergent learning, we selected terms that are frequently used in discussions of supporting learners with social, cognitive, academic, and wellbeing needs. With these terms, we found evidence that research in CSCL  may have included individuals with neurodivergent traits, which is expected due to the high prevalence of this population.  Using such terms as a framework can help researchers situate neurodivergent learning within the literature that could otherwise seem disconnected or even disinterested in supporting equity-deserving students.

In this paper we elaborate on the evidence of the high prevalence of neurodivergent people, demonstrating that both the journal and the handbook present research and findings that connect toneurodivergent learners.
