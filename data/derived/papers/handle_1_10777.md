# Ambient Identity Cues for Minoritized Students in Elementary STEM Classrooms

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Children build identity as early as elementary school as they receive messages about who belongs in which disciplines, often through ambient identity cues. We studied how and why elementary STEM teachers create classroom designs and use teaching materials that serve as material, relational, and ideational identity resources with which children build their disciplinary identities. Two elementary STEM teachers, and three minoritized elementary students participated in this study. Data were collected from classroom design, teaching materials, journaling, and artifact-based interviews. Findings include that teachers’ sensemaking of ambient identity cues varied between objects for classroom design and those for content teaching.

## Introduction

Embedded in classrooms, schools, and communities are ambient identity cues that signal who belongs in the space or profession, and who does not. In three studies o n ambient cues among undergraduates (Cheryan et al., 2009; Davis et al., 2020; Murphy et al., 2007), visual elements in STEM learning environments (e.g., masculine posters and gender-unbalanced videos) were related to female students’ sense of belonging, but not to that of male peers. Ambient cues have also been studied in political segregation (Motyl et al., 2020) and marketing (Koo & Kim, 2013). These studies indicate that the impact of ambient cues depends on participants’ sensemakin g of the cues, which in turn varie s according to identity. For example, when participan ts rated neighborhoods, they interpreted ambient cues in the community (e.g., ma ny ethnic restaurants or many churches) differently  as per their political identities as liberals or conservatives (Motyl et al., 2020). To our knowledge, there is no study examining ambie nt identity cues for racially and/or ethnically minoritized students in elementary STEM education. We study elementary classrooms because stereotypica l conceptions are shaped in early years, and this causes some youth to disidentify with certain subjects and careers (Luo et al., 2021; Van Tuijl & Vander Molen, 2016). Positive early experiences  in STEM can contribute to positive STEM identity even in the face of negative intervening experiences for girls (Cohen et al., 2021).

## Theoretical framework

This study is grounded in the sociocultural theory of identity  (Nasir et al., 2020; Penuel & Wertsch, 1995), and critical whiteness studies  (Applebaum, 2016; Picower, 2009). One’s identity  emerges through interactions with material, relational, and ideational identity resou rces (Nasir & Cooks, 2009). Material identity resources communicate with students about who they are and what they cando  as well as who they can be  and what they will be able to do  (Nasir & Cooks, 2009; Pinkard et al., 2017). Relational resources refer to relationships with teachers, peers, and others that communicate with students about who they are and are not (Nasir & Cooks, 2009; Pinkard et al., 2017). Ideational resources refer to ideas that are comm unicated about what is good and valued (Nasir & Cooks, 2009; Pinkard et al., 2017). Critical whiteness studies attend to invisible structures that generate and sustain white privilege (Applebaum, 2016). Using critical whiteness as a lens in research allows one to make the invisible visible and deconstruct the invisible  (e.g., Le & Matias, 2019; Picower, 2009; Rogers & M osley, 2006). Whiteness in the classroom should be decentered a nd challenged so as to dismantle its hegemonic norm s but also to re-imagine science and beyond with racial diversity (Daly, 2022; Le & Matias, 2019). While examining material, relational, and ideational resources, we also aimed to unmask and decenter invisible structures that can serve to generate and sustain white privilege.

## Research question

What ambient identity cues are used in classrooms, and how and why?

## Setting and participants

We recruited two elementary teachers who teach STEM  in a predominantly White school district. In our recruitment pitch we explained that we would work with them for professional learning about ambient identity cues and the opportunity for critical examination of the  classroom environment and materials in light of amb ient identity cues. Amanda (note: all names have been changed) is a Caucasian female who is a former elementary homeroom teacher  and has a bachelor’s degree in elementary education. Camila is a Latina who is a former mathematics teacher and professional engineer and has a bachelor’s degree in engineering. We also recruited three minoritized elementary students for a lab study. Jada was an African American girl  who was entering second grade. She was homeschooled. Juan was a Latino boy who was entering fifth grade. Miguel was a Latino boy who was entering third grade.

## Data collection and analysis

Teacher data were collected from classroom design (e.g., objects displayed on walls), teaching materia ls (e.g., videos shown to class), semester-long journals in which teachers recorded intentionality for their classroom design and objects and reactions th ey observed, and six one-hour focus group artifact-based interviews. Student data were collected from 40-minute prompted interview s in which they each provided reactions to classroom design materials we collected from the STEM classrooms, as well as a set of posters of influential scientists and engineers. Our theoretical framework informed the coding schem e. Example coding scheme nodes include intentionality in objects, identity consideration, diversity consideration, reactions to objects, and objects that exclude the minoritized. We conducted three rounds of pilot coding to test t he coding scheme, and revised the coding scheme accordingly. Interrater reliability Kappa score s were 0.74 for teacher data  and 0.84 for student data.

## Findings and discussion

Theme 1. Teachers’ intentionality differed when selecting objects for classroom design versus specific content teaching Teachers’ identity considerations were more specifi c to race and ethnicity of adults represented in objects for classroom design than in objects for content teachi ng. For example, Amanda explained her intentionality in displaying a set of posters of STEM professionals in her classroom: “What stood out to me was they [students of color] said they don't see themselves in our classrooms. And that kind of hit me hard. I want them to see themselves in my classroom.” Amanda’s intentionality illustrates her unders tanding of the function of material identity resources. Camila also explained that she included STEM professional posters with diverse races on her classroom bulletin board. We showed the set of posters to our lab participants and asked whom they already knew and about whom they would like to learn. All three student participants knew little about STEM professionals of color, which is  aligned with  W hiteness manifested in classrooms  (Le & Matias, 2019). Our participating teachers’ intentionality in classroom objects exhibited efforts against Whiteness in STEM. However, teachers’ intentionality differed when selecting obj ects for content teaching. Ambient cues in objects used in content teaching were often connected to mi noritized students more with respect to who they are and what they cando than who they can be and what they will be able to do (Nasir & Cooks, 2009; Pinkard et al., 2017). For example, in a video series used in class, children of color pla y the student role, while the teacher role is played by Dr. White (name has been changed) who is a White man with answers to all questions and problems. Children ask scientific questions but cannot find the answer and must seek Dr. White’s knowledge. When the video was shown to youth in our lab study, Jada noted that Dr. White was her favorite character because he “looks like a science teacher.” Dr. White was Juan and Miguel’s favorite too, and they had seen him often in their STEM classrooms. When invited to share ideas for adding new ambient c ues considering no ambient cue connect ed to future identities of minoritized students, both Amanda and Camila acknowledged that such ambient cues are much needed. Nonetheless, they expressed concerns and difficulties in finding teaching materials that address standards, are age-appropriate, and reflect equitable ambient cues. For example, Camila noted, “the resources aren’t out there for all of them... I wonder sometimes if I’m representing enough cultures.” Theme 2. Design against white epistemology and design conforming to white epistemology coexisted Design for classroom or teaching clearly include d teachers’ intentionality to invite interest, curiosity, and engagement of minoritized students with relatable material identity resources. But we also observed unintentional inclusion of ambient cues that conformed to stereot ypical conceptions. For example, Dr. White’s pale skin, glasses, and white gown signals who belongs or does  not belong to science. When prompted to share thoughts about stereotypical adult characters in these videos and other objects, both teachers reported that they paid special attention to content relevance and diverse races and gender of children in the objects. Up to then, no one had discussed with them that those adult characters can reinforce stereotypical conceptions unintentionally. This was not only because of White scientists’ race and gender but also the fact that he is the only one who does authentic science, while children of color and girls struggle. This makes the video series and other materials fortify whiteness in STEM. As Ahmed (2007) explains, whiteness orients “bodies in specific dir ections, affecting how they ‘take up’ space” (p. 150). White male scientis ts have taken up STEM spaces, which directs spaces not only in the present but also in the future, for example, through the videos in the present study. Children of color are the ones who will be part of the future and yet, these ambient cues frame future science with “a hegemonic racial dominance” (Le & Matias, 2019, p. 15).

Ambient identity cues such as Dr. White’s  skin color were obviously visible but not noticeabl e to teachers. Such ambient cues reproduce a white epist emology that mirrors “the economic, social, politic al, or cultural interests of dominant groups in society” a nd maintains “life-worlds that lead to unequal outcomes for racialized people” (Teo, 2022, p. 2). Unnoticeably embedded whiteness in materials demonstrates “how whiteness functions as a habit” (Ahmed, 2007, p. 149). This does not mean that teachers have a habit of practicing whiteness, but that structural systemic racism makes whiteness invisibly ordinary. Our participating teachers indeed desired to show materials that included adults of color, in tuitively, even wi thout an explicitly critical lens against whiteness in STEM. They reported spending many hours to find open resource videos that contai ned characters that her students of color and girls can “look up to”. For example, after extensive searching Amanda found a good video on optical engineering: “I just wanted to show them a real example of real p eople in this career and seeing them excited about it so... if you want a career in this, keep loving science, keep loving math.” Theme 3. Teachers used relational and ideational identity resources to compensate for suboptimal material identity resources, but often with more focus on “all” and “anybody cando STEM” than racially and ethnically minoritized students Teachers recognized their function as relational and ideational identity reso urces. Amanda negated gender discriminatory comments that emerged from material identity resources by communicating the idea that everyone regardless of gender is valued in STEM. She noted, “I emphasize how both boys and girls can be and anybody cando this, anybody can be interested in all of th is.” Her role as an ideational identity resource (Nasir & Cooks, 2009; Pinkard et al., 2017) was coupled with relational identity resources that she strived to create by connecting the idea to students’ identities. These practices can help to buffer inequitable resulting impact of material identity resources in the classroom.

Nevertheless, ambient cues that address intersectionality of identities also considering racial, ethnic, and spatial identities would have included equitable ap proaches specifically for minoritized students of c olor. Approaches for all alone could lead to more focus o n the majority, which is opposite to our teachers’ intention, given the context of predominantly White classrooms. Teachers can learn to become more “racially liter ate” to critically identify how STEM “has become racialized as white property” (Mosley, 2010, p. 452)  through ambient cues in materials. Racial literacy can be instrumen tal to disrupt racialization (Mosley, 2010; Rogers & Mosley,

2006) and deconstruct power and privilege recycled throug h white epistemology (Hazelbaker & Mistry, 2021;

Madkins & Morton, 2021).

## Implications for research and practice

While this study has a small sample size,  it does have some important, yet preliminary implications. It is critical for teachers to understand the importance of not just representatio n of races and ethnicities of students from minoritized backgrounds somewhere within the list of characters in videos or books. Expecting that a child would look to another child of a similar age for repre sentation alone (without seeing future self) is problematic in two ways. First, it does not allow children to imagine a possible future in STEM. Second, children characters in much media are clueless and must rely on adults to impart wisdom. When children internalize this message, they could see themselves as not capable of engaging in STEM a nd unable to control  their own destiny. With inclusion of equitable ambient identity cues in classroom spaces and instruction, learning spaces can invite all students to sense belonging. Knowledge built from this study can contribute to research for addressing educational inequity through equitable designs and also advance sociocultural theories of identity (e.g., Nasir et al., 2020; Nasir & Hand, 2006; Penuel & Wertsch, 1995) and critical whiteness studies (e.g., Applebaum, 2016; Picower, 2009).
