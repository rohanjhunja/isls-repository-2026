# Generative AI and the Next Generation of Feedback Systems: A Hybrid Approach for Higher Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores the integration of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) with peer feedback to leverage their complementary strengths in formative assessment within higher education. Using data from 217 participants across two academic terms, it evaluates the three dimensions of feedback: Quality, Usefulness, and Actionability; and five feedback classifications: Task Constraints, Concepts, Procedure, Mistakes, and Meta-cognition provided by AI and peers. The results reveal complementary strengths of AI and peer feedback: AI excels in structured, precise, and consistent evaluations, while peer feedback fosters critical reflection and human-centered insights. The sequencing of feedback modalities is shown to influence learning outcomes, with AI feedback effectively setting a foundation for deeper engagement with peer feedback. These findings support a hybrid feedback system that balances AI’s consistency with peer feedback’s relational depth, offering pedagogical insights for a scalable, learner-centered approach to enhance educational outcomes in an AI-driven learning landscape.

## Introduction

Feedback is essential for effective learning, helping students deepen understanding, refine their skills, and promote academic growth. However, it faces persistent challenges related to scalability, time efficiency,  and providing personalized guidance (Black & Wiliam, 1998, 2006; Shepard, 2010). A promising solution to these limit ations is peer-to-peer assessment, which engages students in giving and receiving feedback and fosters a collaborative learning environment (Panadero & Alqassab, 2019). T he positive effects of peer feedback on the educati onal experiences of graduate students have been well-documented (e.g., Black & Wiliam, 1998; Falchikov, 2001; Van Zundert et al., 2010). Meta-analyses have shown that peer feedback enhances academic performance and develops critical thinking, self-regulation, and metacognitive skills (Li et al., 20 19). However, the peer feedback can sometimes be inconsistent, and subjective, raising concerns about  reliability and fairness (Van Zundert et al., 2010; Tzirides et al., 2024).

Generative A rtificial Intelligence (GenAI) introduces new possibilities  for formative assessment, offering timely and personalized feedback (Kim et al., 2024), while addressing issues of scalability and consistency (Dai et al., 2023). These systems have shown positive effects on learning outcomes, m otivation, and engagement (Meyer et al., 2024). Additionally, their ability to align with analytic and holistic evaluation rubrics (Puntambekar et al., 2024) ensures structured and reliable feedback (Mizumoto & Eguchi, 2023).  Nevertheless, concerns about over-reliance on AI due to its limitations, including a lack of empathy, contextual understanding, and occasional inaccuracies persist (Otaki, 2023).

Comparative research underscores the complementary strengths of peer and AI feedback. While peer feedback is often valued for its specificity, relat ability, and dialogic nature, AI feedback excels in  providing structured, immediate, and impartial evaluatio ns (Banihashem et al., 2024; Zapata et. al., 2024). This study aims to contribute to the literature on the complementarity of AI and peer feedback by examining students’ perceptions of AI feedback in comparison to peer feedback in online learning environments. Specifically, it investigates how students evaluate the quality, usefulness, and acti onability of GenAI feedback and how these perceptions differ from peer feedback. The study also explores how bot h feedback modalities address key aspects of studen t work, including task constraints, conceptual and procedural knowledge, error identification, and metacognitive insights. To achieve these objectives, we first present our research questions and methods, including analy sis of GenAI feedback provided to graduate students during Spring and Summer 2024. We highlight both synergies and differences between AI and peer feedback, present k ey findings, discuss implications, and provide insights for integrating GenAI with peer learning and feedback practices in higher educ ation. This study extends prior work by incorporating RAG-enhanced GenAI and comparing feedback sequencing ac ross terms, offering a novel analysis of interaction effects between AI and peer feedback.

## Research questions

This study explores the potential of GenAI for providing feedback, focusing on the following key questions:

1. How do participants' perceptions of AI-generated feedback's quality, usefulness, and actionability

compare to peer reviews?

2. How do AI-generated feedback and peer reviews differ in their  effectiveness in addressing various

aspects of student work, such as task constraints a nd requirements, conceptual knowledge, procedural knowledge, error identification, and metacognitive insights?

## Methods

This study examined graduate students'  (N=217) perceptions of using AI-generated feedback alongside peer feedback during the Spring  (n=86) and Summer (n=131) 2024 academic terms at a U.S. -based university. The sample was predominantly female (69.1%) based in No rth America (89.9%), pursuing advanced degrees (70.5% Master’s, 23.5% Doctorate). Ages ranged from 18 to over 55 years. The study received IRB approval (No. 17887), and informed consent was secured from all participa nts in accordance with ethical research standards.  The research focused on the effectiveness of feedback in terms of quality, usefulness, and actionability. It also assessed alignment with five feedback classifications: task constraints, conceptual under standing, procedural guidance, error identification, and metacognitive insights (Narciss, 2002, 2008; Panadero & Lipnevich, 2022).

## Educational context

The study focused on thirteen 8-week online courses during Spring and Summer 2024, where participants worked on self-selected topic for the course projects exploring "cutting-edge innovation" in education. These projects progressed through iterative stages of drafting, feedback, and  revision, using two distinct feedback processes assessed with a common rubric. The provided feedback included ratings from 0 to 4 and qualitative assessment for each criterion through the CGMap tool. The feedback processes were:

1. Peer Feedback: Provided by fellow course participants through blind peer review.

2. AI Feedback: Generated by OpenAI’s GPT-4 enhanced by Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG).

For Spring, students first generated AI feedback using CGMap on their draft work, followed by revisions, peer reviews, revisions again, and then final submi ssion. The sequence was reversed for Summer, with p eer reviews preceding AI feedback. After receiving both  feedback types, students reflected on their utilit y, documenting their insights before submitting the final project for instructor evaluation. Subsequently, students shared their perceptions through post-course surveys administered via the Qualtrics platform. Digital learning tool This study used Common Ground Scholar  (CGScholar), a social learning platform designed to support collaborative dialogue and knowledge exchange. A key feature of CGScholar, CGMap enables structured feedback, including blind peer review, and has evol ved through two decades of development.  In early 2023, the CGMap tool  introduced an AI-driven feedback feature powered by OpenAI’s GPT mod els, accessed via an Application Programming Interface (API). Building on ongoing research into the utility, benefits, and challenges of employing GenAI in educational contexts (Saini et al., 2024; Tzirides et al., 2024; Zapata e t al., 2024), the CGMap tool was enhanced in Spring 2024 by integrating RAG. The RAG combines generative capabilities with retrieval mechanisms, enabling AI to provide feedba ck grounded in external knowledge sources  (Ovadia et al., 2023). These enhancements positioned CGMap to deliver more precise and relevant feedback. Using OpenAI’s GPT-4 model, RAG retrieved targeted information from a 35-million-word vector database that includes students’ work from 2019 to 2024, instructor writings, and other authoritative materials. This RAG used OpenAI’s embedding model and tokenizer to preprocess text and retrieve records based on cosine similarity between query embeddings and stored vectors. The system dynamically retrieved the 7 most relevant records, configurable via a cutoff slider, enabling precise similarity-based searches.

## Data collection and analysis

Data were collected through voluntary  post-course surveys that were administered to participants via Qualtrics. The surveys sought to evaluate participants' experience s with peer and AI reviews during the course using a 5point Likert-scale (0-4) and open-ended questions. For this study, we focus on questions where participants rated the quality, usefulness, and actionability of both AI and peer feedback in improving their work. Participants were further asked to characterize the feedback provider ’s understanding of task constraints, key concepts, procedural guidance, identifying errors and its role in triggering reflective thinking. This exploratory research study adopts a convergent quantitative approach to invest igate the utility of peer reviews and GenAI for feedback. Quantitative data from the surveys were analyzed using STATA software. Non-numeric survey responses were first converted into numeric dummy variables to enable statistical operations. Key analyses included paired samples t-tests to assess differences between AI and peer fee dback perceptions, as well as regression analyses to identify patterns and relationships within the dataset.

In Summer, AI feedback was rated higher than peer feedback for  Quality (t = 3.31, p < 0.001) and Usefulness (t = 3.75, p < 0.001). Summer results showed a similar pattern, with  AI feedback was significant  and rated higher for Task Constraints (t = 3.57, p < 0.001), Concepts (t = 3.62, p < 0.001), and Procedure (t = 2.48, p < 0.05). No significant differences we re observed for Mistakes (t = 1.22, p = 0.22) and Meta-cognition (t = 1.80, p = 0.07).

## Discussion

This study provides insights into the efficacy of A I and peer feedback in educational settings, examin ing their perceived value across Quality, Usefulness, Actionability (RQ1), and feedback classifications such as Task Constraints, Concepts, Procedure, Mistakes, and Meta-cognition (RQ2). The findings underscore GenAI’s potential, particularly when enhanced by RAG, to co mplement and, in some cases, surpass peer feedback in addressing learners' needs. AI feedback ratings lar gely remain consistent across terms, unaffected by contextual variations, whereas peer feedback ratings exhibit greater variability aligning with prior research (Dai et al., 2023; Otaki, 2023). Additionally, the decline in peer feedback ratings for Procedure, Mistakes, and Meta-cognition in the Summer term might point to its reliance on cohort dynamics, engagement levels, and feedback sequencing. This trend suggests that peer feedback is more susc eptible to contextual influences, reaffirming conce rns about its inconsistency and subjectivity (Van Zundert et al., 2010; Tzirides et al., 2024). Moreover, the findings for Mistakes, Meta-cognition, and Actionability reveal the limitations of AI in fostering deeper re flection and humanistic understanding. Peer reviews may introduce learners to nuanced inte rpretations and humanistic perspectives that AI systems, grounded in data-driven objectivity, might struggle to replicate (Liang et al., 2023). Therefore, the study found that the two workflows c ater to different aspects of the learning process, highlighting the critical role of the sequence of different type s of feedback in the overall feedback process. The results showed that when AI feedback was provided first, it created a st ronger foundation for revision and meaningful engagement with peer feedback. This interaction between human and machine feedback represents a broader philosophical discussion about the role of AI in education. Instead of viewing AI and peer fee dback as competing forces, this study suggests that they are  complementary, each addres sing different aspects of the learning process. This duality aligns with recent research supporting integrated feedback models (Dai et al., 2023; Saini et al., 2024; Tzirides et al., 2024; Zapata e t al., 2024). However, f uture research should explore diverse contexts, extend timeframes, and account for group differences, while also adopting a broader research  design and methodology.  The study’s focus on graduate-level online courses at a single US institution, al ong with reliance on self-reported data, limits generalizability. Finally, the potential use of AI in composing peer feedback warrants further exploration.

## Conclusion

This study demonstrates the potential of integratin g GenAI and peer feedback to create hybrid feedback  systems that enhance learning outcomes in higher education.  Together, they form a complementary system that ba lances technical accuracy with relational and dialogic learning benefits. The findings underscore the value of strategically sequencing feedback modalities, with AI feedback se rving as a foundation for revisions and peer feedba ck deepening engagement and critical reflection. This integrative approach combining the strengths of automation and collaboration, creates a scalable, learner-centered feedback ecosystem. By leveraging these insights, educators can harness the power of AI and peer feedback to re imagine formative assessment, preparing students fo r academic success and fostering essential skills for navigating an AI-mediated world. This hybrid model represents a significant step forward in advancing educational feedback practices.
