# Understanding Persistence in Makerspaces: A Socio-Cognitive Perspective on Trouble and Repair Processes

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Persistence is crucial in maker-based learning, where challenges are inherent to the creative process and one of the important behavioral markers of motivation. This study examines how students respond to obstacles during making activities, reflecting persistence, using the socio cognitive theory framework of triadic reciprocal causation. We conducted a multi-level video analysis of two student teams engaged in electronic-making projects as case studies. At the macro level, we visualized 8.9 hours of making activities segmented into 1-minute intervals using making-process rugs. At the intermediate level, we identified ‘trouble and repair’ episodes that required persistence. At the micro level, we analyzed these episodes using interaction analysis, focusing on verbal and embodied actions with peers, facilitators, and the physical environment. By integrating micro-level interaction data into SCT, we provide empirical insights into the interplay of personal beliefs and environmental factors influencing persistence.

## Introduction and theoretical framing

Making is a fundamental human activity that fosters  creativity, innovation, and problem-solving. Engaging in makerspaces develops interest in STEAM (Science, Technology, Engineering, Arts, and Mathematics) caree rs, cultivates 21st-century skills, and promo tes a making mindset characterized by tinkering abi lities and a failure-positive approach (Peppler, Halverson, & Kafai, 201 6; Doorman et al., 2019). Problem-solving tasks in makerspaces require not only cognitive skills but a lso the motivation to persist t hrough challenges and setbacks (Rusk, 2016). Sustaining motivation within learning environments where failure is an integral part of the learning process is evident through persistence. According to Petrich, Wilkinson, and Bevan in the L earning Dimensions Framework, "persistence" is defined as the ability to continue working through difficultie s despite setbacks or challenges (Petrich et al., 2013). Challenges  — anything that impedes progress —serve as opportunities to understand and characterize persistence. By examining participants' responses to these challenges, we aim to gain insights into the mechanisms of persistence within makerspaces. Socio-Cognitive Theory (SCT) offers a framework for understanding motivation through the concept of triadic reciprocal causation, which posits that per sonal beliefs, environmental factors, and behaviors  interact dynamically (Bandura, 1986). Existing studies on motivation have relied on retrospective self-reports or generalized observations, often missing the detaile d, real-time processes that reveal how persistence and motivation unfold in practice (DiBenedetto & Schunk, 2022). We  use SCT lens to analyze the process-level interactions between the learner, the environment (including facilitators, peers, and materials), and behavior. One of the critical moments where persistence is observable is  during the processes of trouble and repair  (Jordan and Henderson, 1995). In the context of electronic making, "trouble" aris es when students encounter disruptions in their work—such as, coding errors, prototyping difficulties or confusion between team members. "Repair" refers to the actions taken to resolve these issues. By examining these repair processes, we can observe  persistence in action as students engage in repeated attempts to r esolve issues, experiment with different strategies, and collaborate with others to find solutions. Specifically, we seek to answer the following research question: How do personal beliefs, environmental factors, and behaviors to influence students' persistence during trouble and repair in making activities?

## Methods

24 ninth-grade students (9 females, 15 males; aged 14–15) from the same secondary school participated, forming eight teams (including one individual). Participant s had limited prior experience in makerspaces but e xpressed interest in hands-on projects. The study occurred within a three-week summer workshop at the school, held five days weekly (1.5 –2 hours/session). Weeks 1 & 2 (Foundational Learnin g): Students learned electronics fundamentals, Arduino programming, sensors/actuators use, and TinkerCAD software in classroom/computer lab settings. Week 3 (Project Development): Participant s, in self-selected teams, identified community/home/school problems and developed electronic-making projects, guided by a facilitator. Optional afternoon sessions were provided; 6 teams utilized this to extend their pro ject development. Project development activities du ring Week 3 were unobtrusively video-recorded (~10 –12 hours per group), capturing detailed verbal and embodied interactions. Ethical approval and informed consent  were obtained beforehand.  A multi-level video analysis combined macro-level visualization (making-process rugs) and micro-level interaction analysis  was used t o explore how persistence manifests during the making process (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). In this paper we focus on team Ace’s whose making process was 8 hours and 55 minutes  by following a case study methodology (Merriam, 1998). The Ace group  consisted of three participants – Garima, Seera, Manvi. They  undertook an imitation project, building a smoke detector prototype based on a tutorial found on YouTube.

## Findings

Personal determinants affect persistence: In one instance Manvi and Seera want to seek help to overcome this challenge. But Garima exhibits the desire to solve the problem by themselves, with out relying solely on the facilitators. The team then moves on to the analysis phase followed by digital modelling, where they try to debug the block-based program. This instance brings out the “resourcefulness” of Garima (Sheridan & Konopasky, 2016). This links to the intertwined relationship between resourcefulnes s and persistence where Garima tries to think of what could be done with what they had. This verbal exchange externalized her personal attitude towards making.

Environmental determinants  (other peers): After trying to troubleshoot for some time, Garima gets frustrated and shifts the position. Initially, Garima was controlling the mouse, now Garima wants to move away and does not work on this prototype anymore. Seera is now in control of the computer system. Seera and Manvi try to trouble shoot, they go through this analysis  and material experimenting phase for four minutes.  They then try digital model making and testing during which Garima notices the serial monitor and joins them making process. This episode shows how the behavior of Seera and Manvi brought back Garima, a part of the environment as peer, into the making process. It is also import ant to note here that, it was the same Garima who exhibited resourcefulness in episode 1, gave up in this episode. Episode 1 took place when the team had just started off their project and this episode occurred towards the end of their project, after facing multiple challenges. Environmental determinants  (facilitator): We noticed that Garima and Manvi of Team Ace face a challenge and start off task actions for nearly 4 minutes till the facilitator arrives. The facilitator now tries to debug the circuit along with the participants. After near ly 8 minutes of interaction, they find the error in  circuit connection and rectify it. As they troubleshoot the error with the facilitator Garima exclaims “Now I’m confident”. P3 and Manvi proceed to material experimenting and presentation material. Here even though they had given up, interaction with facilitator brought them back on t rack and we can also witness Garima verbalizing her boost in confidence. Here the environmental determinant affe cted the personal determinant, after which a change  in behavior is noticed.

This serves as evidence that there is a varying eff ect of these personal and environmental determinant s on persistence and that persistence is not solely influence by personal attitudes.

## Discussion and conclusion

By applying SCT’s concept of triadic reciprocal cau sation, we were able to understand how personal beliefs and environmental factors, interact dynamically to influence persistence during the making process. Personal aspects, such as resourcefulness, sense of ownership, played a significant role in how students approached challenges. For example, Garima from team Ace demonstrated a strong belief in her ability to solve problems independently. This self-reliant attitude aligns with Bandura’s (1997) emphasis on self-efficacy as a critical component of motivation. Environmental factors, including facilitator interactions and peer dynam ics, significantly influenced student’s persistence. The facilitator’s timely intervention after the team  had given up reignited their engagement.  In team Ace, Seera and Manvi’s continued efforts influenced Garima to re-engage with the task after frustration and giving up. This scenario demonstrates the reciprocal nature of  behaviors within SCT, where one student’s persiste nce can motivate others (Bandura, 1986). Following Garima episodes in the findings gives us the fluctuating n ature of different influences (personal, environmental) o n persistence. This also shows that different influ ences are prominent based on the time spent on the project and challenges encountered. This study  makes contributions to the existing literature on m aker education and motivation. By capturing real-time, micro-level interaction data during the trouble and repai r processes, we provide empirical insights into how persistence manifests in the making process. This is important because it demonstrates how SCT can be operationalized to analyze the immediate, mo ment-to-moment interactions that contribute to persistence. Such an approach can inform the development of more effective educational interventions that are sensitive to the complexities of learner engagement in collaborative, hands-on settings. This also provides a scope for analyzing motivation as a process which has been the call in the contemporary field for nearly a decade.
