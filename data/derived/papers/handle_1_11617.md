# Comparing Award-Winning Educational Games: Description versus Experience

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Game-based learning can effectively engage students. However, teachers have difficulty evaluating educational video games (EVGs). Dube and colleagues proposed five benchmarks for the descriptions of learning apps. Independently, we developed the Teacher Lens for Digital Relevance (TLDR) evaluation model for EVG play. We compared the descriptions versus game play of four award-winning EVGs. While the descriptions included all Dube’s benchmarks, game play ratings differed. For EVGs, teachers should consider both the description and the play experience.

## Background

Game-based learning is one effective tool for classroom instruction. However, many teachers do not take it up for several reasons. Teachers may not have the skills o r experience to successfully incorporate games into  their practice, and even when they do, it can be laboriou s to do so (Foster & Shah, 2020; Kangas et al., 201 7). One major initial hurdle is identifying high-quality educational video games to use. Compounding this problem is the fact that there are few models or benchmarks for teachers to use for such an evaluation. To help teachers with this problem, there is a need for a systematic appr oach to video game evaluation that also prioritizes the teachers’ time. A possibl e first step is to identify elements of high-quality video games that can be easily evaluated from the game designer ’s website (Dube et al., 2020). However, this may not be enough on its own. The teacher should also play the  game to understand how and why they might use it i n their classroom (Foster & Shah, 2020; Kaimara et al., 2021).

This paper describes one possible approach to this problem. The question addressed is, “How do the website descriptions of award-winning educational games compare to the played experience?”

## Theoretical perspectives

Dube and colleagues proposed a model for evaluating  educational apps broadly, based on five criteria (Dube et al., 2020; Montazami et al., 2022). These elements were defined as follows: (1) curriculum-using lessons or containing an academic program that is already taught in a school or specific course; (2) feedback-providing information about student performance on a task; (3) scaffolding-using techniques to move students toward a stronger understanding; (4) learning theory-specific pedagogical approach(es) that has been shown to improve achievement; and (5) development team-clearly identifies expertise and includes teachers.  We previously investigated how websites of award-winning games reflected these criteria (Duvall & Shah, 2024). We have also developed an evaluative tool for teachers to use in gameplay: the Teacher Lens for Digital Relevance (Duvall et al., 2023; Duvall et al., 2024). This too l guides teachers to look at three elements: (1) aesthetics/mechanics analyzed during a test play ph ase (Hayak & Avidov-Ungar, 2020; Kaimara et al., 2021; Sanchez et al., 2012), (2) learning opportunities (Gee, 2005) analyzed d uring a deep play phase, and (3) cultural responsiveness evaluated reflectively throughout th e game play experience (Gay, 2000; Ladson-Billings, 1995; Powell et al., 2016).

## Methods

We used a version of directed qualitative content analysis (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005), with the theories mentioned above providing our analytical lens. The team is ma de up of a lecturer and five master ’s students in a learning sciences program at a private US university. We played and evaluated the games separately, then met to discuss the results and resolve any disagreements before arriving at our overall evaluation. We evaluated award-winning video games using a modified version of Dub e’s benchmarks using a qualitative evaluation of the extent to which those  benchmarks were covered, rather than a binary yes/ no to say they were present. All the games we selected did include some representation of the five elements. Next, we used the TLDR approach to evaluate the game play and rated the games on the extent to which they were aesthetically pleasing, provided opportun ities for learning, and provided culturally relevan t gameplay. While Dube focuses on the descriptions of these app lications, which is an important initial step that may save teachers some time, if the game’s description meets  those requirements, the teacher still needs to play the game to understand if it will be productive for them to use in their classrooms.  For this project, we focused on four award-winning K-12 educational video games from the 2023 Serious Pl ay Conference (https://seriousplayconf.com). The games were: Mission US: No Turning Back, Venture Valley, Headlines and High Water, and Pengeby.

## Results

We found that all four games included the five benc hmarks identified by Dube and colleagues. However, No Turning Back and Venture Valley had the highest results when we looked at the extent to which these benchmarks were addressed, with a 92% score on our rubric  (see Table 1). Headlines and High Water  placed second with 84%, and Pengeby was still above average with a 76%.

As one detailed example, No Turning Back includes multiple curricular resources for teachers, including lesson plans. It describes in-game feedback, telling the players that they can use badges to determine the outcome of the game. The website also mentions scaffolding,  with decisions becoming more complex as the game progresses. Regarding learning theory, the website specifies what learning standards the game covers a nd three specific learning goals based on those standards. Finally, the game’s website describes a development team made up of teachers, students, and advisors who experienced some of the events described in the game When evaluating the game play experience, however, Headlines and High Water  placed first, scoring an 86% on our TLDR evaluation. No Turning Back  and Venture Valley were tied for second, with 79%, while Pengeby only scored 58%. As an example, comments related to  Test Play for Headlines and High Water  were that it was “interesting from the beginning.” During Deep Play we noted that there were multiple ways for players to complete the game and checkpoints that allowed the player to go back and replay sections if they wanted to do so. Finally, Reflective  Evaluation indicated that the game empowered player s and taught the whole person-intellectual, social, emotional, and political-but did not offer many diverse perspectives or oppo rtunities for collaboration.

## Discussion

This study further highlights that it is important for game designers to include key information about educational technology benchmarks on their websites. However, that is only the first step for teachers to evaluate these games. Once potentially qualifyi ng games are identified, there is still a need for the teacher to experience the game to understand how and why they might use it in their i nstruction, possibly through demonstrations or tria l versions. It is also important to understand that evaluations  of games, particularly of game play, is likely to be subjective rather than objective. Teachers need tools to help them approach this process in an organized and analytical way. It is important for researchers to work with practitioners to identify how we can support these efforts and do so in a way that is meaningful but also conserves the teachers’ resources, particularly time. As part of the TLDR project, our long-term vision is to have a teacher-centered social media platform that allows teachers to easily analyze educational games, as well as to share that information with ot her teachers and collaborate on when/how/why they use video game s in their unique contexts. For this to be successf ul, however, there must be a systematic, replicable, or ganized approach to this evaluation. Further resear ch and refinement around the usability of Dube and colleagues’ benchmarks and the TLDR model is warranted to create a usable and sustainable approach for teachers to take when evaluating video games.
