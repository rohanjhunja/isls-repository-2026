# Epistemic Commitments: A Lens for Heterogeneity in Science

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research about modeling emphasizes the importance of heterogeneity in science, yet also seeks curricular convergence. In response, we propose two constructs. Heterogeneityseeking curricula emphasize multiple representations of phenomena, offering an image of science as messy and nonlinear. Commitments parallel epistemic cognition by focusing on values that shape students’ modeling; however, rather than looking for disciplinary practices in students’ modeling, we take students’ values as a starting point to articulate commitments. Using commitments, we analyze six implementations of a heterogeneity-seeking 6th grade modeling curriculum, comparing commitments to epistemic criteria and considerations.

## Objectives and significance

Research about modeling in science classrooms highl ights the importance of heterogeneity in science le arning (Rosebery et al., 2010), emphasizing the complexity  involved in developing scientific expertise (Piers on et al., 2021; Jaber & Hammer, 2016; Manz & Suárez, 2018). A t the same time, a growing body of scholarship seek s curricular pathways for convergence of epistemic and representational practices (Barzilai & Chinn, 2017; Berland et al., 2016; Reiser et al., 2021). In this paper, we amplify the productive tension between heterogen eity and convergence with two constructs. Heterogeneity-seeking curricula  emphasize multiple perspectives of phenomena across representational forms. Though we acknowledge shared understandings are essential for classroom praxis, in a heterogeneity-seeking classr oom, knowledge products constitute an emerging, sha red vocabulary that gives voice to students’ ideas and questions rather than being end-points of inquiry. Commitments offer a lens for exploring the values that motivate  and inform students’ interactions with models and with one another. Our notion of commitments, paralleling epi stemic cognition, focuses on students’ values. Howe ver, whereas epistemic criteria (Pluta et al., 2011) and  considerations (Berland et al., 2016) look for dis ciplinary practices in students’ modeling, commitments take s tudents’ values as a starting point, mapping them t o disciplinary resources that are sometimes overlooked in classrooms. These constructs offer lenses for noticing and capturing the discursive life of models in a classr oom community by emphasizing students’ values and i deas, problematizing how phenomena have been positioned a s objective and external to students’ modeling work (Reiser et al., 2021), instead showing them to be a production of the classroom’s discourse.

## Conceptual framework

Though “commitment” has been used in varying ways (e.g., Chinn et al., 2011), we conceptualize commitments as fine-grained resources (Hammer et al., 2005) that shape how students use, value, and learn from models. From a resource perspective, commitments are activated a nd expressed by individuals in response to conceptu al and social contexts (Elby et al. 2016). Yet, commitments do not reside solely in individuals; at a group level, emergent epistemologies are jointly constructed and reconstructed from resources within communities of practice (Goodwin, 2018). As a result, individuals can enact and displ ay sometimes-conflicting commitments (Clark, 2006; diSessa et al., 2004), including those that represent their  membership in multiple communities (Bang & Medin, 2010). We propose that commitments can function in some si milar ways to the “ideals” of the apt-AIR epistemic cognition framework (Barzilai & Chinn, 2017), though their role for researchers and for students extends beyond epistemic cognition. As an analytic lens, epistemic  cognition frameworks take disciplinary values as a  starting point, searching for forms of these values in students’ work (e.g., Berland et al., 2016). In contrast, commitments take students’ values as a starting point (identify ing students’ values and mapping these commitments to disciplinary constructs). This perspectival shift o pens analysis to a wider range of students’ resourc es that may not align with science education’s normative concep tion of disciplinary values. We distinguish between  these lenses in three ways. First, because commitments emerge from students’ modeling activities, they include material and phenomenological aspects that are backgrounded in research about epistemic ideals. Second, while s ome commitments in our data aligned closely with primary epistemic ideals, others would be considered “secondary,” “vague,” “nonepistemic,” or “less sophisticated” us ing existing frameworks for epistemic criteria (Plu ta et al.,

2011) and practical epistemologies (Berland et al., 2016). Third, commitments played different roles for students’

modeling than epistemic ideals; rather than only being used to evaluate models, commitments were also used (a) ICLS2022 Proceedings 885 © ISLS to explain why or how one model should inform anoth er, (b) to position models as complementary, and (c) to relate models to broader contexts. In the context o f this heterogeneity-seeking curriculum, we use a r esourcefocused lens to ask two research questions: What commitments emerged that influenced students’ modeling? and, How can a lens of commitments help science education researchers identify and analyze students’ values and their role in modeling?

## Method

We analyze data collected during a design study (Cobb et al., 2003) that iteratively refined a modeling curriculum about ecosystems. This study was conducted in a public middle school in a small suburban school district in the southeastern US in collaboration with a STEM teacher, Ms. S. Six of Ms. S’s 9-week 6th grade classes participated (August 2018 to March 2020, 105 students total). Fr om each class, 5-6 students were selected by the 6t h grade teaching team as representative of their peers, and these students were interviewed about their experiences in the unit. All iterations of the curriculum foregrounded  modeling. Throughout the unit, students were instr ucted to create diagrammatic, physical, computational, and e mbodied models. The unit was framed by two design challenges: students designed a biosphere, a closed-system physical model that included plants, snails, and guppies (a typical pet fish in the US); and they pr ogrammed a computational model that represented a l arger ecosystem of plants, snails, guppies, and guppies’ competitors and predators. Throughout the unit, the  students gathered information and iteratively re-represented  the guppies’ environment with increasing complexit y. We analyze focal student interviews and whole class di scussions to identify students’ commitments and how  they shaped students’ modeling using inductive coding (C harmaz, 2006). To identify commitments, we transcri bed interviews and whole class discussions with turns o f talk as the unit of analysis. We created initial coding categories in part based on students’ language (e.g., “real world”). We compared these categories to s tudies of professional science and to research about epistemic ideals in science classrooms. In this paper, we have grouped commitments into categories (see Findings). This structure is not hierarchical (no commitment is considered more “sophisticated” than the others), and these commitments could be named and organized in a variety of ways; thus, rather than focusing on the relationship between th ese four commitments, we focus on the nature of the se commitments and their role in a heterogeneity-seeking modeling curriculum.

## Finding: Commitments

In interviews, most focal students (26 out of 33) v alued connecting their models to the “real world.” This commitment maps to several epistemic criteria. From  Pluta et al. (2011), it maps to the primary criter ion of “accuracy” (“how faithfully the model depicts the target”) and the secondary criterion of “realism” (“use the word real”). In prioritizing representing the real world, some students’ responses also map to the primary criterion of “explains-2” (Pluta et al., 2011), because they val ue models that “show how and why” (Carlos: “how a g iant biosphere works”). From Berland et al. (2016), valuing models that “show how and why” addresses the question, “What kind of answer should our knowledge product provide? (Nature [of account])” (p. 9). Explaining “how and why” suggests a mechanistic account, which Berland et al. have characterized as “more powerful” for constructing and evaluating knowledge” (p. 13) compared to a descriptive account (Carlos: “tell how it looks like”). In contrast, in our data, students did not always prioritize mec hanistic accounts; for example, Caleb wanted to exp lore a descriptive question: how much oxygen a guppy needs.

A majority of focal students (24 out of 33) also va lued models that could “show” phenomena to themselves or others. The commitment to seeing and showing phenomena maps to several epistemic criteria. Like the commitment to real-world, seeing and showing in some cases connects to “explains-2” (Pluta et al., 2011) and “Nature of Account” (Berland et al., 2016), because students valued models that showed mechanistic explanations (e.g., Nora: when algae are spread out, guppies die faster) although they sometimes also appreciated descriptive accounts (e.g., Nora: “more environmental” food web representation). This commitment also connects to “clarity” (“model is easy to understand;” Pluta et al., 2011, p. 496) and “audience” (“Who will use our knowledge products and how?”; Berland et al. 2016, p. 9).

Several focal students also valued models that enab led them to “control” variables by holding them constant or to “change” variables by manipulating t hem (15 out of 33). This commitment overlaps with s everal epistemic ideals about evidence; however, whereas these epistemic ideals are primarily evaluative, students in our classroom typically foregrounded controlling and ch anging variables when they were designing models. I n a convergence-seeking curriculum, students might eval uate a model by considering the quantity and qualit y of evidence that was included (“quality of support” and “accuracy” in Pluta et al., 2011; “Nature of acco unt” and “Justification” in Berland et al., 2016). In retrospective reflections, students’ commitments functioned in a similar, evaluative way (e.g., Carlos: “it was kind of like testing soil differently”). Yet, as they were desig ning or using models, students more often emphasized affordances for investigations (e.g., David: “the computational  model ICLS2022 Proceedings 886 © ISLS won’t go wrong unless you tell it too”). This forward-looking stance toward models positions models not only as “knowledge products” that can be evaluated but also as generative tools for exploring ideas. Over half of the focal students (18 out of 33) voiced a sense of responsibility to care for the plants and animals in their biospheres or for the species or e nvironments represented by their models. Caring for  model components and their referents influenced how students thought about designing, using, and learning from models. There is no analogous epistemic ideal in Pluta et a l. (2011) or in Berland et al.’s (2016) epistemic c riteria or considerations. While Barzilai and Chinn’s epistemi c cognition framework includes affective and ethica l dimensions (e.g., “caring about… epistemic performance” and “aspiring to epistemic justice;” 2017, p. 13) based on research that demonstrates that science learning is inextricably intertwined with students’ epistemic affect and epistemic motivation (Jaber & Hammer, 2015), these aspects a re narrowly focused on the production of (disciplinary) knowledge rather than broader axiological dimensions of learning.

## Discussion: Nature and role of commitments

In these classrooms, commitments functioned as reso urces (Elby et al., 2016; Sandoval, 2005). Students ’ commitments were pressing but unstable (interpreted and enacted differently by different students and by given students at different times). Commitments were also linked to models in a perspectival, rather than an  inherent, way (took on different meanings for different students in relation to models).  As a result, commitments were not a “property” of models or of students – they instea d influenced how students valued and interacted wit h their models. In our data, commitments functioned in some ways like epistemic ideals by acting as evaluative resources during modeling. Yet, because each of these commitm ents connects to different, yet overlapping, aims, commitments were not hierarchically ranked. As a result, we would not expect to see students’ progress toward “sophisticated” (Krist, 2020) or “primary” (Pluta et al., 2011) commitments, as they might with epistemic ideals. And because commitments were not stable across mode ls, we also would not expect to see students develo p a shared set of criteria that could be used to evaluate models generally, an activity common in convergence-seeking curricula (Pluta et al., 2011; Ke & Schwarz, 2021).  Instead, a lens of commitments emphasizes making connections across multiple representations and ways of knowing (Warren et al., 2020). First, students used commitments to justify one model informing another. For example, Karla suggested using the plant investigation to inform the computational model. She explained that students “did not really use [the plant investigation] as much as they needed to ” to make the computational model “accurate with re al life.” In other words, she wanted to use the plant investi gation to strengthen connections between the comput ational model and the real world. In other cases, students saw the computational model as informing the design of “real” models. For example, Nataly used her computational model to imagine the guppy in her biosphere jar; he r commitment to caring for the guppy helped her empathize with the guppies. Because she was concerned that the computational guppy looked lonely, she suggested her group include two guppies in their jar. Second, students used commitments to position model s as complementary–by connecting their own models (of different types, e.g., computational, food web), and also by connecting their models to their classmates’. For example, Lacie characterized all her models as “helpful because, like, you can find out different things.” She described how she could change variables in the computational model (“make guppies do what you want them to do) and explained how she could show phenomena in the food web (“helps you show, like, what they’re gonna eat”). Students also positioned models of the same type as complementary. For example, when Nora and S ean presented their food webs, rather than positioning one model as correct, they described how each group’s model showed different perspectives of ecosystems (Nora’s “energy pyramid” and Sean’s “more environmental” model). Just as commitments were not ranked hierarchically, models were not seen as competing with or replacing other models. Instead, each model offered new perspective s that contributed to an assemblage of representati ons that became more valuable as models and perspectives were incorporated. Third, students used commitments to relate their models to other experiences. For example, Wade wanted to give the plants “a chance at life,” and Dolores and Hunter suggested that the biospheres could teac h guppies how to “survive on their own in the wild.” In these cases, a commitment to caring guided how students interacted with their models. Commitments also helped students  think about why models might be used to investigat e phenomena. For example, Micah explained that a scie ntist would need to “test the stuff on the computat ional model and do it in real life [in a biosphere] to se e how the effects would turn out… so you don’t mess  up the river,” explaining how the models could be used to protect (care for) populations in a river. As in these examples, students’ commitments helped them consider the roles of models in the outside world. In summary, while commitments aligned in some cases  with epistemic ideals, their roles in these classrooms extended beyond those of epistemic ideals. Epistemic ideals are used to evaluate models. They support convergence-seeking aims by providing students with  agreed-upon standards for developing and assessing knowledge. To this end, they guide students in crea ting mechanistic and “gapless” explanations (Ambiti ous ICLS2022 Proceedings 887 © ISLS Science Teaching [AST], 2014, p. 3), producing abstract and generalizable knowledge (Berland et al., 2016), and justifying claims with evidence (Krist, 2020; Pluta et al., 2011). These are important goals for epistemic education (Barzilai & Chinn, 2017) that can help students appropriate powerful disciplinary practices. However, exclusively foregrounding these goals for epistemic education could limit the values that students use to navigate their STEM learning. Our data indicate that students candraw on a broad range of values to guide modeling, which sometimes resemble professional practices that are backgrounded in science education.
