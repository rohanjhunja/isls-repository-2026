# Designing an Embodied Learning Environment for Children’s Body-Based Sense-Making

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper outlines the design and implementation of an embodied circulatory system activity in which children in grades 3-5 created wearable, light-up blood cells and enacted the roles of blood cells, parts of the heart, and lungs. Drawing on video data, we characterized and coded two types of student-generated gestures, focusing on their role in supporting bot h embodied modeling and making connections to everyday experiences. These emergent embodied actions provided a window into transitional thinking and revealed how learners made sense of unfamiliar science concepts using gestures. This highlights the importance of attending to gestures produced during learning mome nts as powerful indicators and resources for sense-making in embodied learning environments and offers implications for both the design of learning environments and science education research.

There is an increasing interest around the use and importance of the body for learning and cognition. This literature (e.g., Ba rsalou, 2010; Johnson, 2024) emphasizes the connection between  human processes of thinking and reasoning and the body’s actions and perceptions. Researchers have explored new ways to leverage embodied cognition in learning environments to explore complex concepts (Danish et al., 2015; Lindgren, 2014; Kelton & Ma, 2018). These types of learning environments are deliberately designed to afford the use of gestures, movement, and other body-based forms of expression in learning. However, many interventions have relied on the integration of technology, such as motion tracking tools, mixed reality interfaces, and gamification, to the detriment of exploring more affordable and accessible, low-tech interventions. Further, while this work has provided valuable insights into how to design embodied activities, findings also suggest that children’s attention is often drawn more to the technology than the intended learning content (e.g., Danish et al., 2020). This points to the need for designs that position children’s bodies as primary resources for engaging in the learning process, with minimal  or no  technological interventions. Such an approach is important because prior research  (e.g., Alibali & Nathan, 2012; Abrahamson & Lindgren, 2014) has shown that children’s meaningful movement s alone can support both memory and learning. When children generate meaningful movements tied to conceptual ideas within a learning context (Danish et al., 2015), those movements begin to take on meaning themselves and, in turn, facilitate deeper understanding (Goldin-Meadow, 2003). One such form of movement is gesturing.

Many scholars have studied gestures produced by children. For example, Park et al. (2006) found that sixth and eighth grade students used gestures to co-construct a shared understanding of Earth science content. Similarly, Johnson-Glenberg et al. (2016) demonstrated that students who engaged in high levels of embodied interaction – such as physically swinging an object overhead to explore centripetal force – showed sustained gains in generative knowledge of physics even a week after the activity. However, much of the existing research on gesture focuses on its role during post-activity reflection. For example, children who were prompted to gesture while explaining their math problem-solving process demonstrate greater learning gains from later instruction on the same concept than those instructed not to gesture (Broaders et al., 2007). Very few studies have explored how different types of gestures (e.g., iconic, metaphoric) emerge during the learning process. For context, i conic gestures bear a close relation to the semantic content of speech while metaphoric gestures represent abstract ideas through a physical form (McNeill, 1992).

In addition, there has been limited examination and characterization of spontaneous, children-generated gestures and other embodied actions occur ring in the moment in learning environments that are intentionally designed to leverage embodied learning. This oversight is significant, as such naturally occurring gestures can serve as powerful cognitive resources, as children often gesture to represent new ideas, and as they do this, the gestures reveal what Goldin-Meadow (2003) calls transitional thinking – offering a window into how children are making sense of novel concepts whilst making connecti ons to their prior knowledge. These emergent embodied actions signal readiness for new input and can be leveraged to support deeper learning. The current study, therefore, does two things: (1) designs a low-tech embodied learning activity on blood circulation  in the human circulatory system, and (2) examine s and analyz es the gestures children produce  during the activity either accompanying science talk (iconic) or occurring independently (metaphoric) as they made sense of science ideas. 1447

## Theoretical framework

Both the design and analysis of the activity described here are premised on embodied (Shapiro, 2019), play-based (Piaget 1964), and constructionis t (Papert, 1980; Papert  & Harel, 1991) learning theories. Embodied learning theory promotes the importance of ‘meaningful’ movements and bodily experience in learning and development (Lindgren & Johnson-Glenberg, 2013). Play-based learning involves engaging in imaginary situations and is particularly powerful for elementary students. The imaginary situation creates a context for children to explore in ways that would not otherwise be  accessible to them, allowing play to function as informal inquiry (Youngquist & Pataray-Ching, 2004). Finally, c onstructionism involves learning through making, particularly through the construction of personally meaningful, public artifacts (Papert, 1980). All of these theories emphasize the active role of learners in constructing their understanding through bodily experience. By applying these frameworks to design and analysis, we demonstrated that play-based, embodied, constructionist learning was able to elicit emergent, embodied forms such as gestures from elementary students while they learned about blood circulation.

## Method

The activity took place in a STEAM (science, technology, engineering, art, and math) afterschool club in the Midwestern United States, where a significant number of students came from low-income families. The school where the STEAM club runs has a student population that is 69% low income, 48.7% Black or African American, 28.6% white, 14.5% Hispanic/Latino, 5.6% two or more races, 2.2% Asian, and 0.4% Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander. The demographic characteristics of students in the STEAM  club roughly mirrored those of the school as a whole. The club leveraged STEAM tools, technologies, and practices to facilitate learning. The first author, Yetunde, designed and implemented the activity with support from the second author. Design This design builds on some recommendations from Danish et al. (2020) for creating embodied learning activities. This include s designing for collaborative reflection,  designing for the collective embodiment of collective phenomena, and designing for both individual and collective embodiment, as well as the interaction between the two. Collaborative reflection was integrated before, during, and after the activity  to enable the children to focus on the ideas they were intuitively exploring during the activity, which was itself both an individual and collective embodiment activity, which included collective embodiment of a collective phenomenon. The first step Yetunde took was to activate prior knowledge. Yetunde began by inviting the children to engage in collaborative reflection and discussion, during which they  shared their understanding of how oxygen gets into the body. Next, students were introduced to the fundamental concepts of the circulatory system via a brief lecture that included an image of the heart and a YouTube video  illustrating how the circulatory system works. This was followed by a handson activity where the children created representations of blood cells by constructing paper circuit s (paper cutouts in the shape of blood cell s) with conductive tape, blue and red LED light s and batteries; incorporating the LED lights allowed for the simulation of the color changes associated with oxygenation and deoxygenation. Children were then asked to act out, via role play, the processes of blood circulation based on the video they had watched, with minimal guidance from Yetunde. They also individually represented parts of the heart, lungs and the blood cells by wearing blood cell props or a different costume (e.g., picture of heart chamber or lungs) around their necks, to indicate their role in the system. This collective enactment by the children also mirrors what Ma and Hall (2018) call ensemble learning, which foreground s learning as collective and performative. Following the enactment, they participated in a reflection discussion in which  they shared their experience s of the activity and engaged in sketching to visually represent their understanding of the concepts explored during the activity.

## Participants and data collection

The participants consisted of 17 elementary students in grades 3 –5 (8 to 10 years old) enrolled in a STEAM afterschool program. Of the 17 participants in the STEAM club, 9 agreed to participate in the research study. The activity was conducted over a 1.5-hour session. We developed and recorded a pre and post discussion to enable students to articulate their understanding of the circulatory system and the activity. Video recordings were captured using a stationary room camera and wearable, chest-or visor-mounted cameras worn by four students and one member of the research team. These cameras  captured important details, such as group dynamics, participation levels, and the manner in which students engaged in the embodi ed activity. After the activity, the children were also asked to either create drawings or annotate preprinted diagrams of the heart based on their preferences. These concept diagrams served as tangible artifacts to assess their understanding of blood circulation, the process of oxygen delivery, and the relationships between elements/components of the circulatory system.

## Data analysis

Our analysis here focuses on the video data we collected. To analyze this data, we used reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2019) and microanalysis of video (Erickson, 1992; Goldman et al., 2007). Based on our initial pass on the data, we focused our analysis on how children were representing phenomena with their hands or other parts of their body. Specifically, we looked  for evidence of emergent, embodied forms during the activity, distinguishing between ones that followed talk, occurred with talk, or occurred by themselves. We then isolated these gestures (iconic or metaphoric) from other motor behavior, described their forms, and assigned meaning to them (Goldin-Meadow, 2003).

Gesture as a tool for embodied modeling In the excerpt below from the pre-embodied activity, Yetunde was using a labelled diagram of the heart displayed on the whiteboard to guide the children in reasoning through the process of blood flow and oxygenation as they prepared for the embodied activity. When Yetunde prompted the question “What does the lungs do?”, one learner, who we’ll refer to as Logan (See Table 1) actively participated and described the lungs as "pumping air,"(line 4). Also, as a way of clarifying his understanding, he reiterate d the process of how deoxygenated blood gets oxygenated. He used his hand to make a gesture indicating the lungs’ role in “putting” oxygen into the blood cell (line 6). This transcript shows that Logan’s hand movement is more than a side note – it is a window into his reasoning. His gesture demonstrates how children use embodied actions to construct  and model processes; here, he was making sense of the lungs’ function  by showing the process of oxygen “entering” blood. It suggests he was bridging everyday ideas (“pumping air”) with a more scientific understanding of oxygenation. So, the gesture signals sense-making in progress, revealing how Logan was  beginning to coordinate his prior knowledge (lungs and air) with the scientific concept (lungs oxygenate blood). Gesture as a tool for embodied connection to everyday experiences During the playful, embodied, circulatory system activity, as the children physically enacted the roles of various circulatory system components, such as the different parts of the heart, lungs, and blood cells, those who were blood cells moved along in between the legs of those who represented different parts of the heart. Yetunde wanted those acting as blood cells to move faster to mimic what happens to the blood cell when one is physically active. She said, “This time around I want you to move fast, you know what happens when you are physically active like you are running, what is happening to your blood?” Logan looked at her, demonstrated with both hands and legs and blurted, “Like someone playing basketball, go through”  (see Figure 1b). Logan’s iconic gesture here serves as more than just an expression. It represents the connection he is making to what is happening at the macro level of the human body while these micro level processes are occurring  here. By inviting and demonstrating the idea of someone playing basketball, Logan mobilizes everyday  knowledge as a resource for se nse-making, exemplifying productive hybridity (Ma, 2016) where this learner bridges between out-of-school resources and disciplinary ideas in the space provided.

Our findings build on design recommendations for creating embodied activities  from Danish et al.  (2020), particularly designing for the collective embodiment of collective phenomena  and designing for both individual and collective embodiment, as well as the interaction between the two. Collective phenomena are those made up of multiple interconnected elements (Hmelo-Silver & Azevedo, 2006). In our activity, as in Danish et al.’s prior work, we observed that designing for the collective embodiment of collective phenomena meant that children had to coordinate their actions s uch that micro-level interactions built into macro-level patterns, making features of the whole system more salient. Simultaneously, we designed for both individual and collective embodiment, as well as the interaction between the two. We found that this design principle was also successful, as c ostumes representing blood cells, heart parts, and lungs in our activity  helped children recognize the  individual roles of each, whilst also making sense of the collective phenomenon by interacting with one another.

Our findings extend prior work by also  characterizing and coding the types and functions of the iconic and metaphoric gestures produced by children during the activity. We identified that g estures were used both as a tool for modeling and as a tool for embodied connection to  everyday experiences in the sensemaking process. We posit that by characterizing gestures, we can identify more types that are productive for learning and, in turn, make space for children to produce and use them for their own learning.  This study builds on prior work on embodied learning (e.g., Gerofsky, 2010; Kelton & Ma, 2018) to demonstrate the role that both gesture and wholebody movement play in learning and cognition and, more specifically, how they serve as resources for individual and collective sense-making. Our findings  invite researchers and educators  to structure tasks and design their learning environments in ways that ma ke space for embodied actions. This study also builds on Gaudreau et al.

(2020) work to demonstrate that gestures do not require expensive technology or extensive teacher training; rather,

simply providing space for  students to spontaneously produce gestures can be effective in improving children’s understanding of highly spatial, complex, or abstract scientific concepts.
