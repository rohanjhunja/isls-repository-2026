# Visualization and Reflection to Scaffold Time-Management in a Computer-Based Learning Environment

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Facilitating practice of time-management skills in education is imperative due to its impact on short and long term academic achievement. By bringing tacit processes into students’ conscious awareness via visualizations, computer-based learning environments offer unique opportunities to enhance such practice. Nonetheless, there is a dearth of knowledge on how this impacts students’ learning and time-management, especially when it comes to secondary vocational education. Therefore this study explores how visualizations of performance combined with guided reflection impact students’ (N = 40) time management accuracy and their knowledge acquisition. Technical vocational students who worked in a computer-based learning environment that included a visualization of estimated time accuracy combined with guided reflection, were compared to students who worked in the same environment without this support. Results indicated that higher accuracy and learning gains align, that the added support may have induced an adaptation in time-management, but that learning was not affected.

## Introduction

Training of processes needed to acquire academic sk ills-often referred to as self-regulated learning skills-in education, can have direct as well as indirect effects on students’ academic achievements (Ifenthaler, 2012; Palalas & Wark, 2020; Viberg et al., 2020). But teachers are uncertain how to properly support such training, especially within the practical-oriented secondary vocational training programs (e.g., Christoffels & Baay, 2016; de Bruijn & Leeman, 2011; Eshuis et al., 2021). And research addressing support for this population is rare (Viberg et al., 2020). An example is the process of time management, a key factor of self-regulated learning (Khiat, 2019; Winne & Hadwin, 2013), but to date an underdeveloped and underexamined topic (Wolters & Brady, 2020). In the context of education, time management is the process of scheduling time to complete the learning tasks that are tied to the desired learning goals (Claessens et al., 2007; Khiat, 2019). Researchers established that good time-management has both direct and indirect effec ts on students’ learning effectiveness (Khiat, 2019). A key component in good time management is the accura cy of the estimated time required to achieve the de sired goals (e.g., Claessens et al., 2007; Landy et al., 1991). Experience combined with a continuous cycle of adaptation is needed to achieve this. Providing instructional guidance, like prompts that trigger students to plan and reflect, can help students to successfully engage in the required cycle of adaptation and improve their time management. Prompts are instructional directions that stimulate  and support students to carry out specific actions (Bannert, 2006). They are widely studied, often in the context of reflection, and their effectiveness is generally endorsed (Davis, 2003; Kori, et al., 2014). Though prompts can trigger and support students’ planning and reflection, the outcomes of these activities is sub ject to many factors. One of these being that stude nts tend to underestimate the time they need(ed) to complete th eir tasks (König & Kleinmann, 2007). This impacts t he benefits of reflecting; when students start from in accurate information their reflection is probable t o result in wrong conclusions, even when all steps (i.e., colle ction of information, evaluation of information, go al setting) are carried out successfully (as also described in Eshuis et al., 2019). Here, computer-based learning environments can provide valuable support. Digital tools can track students’ performance in learning environments and provide timely feedback for reflection, for instance via a visualization of students’ estimated time in comparison to their actual time spent. Studies have demonstrated that such ‘comparative feedback’ (feedback that offers a comparison source) is a promising instructional means that can serve as a promoter of reflection (Eshuis et al., 2021). This paper explores such a digital tool. The tool e mployed visualizations (comparative feedback contrasting students’ estimated time with their act ual time) and guided reflection prompts, in order t o support students’ time management (see Figure 1). Two group s of students were compared. All students worked in  a digital environment that required self-regulated le arning. Only one group of students had access to th e time management support tool. Because self-regulated learning processes play an essential role in students’ success in computer-based learning (e.g., Broadbent & Poon, 20 15; Lehman et al., 2014; Wong et al., 2021) and tim emanagement is a key component in self-regulated learning (Winne & Hadwin, 2013), it was expected that more effective time management would positively impact s tudents’ success in the computer-based learning environments. More specific, it was expected that m ore successful time management would relate to bett er ICLS2022 Proceedings 1389 © ISLS performance on domain knowledge tests (1); that students’ time management accuracy would improve over time (2); and that the support would improve time management and consequently improve learning (3). ## Method

This study utilized an experimental pretest (domain  knowledge test) - intervention (two sessions in a digital learning environment) - posttest (domain knowledge test) design with two conditions. All students completed two online learning environments (copies of the environments used by Eshuis et al. (2021)). They contained two online labs, eight assignments each, and instructive multi media material related to electricity and electric power transmission. For the experimental condition, the time management reflection support was added to both learning environments. This was a digital tool which consist ed of three steps: planning, evaluation, and reflec tion. The planning step was carried out at the start of each learning environment. Here students were invited to check the learning goals and the tasks in the environment and schedule how they would spend their 60 minute lesson. After students spent 60 minutes working on their assignments (or when they were done) they completed the second and third step: evaluation and reflection. Here, the to ol presented the students with ‘comparative feedbac k’. This comparative feedback comprised an overview of students’ scheduled time per assignment and the actual time they spent on it (see Figure 1). Via a series of prompts students were invited to check the overview and reflect on their findings. More specific, the prompts requested stud ents to 1) indicate significant differences between  their scheduled time and their actual time spent, 2) expl ain this difference while taking into account the t ype of assignment, their prior knowledge, and skills, and 3) think of ways to improve their time management. Measurements Students’ domain knowledge was assessed with two parallel versions of a paper-and-pencil test. To avoid order effects counterbalancing of the two versions as pre-and posttests was employed. Each test consisted o f 11 constructive response items that aligned with the learning objectives lined out in the learning environments. Each item had two sub questions: one assessed factual kn owledge (i.e., recalling a definition or formula) a nd one assessed procedural knowledge (i.e., applying a formula or explaining a principle). A sub question was awarded one point when answered fully correct. Three scores  per test were calculated: total knowledge (all ite ms), and factual and procedural knowledge separately. Cohen’s Kappa for both pre-and posttest was >0.9. Students’ time management inaccuracy (i.e., the average deviation in time from the scheduled time) was calculated using the differences between students’ scheduled time and their actual time spent per assi gnment. Where a positive score indicated an overestimation of time needed to complete the assignments, a negative score indicated an underestimation of this. A score of 0 indicated perfect time management. ## Results

The data comes from 40 students (all male, Mage 17.3 years, SDage = 8 months) of secondary vocational education (in Dutch: MBO) who completed all four sessions of the experiment. Participants were first-year students from three classes divided over two schools in the Netherlands. Students were enrolled in a technical training program preparing them for a vocational profession (e.g., car mechanic, electrical engineer). ## Discussion

The findings of the current study add to our unders tanding of how digital tools designed to support ti me management can impact students’ behavior in an online learning environment. Specifically the focus group of the current study (i.e., students from technical secondary vocational education) makes this a unique contribution; as Viberg et.al. (2020) justly noted in their review, research often addresses higher education and only rarely addresses secondary education (in their review only 4% of the studies did so). Despite the moderate sample size, the current study provides some evidence that a rel atively simple and short intervention has potential  to foster adaptation. A change was established, but it should  be noted that the change is not yet completely in line with what was desired. When looking at the average time, students were off by a similar time in both environments. It could be that a longer intervention is necessary to move from mere adaptation to adaptations with higher accuracy, or maybe added instructional support is required. T he same goes for the transfer of higher accuracy to  higher learning gains. The results of the current study co rroborate the general assumption that more accurate  time management aligns with higher learning gains. Nonetheless, the intervention in the current study was u nable to elevate students’ accuracy to such a level that significantly higher domain knowledge scores would be achieved. Also here a longer duration of the intervention mig ht be necessary to establish substantial effects th at translate ICLS2022 Proceedings 1391 © ISLS into a difference on this indirect level. It should  be noted that the result is not unusual; Viberg et.al. (2020) concluded in their review there is little evidence of self-regulated learning support tools that manag e to significantly improve learning outcomes. It could b e that differences within the population impact the effectiveness of tools (as also discussed by ter Vrugte & de Jong, 2017) and that certain design choices, like the comparative feedback, may only benefit certain students.
