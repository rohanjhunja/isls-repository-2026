# AI as Learning Partners: Students' Interactions and Perceptions in a Simulated Classroom with Multiple LLM-Powered Agents

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how students interact with and perceive multiple AI agents in a simulated classroom environment. The findings reveal that students actively participate in interactions, demonstrating both cognitive activities and regulatory activities. Students generally hold positive perceptions of AI agents, particularly regarding their cognitive support and ability to provide personalized learning experiences. However, limitations were identified in the emotional domain, with students noting a lack of emotional resonance in their interactions with the agents. These results highlight the potential of multi-agent systems to enrich educational experiences, while also pointing to areas for improvement in emotional engagement.

## Introduction

The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) has brou ght about significant changes across various sectors of society, and education is no exception, with teachi ng and learning processes undergoing significant transformations (Baidoo-anu & Ansah, 2023). LLMs have opened new frontiers for personalised and interactive learning experiences. Within this con text, the interaction between students and LLM-based agents represents a novel form of learning, in which learners are not only consumers of information but also active participants in dynamic exchanges.

LLMs such as ChatGPT have emerged as versatile tools cap able of assuming a variety of roles within educational contexts. These models can function as facilitators, collaborators, resource providers,  and as an extended cognitive capacity to assist students in g athering information and completing learning tasks more efficiently, thereby promoting students’ independent learning (Hmoud et al., 2024; Rasul et al., 2023). To better serve educational settings, various LLM-based educational agents have emerged, tai lored to meet the specific needs of learning environments, providing enhanced functionalities beyond what general-purpose models offer (Du & Daniel, 2024; Gayed et al., 2022).

Traditionally, AI systems have been designed based on single-agent models, where a single AI interacts with users and process es tasks independently. However, the advent of L LMs has catalyzed a paradigm shift towards multi-agent systems, where multiple AI agents collaborate  and interact to enhance problem-solving capabilities and decision-making processes. This transition marks the beginni ng of a new era of collective intelligence, in which the combined efforts of diverse, specialized agents  enable more comp lex, dynamic, and personalized interactions. For example, a  team from Stanford University crafted an artificial  society called "Smallville", consisting of 25 intelligent agents powered by LLMs to simulate human social behavior (Park et al., 2023). These work s are paving the way for collaborative interactions a mong agents with varied roles and functions, enabling them to simulate social scenari os, tackle complex tasks collaboratively, or provid e multifaceted insights into a given problem. The multi-agent frameworks also hold promise in educational s ettings (Mañas-Álvarez et al., 2023; Kandel et al., 2022). For instance, using several agents to  simulate authentic classroom scenarios and realisti c social interactions can offer a more immersive learning experience, enriching the types and characteristics of interactions (Yue  et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Students could practice specific skills su ch as negotiation, teamwork, or conflict resolution (Dell’Aquila et al., 2022) and achieve particular learning objectives in a dynamic and interactive environment. By leveraging the diverse roles and capabilities of multiple agents, such systems can create a more engaging, adaptive, and personalised environment tailored to individual learner needs. D espite its potential, the application of multi-agent systems in education remains limited, and emp irical studies focusing on students’ learning experiences and outcomes within such systems are even rarer.  Understanding how students navigate these interactions and their perceptions of the agents is critical for both educational research and practice. In this study, we aim to delve into the nuanced int erplay between students and LLM-powered agents within a simulated classroom context and understand students’ perceptions of these agents.

## Methodology

The simulated class with multiple LLM-empowered agents This study was conducted within the Massive AI-empowered Course (MAIC) system, an online learning platform powered by  LLM-driven multi-agent technology (Yu et al., 2024). MAIC contains six interaction ag ents, including the AI teacher, AI teaching assistant, and four types of AI students: a Sparker (a student who livens up the atmosphere with humorous expressions), a Questioner (a student who excels at asking challenging questions), a Thinker (a student who engages in critical thinking), and a Note-taker (a student who summarizes and takes notes for the entire class). As shown in Figure 1, the left area of the MAIC interface displays the teaching slides, while the right area serves as the interaction zone, where students can engage with both AI teachers and students. The system also includes a class session controller that manages interactions based on students' messages, learning materials, and chat history. Students can switch between interactive and observational modes. In interactive mode, while the AI teacher is delivering lectures, students can send messages within the interaction area to communicate with various agents. In observational mode, however, students are unable to  send messages; instead, they can observe the interactions among AI agents as the system automatically simulates the classroom environment and conducts teaching activities.

## Participants

In this study, 397 participants from various majors at a university in China were recruited to participate i n the simulated class. Of these, 131 students (30.53% female, mean age=20.06, SD=1.26) completed all six modules of the course. Twenty students participated in the interview after completing the entire course.

## Data collection and analysis

Two types of data were primarily collected and anal yzed in this study: interaction log data and interv iew data. Interaction logs were recorded throughout the course, capturing conversation-based interactions between students and various agents. A total of approximately two million Chinese characters were recorded. Each interaction entry included the following: the role of the speaker, ti mestamp, utterance, slide page, and module during w hich the interaction occurred. All interaction logs were cod ed with the  assistance of LLMs to capture the behavioral, cognitive, and emotional dimensions of student-agent interactions. A multi-step coding process was employed to ensure reliability: a) Initial coding trials were c onducted jointly by human coders and LLMs (GPT-4.0 and ChatGLM with few-shot learning) to refine the coding framework; b) A utomated coding: two LLMs coded the entire dataset, and the results were compared; c) Manual validation: human coders resolved discrepancies between LLMs’ coding, and a random sample comprising 10% of  the dataset was manually coded by human coders to verify reliability. The ICC between human and AI co ding reached 0.95, indicating high reliability of t he coding results.

Interviews were conducted with 20 participants. Thematic analysis was used to analy ze the interview data. Two coders independently generated initial co des from the transcripts, focusing on recurring pat terns and insights. The initial codes were then discussed and consoli dated into overarching themes that reflected studen ts’ learning experiences and perceptions of the multi-agent system.

## Results and discussion

Analysis of the interaction log data revealed that 13.7% (n = 18) of all students who completed the en tire course primarily adopted the “observational mode”, while 86.3% (n = 113) actively engaged in interactions with different agents. Through interviews, we explored students’ choice of learning mode and the underlying reasons. Students who preferred the observational mode believed this approach allowed uninterrupted thinking and provided a more cohesive learning experience. In contrast, students  who favore d “interactive mode”  highlighted it as a key advantage of the simulated class with multi-agents, as it encouraged them to ask questions more freely and receive immediate feedback from different perspectives. Com pared to traditional in-person classes, the interactive mode provided a supportive environment for self-expression, enabling students to overcome barriers (such as social pressure) to ask questions.  The average number of messages sent per module was 5.24 (SD = 6.62), with  an average message length o f 21.28 Chinese characters (SD = 16.94). These resu lts align with prior research on technology-mediated learning environments, highlighting the ro le of interactive elements in enhancing student participation and satisfaction (Kurucay & Inan, 2017; Wang et al., 2022). The attributes of all student utterances were class ified and coded,  broadly reflecting two types of activities: cognitive activities and regulatory activities. The majority of student utterances were related to cognitive activities, such as students asking content-related questions (48%) or expressing their opinion s proactively (13%). Most cognitive activities elicited responses from multiple agents, illustrating the dynamic and interactive nature of the dialogue. For students' cognitive-related utterances, we coded and analyzed the levels of cognition based on Bloom’s Taxonomy. The results revealed that most messages were concentrated at knowledge and understanding levels (70.4%). However, messages  involving the application of knowledge in a specif ic context or those reflecting higher-order thinking, such as analysis, synthesis, or eva luation, were relatively rare (16.0% and 13.6%, respectively). We further analyse d the cognitive levels of utterances made by differ ent AI agents during interactions, as shown in Figure 2. T he results indicated that the “Thinker” agent contributed a higher proportion of utterances reflecting higher-order thinking (90.3%) compared to other agents. Notably, nearly half of the utterances from the AI teacher and teac hing assistant were concentrated at the levels of k nowledge, understanding, and application (49.8% and 50.2%, respectively). Notably, students exhibited regulatory behaviors du ring their interactions with multi-agents, such as monitoring and regulating their learning or managin g classroom dynamics. These behaviors directly cont ributed to the course’s progression or facilitated the delegation of tasks to specific agents. Figure 2 Stacked Proportion of Cognitive Levels in Utterances by Different Agents W e further explored students’ perceptions of specific AI agents through interview s. Although students recognized that all agents were based on the same technolog ical principles, their perceptions were influenced by the distinct identities assigned to each agent. Mor eover, students’ views on AI agents varied significantly among individuals. Overall, students ’ perceptions of the agents can be interpreted from both cognitive a nd emotional dimensions.

At the cognitive level, students tended to have expectations for AI teachers similar to those they have for human teachers. Many students acknowledged that the  AI teacher effectively performed typical teaching tasks, such as delivering lectures and answering questions. This positive evaluation may be related to the fact that the nature of the teacher agents’ responses aligned well with students’ cognitive levels and were perceived as reliable due to their knowledge-based nature. However, some students noticed that the AI teacher occasionally appeared to overly align to their responses, giving the impression of excessive agreement. They expected the teacher to be decisive and independent, providing clear affirmation or constructive criticism. When evaluating AI students, the Questioner receive d the most positive feedback, with students appreciating its thought-provoking questions, some of which were described  as “beyond my imagination ”. Similarly, the Thinker garnered favorable responses, being likened to raising questions in more compli cated contexts. However, some students felt that the Thinker ’s responses were too deeply thought-out, and “exceeded the level of a real student ”. The Sparker elicited mixed reaction s: some students valued its abi lity to stimulate diverse ideas and energi ze the classroom, while others viewed it as a disrup tive presence that hindered the flow of thought and class rhythm. Opinions on the Note-taker were also divided: some students appreciated its ability to summarize class content, while others felt its notes failed  to capture the essence of discussions and critical thinking, falling short of their expectations for effective note-taking. At the emotional aspect, the level of anthropomorphism in AI agents was perceived as insufficient, with students sometimes de scribing their interactions as “detached” and lacking emotional resonance. This feeling may b e attributed to the generally high cognitive performance demonstrated by AI students, or the AI’s limited ability to establish deep emotional connections and trigger empathy and resonance. As students explained, “When I engaged in the class, I felt like those AI c lassmates were more like my teachers. It seemed like I was the only outsider, while they were the hosts, and I was merely a guest.” (S17) “Neither the AI teacher nor the AI students can repl icate the emotional connection of face-toface interactions. A human teacher brings unique in sights, often drawing from personal expertise and experience, which make the subject mo re engaging and thought-provoking. This kind of impact is missing in the AI-mediated classroom.” (S05) Students noted that the emotional depth and empathy expressed by AI agents are critical for fostering a more human-like connection. This type of connection is expecte d by students, aligning with long-standing discussions in the literature regarding emotional interactions betwe en humans and AI (Martınez -Miranda & Aldea, 2005). Emotional rapport is essential for fostering trust and enhancing learning experiences in educational settings, thus developers of AI-based interaction systems should prioriti ze advance ments in emotional intelligence.

## Conclusions

This study explored student s’ interactive experience s with an LLM-based multi-agent system  in a simulated classroom. The results demonstrated that such a sys tem effectively supported cognitive engagement, as students actively interacted with various agents. The findings also highlighted the importance of student agency i n LLMbased multi-agent learning environments, as students exhibited a range of  self-regulation behaviors, such as managing classroom processes  and learning partners. However, this also raised higher demands for stud ents’ ability to take initiative and regulate their learning in these environments, making the development of such skills even more essential. In addition, students’ perceptions of different agents were influenced by preconceived notions about agent roles, such as teacher and student, which underscores the need for thoughtful role design and transparent communication regarding agent capabilities. These findings highlight the ethical responsibilit ies of researchers and developers when designing educational agents. The anthropomorphism of agents —imbuing them with human-like traits— and the implicit authority attributed to certain roles, such as teachers, necessitate careful consideration. It is crucial to strike a balance to  avoid misleading students or fostering overdepende nce on AI agents.
