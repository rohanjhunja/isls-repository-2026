This paper uses an illustrative example to demonstrate how speculative interviewing techniques that ask youth to imagine future worlds activate different knowledge fragments than conventional methods, such as card sorting. Our methodological contribution suggests that incorporating speculative methods alongside traditional tasks reveals the complex, contextual nature of children’s socio-technical reasoning, offering researchers tools to access young people’s authentic perspectives. As we face daunting global challenges, understanding youths’ perspectives of their techno-social worlds helps us better prepare youth for their largely uncertain futures. This poster contributes to a larger effort to understand youths’ everyday thinking about their complex techno-social worlds (Blikstein et al., 2024, 2025) and is rooted in the Knowledge in Pieces (diSessa, 1993) approach, which posits that reasoning consists of multiple, potentially contradictory phenomenological primitives activated by different contexts. More recent extensions apply this framework to sociopolitical domains. Philip’s (2011) Ideology in Pieces demonstrates that ideological reasoning similarly operates through context-dependent fragments, and Wagner’s (2006) Transfer in Pieces addresses how context-sensitive knowledge resources are coordinated differently across situations, supporting the view that apparent inconsistencies reflect sophisticated context-dependent reasoning rather than cognitive deficits. This theoretical foundation presents a methodological challenge: if youths’ reasoning consists of contextually-bound pieces, then studying youths’ complex thinking requires methods that activate complex contexts. Traditional methods, such as card sorting, while valuable for studying categorical reasoning, present relatively confined contexts. We propose novel speculative interview techniques as complementary tools that create rich, imaginative contexts for exploring knowledge and ideological pieces. In this poster, we present early data from one participant interview to illustrate this methodological contrast. Across two activities —card sorting and speculative world-building—different reasoning patterns emerged. This difference represents not cognitive inconsistency, but rather the successful activation of context-appropriate knowledge and ideological pieces. We tentatively propose speculative world-building as a task-based interview technique that gives fuller access to the ecology of youths’ fragmented and contextual socio-technical reasoning. Illustrative example and analysis Semi-structured interviews lasting roughly 60 minutes were conducted with four youth aged 8-15, selected via convenience sampling. All participants lived in major metropolitan areas in the United States. Interviews were conducted over Zoom, automatically transcribed, and verified by the first author. Transcripts were collaboratively and thematically coded (Braun & Clarke, 2006) by all authors to identify cases of interest. The case below comes from one interview with a 13-year-old living in the northeastern United States, who chose the pseudonym Crookshanks. In these portions of the interview, Crookshanks participated in a card sorting exercise in which she was asked, “Out of these activities, which are ok to be done by robots: sorting mail, building cars, making coffee, driving a truck, diagnosing an illness, teaching a class.” For each activity, she was asked to explain her reasoning.  In the next part of the interview, Crookshanks was asked to speculate about the future of food through a world-building exercise where she was initially prompted with the question, “I want you to imagine eating food 100 years in the future. How might people 100 years in the future get food to eat?” This question was followed by continued development of her imagined future through open-ended questions that explored the implications of her speculations on the rest of the world (e.g., “How would this change impact the environment?”). To emphasize the ideas the participant shared, direct quotations presented in the paper have been lightly edited for grammar and clarity.

During the card-sorting exercise, Crookshanks revealed a few of her decision-making metrics for whether certain activities are okay to be done by robots. She often relied on human enjoyment of activities, changes in efficiency, and changes in human risk (“safety” or “health”) as means for deciding whether a given 2728 activity was suitable for a robot (Table 1). Within this section of the interview, her application of these metrics shifted depending on the scenario.  Human risk was more important than human enjoyment when building cars, but when driving trucks, it was more important that humans enjoyed the activity, even though it was “unhealthy.” Throughout her responses, Crookshanks appeared to associate robots performing activities with greater efficiency, improved safety, and the freeing of humans from unenjoyable tasks. This was consistent with her reasoning in the card-sorting exercise, where increased efficiency was a justification for the use of robots. When asked how the robots would be powered, she started with the existing methods of solar, ocean turbines, and burning fossil fuels, but quickly worried that those would not provide enough electricity. She pivoted to the more fantastical solution of having to “siphon the sun” despite the catastrophic implications for all life on Earth. When asked, Crookshanks indicated that she did not want this dystopian future to occur, instead choosing to present her vision of the “perfect future.” In Crookshank’s ideal future, all food is organic to eliminate pesticide use, and everyone is a vegetarian because ranching “is bad for the environment.” Since cows were not being slaughtered for beef, “we have enough cows for everyone” to have their own,  and all food is grown locally by community-operated greenhouses or in personal “Victory Farms” at everyone’s homes. In this vision of the future, humans performed all potentially risky manual labor involved in producing food, which contradicts her card-sort reasoning, in which manual labor and human risk justified robot use. When questioned about how we will be able to produce enough food through crops alone, Crookshanks responded that by using former ranching land for farming, “people could produce food w ay more efficiently.” This also contradicts her reasoning from the card-sorting exercise and her prior dystopian vision, in which robots were considered more efficient than humans.

Our findings suggest that speculative interview techniques offer a methodological tool for triangulating youths’ reasoning about complex socio-technical systems when paired with traditional methods, such as card sorting. During card sorting, Crookshanks applied consistent, rule-based metrics (efficiency, safety/health, enjoyment) aligned with popular discourses around automation. However, speculative world-building uncovered different reasoning. The same principles became fluid and contradictory: her dystopian scenario embraced total automation through robots for efficiency and safety, while her utopian vision rejected automation entirely, with humans performing all of the risky manual labor. These value tensions did not arise in the card-sorting exercise. In our interview, we observed that card sorting’s decontextualized nature elicits normative responses, while speculative interviews served as a helpful triangulation method to gain a deeper understanding of Crookshank’s reasoning. When she considered syst emic implications and lived experiences through the speculative exercise, complex value negotiations emerged.
