# Pieces of the Puzzle: Examining Multimodal Interactions in Two Jigsaw Tasks to Inform Collaboration Support

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examined students’ verbal and nonverbal collaboration across two jigsaw tasks with differing cognitive complexity. Findings show that combining modalities provides a more complete picture of group dynamics and that interaction patterns vary by task complexity. These insights inform the design of AI partners that adapt support to both evolving collaboration and the task’s cognitive demands.

Collaborative knowledge building depends on dialogic interactions (Mercer, 2000), building shared understanding (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995), and regulation (Järvelä & Hadwin, 2013). Sustaining these social and cognitive demands is challenging (Jarvenoja et al., 2019), creating a need for structured support. One widely used approach in CSCL is the jigsaw method (Aronson et al., 1978), which requires students to integrate distributed expertise into shared knowledge. While jigsaw can promote collaborati on (Roseth et al., 2019), it can increase task complexity, yielding mixed outcomes (Vives et al., 2024), prompting the need for continued and adaptive support. Recent advancements in AI have enabled adaptive systems that support collaborative learning by monitoring learners’ cognitive and interactional states. Prior work shows that AI-based scaffolding can support students’ learning (Winkler et al., 2020), engagement (Liu et al., 2025), reasoning (Adamson et al., 2014), and regulation (Järvelä et al., 2023). However, many systems provide limited insight into their underlying mechanisms, focus on individuals, or rely primarily on verbal data. However, effective col laboration requires coordination of verbal and nonverbal behaviors (Barron, 2000, 2003; Roschelle & Teasley, 1995), such as joint attention, which predicts collaborative success (Schneider & Pea, 2013). An overreliance on verbal indicators risks overlooking quieter but actively engaged students (Inigaki et al., 1991; Obenland et al., 2012), highlighting the need for AI systems that attend and respond to multimodal interaction in small-group learning. Our study addresses this need by examining students’ verbal and nonverbal behaviors across two jigsaw activities with the goal of highlighting how these behaviors might be leveraged by AI systems to support collaboration. Building on prior analyses of small-group interaction (Dey & Puntambekar, 2023) and visualization techniques (Dey et al., 2025), we extend this work to a larger set of groups and compare two lessons that differ in cognitive complexity. We address the following research questions: RQ1. What are the differences in verbal and nonverbal indicators between two jigsaw tasks of  different cognitive complexities?

RQ2. What are the patterns and relationships in verbal and nonverbal indicators? RQ3. What do we learn when verbal and nonverbal indicators are examined together?

## Participants and context

This study draws on data from the NSF National AI Institute for Student-AI Teaming, a multi-institutional effort to develop AI partners to support collaborative learning in secondary classrooms. In this study, we use data from an 8th-grade ELA class room in the Western US that implemented an eight-lesson unit on human and AI moderation of online communities. Our analysis focuses on two jigsaw lessons. In Lesson 3 (L3), students examined different community agreements and determined their use in familiar online contexts, emphasizing divergent thinking to develop new ideas. In Lesson 4 (L4), students compared five moderation approaches, and collaboratively summarized and ranked them based on co-developed criteria to reach consensus on which best support inclusive and safe gaming communities. Since consensus-oriented tasks demand greater deliberation and consideration of multiple perspectives (Xia et al., 2021), L4 was considered to be more cognitively demanding. Small groups of 2-4 students were recording using an iPad on a tripod attached to a Yeti microphone. The recordings were transcribed, separated by utterances, and chunked into 5-minute segments. We randomly selected and analyzed segments of four different groups as they engaged in group discussions in L3 and L4. 369 Analytic process We analyzed the data using both verbal (using transcripts) and nonverbal (using videos) coding schemes. Verbal analysis We developed the verbal codes (Table 1) using deductive and inductive approaches, and refined them by connecting them to the learning objectives of the unit. We examined a 10-second segment  of the transcript (including multiple utterances) and assigned a binary code of present (1) or absent (0) to each code (not mutually exclusive. Two authors double-coded 20% of the transcripts for each group, achieving an 86.4% agreement. Discrepancies were resolved through discussions, and one author coded the remaining segments. Codes captured task orientation (on-task/off-task), idea exchange (sharing ideas and ignoring ideas), negotiation, and social dynamics. Negotiation  was differentiated as ‘knowledge building’ or ‘metacognitive’, reflecting either building shared understanding (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995) or coordinating roles and tasks (Järvelä & Hadwin, 2013). Consensus was coded as positive (Consensus+) when groups reached agreement or re-entered negotiation, or negative (Consensus–) when disagreement was left unresolved. Positive (Respect+) and negative (Respect–) respect reflected active acknowledgment or dismissal of peers (Sleebos et al., 2006), while psychological safety captured behaviors that encouraged (building) or discouraged (reducing) open idea sharing and participation (Edmondson, 1999; Miyake & Kirschner, 2014). Nonverbal analysis We coded the nonverbal interactions using the NICE framework (Dey & Puntambekar, 2023). The NICE framework captures students' nonverbal behaviors when working in small groups along three modalities: eye gaze (joint or individual gaze), hand motions  (use of tool and pointing), and body movements (lean toward and away, and head nods).  We coded each  student’s nonverbal behaviors for each utterance, with a “1” indicating the presence of a nonverbal behavior and “0” indicating its absence. The codes were not mutually exclusive.  Two authors double-coded at least 20% of the utterances for each video, achieving a 74.2% agreement. Discrepancies were resolved by reviewing videos and discussion; one author coded the remainder. To provide a uniform metric that could be then used to compare occurrences of an indicator in a certain interval of time (approximately 10 seconds), we normalized the verbal and nonverbal codes by dividing the occurrences of the codes for a 10-second segment, by the number of utterances in that segment. 370 Correlation analysis We generated boxplots to examine the distribution of verbal and nonverbal behaviors over both lessons. We averaged the nonverbal behaviors by group to generate the distribution of nonverbal behaviors by lesson. We conducted Pearson’s correlations for the  verbal codes and nonverbal codes,  and generated a heatmap of the correlations of the verbal behaviors and nonverbal behaviors, individually. To examine verbal and nonverbal behaviors together, we aggregated: ● Positive verbal (verbal_pos) = Negotiation (metacognitive) + Negotiation (knowledge building) + Consensus+ (positive) + Sharing new ideas + Respect+ (positive) + Building psychological safety ● Negative verbal (verbal_neg) = Consensus- (negative) + Ignore ideas + Respect- (negative) + Breaking psychological safety ● Joint behaviors (nv_joint) = Joint visual attention + Look at others + Use common tool + Point at tool + Point at others + Head nods/shakes + Lean towards ● Individual behaviors (nv_ind) = Individual attention + Look away + Use individual tool + Lean away We performed Pearson’s correlations between these four codes to examine verbal and nonverbal together. Verbal and nonverbal interaction patterns across L3 and L4 Verbal behaviors were more variable in Les son 4 than Lesson 3 (Figure 1a). Students stayed on task more consistently in L3, though its open-ended structure also led to more off-task talk. L3 elicited more sharing of ideas, expressions of respect, and metacognitive negotiation, likely due to students’ familiarity with the content. L4, which required integrating less familiar material, prompted more knowledge-building negotiation,  indicating a need for support that helps groups regulate attention in more cognitively demanding tasks. Individual behaviors such as individual attention (IA) and use of individual tools (useindtool) occurred more frequently in L4 (Figure 1b), while joint behaviors, such as joint visual attention (JVA), use of the common tool (usecomtool), and leaning toward others (leanto), were more frequent in L3. Although both lessons followed a jigsaw structure, the higher cognitive demands of L4 may have encouraged students to work independently. Correlations: Verbal and nonverbal indicators, individually Figure 2(a) shows a heatmap of the verbal code correlations. On-task behavior was positively associated with productive collaboration, including knowledge-building and metacognitive negotiation, sharing ideas, and positive consensus, while off-task behavior showed negative associations with these processes, but was weakly linked tonegative consensus, indicating that off-task talk rarely co-occurred with collaborative processes. Psychological safety showed weak relationships with task focus but building was positively associated with metacognitive negotiation and positive consensus. This suggests that feeling safe supports collaborative interactions, even if it occurs alongside off-task interactions. Negative respect was strongly linked to reduced psychological safety, indicating that disrespectful comments, rather than the absence of politeness, undermine trust and represent a key area for intervention. Knowledge-building negotiation was associated with both positive and negative consensus, suggesting that unresolved conflicts often center on conceptual issues, while idea sharing correlated with both deeper negotiation and ignored ideas, highlighting missed opportunities to build on student contributions. These patterns suggest that task design can shape not only task focus but also the quality of 371 collaboration, highlighting the need for supports that sustain attention, foster psychological safety, and help groups acknowledge and resolve conceptual disagreements.

Figure 2 (b) presents the correlations among nonverbal behaviors. Joint visual attention (JVA) was positively associated with common tool use and indicators of joint engagement, such as pointing to the tool and leaning toward the activity area or peers, while individual attention was strongly linked to using individual t ools. This pattern suggests that where students direct their attention is closely tied to how they engage with the task, highlighting the importance of support that promotes shared tool use to sustain mutual attention. JVA was not associated with looking at others or away, suggesting that shared attention can be maintained without frequent shifts in gaze. Head nods were weakly associated with looking at peers, indicating their role as nonverbal signals of attention and understanding. These findings highlight the value of coordinating gaze, gesture, and tool use to support shared engagement, particularly in tasks that risk students working more individually as is done in jigsaws. Positive verbal behaviors were not meaningfully linked to joint nonverbal coordination, indicating that students could remain engaged wit hout aligning gaze or actions. Joint nonverbal behaviors were negatively associated with on-task behavior in L3 and positively associated with off-task behavior in L4, suggesting that shared gestures and mutual attention often occurred during off-task moments that may support social rapport rather than task progress.
