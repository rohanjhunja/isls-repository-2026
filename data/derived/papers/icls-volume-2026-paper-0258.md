# Contextualizing the Self: AI-Supported Personality Alignment Among Pre-Service Teachers

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Personality alignment is the extent to which pre-service teachers (PSTs) gain insight into their personality and engage in self-development for the necessary situational adjustment of their personality dimensions to aid instructional delivery. Grounded in the Five-Factor Model (FFM) and the Theory of Action (Argyris & Schön, 1974), this study investigated the influence of an AI-simulated learning environment (SimNigeria) on PSTs' personality alignment. Pre-service teachers from a Nigerian public university participated in a mixed-methods, randomized matched-subjects post-test design. Findings showed that PSTs who engaged with the AI environment demonstrated significantly higher personality alignment (F (1,73) = 144.63, p <.001, R² =.66). The study points to the potential of AI-simulated environments for expanding PSTs' contextualized self-reflection and situational personality adaptation.

Teacher preparation programs consistently struggle to provide adequate practice opportunities, leaving many new teachers unprepared for classroom realities (Darling-Hammond, 2017). A critical competency often underdeveloped in traditional teacher training programs is pre-service teachers' self-awareness of their own personality traits and their capacity for situational adaptation of these characteristics to meet the needs of diverse learners. Teacher training programs typically address explicit knowledge (know-what) while remaining disconnected from the tacit, contextual knowledge (know-how and know-why) required in authentic classroom environments (Medula, 2017). Challenges including placement constraints and time limitations further restrict the depth of teaching practice experience, limiting the development of competencies such as personality alignment (Oteyola et al., 2020; Ratih et al., 2021). AI-simulated learning environments show promise in addressing this gap. Dai and Ke (2022), in a systematic mapping review, found that such environments offer authentic, context-bound practice opportunities, including the capacity for adaptive personality responses in classroom settings. While simulations like simSchool (simSchool, 2025) represent considerable innovations for teacher education, there is a dearth of  empirical research on their utility for developing personality alignment. This study therefore investigates the influence of an AI-simulated learning environment on preservice teachers' personality alignment.

Theoretical review The Five-Factor Model (FFM) organizes human personality into five broad, stable traits: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism (Soto & Jackson, 2013). For educational researchers, the FFM offers a measurable framework for  examining how personality influences teaching effectiveness (Sarkar & Dutta, 2024). Personality within the FFM is not a fixed output but rather a dynamic interaction between stable trait dispositions and situational demands (Fleeson & Jayawickreme, 2015; Irwing et al., 2020). What this study measures is therefore not a change in personality per se, but a shift in teachers' perception and situational awareness of their personality dimensions. Studies consistently identify high openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness  and low neuroticism, as a pattern of personality characteristics frequently observed in studies of teacher personality (Chimezie, 2020; Anastasiadou, 2019; Digić, 2018; Devi et al., 2017). This pattern is particularly pronounced for extraversion among students of social and humanistic sciences, where social interactions are considered more important (Göncz, 2017). This profile served as the situative reference for computing personality alignment scores in this study. Extending the ideas of Buendía -Arias et al. (2020), personality alignment is defined as the intentional adaptation of educators' personality characteristics relative to learners' personality profiles to facilitate learning out comes. While Buendía-Arias et al. (2020) describe this as personality reconfiguration and Ratih et al. (2021) as interpersonal adjustment, the core processes of self-reflection, situational adaptation, and response to diverse learner personality dimensions are consistent with the operational definition used in this study. The reference profile functions as a situative benchmark supported by extant literature, not as a universal or culturally invariant standard. It functions as a structured lens through which PSTs may develop self-awareness, not as a prescriptive target for personality change. 1772 The simSchool platform was selected because its psychological architecture is directly based on McCrae and Costa (2008) OCEAN model. Each virtual student possesses a unique personality profile, meaning a teaching strategy effective with one simStudent may fail with another. To be effective, the teacher must move beyond a one-size-fitsall approach. PSTs must reflect on their own default personality expressions and adapt their interpersonal style to better connect with and manage the diverse personalities of  their digital classroom. The theory of action (Argyris & Schön, 1974) helps explain the mechanism of personality alignment observed in this study. Argyris and Schön (1974) distinguished between two aspects of a theory of action: espoused theory, which reflects an individual's aspirational or intended persona (how they believe they should behave or who they believe themselves to be), and theory-in-use, which represents the actual dispositions, beliefs and values that underlie their concrete actions. PSTs who have not yet encountered the realities of classroom practice tend to operate from their espoused theory: an idealized self-conception. Their personality self-assessments are therefore shaped more by who they believe themselves to be than by who they demonstrate themselves to be in context. The AI-simulated classroom functioned as an experiential trigger that shifted PSTs from espoused theory toward theory-in-use. When a PST who considers themselves highly agreeable encounters a simulated student whose behavior resists that approach, or one who self-identifies as emotionally stable finds themselves reacting with unexpected frustration, the gap between espoused and in-use theory becomes visible. This productive dissonance drives the personality alignment process as defined in this study.

The study employed mixed-methods design. A randomized, matched-subjects, post-test only control group design was employed for the quantitative component. This design guards against the reactive effects of pretesting, including sensitization and practice effects, while enabling comparison of treatment outcomes (Bhattacherjee et al., 2019). Participants were first matched into pairs based on department, gender, age, computer skill level and prior familiarity with computer-based simulations. Within each pair, one participant was randomly assigned to the experimental group and the other to the control group via coin toss, ensuring group equivalence. The post-test only design is supported by three safeguards: matched-subjects randomization, pilot-confirmed personality homogeneity, and group assignment by coin-toss. The characterization of SimNigeria as safe, repeatable and feedback-rich is supported by prior evaluations of simSchool (Hopper, 2018; Knezek et al., 2015; Medula, 2017). A pilot study conducted prior to the main study confirmed homogeneity in aggregate personality dimensions across both groups, strengthening the assumption of baseline equivalence without requiring a pretest. Focus group discussion was employed for the qualitative component.

The sample consisted of eighty second-year pre-service teachers from the Faculty of Education at Obafemi Awolowo University (OAU), Ile-Ife, selected based on willingness to participate, basic computer skills, and prior teaching experience. OAU was purposively selected for its standing as a leading ICT institution in Nigeria and its hosting of the Africa Higher Education Centre of Excellence (Ace Partners, 2022; EduRank, 2025). Six participants in the experimental group did not complete all sessions due to l ogistical constraints, resulting in a final experimental sample of n = 34. The control group maintained n = 40 (total analyzed: n = 74). Ten pre-service teachers were randomly selected for focus group discussions (five per group). To assess representativeness, personality profiles of focus group participants were compared to those of the broader sample. No significant differences were found across OCEAndimensions.

## Methodology & Study Context

Data were collected using a questionnaire and a semi-structured interview protocol for focus group OCEAN scale (Cronbach's α =.93). All items used a 4-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 4 = Strongly Agree); Neuroticism items were reverse-scored prior to analysis. Personality alignment was operationalized as the absolute difference between a participant's mean aggregated OCEAN self-rating and the mean of the reference teacher personality profile (maximum ratings on Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion and Agreeableness; minimum on Neuroticism). A smaller difference score indicates higher alignment with the reference profile. A larger score indicates greater misalignment. A semi-structured interview guide elicited qualitative data on PSTs' self-awareness of personality traits and experiences with personality adaptation. Qualitative data were analyzed using inductive coding. Outcomes of the thematic coding were shared with participants for member checking (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2022). The stimulus material was SimNigeria, an adapted version of simSchool contextualized for the Nigerian educational setting. SimNigeria presents PSTs with a dynamic web-based virtual classroom populated by AI-driven simulated students (simStudents) whose personalities are built on the OCEAN framework. Each simStudent exhibits distinct behavioral patterns and emotional responses. Some smile or raise their hands when engaged. Others express frustration or show signs of stress. These reactions emerge in direct response to teachers' instructional decisions, which may themselves be influenced by teachers' personality dispositions. Teachers observe simStudents' reactions in real 1773 time and must adapt to accommodate this diversity (Hopper, 2018; Knezek et al., 2015). In doing so, PSTs are brought face-to-face with their own personality dispositions in action. The experimental group underwent six structured 2-hour weekly sessions over six weeks. Session 1 covered platform orientation and account creation. Session 2 engaged PSTs with Module 1 (learning modalities), including teaching a simulated class and reflect ing on simStudent feedback. Sessions 3– 4 deepened engagement through independent navigation of student personality profiles, differentiated task assignment, and classroom management practice. Sessions 5–6 consolidated learning through individualized instruction. Throughout, PSTs received automated formative feedback after each interaction, informing real-time reflection on instructional approach, personality awareness and interpersonal adaptation. The control group continued with the regular teaching practicum activities of the faculty. Both groups completed the research instruments at the end of the six-week period. An independent samples t-test indicated a statistically significant difference in personality alignment scores between the control group (M = 0.83, SD = 0.31) and the experimental group (M = 1.83, SD = 0.41), t (72) = 12.02, p <.001. This was confirmed by a one-way ANOVA: F (1, 73) = 144.63, p <.001, R² =.66, representing a substantial effect. PSTs who engaged with the AI-simulated environment demonstrated significantly higher personality alignment than those in the control group. The direction of these scores warrants clarification. Alignment is measured as the absolute difference between a participant’s mean OCEAN self-rating and the mean of the reference profile. A smaller difference indicates higher alignment. The control group returned higher OCEAN self-ratings, producing values closer in magnitude to the reference profile and consequently a lower mean alignment score (M = 0.83). The experimental group returned lower, more moderate self-ratings, producing a higher mean alignment score (M = 1.83) because their self-perception was more critically calibrated, reflecting a more critical engagement with their personality based on their encounters with diverse simulated student personalities. Improving alignment depends on more realistically formed self-ratings, not from ratings closer to the reference in absolute terms. The experimental group's reflections were more self-critical, indicating a process of re-evaluation. Inductive coding of qualitative data revealed two prominent themes: (1) recalibration of self-perception, evident in change utterances ('became more open minded,' 'realized that,' 'had to learn patience and adapt'); and (2) recognition of prior overestimation ('I used to have an exaggerated sense of'). These themes converged with quantitative findings, providing qualitative corroboration that the AI environment prompted personality awareness and self-adjustment. This pattern is consistent with the Theory of Action framework.  PSTs in the experimental group showed evidence of movement from espoused theory toward theory-in-use. The results suggest that the AI-simulated learning environment significantly influenced personality alignment among PSTs. The control group's higher OCEAN self-ratings appeared to reflect idealized self-conceptions, consistent with an espoused-theory-dominant orientation. This suggests that traditional teaching practice, despite offering comparable on-task demands, may not have challenged participants' self-perceptions in the same way as exposure to diverse simulated student personalities. The experimental group's lower, more calibrated self-ratings produced higher mean alignment scores, reflecting a shift from idealized self-conception toward situated self-knowledge. This is consistent with Buendía-Arias et al. (2020), who documented a similar process of personality reconfiguration during teaching practicum. This study does not claim that the simulated experience durably altered teachers' underlying personality traits. Consistent with Fleeson and Jayawickreme's (2015) whole trait theory which holds that pers onality expression flexibly varies around stable trait levels in response to situational demands, the AI environment functioned as a situational prompt making salient the gap between idealized self-perceptions and the behavioral demands posed by diverse si mulated student personalities. The Theory of Action framework (Argyris & Schön, 1974) explains the mechanism. The simulation created conditions that exposed the gap between PSTs' espoused theories (who they believed themselves to be) and their theory-in-use (who they demonstrated themselves to be when confronted with the situated demands of diverse learner personalities). The AI-simulated learning environment (SimNigeria) measurably impacted personality alignment among pre-service teachers by moving self-perception from idealized espoused-theory-dominant conceptions toward more critically calibrated, theory-in-use-grounded self-awareness. This reflects a shift in personality awareness and perception, not a claim of durable personality change. Several limitations deserve mention. The post-test only design, while supported by matched randomization and pilot homogeneity checks, limits definitive causal claims. The control group's condition of tra ditional practicum introduces a potential time-on-task disparity that should be considered when interpreting observed differences. Also self-reported outcomes are susceptible to social desirability bias and the reference personality profile may carry cultural assumptions that warrant calibration in future cross-cultural replications. These particularly for contexts facing logistical placement constraints. Future research should examine the long-term effects of AI-mediated personality alignment on classroom performance and investigate whether specific personality dimensions are more responsive to simulation-based intervention.
