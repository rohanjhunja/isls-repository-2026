# The Nature(s) of Embodied Mathematical Failure

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The purpose of this symposium is to consider the embodied nature of failure in different learning environments, contexts, mathematical situations, and age groups. Our exploratory work was guided by the following questions: (a) How is failure represented through our bodily actions? (b) How is failure co-constructed through our bodies? (c) How are our reactions to failure embodied? (d) How might embodied failure be framed or conceptualized across mathematical situations? We explore these questions through a close examination of the intersection of embodied cognition, failure, and mathematical activity as presented and challenged by a diverse spectrum of scholars. While grounded in mathematical activity, this symposium will contribute to the ongoing state of learning science research regarding how the mind, body, and environment work together to highlight student’s reasoning and thinking, particularly within moments of failure.

This collective work is situated w ithin mathematical activity as we are focused on the acti ons and behaviors of participation in different mat hematical practices (e.g., relating, problem solving) across a variety of environments, contexts, and situations  (Plaxco & Wawro, 2015). As described by Rasmussen et al. (2015), as students engage in mathematical activity, they enact their understandings while also building and/or exp anding their ways of knowing and reasoning. As such, we contend that the implications of this symposium lie s in the potential to develop a range of descriptio ns that highlight the multiplicity of established and poten tial relationships between embodiment and failure (i.e., embodied failure) and inform the theoretical and/or empirical groundings of ongoing and future scholarship. Shared theoretical groundings Each contributor thinks critically about embodiment  and failure within mathematical activities– but in  a wide variety of ways. We share theoretical groundings in the sense that each contributor considers these elements to be crucial – but how we perceive, operationalize, and analyze each are different. That difference is the subject of this symposium, so we only briefly describe foundational work for embodied cognition and failure below. Embodied Cognition Recently, learning scientists argued that human cog nition is embodied, human abilities to solve proble ms are deeply intertwined between body and brain (e.g., Ho llan et al., 2018). Our gestures and bodily action can be considered cognitive components because they shape our thinking (Krish, 2013). Gestures play an integral role in interpersonal communication where core problem doma in concepts may be difficult to explicate in langua ge ICLS2022 Proceedings 1788 © ISLS (Nathan et al., 2013; Reynolds & Reeve, 2001), and gestures can contain unique communicative content separate from spoken language (Williams-Pierce et al., 2017). In addition, full-body movements are vital resour ces for sense-making and understanding and using mathematical concepts (e.g., Hall & Nemirovsky, 2012; Vogelstein, 2020). Central to embodied perspective in learning science is understanding learning in terms of learn er’s “participation in practices of inquiry and discours e that include interactions with others and with th e material, symbolic and technological resources in their envir onment” (Kozma, 2013, p 206). In this way, an embod ied perspective on the nature of tools and representati ons includes attending to how tools and representat ions are perceived and used alongside language, bodily actions, and gesture, as well as their roles within rich  social and physical interactions (White & Pea, 2011).

Failure Prior research has highlighted the complex nature associated with failures as it can be viewed as “whatever [felt] like a failure to the person experiencing it” (Bishop, 2017, p. 962), or synonymously referred to as mistakes, errors, desirable difficulties, and unhappy eventualities to name a few (e.g., Simpson et al., 2020). Some consider failure as productive, an opportunity to develop conceptual  understanding and engage in the iteration design p rocess (Kapur, 2015; Searle et al., 2018). Conversely, others, for example educators, feel uncomfortable allowing youth to experience failure due to the negative associations and feelings regarding failures within learning environments (e.g., Lottero-Perdue & Parry, 2017). Further, perc eptions and feelings about failure experiences do n ot necessarily transcend learning environments as fail ure in an academic context may be viewed as a negat ive, debilitating experience while failure in a DIY-making context as a positive, enhancing experience (Simpson et al., 2018). In the field of mathematics, students too often withdraw from completing a task or a set of problems that is perceived to result in an incorrect response, or  in failure, which in turn leads to learned helples sness and a negative attitude toward mathematics (e.g., Chinn, 2012). As this scholarship implies, failure cannot be explicitly or clearly defined as what it means to experience a “failure” is dependent on many factors such as the individual perspective, the environment, the activity, and the interactions with self, others, tools, and materials. Learning: When our bodies contradict known facts Hortensia Soto It has been said that geometric reasoning is to ges ture (or other utterances) and diagrams as algebraic reasoning is to speech and symbolism (Soto-Johnson & Troup, 2 014). A goal for our students is for these two type s of reasoning to work in tandem. Unfortunately, this is not always the case, and in general, students tend to question their geometric reasoning even if their embodied ex periences support their geometric reasoning. There are also instances where students believe in the embodied ex perience, even though it conflicts with mathematica l facts that can be proved algebraically. This phenomenon h as been observed repeatedly when learners are asked  to traverse the sides and internal angles of a triangle. Instead of traversing the internal angles, learners traverse the exterior angles and thus, convey that “maybe the su m of the interior angles of a triangle is 360 degre es.” Thus, embodied failure can manifest as a disconnection be tween the two types of reasoning. This may emerge w hen learners fail to believe their embodied actions or fail to recognize the inaccuracy in their embodied actions. Generalizing beyond body-supported dimensions Amy Ellis Our talk will consider the effects on students' gen eralizing of transitioning from contexts in which s tudents can use embodiment to imagine, visualize, act, and determine failure to contexts in which they cannot. Specifically, we report on a teaching experiment with two secondary students who explored the volume of a growing cube and determined expressions for additional volume as the  cube grew. In the three-dimensional context, stude nts engaged in embodied actions with physical cubes to validate or invalidate their hypothesized expressions. When shifting to higher-dimensional hypercubes, students no longer had embodied contexts to support their reasoning, and consequently moved from a physical representati onal register (Duval, 2006) to a combinatorics representational register. We identified and explor ed these shifts in registers as a supporting mechan ism for students' generalizing activity.

Twisty puzzles and intentional temporary failure David Plaxco In my recent work, I am developing instructional materials that leverage the Rubik’s cube and other twisty puzzles to support mathematical content and practices during transition to upper-level mathematics courses. When solving a twisty puzzle, part of what makes the process so difficult is that the object itself requires the solver to temporarily un-solve the parts that they have already solved, r equiring a deal of faith in the algorithms the solv er uses to manipulate the pieces of the puzzle. I have come to  view this moment in which the solver has (necessar ily and intentionally) “messed up” their prior work as a te mporary fail state. Naturally, when first learning to solve the cube, this process is not always successful and the  solver is set backward in their journey (making th e fail state slightly more permanent). Overall, the solving proc ess can be viewed as a fluctuation between fail sta tes and equilibrium states at which the solver has resolved  more pieces of the cube. At the moment, this is on ly a theoretical framing; in my future work, I will apply this lens to actual scenarios that involve participants in order to refine the perspective.

Gesture-failure-feedback (GFF) loop in games Nihal Katirci & Caro Williams-Pierce Tablets and mobile phones games have touchscreen technology that promotes the interaction between touchscreen devices and humans through touchscreen gestures. These touchscreen gestures are, for example, tapping (touching the screen with a fingertip), dragging (moving fing ertip over the surface without losing contact), and  pinching (touching the surface with two fingers and bringing  them closer together) (Villamor et al., 2010). The se touchscreen gestures, notwithstanding success or fa ilure, is paired with feedback (Williams-Pierce, 20 19). The feedback is dependent on how game designers created the system feedback of the game. In many mathematical education games, there is just one correct answer, and the game gives only summative feedback. When the player submits the answer, the game system compares the player’s answer and the correct answer. If the player’s answer is not the same as the correct answer, the game pen alizes the players. However, in well-designed educa tional ICLS2022 Proceedings 1790 © ISLS games, games provide feedback in three different ways: formative (Heron, 2010), summative (Deci et al., 2001), and informative (Kim et al., 2018) feedback. Formative feedback is given during the solving of the puzzle and it helps players avoid or learn from failure. Summativ e feedback is given after the player solves the puz zle. Informative feedback is given when players need extra information or clues to solving the puzzle, such as clicking the Hint icons on the screen. Well-designed games a lso give space to the players to do comparison them selves. Hence, when players are playing, they are also learning. Gestures in productive failure Manu Kapur In productive failure, students first engage in pro blem-solving activities that are deliberately designed in a way that students are unable to generate the optimal or correct solutions. Yet, it is precisely this process of generation sub-optimal or incorrect solutions that is the locus of deep learning as it prepares them to learn from subsequent instruction. In the absence of canonical tools and language, students invariably resort to a diverse s et of motor actions and gestures to communicate their ideas, re presentations and solutions. These spontaneously oc curring gestures function as grounding actions, anchor and communicate intuitive understandings, aid the development of a shared language during collaboration, and form  a bridge for understanding the canonical solutions subsequently.

Mathematizing embodied play and failure Justin Baker & David DeLiema In a classic text on mathematical cognition, Lave et al. (1988) argue that “snags” during grocery shopping cause shoppers to question and revise their arithmetic pr oblem-solving. More recent studies on informal pare nt-child engineering tasks have revealed the role of interac tions with physical objects in stimulating external ized mathematical thinking (Simpson et al., 2021), and the impact of caregiver scaffolding and questioning in fostering understanding of mathematical concepts involved in an engineering design task (Simpson & Zhong, 2021).  We extend these arguments about the situated nature of mathematical thinking to naturalistic, outdoor play between parents and their young children (age 2-8). We exam ine how embodied failures during informal play acti vities serve as generative moments for the intergeneration al discussion of mathematical features of problems.  Using interaction analyses of video data (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) of naturalistic, outdoor play captured by families themselves, we highlight three play episodes in whi ch children's self-directed pursuits (reaching for a rope, running down a ramp, passing objects through a pool noodle) and subsequent impasses (failing to reach the rope, falling down the ramp, getting a stick stuck in the  pool noodle) create an occasion for talk about mat hematical features (distance, speed, and size respectively). The talk about mathematical features serves the specific purpose of clarifying the causes of the impasse and/or solu tions to the impasse. Children's intrinsic desire t o pursue challenging play tasks, and the inevitable embodied  failures that occur, combine to create a scenario ripe with potential for this kind of mathematical learning.

## Conclusion

Collectively, the presentations will contribute to exploring the intersection of embodied cognition, f ailure, and mathematical activity, as well as consider the embo died nature of failure in different learning environments, contexts, mathematical situations, and age groups. As such, we contend this symposium addresses the conference topic, elucidating theories and processes of learni ng and teaching, as we consider the inclusion of fa ilure to contribute to the ongoing theory development of emb odied mathematical activity and learning (Hall & Nemirovsky, 2012). As described by Tall (2004), “There is a period of exploration and incubation that precedes the eventual formulation. In this case of the theory…, it is moving towards a stable form, it is still in the process of being filled out and refined” (p. 29). By bringi ng together a wide variety of scholars who explore embodied mathematical failure in various ways, this symposiu m will move us towards a more stable and refined understanding of our guiding questions: (a) How is failure represented through our bodily actions? (b)  How is failure co-constructed through our bodies? (c) How are our reactions to failure embodied? And (d) How might embodied failure be framed or conceptualized across mathematical situations?
