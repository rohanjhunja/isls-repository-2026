# Fictional Futures, Expanded Possibilities: Imaginative Writing to Orient Teachers’ Agency Towards the Projective

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As interest in teacher learning towards transformative education has increased, so too has the need for learning environments that conceptualize learning as imagining possibilities for future social action. In this study, I explore how fiction writing can orient teacher learners towards the projective—or future-focused—dimension of their agency. One teacher’s discourse illustrates how narrative mediates learning through the release from systemic constraints and expands pathways for future action.

## Introduction and theory: “More creativity than a lot of teacher education”

Teacher learning for transforming the education system is a pressing issue for many fields, including STEM education and the learning sciences. Typical learning environments for teachers center reflections on past experience and practice in the present with the hope of expanding available actions within schools. These foci can be theorized  through sociologists Emirbayer and Mische’s (1998) framework for agency, where agency is operationalized through  Iteration (constructions of the past) and Practical-Evaluation (acting in the present). Partially in response to Bourdieu’s (1977) theories of Habitus, however, Emirbayer  and Mische posit a third dimension of agency: Projection. The projective element encompasses an actor’s imagi ned future possibilities crafted through creative experimentation. Explicit attention to the projective dimension is missing in most learning environments for teachers y et is conducive to actors choosing paths that are not solely defined by their past experiences and present conditions (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998), i.e., to enact transformative education. To forefront the projective, a colleague and I redesigned a STEM pedagogies course where teachers imagined social and educational change through writing fictional first-person narratives from the perspective of a K-12 student. With the view of learning as the reorganization of future trajectories (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016), we designed this writing engagement as a site of learning for the generation of paths that lead toward an educator’s imagined future. The written narrative could also be seen as a prefiguration: an experimental enactment of an activist’s political (and educational) ideal (Curnow & Uttamchandani, 2022). In this study, I explore how writing fiction can orient learners toward the projective dimension of agency and expand pathways for transformative action.

## Methods: “Today, we’re going to school together”

I seek to illustrate  the impact of futurity, agency, and narrative throu gh the words and experiences of one participant in our course. The course was the initial cycle of a social design-based research project (e.g. Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016) exploring activism, agency, and imagination in form al STEM teacher education  at an urban university. The discussion-based course was intentionally ungraded, anti-homework, and prioritized the voices of the candidates over the instructors —using prefiguration as a design tool.  The focal teacher, who chose the pseudonym Emily, is enrolled in a master’s and initial licensure program. At the time of the study, Emily had just completed their first year teaching both math and E nglish language arts in a local public middle schoo l. Emily, who identifies as Asian American and uses they/them pronouns, was selected for this case study because they articulated alignment with the instructors’ justice  orientation —a requirement of prefigurative learning spaces (Curnow & Uttamchandani, 2022). The data explored here  include Emily’s final narrative and an hour-long interview between Emily and me. The interview —which invited Emily to share their process in writi ng their futuristic fictional narrative and make connections to their past and perceived educator agency—was recorded and transcribed for analysis. To analyze these data, I used open coding to thematically analyze both Emily’s narrative and interview. I then  coded the data for a second time using a priori codes aligning with the iterative/pr acticalevaluative/projective dimensions of agency.

## Findings: “The smell of fresh bread wakes me up”

Emily’s imagined future begins as a fictional first-person narrator, Zera, wakes to her mom cooking breakfast before school. Zera and her mom walk to school, where Zera’s mom, an engineer and sustainable farmer, teaches Zera’s class about environmentalism and Indigenous land practices and takes the class to the community garden and farm. Throughout their narrative, Emily used comparisons with the past as a rhetorical device to illustrate the realities of their fictional world. These “pasts” were Emily’s present; through projective imagination, Emily reinvented aspects of their present they found constraining. In Table 1, I present excerpts from Emily’s writing that illustrate how they contrasted Zera’s reality with the present day. In each excerpt, the yellow-highlighted text describes an aspect of Emily’s present they reimagined in their story, and the green-highlighted text illustrates their projective vision. By engaging Emily in the process of writing a futuristic fiction, the projective dimension of their agency was bolstered; Emily imagined a world where systems and learning align with their values. ## Discussion: “The story I wrote was definitely something tangible”

Emily restories wide-spanning concerns, from classroom roles to societal values and structures, through their narrative. The use of narrative releases Emily from the system ic and political constraints they experience as a teacher and facilitate s the reorganization of future trajectories to expand possibilities for action (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998). This learning process and politicized dreaming not only supports, as Emily says, “being able to envision,” but also their projective agency in identifying the “steps to get there.” Emily’s narrative functions as a flexible, experimental prefiguration that supports their orientations towards enacting t ransformative education in their school and community contexts.

Through Emily’s discourse, I highlight how learning might contribute to the enactment of a gency. The open question of whether Emily’s contexts will afford them the necessary resources situates this work within broader utopian, revolutionary, and transformative projects. It is also unknown what skills Emily will need to make progress towards their imagined future. However, this course experience was successful in engaging Emily with projective thinking, which is often  difficult in light of the realities of teachers’ daily work life. T he affordances and constraints of imagining as learnin g, the theorizing of narrative as prefiguration,  and viewing Emily’s discourse through this tripartite framework  for agency offer rich opportunities for discussion  across the fields of teacher education, social movement studies, design research, and the learning sciences.
