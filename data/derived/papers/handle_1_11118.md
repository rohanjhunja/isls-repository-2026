# Multilevel Engagement in Learning Architectures for Integrated STEM Education Innovation

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Integrated STEM education innovation cherishes the goal of preparing students for a rapidly changing world by developing their ability to generate knowledge and solve complex real-life problems. To achieve this, it is necessary to support teachers in curriculum innovation that aligns with the goals of the innovation. However, school-level constraints often hinder teachers’ innovation and learning. Fostering a conducive environment for innovation might necessitate school leaders’ learning. To investigate how teacher learning for STEM education innovation relates to school leaders’ learning, we used the MultiLevel MultiScale Model as a framework to examine the interdependency of learning at various levels for scalable innovation. This study was conducted within a design-based implementation research context and involved a multiple case study with four schools. The results showed that the engagement of school leaders can facilitate the establishment of school-level infrastructures that support teacher learning for curriculum innovation, leading to positive innovation outcomes.

## Introduction

Unlike traditional classrooms where students passiv ely receive knowledge, integrated STEM actively inv olves students in the knowledge construction process. Thi s shift in pedagogical paradigm, which is at the co re of integrated STEM education innovation, requires teachers to design new learning experiences for students. Several frameworks have been developed to guide and support  teachers in creating learning opportunities that e ngage students in STEM practices, such as scientific inve stigation and engineering design processes (e.g., D uschl & Bybee, 2014; Estapa & Tank, 2017). However, even when teachers enhance their self-efficacy in designing STEM learning experiences, implementation can be hindere d by contextual constraints such as limited flexibi lity in school schedules (Ríordáin et al., 2016) and resources (Fore et al., 2015). A pressing inquiry is how to support teachers in steering educational innovation in their school contexts. This involves not only providing favorable working conditions for innovation but also creating an envi ronment that encourages teachers to learn through collaboration during the innovation process. Situated learning can occur in the workplace (Lave & Wenger, 1991) in communiti es of practice (Wenger, 1998). To effectively integrate innovative elements into their school contexts, teachers must develop knowledge-in-practice (Kelly, 2006). Leadership support for teacher learning in integrated STEM innovations (Lo, 2021) must extend beyond merely facilitating teachers ’ participation in professional development programs (Boice et al., 2021). School leaders are believed to play a crucial role in fostering change by (re)designing architectures for learning (Scanlan, 2013). In short, architectures for learning (AfL) a ddress how conditions in communities of practice offer opportunities for the evolution of innovative practices (Stein & Coburn, 2008). The process of change is inherently a learning process. To effectively implement change s in their schools ’ AfL, school leaders must also learn. It is hypothesized that engaging school leaders in learni ng is essential for teacher learning in curriculum and pedagogical innovation within integrated STEM.

Research-Practice Partnerships (Coburn & Penuel, 2016) in de sign-based implementation research (DBIR) (Fishman et al., 2013) channel intervention and involvement at different levels of an education al ecosystem to develop collective capacity for educat ional innovation. School leaders and teachers  in undertaking different roles in the implementation of  an innovation can learn through collaboration within DBIR contexts, thereby enhancing the school’s collective capacity for educational innovation. Thi s study, conducted within the context of a DBIR network, aims to explore whether and how school leaders’ learning while engaging in a DBIR network affects teacher learning and innovation outcomes. This study focuses on two research questions: RQ1: What innovation-related AfL in the DBIR network were engaged by school leaders and co-design team teachers for integrated STEM innovation?

RQ2: Whether and how did AfL engaged by school leaders relate to co-design team teachers’ learning?

## Conceptual framework

Situated learning and infrastructuring Studies on teacher learning for STEM integration sh ow that there can be gaps between what teachers lea rn in professional development programmes and what they e nact in the classroom (Estapa & Tank, 2017; Han et al., 2015). This attests to the limitation of using cognitivism to understand teacher learning (Kelly, 2006). To explain how contextual constraints could limit the implementation outcomes, we adopt the lens of situated learning (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Learning through social interactions in communities of practice  occurs through the processes of participation and reification  (Wenger, 1998). Through participation, people learn  how to interact with others through interaction mechanisms such as collaborativ e design processes in their work environments and g enerate patterns of actions or routines to satisfy their sh ared objectives. Through reification, people share their learning history in artifacts as shared repertoires, such as policies and curriculum materials. The work environment is also a learning environment for shaping practice.

In the context of curriculum and pedagogical innovation, re-designing work environments to support the learning of new teaching and learning practices is often essential. This re-design of educational infrastructures to support participation and reification provides conditions that foster situated learning. Educational infrastructures, such as policy, standards, meetings, and curriculum materials, are hidden substrates for shaping practices through social interactions (Penuel, 2019). Redesigning educational infrastructures is needed to shape new practices in educational innovation. Th is redesign process is referred to  as infrastructuring (Penuel, 2019). Education infrastructures for school leaders and teachers to learn new practices through participation and reifi cation are innovation-related architectures for learning (AfL) (Law et al., 2015; Stein & Coburn, 2008; Wenger, 1998). School leaders and teachers might play different ro les in an innovation and engage in different parts of the associated AfL.

MultiLevel MultiScale Model (MLMS) of Connected Learning To sustain innovative practices among school commun ities, agents at different level s of an education ecosystem need to engage in continuous learning through the change process (Daly et al, 2020; Law & Ko, 2023). Furthermore, changes at different levels are interdependent. Consistent with an ecological perspective of change, learning at one level (e.g. teachers) is affected by what happens at another level (e.g. students) (Law et al., 2016). What are the possible mechanisms for this mutual influence between teacher learning and leadership learning? The examination of the influence of school leaders’  learning on teachers’ learning can be guided by th e MultiLevel and MultiScale Model (MLMS) (Law et al., 2015) of connected learning. The “Multilevel” construct in the model highlights the need to attend to learning at multiple levels, such as district, proj ect network, school, teacher and student  to achieve  scalable innovative practices in the education ecos ystem. The “MultiScale” construct highlights that  the interacting entities involved in the learning interactions can be of different scales, such as individuals within the same unit, or organizational units at the same level (e.g. among teachers in a codesign team or among schools within the same innovation network). It is grounded in the theory of knowledge building (Scardamalia, 2002), which values idea diversity, laying the foundation for knowledge construction that rises above individual contributions. Idea diversity is usually ric her in group s with participants coming from different organizations. If a school is defined as a unit, cross-unit interactions among teachers and/or school leaders from different schools could lead to more diverse ideas in the innovation process. Cross-unit learning interactions offer a more democratic environment for the exploration of ideas than within-unit interactions as the former type of interactions do es not have decision-making power  over individual schools. Cross-boundary interactions across organizational units provide an  impetus for innovation s to align with reform goals (Stein & Coburn, 2008). A school should have autonomy in mak ing decisions for change and creating new strategie s via within-unit interactions.

The MLMS model examines learning conditions for different scales of interaction at different levels with four major components of AfL: (1) organizational structures  (OS) such as teacher collaborative teams, (2) interaction mechanisms  (IM) such as new routines, (3) reification artifacts  (A F) such as protocols, policy documents, curriculum materials and standards for g uiding actions and decisions, and (4) technology (Tech) for supporting interactions or as reification artifacts. These four components are categories of education al infrastructures that can be designed or adjusted for shaping innovative practices.

## Multiple case study design

This study adopted a multiple case study design. Wi th multiple cases, we can compare processes and outcomes by identifying how and why certain processes and outcomes occur in specific contexts (Miles et al., 2020). This study was conducted within a DBIR Network, supported by a government funded university-school partnership program that aimed at supporting capacity building for advancing integrated STEM education. T he project network comprised 32 schools. Four of the network schools (A, C, X and Z) were purposefully selected as four cases for this study. Two of them (A and C)  were secondary schools and another two (X and Z) w ere primary schools. The first author was the consultan t assigned to these four schools and spen t the most time with them (Stake, 2005). The first author, assigned as a consultant to these four schools, spent the most time with them (Stake, 2005). Close collaboration with teacher participants in these schools allowed for the collection of rich data for the case study. In each case, principals, senio r teacher leaders, and co-design team teachers formed different parts of the analysis.

Professional activities for school leaders and teachers There were two levels of professional activities in  the DBIR for school leaders and co-design team teachers. At the network level, professional activities were pro vided for school leaders and co-design team teachers. Leadership circle meetings aimed at facilitating pr incipals and senior teacher leaders to exchange and  reflect on their school-level strategies for the integrated STEM innovation. Monthly professional development  (PD) workshops were held to foster  understanding of the self-directed learning (SDL) and STEM integration  design principles, as well as to share how different schools apply the MLMS  model in their innovation implementation strategies. Open classroom observations were also organized to support peer learning within the Network. At the school-level, e ach participating school had committed to two implementation cycles of co-design, lesson observation and debriefing (COLO) of STEM curriculum units. A key guiding principle for the university partner was to encourage and support school and teacher autonomy. The teachers within each school decided on the topic for their innovative  curriculum units, grade level and the number of les son periods  involved for e ach of the innovation implementation cycles and drafted the initial learning design for the unit. Then, they discussed their designs with a university consultant. Teachers received feedback linked to the design principles related  to students’ self-direction and STEM integration from the consultant. The teachers then decided on how they wanted to revise the design for the actual implementation. The university team observed the classroom implementations and facilitated teachers ’ reflections on their design  and implementation during the post-observation debriefing. The schools decided on the composition of their own innovation team and school-level strategies.

## Data analysis

The AfL engagement of the principal, senior teacher leaders (vice-principal or curriculum head) and the teacher co-design team members were analyzed both quantitatively and qualitatively. First, their participation frequency in PD and open classroom  observations at the network level  were examined based on documented participation data. The participation frequency indicated the intensity of their engagement in the network-level AfL. The school-level AfL engaged by these change agents at different hierarchical levels was analyzed using data from the transcriptions of year-end principal-and teacher-interviews in each school. The teacher interviews were conducted in the form of a focus group involving the senior teacher leader and co-design team teachers. This analysis involved several steps. First, the year-end interview data were preliminarily coded according to the four components of the AfL (organizational structures, interaction mechani sms, reification artifacts and technology) to create categories under each AfL component. Then, the interview data were systematically coded with  the categories again by two coders. After the coding, the two coders discussed the data until all disagreements were resolved. Then, the categories of AfL in each school were triangulated, for example, with sociograms drawn by teachers during one of the network PD workshops held at the end of the school year. The teachers were asked to draw sociograms to represent the organizational structure and interaction mechanisms that their school constructed for its implementation. The set of documented AfL in each school was mapped to  the MLMS model for further analysis for the construction of the contrast tables (Miles et al., 2020) to compare the opportunities to learn about different aspects of STEM implementation for change agents at different hierarchical levels in the four schools.

Using data from teachers’ learning designs, lesson observations and learning artifacts created by students, we identified and compared the changes in learning opportunities related to STEM practices across schools. These changes were regarded as co-design team teacher learning outcomes. Then, we examined the relationship between patterns of engagement in AfL at different levels and the learning outcomes of co-design team teachers.

The curriculum head facilitated the process by rescheduling class timetables. In addition to the school’s open classroom arrangements, non-team teachers were encouraged to observe other STEM lessons taught by the team.

The principal and the curriculum head were also involved in infrastructuring to create learning conditions for teachers. Consistent with the school ’s goal of promoting SDL, the curriculum head encour aged teachers to evaluate lessons using a criterion-referenced SDL rubric developed by the university t eam in the network. This allowed teachers to reflect on the level of SDL in their lesson designs. Various opportunities were pr ovided for team teachers to showcase and share their implementation experiences, which allowed teachers to reflect on their implementation and rece ive affirmation. Although the teachers’ designs in the first implementation provided few learning opportunities for STEM practices, they bec ame more capable of providing STEM learning opportunities for students to take on STEM practices in the second implementation. ## Conclusion and implications

This study used the MLMS model to analyze what AfL was engaged by school leaders and teachers in four different schools. The results illustrated how vari ous AfL components—organizational structure, interaction mechanisms, artifacts and technology at the network  and school levels—support teachers in deploying self-directed learning pedagogies in integrated STEM innovation. While it is generally believed that school leadership is crucial for teachers’ professional development (Lo, 2021), this study provides evidence of how school leaders’ engagement could influence the emergence of new AfL to facilitate teachers’ situated learning. School  level interaction mechanisms for  leaders to understand teachers’ needs can facilitate  teachers’ contributions to infrastructuring, to advance innovation. While school leaders play a role in designing school-level AfL (Scanlan, 2013), this study also suggests that school leaders need AfL to enable them to respond to emergent teachers’ needs in the innovation process and redesign the organizational environment to build collective capacity. This study examined only one year of the innovation implementation. A longitudinal study can further explore the long-term impact of multilevel engagement in AfL. Further research can also be conducted to explore whether and how school leaders can proactively use the MLMS model to support them in designing organizational environments for teacher learning and scaling up curriculum and pedagogical innovation in other contexts.
