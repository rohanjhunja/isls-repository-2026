# Educational Attainment, Diversity, and Trust Dynamics in Nigeria

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Trust is a fundamental pillar of societal cohesion, facilitating collaboration and progress. However, trust dynamics in diverse societies remain underexplored, particularly in non-Western contexts. Using the Afrobarometer 2022 dataset, this study investigates how education influences social and institutional (government) trust in Nigeria, one of the world’s most structurally pluralistic nations. Findings reveal that education was initially negatively associated with trust, but its effect diminished when religion and ethnicity were accounted for in a stepwise multiple regression analysis. Lower-educated groups, such as Hausa, Muslims, and non-English speakers, reported higher trust on average, while their higher-educated counterparts exhibited lower trust, reflecting an intricate balancing act that neutralized education’s net effect. Drawing on theories of structural pluralism, social capital, and human capital, this study highlights the importance of inclusive education policies to bridge divides and build trust in pluralistic societies like Nigeria.

## Introduction

Trust is a critical component of human society. It is essential and needed to a certain degree for effective functioning of individuals, family, communities, and nations. It allows cooperation in large groups and leads to social, cultural, and economic pro gress (Fukuyama, 1995). At its core, trust underpin s social capital, which encompasses the networks, norms, and relationships that enable collective action (Putnam, 2000). In to day's increasingly diverse societies worldwide, structural pluralism, characterized by ethnic, linguistic, religious, and cultural diversity, shapes interpersonal and instit utional trust dynamics (Donohue et al., 1973; Unite d Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, 2020). A t the same time, human capital —often operationalized through its key component, education —also plays a role in shaping trust by influencing a wareness, critical thinking, and engagement with societal structures (Becker, 1964). In this study, we are interested in understanding the relationship between social capital (represented via trus t), structural pluralism (represented via demograph ic diversity), and human capital (represented via education). Specifically, we analyze the relationsh ip between education level (predictor variable) and two forms of trust: social trust (trust in others) and government trust (trust in government) in Nigeria, while accounting for the complex, multicultural nature of its society. Nigeria’s unique context makes it an ideal site for  studying trust dynamics. As Africa’s most populous country, with over 250 ethnic groups and a populati on surpassing 230 million, Nigeria represents one o f the world’s most structurally pluralistic societi es (Bayer, 2012). Notably, much of the existing res earch discussing education, social capital, and structural pluralism  has focused on Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) nations (Putnam, 2000; Uslaner, 2002). Henrich et al. (20 10) coined the term WEIRD, highlighting a significant problem in social sciences: much of the research is disproportionately based on WEIRD populations, which are not representative of  the globe. This is significant because, apart from high structural pluralism, Nigeria also has high levels of poverty, perceived corruption, and lower education attainment (Bratton & Gyimah-Boadi, 2016; World Bank, 2020). This structural div ersity, coupled with oth er socio-economic challenges, underscores the importance of ex ploring trust in this unique context. By focusing o n Nigeria, this study fills a crucial gap, shedding light on how tr ust associates with education attainment in cultura lly and demographically diverse settings, where ethnic and religious affiliations often play a crucial role (Bayer, 2012). Past studies that have examined the relationship bet ween education and trust have generally found that higher schooling is associated with higher social t rust (Bjørnskov, 2009; Papagapitos & Riley, 2009; P utnam, 2000), likely due to its role in fostering civic engagement and interpersonal interaction. However, its relationship with trust in government is less consistent; education can enhance scrutiny of institutions, making trust contingent on perceived government performance (Tyler, 2006). Bayer’s (2012) analysis using the World Values Surv ey (WVS) dataset in Nigeria highlights the influence of ethnicity and religion on trust, i dentifying significant trust gaps between groups. However, Bayer’s study relied on a dataset collected in 2012, lacking the temporal relevance to capture the evolving dynamics of trust in Nigeri a’s rapidly changing landscape. New analysis is essential to advance our understanding.

Amid rising tensions  in Nigeria, this study is particularly timely. Incr easing incidents of corruption, declining political trust, and the pervasive influe nce of social media on public perceptions have exac erbated societal divides (Eke, 2020; Jimoh et al., 2024). These challenges necessitate a deeper understanding of how trust is situated in Nigeria.  In exploring these questions, this study contribute s to the global discourse on trust by offering insights into understanding how education is associated with trus t in a non-WEIRD country. By examining how education interacts with trust in a p luralistic nation like Nigeria, we provide a study design framework for understanding similar dynamics in oth er pluralistic societies by answering the following  two research questions:

1. How does the level of education relate to social trust in Nigeria when controlling for religion, ethnicity,

and other demographic factors?

2. How does the level of education relate to trust in government, while controlling for demographic factors?

## Theoretical framework

Structural pluralism The concept of structural pluralism stems from earl y work in sociology, communication studies, and pol itical science to understand how diversity impacts social cohesion, conflict, and policy-making. Structural pluralism refers to the degree to which a community's social, economic, and political structures are diverse and differentiated (Donohue et al., 1973). Highly structurally plurali stic communities are characterized by numerous dist inct and relatively autonomous subgroups or institutions. Th ese communities often exhibit more diverse viewpoin ts, a greater variety of media outlets, and complex power dynamics. Conversely, less pluralistic communities are more homogenous and centralized, with fewer competing or distinct groups. This concept is particularly relevant in Nigeria, where structural pluralism is deeply embed ded. With over 250 ethnic groups (with Hausa, Yoruba, and Igb o as major groups) and multiple religious groups (w ith Muslims and Christians as major groups), Nigeria ex emplifies the challenges and opportun ities of pluralism (Agbiboa, 2017; Ibrahim Index of African Governance, 2022). Scholars have observed that higher pluralism can lead to lower levels of trust within a population, where intergroup relations often mediate individual  perceptions of broader societal and institutional trust (Cook &  Cooper, 2003; Kandori, 19 92; Leigh, 2006; Zak & Knack, 2001). Thus, trust can be contingent on the social composition of a nation with varied ethnic and religious diversity and depend on whether individuals perceive their id entity groups as adequately represented  and empowered or not.

Social capital theory Social capital theory encompasses the shared ideas,  norms, values, and practices that facilitate colla boration between and among individuals and groups, with trust at its core. Coleman (1988) formalized the concept, linking it to structural pluralism and educational outcomes. He argued that social capital inheres not in individuals but in the relationships among them, enabling access to re sources and fostering cooper ation. For the purpose of this study, we focus on social trust, a key element of social capital. Putnam (2000) explained two types of social trust. Bonding social trust occurs within groups  (like with family members), while bridging social trust occurs across groups (like with strangers). Both types are critical for maintaining societal trust and cohesion. We conceptualize trust as a whole ranging from within family to other Nigerians (see Methodology). Social capital theory also intersects with social contract theory, a philosophical foundation laid by Locke

(1988) and Rousseau (1762). Locke posited that governments exist through a mutual contract to protect individual

rights in exchange of civic duties, a premise that underpins trust in governance. Rousseau expanded on this, arguing that trust emerges when governments reflect the "general will" of the people. These ideas are particularly relevant in studying public perceptions of governme nt performance. In pluralistic societies like Niger ia, trust in government often hinges on perceptions of fairness,  representation, and the ability to balance diverse  group interests. Thus, we conceptualize two types of trus t in this study: social trust (based on social capi tal theory) and trust in government (based on social contract theory).

Human capital theory Human capital theory, pioneered by Becker (1964) and Schultz (1961), views education as an investment that enhances individual productivity and societal prosperity. Education equips individuals with skills and knowledge, fostering participation in civic li fe and critical engagement with institutions. Colem an (1988) extended this by linking human capital to social capital, arguing th at the two reinforce each other. For example, educated individuals often build networks that amplify trust and collective action. In Nigeria, however, this interaction between human  and social capital is complicated by structural pluralism. While education is traditionally linked to higher trust levels, it can also lead to greater  scrutiny of governance, awareness of corruption, and reduced tr ust in some contexts. This study builds on these th eories to examine how education shapes trust dynamics, while accounting for diversity factors, to address two key research questions: the role of education in fostering social trust and its relationship with trust in government.

## Methodology

Data source and design This study utilizes data from the Afrobarometer Round 9 survey (2), conducted in 2022, which provides nationally representative insights into public attitudes on so cial, political, and economic issues across Africa.  For Nigeria, the dataset includes 1,600 respondents sampled through a stratified, multi-stage probability design to ensure geographic and demographic inclusivity. Enumeration  areas were selected proportionate to population si ze, with survey weights applied to adjust for sampling desig n. As a result, we assume the findings presented in this study are generalizable to the national population of Nigeria. Parameters The study examines two key outcome variables: Socia l Trust and Trust in Government. Social Trust was constructed as a composite of trust in all the item s in Q46 (other Nigerians, relatives, neighbors, pe ople known and unknown, and other religious and ethni c groups), with items measured on a 4-point scale from "Not At All"

(0) to "A Lot" (3). Similarly, Trust in Government was measured as a composite var iable of all the items in Q86

capturing perceptions of how well the government handles issues such as the economy, unemployment, education, infrastructure, and rights, averaged across items o n a 4-point Likert scale from "Very Bad" (1) to "Very Good" (4). The primary predictor, Education Level, was measured on a 10-point ordinal scale, ranging from "No Formal Schooling" (0) to " Post-Graduate" (9), and treated as continuous variable fo r interpretation and comparability. Control variables included Age (a continuous variab le), Gender (Male or Female), Ethnicity (Hausa, Igb o, Yoruba, Nigerian Only, Others), Religion (Christian s or Muslims), Primary Home Language (English or Nonenglish), and Living Conditions, which were self-reported on a 5-point scale ranging from "Very Bad" to "Very Good." Continuous predictors, including education, age, and living conditions, were standardized for comparability across models.

## Results

Our analyses explored the role of education in shap ing social trust and trust in government, considering the influence of demographic factors. Using nationally representative survey weights, we systematically ex amined how these relationships evolved across nested regression models.

## Research question 1: Educational attainment and social trust

We examined the association between education level and social trust, progressively adding demographic factors across six models. In Model 1, we found a significant negative relationship between education and social trust (β = −0.19, p < 0.001), suggesting that individuals with higher education level s reported lower social trust. Adding living conditions in Model 2 slightly reduced this effect (β = −0.18, p < 0.001). Including age and gender in Model 3 maintained the negative association (β = −0.20, p < 0.001), with female participants reporting significantly lower social trust compared to  their male counterparts (β = −0.16, p < 0.001). The addition of primary home language in Model 4 attenuated the effect of education (β  = −0.18, p < 0.001), and non-English speakers reported higher social trust (β = 0.31, p < 0.001).

In Model 5, religion further reduced the significance of education (β = −0.06, p = 0.05), and Muslims exhibited higher social trust compared to Christians (β  = 0.55, p < 0.001). In Model 6, the inclusion of ethnicity rendered education non-significant (β  = −0.01, p = 0.62). Ethnicity emerged as an important  predictor, with Yoruba (β = −0.43, p < 0.001), Igbo (β = −0.47, p < 0.001), and Others (β = − 0.21, p < 0.01) groups reporting lower social trust compared to the reference group that identifies as "Nigerians Only", whereas Hausa was slightly higher (β = 0.07, p = 0.34). These results indicate that the initially strong negative association between education and social trust is largely mitigated by demographic factors, particularly religion and ethnicity. The more trusting groups —Muslims and Hausa —are overrepresented among lower education levels, while the less trusting groups —Christians, Yoruba, and Igbo — are overrepresented among  higher education levels. These opposing patterns effectively cancel out th e effect of education, rendering its net association with trust  weak or non-significant. These findings highlight how demographic factors, particu larly religion, ethnicity, and language, influence the relationship between education and tr ust. This underscores the importance of considering Nige ria's unique sociocultural context when interpreting the complex interplay between education and trust, as these factors collectively shape societal cohesion and trust in governance. ## Discussion and future direction

These findings underscore a key insight: education alone does not determine levels of trust —cultural and demographic identities such as religion and ethnici ty shape how trust is formed and expressed. While e ducation initially showed a strong negative assoc iation with both social trust and trust in governme nt, this relationship disappeared once cultural factors were accounted fo r, suggesting that trust dynamics in Nigeria are de eply embedded in its pluralistic social fabric. At the same time, Nigeria has made notable progress in expanding access to education, with over 70% of children aged 6–11 now attending primary school (UNICEF, 2024). Yet, regional, religious, and gender disparities persist. These patterns raise a critical question: Can expanding education help bridge social divides in diverse societies, or—without deeper structural reforms—might it reinforce them? This study contributes new insight into the role of  identity in shaping trust in pluralistic societies. Our findings reveal that Hausa, Muslim, and non-English-speaking groups report higher levels of both social  and governmental trust. This challenges earlier findings such as Bayer (2020), which reported lower generalized trust among Hausa and Muslims. Such discrepancies may ste m from differences in context, methodology, or timi ng, emphasizing the need to revisit assumptions about m arginalized groups in trust research. Unlike much o f the existing literature, which focuses on aggregate lev els of trust or formal institutional analysis, this  study underscores how group-based identities and cultural positioning intersect with education to shape trust in nuanced ways. In a country navigating ongoing challenges related to governance and institutional trust (Aliu, 2019; Jimoh et al., 2024), our findings may point to the possibility that in-group cohesion and cultural belonging play a role in shaping trust, particularly among certain demographic groups. These dynamics are rarely captured in large-scale quantitative studies, pointing to the need for futu re mixed-method or qualitative research to further explore h ow trust is built, expressed, and maintained across Nigeria’s diverse social fabric. The interplay between education and trust in government also reflects the transformative and sometimes polarizing effects of education. While higher education fosters critical thinking and political awareness, it can also magnify scrutiny of governance inefficiencies, pote ntially lowering trust in government (Putnam, 2000;  Tyler, 2006). This tension aligns with findings in Sub-Saharan Africa, where digital media has played a gr owing role. Bello (2024) found that digital platforms increase political awa reness and access to information, yet Kipkoech

(2023) found that social media in Kenya had an eroding effect on political trust, particularly when misinformation

or biased narratives proliferate. In Nigeria, the r ise of digital connectivity may be similarly reshap ing trust perceptions, particularly among the educa ted populace. Future research aimed at  understanding how education intersects with these media (both traditional media  and social media) is vital for equipping citizens in assessing governance and institutions.

Ultimately, we want to strive for higher levels of education amo ng different groups in Nigeria while maintaining and fostering trust among people for be tter collaboration and harmony. By addressing syste mic inequalities in educational access and quality, cor ruption fre e institutions, and balanced representations in positions of power, Nigeria can foster intergroup u nderstanding and cultivate both bonding and bridgin g trust. Ensuring that education serves as a unifying force rather than a divider requires deliberate policies. This focus on building trust from the ground up offers hope for a more cohesive and resilient Nigeria.

## Concluding remarks

This study examined the interplay between education, structural pluralism, and trust in Nigeria, shedding light on two distinct dimensions of trust: social trust and trust in government. The findings reveal that education does not appear to be significantly associated with trust when controlling for ethnic a nd religious demographic  factors. Groups with lower educational attainment, such as H ausa, Muslims, and non-English speakers, contribute to higher levels of trust, while higher-educated counterparts tend to report lower trust towards both society and government, with a nonsignificant net effect. These contrasting dynamics underscore the importa nce of understanding how structural pluralism moderates the effects of education on trust. At the same time, Nigeria’s expanding access to education, combined with rising digital engagement, is reshaping how citizens interact with governance and institutions, particularly among educated groups. To ensure that education becomes a bridge rather than a barri er in Nigeria’s pluralistic society, future efforts must focus on equitable access, inclusive representation, and tru st-building policies from the ground up.  Ultimately, this study contributes to a broader discussion of trust dynamics in diverse societies, offering in sights for building cohesive communities in Nigeria and beyond.
