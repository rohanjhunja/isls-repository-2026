# “A space to think, read, and observe the world”: What do RPPs Mean to Practitioners?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research-Practice Partnerships (RPPs) are designed to ensure the consequentiality of educational research by focusing on real-world problems of practice. Most of what we know from research on RPPs is grounded in the assumption that the purpose of RPPs is to provide multiple stakeholders with empirical findings that can inform educational policy and practice. This perspective, however, is largely researcher-focused and may not reflect practitioners’ aims and motivations. In this paper, we ask what RPPs mean to practitioners. Using ethnographic and discourse analysis methods, we show that for practitioners, RPPs can serve objectives such as fostering intellectual engagement and mediating cultural change.

## Background

Research-Practice Partnerships (RPPs) have recently emerged as an increasingly popular research paradigm among learning scientists who seek to bridge  the gap between academic research and educational p ractice. Defined as “long term collaborations between practi tioners and researchers that are organized to inves tigate problems of practice and solutions for improving sc hools and school districts” (Coburn  & Penuel, 2016, p. 48), RPPs are designed to ensure the consequentiality of academic research by establishing routines and infrastructures that (1) bring research to bear upon problems of pr actice; and that (2) create new pathways for resear ch findings to inform policymaking and pedagogical design (Farrell et al., 2022). The relationship between research and practice are context dependent in each RPP. For instance, Sjolund et al.’s (2022) systematic review of the literature  showed how in some cases research informed the con tent of educational interventions, while in other cases res earch informed models of practice. In a similar vein, practitioners’ and researchers’ roles in RPPs also tend to vary (Hadar et al., 2024). Yet notwithstanding these variations, underlying most of the existing research on RPPs lies one core assumption: that the primary aim is for research to inform educational policy and/or practice. Indeed, some have gone as far as proposing that the degree to which research informs policy and practice ought  to serve as a measure to assess the quality of educational research instead of the traditional notion of methodological rigor (Welsh, 2021). Our aim in this short paper is to challenge the ass umption that the ultimate purpose of an RPP is necessarily tied to the degree to which academic re search informs practice and policy. When RPPs are v iewed through the eyes of educational scholars, this assu mption may seem self-evident and indisputable. However, ethnographic data from a recent study we conducted shows that practitioners’ view s of an RPP, including how they understand its aim and purpose, can be quite d ifferent. Our findings show that the RPP  meant to these practitioners was shaped by their unique professional identities and positionalities.

## Context

The data for this short paper come from a RPP that saw two higher education institutions partner with 21 Israeli schools to advance efforts at pedagogical innovation through practitioner-led research. Within each school, a small group of teachers and/or administrators collaborate d with an academic faculty member to form a local r esearch lab that generated a meaningful research question, collected and analyzed empirical data, and informed  school policy and/or pedagogical design. Practitioners’ and faculty’s roles in each of the participating scho ols varied in response to local contexts (Hadar et al., 2024).

## Data collection and analysis

Etan, the first author, served as one of the faculty me mbers representing The Ben-Gurion University in the RPP. Hadar and Linor, the second and third authors,  served jointly as co-directors of the RPP. The data for this paper was collected in the 2022-2023 school year, amid Etan’s partnership with Oasis Primary School (pseudonym s were used to protect the privacy of the school and individual research subjects). At Oasis, Etan collaborated with Josh – Oasis’s principal – and a group of five teachers, and facilitated their school-led research aimed to evaluate a school mentorship program they had begun to opera te two years prior. As participant-observer, Etan collected various ethnographic data over the course of the year, including field notes, recordings of all meetings with Oasis staff, two structured interviews with Josh, and all written communications surrounding the RPP (e.g., emails and WhatsApp messages).

Our approach to data analysis drew from ethnographic and discourse analysis methods. For the purposes of this paper, w e began with a review of all field notes to locate a rich point  in our data. Rich points are observations that confound an ethnographer while st ill making sense to research subjects. This gap in understanding implies that interpreting rich points can shed a great deal of light on the culture under investigation, which is a primary goal of ethnography (Agar, 1994). We t hen drew on Gee’s (2014) approach to discourse analysis to interpret the interactions at the heart of the rich point that was identified. Finally, we reconsidered the entirety of our ethnographic data in light of the insights that emerged during the discourse analysis, to understand the meaning of the RPP to the practitioners at Oasi s Primary. The subsequent findings section is organ ized according to this logic. It begins with an overview  of the context, representing preliminary ethnograp hic understandings; proceeds with discourse analysis of  the rich point we selected; and concludes with a  second, thicker, description of the school community and th e tensions between the principal and some teachers, as a lens for explicating their understanding of the RPP.

## Findings

Oasis primary, Josh, the mentorship program, and the RPP Early on, we identified a tension between two unique attributes of Oasis Primary, where Josh served as principal. On the one hand, the way Josh described Oasis made it sound utopian. Classes were exceptionally small, and the school had vast financial resources used to fund ex tracurricular programs like dog training and garden ing. Josh seemed uniquely attuned to the humandimensions of education often overlooked in our bureaucratic society. For example, he had adopted and/or developed various pe dagogical means to engage students in authentic dia logue. He explained that to this end, the school had recen tly adopted some key elements from the Montessori a pproach and had launched a new mentorship program. The ment orship program, which was voluntary, paired studen ts with teachers and allocated a weekly one-hour slot to discuss whatever was on students’ mind s in a safe and intimate environment that aimed to support students’ growth, development, and social-emotional wellbeing. But on the other hand, Oasis also faced a unique ch allenge. Due to its remote location, Josh had no leeway in terms of the makeup of his teaching staff. He could rarely recruit new teachers because the school was so distant from large population centers, nor could  he allow himself to let anyone go  for the same reason. Josh’s primary concern vis-à-vis the RPP was that recruiting teachers to join the research team would be difficult, as he believed most teachers at Oasis were unfit in terms  of their dispositi ons, skills and education. He also thought many of them tended to shy away from taking on new commitments that might come with any extra work and/or responsibility.

Several things about Etan’s conversations with Josh set them apart from those he had with the principals of the other schools he partnered with. First, it became immediately apparent that Josh was his primary contact at Oasis. As Josh had anticipated, he struggled to recruit teachers, and each time they met over the first few months, the makeup of the group changed, and the  other group members’ participation in the meetings was mi nimal. In addition, Josh and Etandiscussed the research project surprisingly little and spent much of their time discussing broad topics such as the purpose of humanities educ ation, the relationship between academia and the ed ucation system, and Josh’s most recent book: a memoir of hi s experiences as school principal. Etan enjoyed these conversations, but at the same time, he did not fully understand the unexpected turn that the RPP had taken. The school-led research eventually materialized, and the resea rch team that came together consisted of Josh and five teachers, all of whom were directly i nvolved in the mentorship program. The stated goal of the school-led research was to compare teachers’  and students’ perceptions of the mentorship program  and identify any overlap and/or gaps between them. The group cho se this research topic and question, and over the c ourse of the year, designed the research methodology, collec ted interview data and analy zed it, and presented their findings.

A rich point in the data In June 2023, the school research team presented their findings to the rest of the teaching staff. We identified part of this conversation as a rich point, because Ellyn, one of the teachers who declined to join the rese arch team, responded surprisingly ha rshly to the presentation. Her opposition confounde d our research team but seemed to make perfect sense to everyone else present during the presentation of the findings, so we identified this interaction as an opportunity to learn more about the culture a t Oasis and the meaning of the RPP within it. Below  is a brief excerpt from this conversation: Josh: Okay, mentorship. Let ’s talk about the mentorships and about the research. Some statistics from this year. ((Indicates to Anne)) Go ahead, present the PowerPoint. Anne: ((Reluctantly shows the first slide)) Five staff me mbers, each of whom got 2-4 students [to interview] depending on availability. In total there were 16 students [in the study]. Josh: The number of students in the mentorship program declined significantly this past year… There were students from all age groups except firs t grade, which didn ’t participate for reasons we are all well aware of. Hopefully this wi ll change. In all grades, Ellyn, so that everyone can be a part of this program.

Ellyn: Over my dead body. ((Long silence)) Several discourse analysis “tools” (Gee, 2014) can help illuminate this brief interaction. For instanc e, the fill in tool asks what needs to be filled in to achieve clarity. In this case, when Josh said that the first grade (where Ellyn was homeroom teacher) didn’t participate “for reaso ns we are all well aware of,” we initially did not understand what he was referring to. A pplying the why this way and not that way  tool means asking how else an individual may have conveyed the same ideas and why they chose  this way instead. Why did Ellyn voice her oppositi on so strongly? This seemed like an important question. One compelling answer, which employed the doing and not just saying tool, was that Ellyn was trying to distance herself from something larger than the mentorship program, which she perceived as a threat to her professional  identity and educational philosophy. Armed with th ese questions and insights, we returned to our ethnogra phic data, re-coded our field notes, and revised our analysis. This second reading of our data helped answer our research question, and shed light on the meaning of the RPP. The meaning of the RPP Josh was an unconventional educational leader whose  vision often clashed with the established norms of  the school and its teaching staff. Unlike many school principals who rose through the ranks within their schools, Josh joined Oasis with a rich background in academia, ha ving completed a PhD in the humanities and a postdo ctoral fellowship in Europe. His educational philosophy was influenced by existential and radical theories, leading him to challenge the traditional structures and values of the education system. He was critical of the system’s emphasis on discipline, grades, and conformity, and this put  him at odds with some teachers at Oasis, who large ly saw discipline as fundamental to effective learning. Josh’s dissatisfaction with the education system motivated him to introduce new initiatives at Oasis, such as the men torship program, in an effort to  create meaningful learning experiences that challenged conventional power dynamics and fostered a more student-centered approach. In a similar vein, the RPP was first and foremost a solution to an ongoing problem. But this problem was not empirical. Josh did not feel the need to conduct school-based research at Oasis because there was a particular project or policy he thought would be well-served by empirical data that would allow him to make more informed decisions. Rather, his problem was personal and exi stential. He found his job at Oasis frustrating bec ause it did not provide him and his teaching staff with a stimu lating intellectual environment of the sort he had encountered in academia. Thus, the school’s participation in th e RPP was essentially another attempt to disrupt th e current state of affairs at Oasis and reshape the school’s discourse and culture. Both of Josh’s initiatives s parked debate among the staff. His mentorship program, aimed at d isrupting traditional power hierarchies by giving s tudents more agency and fostering deeper relationships betw een teachers and learners, exemplified his alternat ive educational vision. T eachers like Ellyn correctly saw such efforts as attempts to undermine  established norms, which they opposed on principle. This difference in  perspectives was evident in other exchanges betwee n Josh and Ellyn that night, which were more explicit. For instance: Josh: Again, a key principle in the mentorship program is that in these interactions, the adult loses something of his or her authority. Most interactions [with students] feature clear power dynamics, with adults possessing the knowledge and making the decisions. The power dynamics at school, by its very nature, are c lear. In the mentorship program, some teachers spoke [in their interviews] about feeling that not only do they not have any advantage over the child, but that sometimes th e child even teaches us. The child leads the flow. This is a significant and dramatic shift… Ellyn: We should add something about the fact that the Min istry of Education already provides the resources necessary for dialogic educa tion – twenty minutes [per week] for every child, not just the sixteen lucky ones yo u chose to participate in the mentorship program, but for the entire class. And t his is the homeroom teacher ’s responsibility.

Josh’s participation in the RPP was another point o f contention, because it was also associated with h is critique of the current state of affairs  and constituted an attempt to disrupt them, which E llyn opposed. Indeed, Ellyn conflated between the two programs, and vehemently opposed both, because at Oasis Primary, the mentorship program and the RPP were essentially two sides of the same coin, both constituting efforts to reshape the discourse at school, in ways that were less bureaucratic, more authentic, or more rigorous. These disagreements revealed the underlying tension between Josh’s desire to res hape the school’s culture and the resistance from t hose who were more comfortable with the existing framework. The following excerpt does a good job of capturing this dynamic: Josh:  Let’s stop the PowerPoint and talk about the research. The school [Oasis] joined a community of schools [the RPP], each of which took upon itself to investigate a topic that is occupying the school, whether pedagogical o r educational. We chose to focus on the mentorship program.

Ellyn:  You chose! ((followed by a long and awkward silence)) Josh:  Not I, we chose to focus on the mentorship program… I fully believe in two principles. First the school’s staff ought to create space within your professio nal life where you can think, read, and observe the world, not just ac t upon it. I believe that. Second, we often act intuitively, we rely on perception and experien ce, what the research tried to do was ground our activity in data. This was done collaboratively with the university… Esti: You led it [the research] and obviously it was [on]  the mentorship program and that’s why I didn’t join it.

Applying the connections building tool  (Gee, 2014), which asks how interlocutors use langu age to connect between things and make them relevant to each other, is illuminating here. It shows how for Ellyn, the mentorship and the RPP were directly tied to one another and to Josh’s educational philosophy more broadly. She believed that Josh had chosen to focus the RPP on the mentor ship program to reinforce it. Her own refusal to pa rtake in the school-led research came in response to this connection. At the same time, the significance building tool (Gee, 2014), which asks how interlocutors build up and re duce the significance of some things over others, i s also telling. Josh believed that joining the RPP would advance two goals – a space for intellectual growth and a means to ground practitioner work  in empirical research. By listing them in this orde r, Josh indicated that the first goal was more important to him. This interpretation was reinforced by the fact that, throughout the year, most of Josh’s conversations with Etan had focused on various themes that were not directly tied to the school-led research.

## Discussion and conclusion

The findings of this study reveal that the meaning of the RPP to Josh and Ellyn had little to do with directly informing school policy or practice. Rather, despite any differences they had, Josh and Ellyn both viewed the RPP as an effort to disrupt the traditional discourse a mong the teaching staff at Oasis. Josh saw this dis ruption as a positive shift – as a means to implement his educational vision: “space within [a teacher’s] professional life where you can think, read, and observe the world, not jus t act upon it.” For him, the empirical data was seconda ry, and the fact is that no concrete changes or decisions were made because of the RPP. Ellyn was opposed to any shift in the discourse and to Josh’s critical view more broadly. She identified strongly with the existing state of affairs and saw Josh’s efforts to change it as an unjustified act of interference. For this reason, she declined the invitation to join the research team even before the decision to focus the research on the mentorship program. The findings presented in this short paper generate d an important insight into the meaning and purpose of RPPs. Traditionally, the aim of RPPs has been un derstood as informing educational policy and practi ce. Our findings suggest that this assumption repr esents a researcher-centric viewpoint. When viewed from the perspective of practitioners like Josh and Ellyn, t he meaning and purpose of an RPP can be quite diffe rent. This study thus challenges the prevailing assumption that RPPs’ value lies solely in their capacity to impact policy and practice, emphasizing instead the potential of RPPs to provide a space for intellectual engagement or to transform school culture.
