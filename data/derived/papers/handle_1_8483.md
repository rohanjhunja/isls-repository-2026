# “What a Lovely Discussion!” Reconciling Disparate Assessments of Small-Group

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examined student participation during one small-group calculus task, comparing students’ firsthand accounts of their learning experiences to observations made by their teacher, student teacher, and peers. Taken together, these varying perspectives paint a more comprehensive (though sometimes contradictory) picture of what happened during the task. Working toward participatory equity – cultivating classrooms with fair (not necessarily equal) opportunities to participate – requires a deep understanding of students’ learning experiences. Findings revealed students’ accounts of the task differed in consequential ways. Despite the teacher’s positive assessment, considering student perspectives suggested this experience was equitable for the three male students, but not for Guadalupe, a young Mexican woman. She earned A’s in the course, yet still encountered barriers to participation resulting in her disengagement from the task. Exploring further how students’ learning experiences are shaped by gender, race, and other contextual factors are important directions for future research.

## Introduction

Working toward participatory equity – cultivating c lassrooms with fair (not necessarily equal) opportu nities to participate (Esmonde, 2009; Secada, 1989) – requires a deep understanding of students’ learning experiences. As researchers, we can observe how students participate during lessons, we can compare participation patterns across groups of students by gender and/or race, and we can judge what seems equitable based on expected distributions of talk (e.g., Shah & Crespo, 2018). However, if ou r analysis stops there, we are missing critical pie ces to the equity puzzle. Fairness is connected to power, privilege, and participatory expectations (Rasooli et al., 2019) and is not always visible to outside observers. To assess the extent to which every student has fair opportunities to participate in support of rich content knowledge and positive mathematical identity (Esmonde, 2009; Schoenfeld, 2014), we must examine learning experiences from cl assroom participants’ perspectives (Sengupta-Irving  & Vossoughi, 2019). Specifically, we need to privilege the perspectives of females and racially minoritized students, who often face additional barriers to participation in mathematics classes (Leyva et al., 2021). This study examines the experiences of four student s working on a small-group calculus task from multiple perspectives. I pay particular attention to what each perspective adds to the story of what h appened for these students. Questions are: 1) How did the teacher and student teacher make sen se of student participation during this task? 2) How did students make sense of their own and their peers’ participation during this task?

## Theoretical framework

Teaching and learning are considered sociopolitical  endeavors through which power is distributed, eith er perpetuating persistent inequities or challenging existing hierarchies and injustices (Gutiérrez, 2013; Philip et al., 2017; Valero, 2018). Learning is defined as changes  in students’ participation in collective classroom  practices (Lave & Wenger, 1991) and is a function of students ’ opportunities to participate (Gresalfi et al., 20 09). Opportunities to participate are shaped by the role s and responsibilities students are assigned throug h acts of positioning (van Langenhove & Harré, 1999) and influenced by racialized and gendered narratives about who can and should do math (Esmonde & Langer-Osuna, 2013). As students interact, expectations are negotiated for how each student should participate, distributing power  among students (Herbel-Eisenmann et al., 2015). Students positioned with competence and authority have more opportunities to participate in consequential ways, offering better access to rich math learning and identity development (Langer-Osuna, 2011).

## Data collection

This study took place during the 2020-2021 school year; all classes used Zoom. The focal group consisted of four 12th-grade students in a Calculus AB course at a US public high school: Yonas (“Black”), Guadalupe (“Mexican”), Hosein (“Mixed”), and Elijah (“White”). Guadalupe was the only female student. Ms. B was the calculus teacher (“White” female). Mr. K was the student teacher (“M exican” male). Quotation marks indicate words used by ICLS2022 Proceedings 1345 © ISLS participants to describe themselves. Names are pseu donyms. Students were given a “related rates” group  task without formal instruction on the topic. The teache r wanted them to think about the underlying ideas b efore formalizing strategies. The problem read: 1. A ladder leans against a wall. It begins to slide down the wall. Does the top of the ladder move at the same rate as the bottom of the ladder? 2. Suppose the bottom slides away from the wall at a rate of 1 ft/sec. How fast is the top of the ladder sliding down the wall when the bottom of the ladder is 6 ft from the wall? Assume the ladder is 10 ft. The group was video recorded through Zoom. Students spent 10 minutes on the task. During one-on-one stimulated-r ecall interviews (Erickson, 2006) with the teacher,  student teacher, and the four students, participants were shown the video and asked to share their observations of the task. Questions included: What stands out to you as noteworthy? What do you wish had gone differently? In what ways were these interactions typical and/or atypical? Students were also asked: How comfortable were you working on this task? How easy was it for you to participate? Would you say that you enjoyed this experience? Fol low-up questions probing for more details included: Can you say more about ___? And what does that mean to you?

## Data analysis

Interviews were transcribed then divided into observation units, operationalized as talk in which a person attended to or interpreted a particular part of the task vid eo. When the subject, topic, or timeframe of the ob servation changed, a new observation unit was assigned. I developed codes through an open-co ding process to identify similarities and differences in data and to facilit ate comparison across the participants’ observations (Corbin & Strauss, 2014). I used an iterative process of rede fining codes to eliminate redundancy and group simi lar codes together. For each observation unit, codes were assigned to capture the subject ([student name], teacher, student teacher, group, task); topic (e.g., actions, feelin gs, thinking, ways of being); timeframe (this day, different day, this year, previous year(s)); observation type (person, interaction, other); and tone (positive, neutral, negative).

## Student perspectives

The four students reflected on their own and their group’s participation after watching the video (Figure 2). Figure 2 A Summary of each Student’s Task Observations Yonas was confused but still comfortable sharing because it was “a good group,” but expressed disappointment that they did not work together and did not reach a  final solution. He also noted the conversation was  male-led. Guadalupe was thinking hard about the problem but felt her ideas were not valued by her peers. She felt excluded from the conversation and felt it was not going to get any better, so she disengaged. Hosein enjoyed this experience because he felt they “moved together into understanding.” He also acknowledged the conversation was ma ledominated and talked about wanting to be more inclusive. Elijah thought hard about the problem and seemed to enjoy it but would have preferred to work alone. He thought everyone was nice and worked well together.

## Discussion

In this case of four students working on a small-gr oup task, findings revealed students’ learning expe riences differed in consequential ways. From the teacher’s perspective, the group’s “lovely discussion” was re spectful, ICLS2022 Proceedings 1347 © ISLS joyful, and fearless, and the students thought deeply about the intended math. But, to assess participatory equity, the question is… to what extent did every student have opportunities to participate and be s een as an important mathematical contributor? From the students’ perspe ctives we learned that Yonas, Hosein, and Elijah fe lt comfortable thinking and talking about the math, but Guadalupe did not. She was confused, felt unsupported by her peers, and eventually disengaged. Everyone except Elijah noticed Guadalupe spoke less than her peers. Yonas and Hosein reasoned it was related to the pattern of male-led class conversations. Ms. B thought Guadalupe had something on her mind. Mr. K got closest to Guadalupe’s reality with his speculation that she felt ignored. Despite the teacher’s overall positive assessment of the group’s work, a closer look at students’ accounts sug gested this small-group task experience was equitable for the three male students, but not for Guadalupe, a young Mexican woman who was “a pretty confident person” and was u sually “leading the charge.” Guadalupe got As in th is course and earned a full scholarship to a top US university, but she was not immune to the barriers to participation faced by many females and students of color in advanced math courses. Exploring further how students’ learning experiences are connected to gender, race, and other contextual factors are important directions for future research.
