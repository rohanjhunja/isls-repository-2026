# Student Motivation in Science Learning With Embodied Computational Thinking

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In an on-going multiple-case study, we investigate the use of unplugged, embodied Computational Thinking activities to learn topics within biology and chemistry. We utilize Educational Design Research in collaboration with 4 teachers at 2 upper secondary schools, focusing on students’ experience and how the activities affect their motivation for learning the topics in question. Tentative results indicate that students find such activities fun, allowing them to address curricular content in new ways, but also confusing.

Introduction: Why use embodied Computational Thinking to learn science? Computational thinking (CT) is argued to be of prime significance for all citizens in the 21st century (e.g. Vuorikari et al., 2016; Wing, 2006). Wing’s definition of CT is often cited: “[T]he thought processes involved in formulating problems and their solutions so that the solutions are represented in a form that can be effectively carried out by an information-processing agent” (Wing, 2011). This  definition determines CT as algorithmic problem so lving without reference to specific domains, implying that CT is a set of abstract skills that can be generally applied – in Wing’s words “a “universally applicable attitude  and skill set” (Wing, 2006) that can be used to le arn and investigate any subject or discipline. Some dispute  remains as regards which skills the set comprises,  but decomposition, logical data analysis, modeling, alg orithm design (including sequencing in step-by-step procedures), automatization, and generalization are  usually listed (e.g. Chongtay, 2018; Shute et al.,  2017). A large strand within the literature emphasizes that CT can be performed ‘unplugged’, that is, without t he step of coding the designed algorithm into a computer progr am (Caeli & Yadav, 2020; CS Unplugged). The claimed pedagogical advantage is that students become familiar with the steps of the problem-solving method without the additional complexity of having to learn a code language. This is particularly relevant when CT is utilized to learn other disciplines than computer science. Here, premature introduction of coding may confuse learners and affect their motivation for learning both CT and the discipline in question (Chongtay, 2018). The mainstream understanding of CT as a set of abstract skills is challenged by research within the fields of learning and practicing arithmetic, logical, and algorithmic thinking. Research in situated learning (Lave, 1988) and embodied cognition (Abrahamson & Bakker, 2016; Johnson, 1987) show that such thinking processes ar e learned situatedly and crucially involve embodied cognitive processes. As these thinking processes are adjacent to or part of CT (Wing, 2011), the implication is t hat CT skills are also situated and will concretize  differently within different disciplines. It is further to be expected that their development and use within other disciplines can advantageously be supported – both cognitively and motivationally – by involving embodied activities. In the present on-going multiple-case study (Yin & Yin, 2018), we investigate the use of unplugged, embodied CT-activities to learn topics within biology and chemistry, acknowledged by researchers (e.g. Düsing et al., 2019) and teachers in practice to be particularly difficult for students to learn. The activities engage the CT skills of decomposition, modeling, sequencing, and generalization. We focus on how students experience  these embodied CT-activities, and, in particular, how they affect their motivation for learning the topics in question.

## Methodology

The advantage of the multiple-case approach is that  it provides opportunity to achieve a high degree o f both ecological and external validity. Our sample involves four teachers from two urban upper secondary sch ools in Denmark and in all six classes of biology and chemistry. The average class size is 26 students. The multiple-case study utilizes Educational Design Research (McKenney & Reeves, 2014) and activities are therefore deve loped in collaboration with the teachers in a series of i terations. Activities are designed for the individu al cl asses’ curricular content and the teachers share their materials for inspiration. Content themes are carbon cycle, nitrogen cycle, phosphorus cycle, chemical analysis, protein synthesis, and balancing chemical equations. All tasks address ‘algorithmic processes’ through unplugged, embodied activities. In the carbon cycle task, for example,  students take on the role of a carbon atom and move around different stations to simulate the dynamics of the cycle. This involves decomposition of the carbon cycle, embodied modeling of the dynamics, and sequencing. For instance, from the Atmosphere station, students may be absorb ed into a plant leaf and move to the Plant station.  During each move, students note stage and form of carbon. Based on the movements outlined in their notes, they produce a diagram of the carbon cycle and the route of the carbon atom through the cycle. Comparing the routes allows ICLS2022 Proceedings 1850 © ISLS them to generalize their model.  Another task utilizes LEGO bricks to model the balancing of chemical equations. Students decompose the reaction by letting bricks in different colors represent atoms. They then build reactants and products and reconcile reaction equations by counting bricks in each color on both sides. Finally, they list the sequential steps needed to determine the reactions and try to generalize the list for further balancing tasks. To evaluate students’ experiences, we used self-report with open questions supplemented by classroom observations and informal interviews. In the self-r eport, students were asked about their positive and  negative experiences and whether the activity made the content easier to understand. Observations focused on situations in which students were engaged and on situations where they seemed confused or demotivated. In short (1-4 minutes) interviews, students were asked how they experienced a given situation, why it was or was not motivating. In the analysis, we searched for signs of how embod ied involvement impact students’ self-reported understanding of the science content and of motivat ion. In our search for signs of motivation, we did not limit ourselves to one theory but were open to statements being interpreted inductively in different directions, especially interest (Hidi & Renninger, 2006), self-efficacy (Bandura, 2001), and utility value (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020).

## Tentative results

The very tentative results suggest that students find embodied CT-activities fun and interesting, allowing them to address curricular content in new ways. At the same time, many students also report that the CT activities are confusing. Students are challenged as regards e xperiencing the full picture of the processes, as t hey tend to focus on specific elements. Besides, they are chall enged in making connections to textbook content and  in generalizing their enacted algorithmic processes. They need support to understand physical models of processes.
