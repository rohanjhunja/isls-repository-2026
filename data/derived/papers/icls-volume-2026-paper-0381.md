# Getting Support and Gaining Clarity: Computational Thinking Pathways Communities of Practice

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In response to increasing demand to integrate computational thinking (CT) into K-12 education systems, districts are developing CT P athways that offer cons istent, cumulative, competency-based exposure to CT. The development of these Pathways can be demanding and isolating work. This study examines how communities of practice (CoPs) may be supportive for districts designing and implementing CT Pathways. We found shared ownership in the CoPs was essential for districts participating in CoPs as it promoted ideation, iteration, and knowledge sharing in support of district CT work.

Computational thinking (CT) has consistently been recognized as an important approach to broadening participation in computer science (CS), an essential problem solving skill, and more recently, as foundational for addressing the challenges and opportunities of generative AI and emerging technology (Roschelle & Burke, 2020; Kafai & Grover, 2025). Defined as a set of practices and skills originating in computational methods that can be used to solve interdisciplinary and real-world problems (Mills et al., 20 25), CT is a requirement in many states (e.g., Maryland State Department of Education, 2018). Districts have addressed this requirement with the articulation of district-specific CT Pathways, learning progressions that outline CT experiences and activities  for K-12 students that are to be implemented consistently and cumulatively (Burke et al., 2020). District leaders and teachers need support to integrate CT (Dunbar et al., 2025). Communities of practice (CoP) have been shown to be a promising practice for  supporting teachers in their implementation of CT (Killen at al., 2020). This paper reports on how the design and formation of communities of practice (CoP) supported a range of school districts to meet the challenges of communication, ownership, and responsiveness of their respective CT Pathways. Lave and Wenger’s (1991) notion of CoPs provides a useful framework for understanding how CoP operate, emphasizing that capacity-building ultimately emerges through sustained social participation, where members share best practices, solve problems collectively, and build a shared identity around their work. To meet their common goal, CoPs often engage in knowledge sharing and the development of common practices that are applied in their individual work (Hoadley, 2012). Wenger (1998) identified three main components of successful CoPs: joint enterprise, mutual engagement, and shared repertoire. In our research practice partnership (RPP), we brought together eight unique school districts that varied in the populations they served (from 985 K-12 students to over 250,000) and in their contexts for CT integration. They were collectively aligned howev er on a shared purpose of more effectively implementing and communicating their respective Pathways and ensuring such Pathways stayed updated and inclusive of other district initiatives, such as those around generative AI. We addressed the following two research questions: (1) In what way are multi-district communities of practice supportive of districts in the context of CT Pathways implementation? (2), What, if any, features of these multi-district communities of practice are particularly supportive of districts in their endeavors around consistent CT integration across their schools? To facilitate the formation of CoPs, we first identified shared interests and goals across the districts. Using data from a landscape scan (Dunbar et al., 2025) and from action plans created during monthly meetings with individual districts, we determined the topics and composition of each CoP. The three CoPs were organized around the following topics: “AI in CT Pathways”, “Building Awareness and Ownership of Your CT Pathway”, and “Designing Community Connected CT Pathways”. CoP’s were launched at a three-day, in-person convening followed by monthly meetings, and a virtual convening held at the end of the school year. CoP participants completed a questionnaire about their CoP experiences. This questionnaire consisted of 14 Likert-style items, two ranking questions, and six open-ended questions related to CoP goals, structure, activities, capacity building, and sense of belonging and ownership. We used descriptive analysis to examine the Likert and ranking questions and conducted thematic coding on the open-ended responses. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with twenty CoP participants. One member of the research team open coded the interviews (Saldaña, 2015). Coded results 2569 were reviewed by the full research team with any disagreements over code applications resolved through shared repertoire, mutual engagement, and joint enterprise (1998). Districts felt that CoPs were supportive of their next steps with their CT Pathways, demonstrating shared enterprise. Of the 15 respondents to the CoP survey, nine agreed and six strongly agreed that “ Participating in a cohort helped me with implementation of my district’s CT Pathway. ” This alignment led to clarity on next steps for each district as a result of their CoP work. Participants in the AI Literacy CoP felt AI literacy was a topic that was both emerging and critical as well as connected to the CT work. A district leader shared, “I think the biggest thing was our group was tasked with an evolving topic and subject matter that really connected to computational thinking.” For the AI and Building Awareness CoPs, the CoP supported the development of resources for their CT Pathway. However, districts in the Community Connected CT Pathway felt less connected to their CoP topic. One participant explained, “I kind of felt like the topic of our [CoP] was sort of forced. It was like, ‘Well, we wanna put you guys together, cause you're the new people.’” Even though both districts in this CoP shared a common focus around initiating and promoting their CT Pathways, the participants found it difficult to engage in the work together as they lacked shared ownership in the formation of the CoP and the problem of practice. District participants emphasized that sharing similar struggles of implementing CT Pathways was directly supportive of their mutual engagement in the CoPs. Districts felt that it was helpful to be able to share their own experiences with other educators that were facing similar struggles. A district leader stated, “It's also just nice to validate yourself that no one's got it figured out. And then it's nice to hear that others are struggling just as much as you are.” Similarly, a principal admitted it was good to know, “we're not alone in not being as far along as we would have hoped.” This common factor was shared by multiple district partners as being helpful to create mutual engagement during CoP meetings.

Participants found that the structure of the CoP meetings supported specific CT Pathway outcomes by encouraging the sharing and refinement of resources. We found that a monthly meeting cadence and the use of protocols during which districts could act as sounding boards for each other resulted in specific outcomes for districts’ CT Pathways. Using a common meeting routine in which districts shared products and solicited feedback further supported the development of a shared repertoire in the CT Pathway CoPs. A district leader shared that she found both “having other districts as sounding boards” and sharing their own best practices was beneficial and “caused us to think a little differently about things.” Districts found that by sharing their ideas and gettin g feedback from other districts, they could collaborate constructively on products that teachers and administrators in their district could use. A principal concluded that, “That's where the worth lies” in the CoPs. We found that by participating in CoPs, districts can provide support and encouragement in what can otherwise be isolating work, and this is essential for sustaining district efforts to design and implement CT Pathways. While a supportive system is important in the challenging work of implementing CT Pathways, it is equally as important that CoPs move past congeniality to more criticality in order to advance the goals of the CoP members (Owen, 2014). To engage in more criticality in CoP work, participants must have shared ownership to push the work forward via ideation, iteration, and knowledge sharing. In our CoPs we saw that a sense of ownership in the problems of practice each CoP developed and in the protocols used in CoP meetings was essential. If the problem of practice was technically one that was shared by participants, but there was not shared ownership in that problem of practice, that topic felt removed from the immediate needs of the participants thereby limiting resourcefulness and ingenuity across the participating CoP members.

Implementing a CT Pathway is challenging and can be isolating, requiring systems of support to respond to growing state mandates and the increasing urgency to provide a foundational base for the use of AI. Researchers in a RPP have the opportunity to not only monitor progress of CT Pathways but also support by serving as a bridge and relationship-broker among those doing this work. CoPs that use structures that facilitate relationship building and actionable feedback can lead to clarity for district leaders  about how to move past barriers they are facing to continue the work of cumulative, consistent, competency-based CT Pathways. Education is full of big, thorny problems similar to CT. Connecting districts to each other can help to give support to district leaders that may be leading transformational work alone. Forming CoPs around manageable problems of practice that are aligned with district needs can provide clarity for district leaders around next steps. Incorporating protocols and routines can lead to refinement of resources and tools needed to lead transformational change.
