# Resolving Expert Disagreement by Evaluating Misrepresentations

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how undergraduate students address expert disagreements when one expert misrepresents another’s research—which occurs frequently in popular media. An experiment with undergraduates indicated that the students noticed misrepresentations and lowered trust in the expert “guilty” of misrepresentation, but only when the misrepresentations were explicitly called out. This suggests the need for students to learn to be alert for such misrepresentations when making judgments.

## Introduction

The contemporary digital society is rife with disagreements. On some topics, such as climate change, experts are in broad consensus, and a reasonable strategy for laypeople  is to trust that consensus. O n other topics, the experts themselves disagree (e.g., whether direct instruction is more effective than inquiry-based learning or how best to address inflation). What are laypeople to do when experts disagree? Barzilai and Thomm (2023; Thomm et al., 2017) have shown that people may attribute expert disagreements to reasons such as topic complexity or expert interests (or biases). Thomm et al. (2017) showed that undergraduates find it challenging to resolve expert disagreements even when they identify reasons for these disagreements. Given the prevalence of expert disagreement, there is a need for more research on how people understand and attempt to resolve these disagreements. Goldman (2001) proposed that non-experts could adjudicate expert disagreements using strategies such as examining the content of experts ’ arguments, assessing experts ’ credentials and track records, evaluating independent evidence supporting the experts’ claims, and examining the experts’ interests and biases. When evidence is technical, it is difficult for laypeople to evaluate the content of evidence or arguments. But evaluating bias is one avenue that may be available to laypeople (Collins, 2014). One way to identify expert bias is to identify when experts clearly misrepresent other research in a way that serves their own position. Martella et al. (2021) showed that such misrepresentations are not uncommon among educational scholars. Thus, it could be productive for laypeople to try to identify misrepresentations of research as an indicator of bias. In the present experimental research, therefore, we investigated whether people detect such misrepresentations when they occur and whether they reduce trust in claims of experts who perpetrate such misrepresentations.

## Method

Participants were 183 undergraduate students enrolled in an education class at a national Japanese university. They participated as a class activity. Participants were randomly assigned to the conditions described below. The topic that students considered in the study was the relative instructional effectiveness of two forms of instruction: direct explanation and guided inquiry. In all conditions, students followed this procedure. The steps were identical across conditions except the second step (Exposure to expert views on DE and GI, as below).

• Pre-tweet activities. Participants read a short text explaining Direct Explanations (DE) and Guided Inquiry

(GI) and completed a pre-survey on initial beliefs about DE and GI.

• Exposure to expert tweets on DE and GI. Participants read two tweets from two experts about DE and GI.

The content of these tweets varied depending on the assigned condition (described below). In all conditions, Expert #1 advocated for DE on the basis of a review of over 70 studies written by Expert #2. Then Expert

#2 also tweeted about the review she had conducted.

• Tweet on a second topic. Participants read Expert #1’s tweet on a separate educational controversy (reading

on paper vs. on screens). Expert #1 strongly recommended reading on paper, not screens.

• Post-tweet survey: A series of questions followed, where participants rated their beliefs about DE and GI,

the trustworthiness of the experts, and their opinions on the new educational topic (reading on screens versus paper). All ratings were on a 7-point scale; participants also provided reasons for their ratings. The experimental design was a 2 x 3 factorial experiment, as explained below:

• Factor 1:  How Expert #1 misrepresented Expert #2’s study. In fact, Expert #2’s review—according to her

For half of the participants, Expert #1 selectively reported only the pro-DE part of Expert #2’s findings. For the other half, Expert #2 actively distorted the findings, stating that the review showed that DE produced greater learning gains than GI.

• Factor 2:  How Expert #2 responded to Expert #1’s misrepresentation. This factor had three levels. In the

Control condition, Expert #2 simply encouraged readers to read her review of effective forms of instruction. In this condition, there was no way for participants to know that Expert #1 had misrepresented the findings of Expert #2’s review. In the Full Results condition, Expert #1 presented the full results of her review— asserting that both DE and GI are effective. In this condition, participants might notice that Expert #2 had contradicted Expert #1’s misrepresentation, but the discrepancy was not explicitly called out. In the Full Results & Contradict  condition, Expert #2 both stated the full results of her review and explicitly contradicted Expert #1’s claims, adding that Expert #1 should tell readers about all the results, not just results that fit Expert #1’s own theory.

An analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted for each variable. On statistically significant results, Bonferroni post-hoc comparisons were run to ascertain which conditions were different. Because for most variables, there was no difference across Factor 1, we report results collapsed across this factor in this brief poster.

## Results

We report on two crucial post-reading variables. First is participants’ rated agreement with the assertion that “Direct explanation is a more efficacious form of instruction than guided inquiry.” These ratings were affected by Expert

#2’s response (F [2, 177] = 9.72, p <.001) (Means: Control = 4.04, Full Results = 3.92, Full Results & Contradict =

3.01). When Expert #2 presented full results and contradicted Expert #1’s claims that DE is more efficacious than GI, students gave lower ratings of agreement with this statement than in the other two conditions. When Expert #2 merely presented full results without contradiction, there was no difference with the control group. Exactly the same pattern of results arose with students’ agreement with the statement that “Students learn more when they read from paper than when they read from screens” (F [2,177] = 6.59, p <.002). (Means: Control = 4.89, Full Results = 4.99, Full Results & Contradict = 4.02) When Expert #2 explicitly contradicted Expert #1 while presenting full results, students gave lower ratings of agreement with this statement than in the other two conditions. When Expert #2 merely presented full results, there was no difference from the control group. The ratings results were consistent students’ reasons for their ratings. In the Full Results & Contradict condition, students were much more likely to allude to the contradiction than in the other two conditions.

## Discussion and conclusion

This initial study, of course, needs to be replicated with other contexts, participants, and topics. Our research to date demonstrates that undergraduates downgraded the credibility of an expert’s claims when the expert misrepresented others’ research in a self-serving way, but only when that expert was explicitly called out for the misrepresentation. This reduction of trust extended to an entirely different topic where the “guilty” expert had not been shown to engage in any misrepresentation. Explicit calling out of the misrepresentation was needed for students to notice them, but once noticed, students took the misrepresentation seriously. An implication for science communications is that when authors have misrepresented other’s findings, these misrepresentations may need to be explicitly called out for readers  to notice. But when they do notice, they may make appropriate reductions in trust in the statements made by those guilty of misrepresentation. An implication for education is that students should learn to carefully check whether what authors say about the research they cite matches what they can learn of that research from other sources. They should learn to be more attuned to discrepancies, even when these are not called out. Misrepresenting research is common among those  purveying misinformation, so this is a potentially powerful method for countering misinformation.
