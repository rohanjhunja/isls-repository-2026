# Integrating AI and Interaction Analysis: A Mixed-Methods Approach to Tracing Object-Based Participation in CSCL

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study introduces and employs a novel mixed-methods design that integrates computer vision, optical character recognition (OCR), and qualitative video-based interaction analysis to examine multimodal participation in Computer-Supported Collaborative Le arning (CSCL), with a focus on typing as a key form of object-based interaction. This approach integrates two computational tools: a neural network–based system for mapping typing activity in group interaction videos and a screen recording analysis system for extracting typed input. Analyzing a collaborative robotics programming session among middle school students, the findings identify who typed, when, and what was typed, as well as dynamic shifts in participation, tool access, and role negotiation. The study contributes to more inclusive, scalable and grounded analyses of CSCL processes by bridging Artificial Intelligence-enhanced automation with human insight, surfacing patterns that warrant deeper qualitative investigation.

Understanding CSCL processes requires attention to the complex, multimodal nature of student participation (Martinez-Maldonado et al., 2021; Ouyang et al., 2023; Worsley et al., 2021). Multimodal participation dynamics, as conceptualized by Lee et al. (2026), refer to the dynamic interplay of these diverse modes of contribution as students engage with peers, tools, and artifacts within learning environments. Recognizing nonverbal and objectbased forms of participation is critical for valuing the contributions of diverse students (Lee et al., 2026; Miller et al., 2022; Sinclair & de Freitas, 2019; Yeh et al., 2025). Video-based interaction analysis (Erickson, 2012; Jordan & Henderson, 1995) offers rich insights into multimodal participation dynamics but is highly labor-intensive. Recent advances in multimodal learning analytics (MMLA) offer promising tools for capturing and analyzing diverse data streams. However, many existing approaches remain costly, intrusive, and difficult to deploy in realworld settings, with limited application in naturalistic environments (Cukurova et al., 2020; Worsley et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2022). Recent work (Lee, 2025; Lee et al., 2026) contributes to addressing these challenges by using real-world collaborative group interaction videos as a primary data source for MMLA. By leveraging Artificial Intelligence (AI)-based image/video analysis technologies—particularly computer vision and neural networks— these studies enabled scalable and less intrusive extraction and quantification of multimodal human activities in authentic CSCL environments. For instance, Lee (2025)  developed an AI-powered Activity Mapping Video sociocultural learning theories (Engeström, 1987; Vygotsky, 1978), the current study extends this line of work by emphasizing object-based participation as a critical component of CSCL and introducing novel analytical tools and approaches. Particularly, we focus on typing  activity associated with a shared computer within collaborative student groups to explore the multimodal, specifically object-based, participation of middle school students. Building on our Activity Mapping Video Analysis system, which uses neural network  models for typing activity detection, this study introduces a novel complementary tool: a Screen Recording Video Analysis system that leverages Optical Character Recognition (OCR) to extract the content being typed on screens.  Together, they enable the detection of who is typing, when, and what is being typed from large-scale video data. By further integrating these systems with qualitative interaction analysis, this paper demonstrates a scalable yet context-sensitive approach for studying multimodal participation in real-world CSCL environments. Ultimately, this study contributes to more inclusive, scalable, and grounded analyses of CSCL by bridging automation with human insight, particularly through surfacing patterns and moments that warrant deeper qualitative investigation. Video-based interaction analysis (Erickson, 2012; Jordan & Henderson, 1995) was used to analyze how students’ roles evolved, how their interactions shaped the CSCL process, and to synthesize results from prior phases.
