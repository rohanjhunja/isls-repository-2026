# When Care Is Not Enough: A Call for Critical and Sociopolitical Conscious Care

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Noddings’ (1988) argument for reframing of the aims of education around care centers modeling, dialogue, practice, and confirmation. Roberts (2010) calls for culturally relevant critical teacher care (CRCTC), which integrates tenets of culturally relevant pedagogy (Ladson-Billings, 1995) and critical race theory. In this study, we use culturally relevant critical care within the context of multiple teacher education programs. Results include a) considering socio-political contexts of learning and being when enacting care and b) modeling care and vulnerability. This work contributes to a revisioning of Noddings’ (1988) framework to be more inclusive and racially conscious.

Major issues addressed In Noddings’ (1988) argument for reframing the aims of education around care, the pedagogical practices of modeling, dialogue, practice, and confirmation take center stage. She defines modeling as when “teachers treat students with respect and consideration and encourage [students] to treat each other in a similar fashion” (p. 223). She defines dialogue as ongoing conversation where “conclusions are not held by one or more of the parties at the outset” (p. 223). In dialogue, teachers model respect and consideration by co-constructing solutions with students. Noddings states, “in a classroom dedicated to caring, students are encouraged to support each other; opportunities for peer interaction are provided, and the quality of the interaction is as important as the academic outcomes” (p. 223). By confirmation, Noddings describes a dynamic whereby teachers empower students to identify and clarify their purpose in life and work to support them in that effort. These practices both constitute specific teaching moves, and they work in concert to re-establish classrooms as places where teachers work with students to establish more ethically-and morally-just learning spaces. Noddings’ (1988) care framework has provided meaningful guidance for educators seeking to care for their students. Research exploring the use of this framework over time varies in both application and understanding of care. For instance, one qualitative study (Walker & Gleaves, 2016) explored higher education teaching faculty perceptions of care in teaching. Results indicated that caring teachers had four key characteristics: (1) a relationship at the center (i.e., quickly learning students’ names, responding to emails in a timely manner, initiate contact with students when challenges arise); (2) compelled to care (i.e., spending multiple hours supporting students, facilitating learning in various ways, explicitly support student diversity);

(3) caring as resistance (i.e., defend students when needed, question policies); and (4) caring as less than. This

final point referred to teachers who felt passionately about teaching but who felt the need to justify their positions through awards or publications rather than focusing on students. Another study (Anderson et al., 2020) that focused on student perceptions (n = 55) of good teaching at a New Zealand research institution found: “[Students] described good teachers as people who care about their discipline, care about teaching and care about students, powerfully influencing students’ engagement with subject matter, enthusiasm for learning and aspirations for the future” (p. 1). Perceptions of care from both the teacher and student perspective have variations, but they appear to focus on relationships. However, other studies focused on the use of care with marginalized populations highlight the need to support students beyond the care framework. For instance, one ethnographic research study (Hos, 2016) explored the experiences of refugee students who were recent immigrants to a U.S. urban secondary school. The study found that the teacher exhibited ethical care for these students, and yet these students needed additional support in order to be successful in their new lives in a U.S. school. Another study exploring the intersection of race and care analyzed the experiences of middle school girls from Puerto Rico (Rolón-Dow, 2005); this research advocates for the use of “counternarratives to provide more intimate, caring connections between teachers and the Latino communities where they work” (RolónDow, 2005, p. 77). While a care framework is still necessary, the need to expand its application is critical for supporting all learners.

These studies demonstrate the benefits of care within teaching, while also identifying the need to examine a) how care is being defined and bounded and b) question who is defining care. Care is typically defined and enacted within powered relationships and structures within education (Noddings, 2012; Roberts, 2010). An authentic narrative of care must consider factors beyond the classroom that impact student learning ICLS2022 Proceedings 1473 © ISLS and could redefine how care is understood by educators and students alike. This understanding of care shifts the role of protagonist to students within teacher-student relationships, where students help define care within the contexts of their lives (Roberts, 2010). Framed within the context of teacher preparation, culturally relevant critical care requires both providing this form of care to teacher candidates and also supporting candidates’ learning and teaching practices focused on culturally relevant critical care. For our study, we sought to understand socially conscious perspectives of care within teacher education programs during the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic.

## Potential significance

This study highlights the need for teachers to listen to the expressed needs of their students, particularly as those needs relate to the sociopolitical context of the time. Demonstrating care for students should not be a simple show of values, which could be detrimental in promoting imbalanced power dynamics (Noddings, 2012) or deficit models of students, perpetuating racist thinking (Matias & Zembylas, 2014). Rather, care entails coconstruction of learning between the teacher and the student. This collaborative relationship can relate to assignments, instructional activities, and discussions. Au (2017) calls for teacher activism to disrupt the injustices seen in schools and districts across the U.S. to “help build student and teacher critical consciousness around what is happening right now in the United States. For instance, we ought to complicate students’ understandings by analyzing the history, economics, politics, environment, and human experiences of the U.S.Mexico border” (Au, 2017, p. 148). Addressing immigration is only one issue that is relevant to our students and teachers alike. These social and political issues must be integrated into the curriculum as we prepare teachers to respond to the calling to be an educator. This collective consciousness focused on working for justice can help to create honest, authentic, and caring learning environments that honor the lived experiences of our diverse students. Doing so can contribute to a revisioning of Noddings’ (1988) framework to be more inclusive and racially conscious.

Theoretical and methodological approaches Research studies that focus on critical care for students utilize a variety of terminology in their understanding of care and advocacy for students. Rector-Aranda (2018) calls for teacher education programs to integrate critically compassionate intellectualism (CCI), which combines critical pedagogy, authentic caring, and social justice curriculum. Authentic caring is defined as relationship-focused teaching that promotes a respectful teacher-student relationship filled with admiration and love (Cammarota & Romero, 2006). Additionally, Roberts (2010) calls for culturally relevant critical teacher care (CRCTC), which integrates tenets of culturally relevant pedagogy (Ladson-Billings, 1995) and critical race theory. This framework entails a focus on students’ voices, counternarratives, and student futures in addition to a consideration of political and ideological thinking, especially related to race and power (Roberts, 2010).

One caution in the literature is to avoid deficit model thinking of students shrouded as caring. For instance, Matias and Zembylas (2014) argue that predominantly white teacher candidates must interrogate their emotions of care and sympathy, particularly towards students of color, and how these emotions intersect with whiteness. These authors argue that antiracist teachers must explore their own narratives around caring that “are sometimes hidden expressions of disgust for the Other” (p. 319). They advocate for patience on the part of teacher educators in working towards an authentic care narrative: We, as teacher educators, have to be prepared to accept that this process is long and painstaking, and it will be full of emotional landmines, but it is of utmost strategic importance to expose the insidious emotional power of racism and its affective investments in white teacher candidates’ identities (Matias & Zembylas, 2014, p. 333).

This call to action regarding antiracist pedagogy in teacher preparation can help inform understandings and enactments of care in teacher education.

## Methods and Data Sources

This qualitative study analyzed transcript data from seven focus group interviews. These interviews occurred with students from five separate methods courses at four universities, in the fall semester of 2020. These universities spanned the Northwest, Midwest, and East Coast of the U.S. All students were enrolled in a teacher preparation program at their respective institutions.

The data analysis entailed a multi-step, collaborative process. First, the research team collaborated in real time via Zoom to review focus group transcripts, which had been uploaded to Dedoose. The research team utilized protocol, or a priori, coding, in which we identified a set of pre-established codes that could be assigned to the data based on the literature review and our own experiences as researchers in this study. This form of ICLS2022 Proceedings 1474 © ISLS coding is recommended for research teams analyzing data collaboratively (Saldaña, 2016). This form of coding was also useful due to the use of Noddings’ (1988) preexisting care framework from which we were framing our exploration. In addition to the protocol codes, the team also conducted open coding of the data, where parts of the qualitative data were broken down into meaningful codes for comparison across the data set (Saldaña, 2016). After this first cycle of collaborative coding, the research team individually coded the remaining transcript data utilizing the existing codes. After all data were analyzed by each researcher, the codes were transformed into broader themes that assigned meaning to our initial codes.

## Major findings

Considering sociopolitical contexts of learning and being In creating a setting for care, we wish to highlight the ways in which teacher educators were aware of the social and political contexts in which their students existed. We frame this first theme as teacher educators considering the contexts of learning and being because of ways that the socio-political contexts of students’ lives impacts their learning and the ways in which they exist in the world. Further, we argue that not considering our students’ socio-political contexts of learning and being at best creates contexts of siloed care, where the care we show students does not help them navigate the world outside of our classrooms, and, at worst, perpetuates racism, sexism, homophobia, xenophobia, transphobia, and additional phobias. In this way, we view critical care as a method for dismantling systems of oppression through the individual relationships we build with students. Some of the ways that teacher educators demonstrat ed their consideration of their students’ sociopolitical contexts of learning and being were supporting students as they navigated the dual contexts of a global pandemic with COVID-19 and the Black Lives Matter social movement following the murder of George Floyd. One student reflected on their professor’s support during these contexts: And I think I sat in a call with him and I just started crying about like how hard my teaching and teach classroom placement has been going because of my situation. I was homeless. I like got kicked out. My mom kicked me out of my house because I'm dating a Black woman. And then my dog almost died because people wouldn't, the vets wouldn't take my dog in because my fiancé was Black. And it was all in the midst of the Black Lives Matter protest and it was just really fucked up and like in the midst of all of this, I was just trying to figure out how to do schoolwork. In this quotation the student does not necessarily highlight what responses the teacher educator had, but that they felt open to reaching out and discussing the contexts in which they were struggling. The socio-political contexts that teacher candidates participated in did not only emerge when discussing their personal struggles, but also when thinking about teaching as a practice. Another teacher candidate from a different institution talked about how their practice as a teacher existed in the context of historical racism and racial injustice. I just speak a lot of truth when it comes to racism and making sure that people are seen, especially if it's the indigenous people or Black people and just making sure that the validity and white fragility isn't just taking over the conversations because I'm-and I know that my ego showed up-and my triggers of, like, I'm sick and tired of white fragility taking over the conversation and me being attacked-took over the conversation and I definitely apologize for that. In this quotation, the teacher candidate speaks to how teaching practice cannot exist outside of the sociopolitical contexts in which students and teachers exist. Doing so perpetuates white fragility and racism. This quotation recognizes teaching as an act in participation with the world and how teaching can be an act of social justice, seeking to influence and change the socio-political contexts in which we live. Modeling care and vulnerability Another key theme that arose in the data was modeling care for students. This modeling was largely on the part of the teacher educator modeling care for the student teacher. However, there were instances where the student teacher also mentioned experiences where they were modeling for their own K-12 students as well. Modeling occurred in multiple ways: modeling vulnerability through reflective practice, modeling teaching methods, and modeling social and emotional health.

First, when teacher educators modeled vulnerability, the student teachers voiced really appreciating this learning experience. The vulnerability within these classes was not only for the teacher educator, though; students also highlighted feeling vulnerable as learners, particularly when they had to watch and analyze videos of their own class, but this experience appeared to be a positive one. For instance, one participant said: It was a lot of the meta-analysis where we would record our own classes, and then the next class period, watch ourselves and go, oh, what were we doing and what we weren’t we doing, so I think that ICLS2022 Proceedings 1475 © ISLS meta-analysis via video was odd for me to analyze a video of our own class period, but I think it was also really helpful.

Furthermore, the vulnerability of the teacher educator in this meta-analysis was also valued by the students. For example, one participant discussed an instance when the teacher educator highlighted in the video the point at which she gave instructions to the class as being a poor example of how to provide instructions. The student participant reflected: She gave us all of these instructions that we were supposed to do in a video and then we were talking about it afterwards. And she was like, that was just that was bad, like, don't, don't do that...There was no way you guys were going to be able to focus on all of that stuff because I had you all doing like four or five things once. And it was really helpful for her to do that because, I mean, obviously I had partaken in the activity, cuz I was in class. And I think when I was doing it, I think I remember trying to do all the things, but I didn't cognitively realize that it was difficult. I think I was just attempting to do it all for her. But then, having her step back and be like, this is too much, don't put that on your students was really helpful for me when I have been planning lessons since then, to be like okay, I need to step back and make sure to pare down a lot of what I'm expecting from people, expecting for my students and having to see her struggle with that…was super helpful and something I've implemented. The student appreciated the reflectivity of the experience of watching their own learning, but they also mentioned the transferability of this experience into their own teaching practice. Further, another student highlighted the struggles one teacher educator was having in facilitating online discussions: “It felt nice knowing she was in the same, our professor was in the trenches with us in some sense, and I think that was really admirable to watch her go through.” The teacher educator and the student teachers were learning together how to navigate online learning spaces, and the vulnerability of this experience demonstrated the care the teacher educator had for the students, and vice versa. An additional student said of another teacher educator: “[He] is very, very good about introducing his own vulnerability to the classroom and leading with that...it's definitely one of the biggest lessons that I've taken from this whole program is how our students are not going to be vulnerable.” Perhaps the vulnerability modeled by the teacher educators in these courses will help students model their own vulnerability for their K-12 students. One specific quality of vulnerability that we wish to highlight is how modeling vulnerability was done as part of a process of building trust and creating a safe space from which the sociopolitical contexts of students’ lives can be incorporated, discussed, and supported. It was necessary for teacher educators to model vulnerability and establish safety as a component of the classroom community in order for students to then make themselves vulnerable. Doing so, we argue that teacher educators need to be responsive to these lived realities in order to care for students.
