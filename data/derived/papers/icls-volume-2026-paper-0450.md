# Modular Worlds of Co-Design: Mapping Research and Practice Across the Pluralistic Field of the Learning Sciences

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study begins to examine how co-design has been enacted in different ways across two decades of Learning Sciences research. Network analysis of 136 coded ICLS papers (2004–2025) revealed three modular configurations of practice linked by bridging nodes such as museums. The analysis demonstrates how open data on research and bibliometrics can help the pluralistic field of the Learning Sciences better understand and shape how and why co-design is deployed in research and practice.

Co-design as a practice holds promise as a way to incorporate stakeholders’ perspectives into the design process. Doing so can create a unique opportunity to improve design products’ usability and application to educational settings, while also holding the  potential to be inclusive and responsive to the needs, identities, and histories of stakeholders (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Pitt, et al., 2019; Tierney et al., 2021). However, to do so, requires leveraging stakeholders’ experiences, perspectives, and expert ise into the design process and, ideally, the final design product (Tierney et al., 2024). The promise of co-design has made the practice particularly appealing to researchers in the Learning Sciences, who frequently deploy co-design methods in the context of research practice partnerships and the creation of engaging learning technologies, for example. Prior studies suggest that while co-design has become increasingly common in the Learning Sciences, it likely varies considerably across the field’s interdisciplinary landscape and may, in practice, mean very different things as it unfolds within diverse design and educational infrastructures (Papendieck et al., 2025). And, while co-design in theory and concept is broadly valued and promoted for its potential to make design processes more effective, equitable and just, co-design in practice and in context may play out quite counterproductively (Papendieck et al., 2024). Therefore, as research on and with co-design grows, there is a need to better understand the communities, conditions and practices in which it is occurring. Building on nascent efforts to better leverage open and digital data streams and contemporary analytical Papendieck at al., 2025), this study advances early efforts to show how open data on research and bibliometrics available via the ISLS archives can be used to refine our understanding of core methods, questions and practices of broad interest to the learning sciences. The research question was: What are the configurations of co-design practice as represented in papers from the International Conference of the Learning Sciences in terms of contexts, who participates, and what disciplines are involved? In the full presentation, the network analysis presented here will be elaborated with longitudinal visualization and analysis of topics and communities over time generated via complimentary methods based on topic modeling and semantic similarity.

## Method

This proof-of-concept network analysis study focused on three dimensions identified in prior work as important to variation in co-design practice (Papendieck et al., 2025): who the co-designers are, the contexts of co-design, and the disciplines represented on each project. To understand the relationships between these elements, the full text of papers from the International Conference of the Learning Sciences (ICLS) were collected and analyzed. A total of 136 ICLS papers from 2004 (the first year the terms “co-design” or “codesign” were used) were manually coded in MAXQDA Analytics Pro by the first author with support from the second author and suggestions from a generative AI tool. Coding was conducted for the categories of the co-design context, the composition of the co-design team, and the disciplines involved. Coding resulted in 22 codes covering 1,981 coded segments, with disagreements resolved through iterative discussion among the research team. Analysis was conducted for blocks of years: 2004-2010 (25 papers), 2012-2018 (49 papers), and 2020-2025 (62 papers). Correlation analysis determined the likelihood of any combination of codes co-occurring within the same paper. These were used to construct a network map, where the Girvan-Newman algorithm (Girvan & Newman, 2002) identified clusters by iteratively removing the edges with the highest betweenness centrality. 2779 Network analysis of correlations at p <.01 resulted in a network map of three distinct clusters with a modularity of Q =.53 (see Figure 1). The cluster of purple triangles demonstrates the existence and usage of co-design specifically in context of the u niversity or college environment with an interconnected co-design relationship between educators and learners, researchers and pre-service educators, and peer practitioners. This appears to be exclusively concentrated on the development of future teachers.  Pre-service teachers are co-designing in the role of peer practitioners, or potentially with in-service teachers as co-designers. Figure 1 Clustered Network Map of Co-Design Contexts, Co-Designers, and Disciplines The salmon circles cluster depicts co-design in informal and non-traditional social contexts, navigating complex social issues in learning by fostering social justice and equity. Surprisingly, this sometimes involved codesign within highly constrained or mandated environments. This primarily entails researcher-community collaboration, bridging the environment of traditional, formal learning to the informal, non-traditional learning environment taking place amongst the greater community and family members. The green squares cluster represents co-design in the role of innovation and design of formal K-12 curricula within established educational systems. The relationship between design and innovation is highlighted by the interdisciplinary collaboration (inclu ding learning sciences, education, design, STEM, computer science and human-computer interaction) between researchers and professionals belonging to these disciplines. The overall map suggests three distinct, yet interconnected configurations for co-design within the field of learning sciences. The clusters are largely separated, indicating that these different forms of co-design often operate with different sets of part icipants, in different contexts, and with different patterns of interdisciplinarity. Museums serve as notable contextual bridges between formal and informal settings, suggesting they may function as boundary-crossing sites where the otherwise separated con figurations of co-design practice could come into productive contact.

## Significance

Visualizing and investigating modularity in this network helps us begin to understand how co-design has been enacted differently across different “worlds,” enabling us to trace and compare who designs, what, with whom, and for what purposes. The visualization highlights bridging nodes (like museums) that might be investigated and invested in as valuable sites for boundary crossing, where interdisciplinary fluencies, collaboration, and methodological innovation among co-design practitioners can emerge. Such points also present themselves as conduits for translating and aligning broader concerns around learning, equity, and impact. From a disciplinary analytics perspective, the analysis demonstrates how open bibliometric and research data from the ISLS archive can help learning scientists make sense of their sprawling, multidisciplinary “big tent” (Nathan et al., 2016) of a field. Such analytics could support equitable and representative symposium construction, offer synoptic views of research trends for early-career scholars who are getting their bearings in a sprawling and pluralistic field, and strengthen reflexive understanding of the field’s evolving infrastructures of design and participation. 2780 Bang, M., & Vossoughi, S.
