# The Impact of Artificial Intelligence Applications on the Development of Techno-Mathematical Literacy Among Pre-service Mathematics Teachers

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Recent studies have increasingly focused on how techno-mathematical literacy (TmL) develops among teachers. This study examines the effects of artificial intelligence (AI) applications on pre-service mathematics teachers (PMTs’) techno-mathematical literacy (TmL) and AI self-efficacy perceptions. In the study, Solomon four-group experimental design was used to control the effect of pre-test on the results. Eighty PMTs’ selected by simple random sampling method were assigned to two experimental and two control groups. In experimental groups, AI-oriented activities were implemented in addition to the standard content of the technology-assisted mathematics teaching course. Data were collected using the Techno-Mathematical Literacy Scale (TmLS) and Artificial Intelligence Self-Efficacy Scale (AISES) and analyzed with parametric tests such as independent sample t-test and analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). As a result of the 10-week study, it was found that the AI practices had positive effects on PMTs' TmL levels and AI self-efficacy perceptions. The study reveals that the integration of AI into teacher education programs offers an important opportunity to develop PMTs' technological and mathematical competencies.

## Introduction

The rapid increase in the use of digital technologi es in education necessitates reshaping teachers' pr ofessional competencies. Along with these developments, it has  become essential for pre-service mathematics teachers (PMTs’) to acquire specific skills to effectively integrate technology into the teaching process and provide students with a learning experience that meets the demands of the era (Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2010). Particularly in mathematics teaching, the use of technology and digital tools strengthens student s' ability to solve complex problems, analyze data, and acquire mathematical kn owledge associated with the real world (Hoyles et a l., 2010). In this context, techno-mathematical literacy (TmL), which refers to the in tegration of digital literacy with mathematical knowledge and skills, emerges as a crucial concept (Hoyles et al., 2007). The term TmL, which defines 21st-century skills for the field of mathematics, was in troduced by Kent et al. (2005). TmL can be described as a combination o f mathematical, statistical, and technological skil ls that individuals need in their work. The concep t of TmL aims to enhance PMTs' problem-solving, data analysis, and technical communication skills by supporting mathem atical thinking processes with digital tools (Bakke r et al., 2011). These skills are not limited to academic fie lds but are also highly significant in today's prof essional world. Integrating TmL into teacher education, particularly with the increased use of technology in mathematics teaching, lays the foundation for the digital competencies PMTs’ will require in their professional careers (Anderso n-Pence, 2017). For instance, Ovadiya (2023) observed signif icant improvements in TmL levels among teachers who developed problem-solving and teaching activities using digital tools. Ovadiya’s study provides valuable insights into how teachers can ef fectively use digital tools to support mathematical  thinking. These tools enhance not only PMTs’ access to information but also their ability to i nterpret and apply it. At this point, the developme nt of TmL serves as a critical cornerstone for creating meani ngful learning experiences for students, alongside improving PMTs’ professional skills. Similarly, Jacinto and Carreir a (2023) demonstrated that developing TmL skills he lps teachers present mathematical concepts more comprehensibly, thereby increasing students' motivation to learn. The development of TmL is significant not only for teaching but also for providing students with opportunities for deeper thinking and analysis.

Another essential concept that enables PMTs’ to benefit from such practices is self-efficacy. According to Bandura’s (1997) self-efficacy theory, individuals’ beliefs in their ability to perform a specific task directly influence their efforts, resilience, and performance concerni ng that task. Educa tional research shows that teachers with high levels of self-efficacy are more successful in providing students with effective learning experiences through technology (Tschannen-Moran & Woolfolk Hoy, 2001). For PMTs’, developing self-efficacy regarding the use of digital and technological tools in education also e nhances their adaptation to these technologies (Wan g & Chuang, 2023).

Artificial Intelligence (AI) in education refers to the use of AI technologies to enhance learning processes, reduce teachers' workload, and offer personalized e ducation. AI has the potential to make learning mor e effective and efficient by considering individual differences in education. When working with complex technologies like AI applications, PMTs’ need a high level of self-efficacy to use these applications efficiently in c lassroom activities. High self-efficacy enables PMTs’ to adopt AI applications effectively, provide innovative learning opportunities for students, and use these tools creatively in their l essons (Holmes et al., 2019). The development of se lf-efficacy not only supports PMTs’ ability to use technology but also enhances their capacity to apply it meanin gfully. Scherer and Siddiq (2019) emphasize that teachers with high  self-efficacy in using digital tools for education emplo y these tools more creatively and effectively in classroom activities. Research on the role of AI in education shows that AI applications enhance PMTs’' abilities to analyze student data, develop individualized learning paths, and provide personalized feedback to students (Lu ckin et al., Woolf, 2010). Luckin and colleagues (2016) demonstrated how AI tools could assist PMTs’ in deeply analyzing the learning process. However, some critical studies ar gue that the overuse of AI-supported educational technologies might overshadow the human elements in education (A oun, 2018). Therefore, balancing the use of AI applications in education requires PMTs’ to focus on both technological knowledge and the et hical pedagogical application of this knowledge.

This study addresses a significant gap in the literature by examining the impact of AI applications on PMTs' TmL and AI self-efficacy levels. Although previous research has highlighted the importance of TmL in supporting meaningful learning experiences (Jacinto & Carreira, 2023), the role of AI tools in this process has n ot been sufficiently investigated. This study aims to contribute to the field of learning s cience by exploring how AI-supported teaching practices enhance PMTs’ cognitive and technological capacities. Our research seeks to provide experiences that contribute to PMTs’ digital literacy and their capacity to use technological tools in connection with mathematics. Accordingly, our general research question was determined as follows. RQ1: What are the levels of PMTs' techno-mathematical literacy and artificial intelligence self-efficacy? RQ2: What is the effect of artificial intelligence-supported instruction on PMTs' techno-mathematics literacy and artificial intelligence self-efficacy?"

## Method

In this study, Solomon four-group design was used to examine the effect of an a rtificial intelligence-supported curriculum on PMTs' techno-mathematical literacy (TmL) and artificial intelligence self-efficacy levels. This design is an appropriate research design when the effect o f pretest on posttest should be controlled (Campbel l & Stanley, 1963). In our study, Solomon four-group design was preferred for the TmL scale, considering that the pretest could affect the posttest results. It was hypothesized th at TmL levels may change gradually throughout the develo pment process of PMTs’ and that more reliable data would be obtained by considering the effect of the pre-test. However, it was evaluated that the retest effect was less li kely in the artificial intelligence self-efficacy scale. This is because self-efficacy measures individuals' beliefs about a particular subject and it is assumed that no significant retest effect will occur in the measurements after a short-term intervention (Bandura, 1997).

## Participants

The participants were 80 teacher candidates enrolle d in an elementary mathematics education program at  a public university. A simple random sampling method was emp loyed to ensure the diversity and representativenes s of the sample. These individuals had varying levels of exp erience with technology and AI, making them an idea l group for examining the effects of the intervention.

## Discussion and conclusion

The findings of this study, which demonstrate the significant positive impact of AI tools on the development of both techno-mathematical literacy and AI self-efficacy among PMTs’, are well-supported by existing research. As the role of AI in education grows, studies consistently  show that AI can enhance both mathematical thinkin g and technological literacy. For instance, Holmes et al.  (2019) emphasize that AI tools facilitate deep er comprehension of mathematical concepts by enabling teachers to visualize and manipulate complex problems. This aligns with our study’s results, which revealed improved mathematic al knowledge and skills development in the experime ntal group.

Noss and Hoyles (1996) highlight the role of techno logy in connecting abstract mathematical concepts to real-world applications. Our findings align with this, demonstrating that AI tools enhance teacher candidates’ ability to use technology creatively, consistent with Selwy n’s (2020) view that AI fosters innovative teaching  practices. Similar to these perspectives, Drijvers et al. (202 3) underline the importance of integrating techno-mathematical literacies (TML)—a blend of mathematical, technological, and communi cative skills—into STEM curricula. Their study not only designed a TML-focused course but also developed instruments to measure learning gains, providing further evidence that structured instructional interventions can improve techno-mathematical competencies. Improvements in algorithmic thinking and problem-solving also reflect Papert’s (1980) emphasis on technology shaping both problem-solving outcomes and cognitive processes. While AI provides significant benefits, Aoun (2018) warns against over-reliance, emp hasizing the importance of balancing technology wit h human elements like empathy. Our study supports this, sho wing that AI complements traditional methods rather  than replacing them. Additionally, Bandura’s (1997) theory of self-efficacy highlights that confidence drives technology adoption. Significant gains in AI self-efficacy among PMTs’ in our study underline the importance of hands-on training to address resistance due to inadequate pr eparation (Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2010). Integrating AI into teacher education enhances technological proficiency and prepares teachers for modern classrooms. In a similar vein, Ovadiya (2023) demonstrated how in-service teachers developed techno-mathematical literacy by engaging in digital problem posing and reflection through action research. Her findings show that using digital tools in classroom contexts can trigger transformative understanding o f mathematical concepts and pedagogy —emphasizing the role of reflection and teacher agency in TML development. However, ethical concerns like data privacy and ine quality (Selwyn & Facer, 2013) remain critical and should be addressed in future research. Ultimately, these findings underscore the transformative potential of AI in developing techno-mathematical literacy and self-efficacy, while also highlighting the importance of  thoughtful, balanced integration to ensure sustainable and ethically grounded innovations in t eacher education. Teacher education programs should  aim to strike a balance between leveraging the advantages of AI and addressing its challenges. By fostering both technical proficiency and ethical awareness, teacher educatio n can continue to innovate while responding to the complex needs of modern classrooms. This balanced approach ensures that future educators are prepared to integ rate AI effectively while maintaining the human elements that are essential to successful teaching.
