# Bloom’s Waterfall

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster, a conceptual contribution, reframes Bloom’s Taxonomy’s pyramid model, proposing a waterfall frame where creation is the generative source. Higher-order thinking cascades downward, naturally engaging lower-order skills. Grounded in constructionist learning theory, this model suggests learning starts with complex creation, not remembering or even understanding. It challenges traditional instructional design sequencing and offers a new perspective for designing rich learning environments that better reflect how humans naturally develop deep, contextualized enactive knowledge and skill. The problem with pyramids Bloom's Taxonomy has been a foundational framework for educational design since its introduction in 1956 (Bloom, 1956) and subsequent revision in 2001 (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001). The traditional pyramid representation creates persistent misconceptions about how learning unfolds in authentic contexts. The pyramid suggests a hierarchical climb from simple recall to complex creation, implying that learners must master lower-order skills before attempting higher-order thinking. This bottom-up interpretation is at odds with established learning sciences principles that view knowledge as enacted and learning as a situated, participatory, and tool-mediated process (Fischer et al., 2018; Nasir et al., 2020; Sawyer, 2022). Several scholars have critiqued this pyramidal structure (Crawford & Smith, 2015; Murtonen et al., 2017; Wineburg & Schneider, 2010), arguing that it misrepresents the dynamic and interconnected nature of cognitive development, yet none have proposed a learning-theory-grounded alternative. When learning designers treat the taxonomy as something to climb, they often design learning experiences that isolate knowledge and skills from meaningful contexts. They assume that learners must spend considerable time memorizing facts and practicing decontextualized procedures before being allowed to engage with complex, authentic problems. This is in tension with what learning sciences research suggests about the nature of learning (Donaldson & Allen-Handy, 2019, 2023; Schank, 2016). The waterfall metaphor captures how creation flows through evaluation and analysis, gathering force 2626 as it activates and develops remembering, understanding, and applying along the way. Water flowing down a waterfall gains momentum and power as it descends to the pool at the bottom, just as learners engaged in creative and analytical work naturally develop knowledge and skills. The waterfall model challenges conventional instructional sequencing by suggesting that learners should begin with complex, generative tasks rather than working their way up from basic recall. When students create artifacts, evaluate designs, or analyze systems, they must necessarily remember information, develop understanding, and apply concepts. These lower-order skills emerge organically within meaningful contexts rather than requiring separate instructional time. This reframing is grounded in constructionist learning theory (Kafai, 2006; Papert & Harel, 1991), which argues that deep learning occurs when people construct external artifacts that serve as tools for thinking. Constructionism naturally emphasizes the Create level of the taxonomy, positioning making and building as primary learning activities rather than culminating achievements. The theory suggests that learners develop their most robust and transferable knowledge through the process of designing, constructing, and refining tangible or digital objects. Implications for learning design The waterfall model carries significant implications for how researchers and practitioners design learning environments. Rather than sequencing instruction from simple to complex, designers should create rich problem spaces that invite learners to engage i mmediately with high-level cognitive work. This approach does not mean throwing students into deep water without support. Instead, it means structuring activities so that scaffolding and guidance help learners succeed at complex tasks while developing enactive knowledge (Newen et al., 2018) in context. Project-based learning exemplifies this approach. When students tackle authentic problems that matter beyond the classroom, they must create solutions, evaluate alternatives, and analyze complex systems. The project naturally integrates all levels of the taxonomy, with creation serving as the driving force that pulls other cognitive skills into play. This also clarifies the mechanism behind knowledge building communities (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2022), where the collective goal of creating new ideas naturally requires members to remember, apply, and synthesize information in service of that higher-order goal. In these environments, students collectively work in generative modalities to advance community knowledge through sustained inquiry. They create theories, evaluate evidence, analyze explanations, and synthesize ideas. The community's shared epistemic goals situate lower-order cognitive work within the higher-order work of knowledge creation. Students remember information because they need it for their theories, not because remembering was specified as a learning objective. The model also suggests reconsidering how we write learning objectives. Traditional instructional design often generates separate objectives for each taxonomy level, treating them as discrete competencies and often requiring objectives targeting the bottom two levels before addressing higher levels. The waterfall model advises writing learning objectives around high-level outcomes. Achieving these complex outcomes will inherently require the development of supporting skills. An objective might specify that students will design and evaluate a solution to an engineering challenge, with the understanding that this work requires applying concepts, understanding principles, and remembering relevant information. Challenges and considerations Implementing waterfall-based design requires significant shifts in educational practice. Assessment systems built around recall and comprehension must evolve toward complex and holistic enactment or performance-based evaluation at the individual level, or collective evaluation at the activity system level (Engeström, 2022). Learning facilitators need professional learning opportunities to develop facilitation skills for supporting students engaged in open-ended creative work. Institutional structures like pacing guides and standardized curricula may need redesign to allow time for sustained, complex projects. The model also requires careful attention to scaffolding and support structures. Starting with creation does not mean removing guidance or structure. Research on productive failure (Kapur, 2008) demonstrates that struggling with complex problems before receiving instruction can enhance learning, but this requires thoughtful design of the problem space and strategic timing of support. Learners need access to resources, models, and feedback that help them succeed at challenging work while developing expertise. The learning sciences community has long emphasized authentic activity (Papert & Harel, 1991), legitimate peripheral participation (Lave & Wenger, 1991), and learning through designing (Kolodner et al., 2003). The waterfall model of Bloom's Taxonomy offers a way to connect these theoretical commitments to a widely recognized framework. Generative artificial intelligence (AI) has entered educational spaces at an unprecedented pace (Kasneci et al., 2023). These technologies hold considerable promise for providing personalized tutoring, explaining difficult concepts in multiple ways, and offering immediate feedback on student work, yet they have also sparked concerns about academic integrity, overreliance, and cognitive offloading —where students transfer cognitive effort to external tools rather thandeveloping their own problem-solving skills (Giannakos et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2024; Ng et al., 2025). What underlies these mixed reactions is, in part, a fundamental gap in our understanding of how students actually use generative AI tools in their day-to-day learning. What makes this issue even more complex is that students do not approach learning technologies as a homogeneous group. Decades of educational research have shown that students vary considerably in their academic motivation, and these differences shape how they engage with learning opportunities (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002; Wigfield & Eccles, 2000). According to situated expectancy-value theory, students’ motivation stems from two key beliefs: their self-concept about own competence in a domain and their subjec tive values regarding that domain (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). These motivational beliefs are not static traits but are situated within specific contexts and can vary across different subjects and situations. The current study addresses this gap by examining how students with different motivational profiles use generative AI tools in their learning. By connecting motivational theory to emerging patterns of AI use, we aim to provide a more nuanced understanding of student experiences that can inform both educational practice and the design of AI-supported learning environments.

## Participants

In collaboration with the Ministry of Education in Chihuahua (the second-largest state in Mexico) and public high schools across the state, we conducted an anonymous online survey with 7,739 high school students in their final two years (61.5% female). To ensure data quality and relevance, we excluded responses from students who failed attention checks or indicated no prior experience with generative AI tools, resulting in a final analytical sample of 6,793 students.

## Measures

Student motivation was assessed through two key constructs derived from situated expectancy-value theory (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020): self-concept (beliefs about one’s own abilities) and perceived subject value (beliefs about the importance and enjoyment of a subject). Self-concept was measured using a 5-item survey, with each item rated on a scale ranging from 1 (not good at all) to 7 (extremely good), while perceived subject value was assessed using a 3-item survey, with each item rated on a scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 7 (very much). Students reported their self-concept and perceived subject value across two subject domains—math and writing — under scenarios with and without generative AI availability. AI usage patterns were assessed separately for each subject domain using a Likert scale from 1 (never) to 5 (almost always). For math, students reported their frequency of using AI for formula/background retrieval, direct answer copying, problem interpretation, step-by-step guides, solution verification, and practice problem 2629 generation. For writing, they reported their frequency of using AI for idea brainstorming, outline production, supporting evidence, language/grammar improvement, writing feedback, and requirement adaptation. All instruments were administered in Spanish and subsequently translated into English for analysis. We conducted K-means clustering analysis based on students’ self-concept and perceived subject value, performing separate analyses for the math and writing domains. The optimal number of clusters was determined through convergent evidence from three statistical criteria: the elbow method, silhouette analysis, and the gap statistic. All three methods consistently indicated that a three-cluster solution provided the most meaningful and interpretable partitioning of the data across both domains. Following cluster identification, we conducted pairwise comparisons to examine differences in each specific way of AI usage across the three motivational profiles. Three motivational profiles The clustering analysis revealed three distinct motivational profiles that emerged consistently across both math and writing domains. The largest profile was characterized as Aspirational, representing students who highly valued the subject despite having low confidence in their own abilities. The second profile, Confident, represented the most academically engaged students who combined strong confidence in their abilities with high appreciation for the subject’s value. The third profile, characterized as Disengaged, represented students who demonstrated both low confidence in their abilities and low appreciation for the subject’s value. AI usage pattern by motivational profiles In math, the three motivational profiles demonstrated distinct approaches to AI usage. The Aspirational group (n = 2,809) used AI primarily as a compensatory tutor. These students reported the highest frequency of using AI for step-by-step problem guides (M = 2.81) and for interpreting or rephrasing complex problems (M = 2.59). The Confident group (n = 2,207) exhibited selective usage consistent with treating AI as a partner for refinement and exploration. They were less likely to rely on AI for fundamental tasks such as formula retrieval (M = 2.50) and step-by-step guides (M = 2.60) compared to the Aspirational group (M = 2.64, p <.001; M  = 2.81, p  <.001). Additionally, they showed distinctively higher usage of AI for generating practice problems (M = 2.48) compared to Disengaged students (M = 2.32, p <.001). The Disengaged group (n = 1,777) displayed a concerning pattern of shortcut-seeking behavior. While they used AI for formula retrieval (M  = 2.65) and step-by-step guides (M = 2.80) at rates similar to Aspirational students, they reported the highest frequency of directly copying answers (M = 2.69), significantly exceeding both Aspirational (M = 2.53, p <.001) and Confident students (M = 2.18, p <.001).

In writing, analogous but distinct patterns emerged across the three motivational profiles. The Aspirational group (n = 3,191) again demonstrated a compensatory tutoring pattern. They were most likely to use AI for brainstorming ideas (M = 2.83) and producing outlines (M = 2.72). The Confident group (n = 1,915) used AI primarily as a partner for refinement and quality assurance. They reported the highest frequency of using AI for language and grammar improvement (M  = 2.79), as well as for seeking writing feedback (M = 2.67). The Disengaged group (n = 1,687) reported the lowest overall AI usage across most writing tasks. Our results showed that students with different motivational profiles used AI in different ways, and these usage patterns are domain-specific. These findings challenge one-size-fits-all approaches to AI integration and advocate for more targeted, motivationally-informed interventions that recognize students’ characteristics. Eccles, J.
