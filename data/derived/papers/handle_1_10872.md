# Exploring the Interplay between Criteria and Constraints in Authentic Epistemic Practices

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Engineering design has been widely implemented in K-12 curriculum in combination with numerous pedagogical tools to foster future engineers. To foster epistemic agentic learners, understanding how learner negotiate between criteria and constraints in authentic epistemic practices is important. Preliminary findings showcased three different patterns of how learners undergo engineering design processes. Practical implications will be offered at the presentation.

## Introduction

To date, the shortage of engineering labor force has constrained the economic growth in the United States. Much effort has thus been invested in fostering future engineers by incorporating engineering desig n in the current K-12 curriculum. Engineering design is expected to no t only equip students with knowledge about engineer ing concepts but also the capabilities of solving authe ntic engineering problems (C unningham & Kelly, 201 7). Engineering problems are usually ill-structured, without an exclusive answer but usually refined solutions in line with multiple criteria (Jonassen, Strobel, & Lee, 2006). In addition, solutions to engineering problems often need to abide by some non-engineering constraints, such as environmental or cultural constraints (Jonassen et al., 2006). Students need to make a trade-off between criteria and constraints to solve engin eering problems (Kelly & Cunningham, 2019), but the interplay between criteria and constraints is underexplored. To fill the gap, we aimed to explore the interplay between constraints and criteria in authentic epistemic practices.

## Participant and context

A total of 83 seven-grade students from four science classes at a public middle school participated in the study. Participants were taught by a male teacher  who attended workshops and used course materials develo ped by our team. The research team took the position of participant-observer (Spradley, 1980). The overarching project was to “Solarize Your Schoo l” which required participants to create a solar panel array design and present it to the school board. Th e criteria for this engineering design project are twofold

1) Solar offset: The system should produce as much as electricity to offset the annual energy consumed  in the

school; and 2) Return on Investment: The payback pe riod of the project should be as short as possible.  The constraints involved 1) budget constraints; and 2) environmental constraints (e.g., the landscape of the school). The project lasted for five days, 50 minutes per day. Each student worked independently  on a laptop. The first day featured a project overview with tutorials and guided exploration on the platform, Aladdin (https://intofuture.org/aladdin.html). On the second day, students were guided to apply science concepts to solve problems. Specifically, students were given an init ial design package including a house, two trees, and a single solar panel on the yard.  The background story for this initial package was that the homeowner planned to use this solar panel to generate power for outdoor lighting, with an annual energy output of 100 kWh. The initial task was to help the homeowner increase the annual energy output of the panel to 800 kWh by modifying the properties of the solar panel and changing how it was positioned and installed. In Day Three, students we re introduced to in-depth knowledge about solving engineering problems.  In the design task, they addressed the need from a  client who wanted to build an energy-efficient colonial house. The house should p roduce renewable power that can offset the energy it consumes over the course of a year. In addition, students’ design should meet spe cific constraints, such as a budget less than $220,000, area 120-160 square meters, and height 8-10 meters. The last two days (Day 4 and Day 5)  featured the final design task customized to their own context-solarize your school. The design background was set as a competition organized by the school board as the board wanted to replace non-sustainable fossil fuels with solar energy to power  the school. Participants were asked to present a solar array design that could provide eno ugh electricity to offset the school's annual energ y and had a payback period of fewer than ten years. A three-dimensional simulation of the students' school  landscape was provided. Students were advised only to install solar panels on the building roof or in the parking lot areas, abiding by the constraints of a real-life condition.

## Data collection

We collected data from 57 students who provided parental consent forms and personal assent forms. The platform logged processes of users’ actions in the design task of solarizing their school. A total of 79 types of actions were logged, such as rotating cameras, adding solar panels, setting cell efficiency for solar panels, removing walls, and running annual energy analysis. The platform also logged the sequences of actions and specific change s when performing specific actions, such as showing the detail about which directions cameras were rotated in the action of rotating cameras. We only recorded 40 students’ log data to examine their intera ction patterns. Missing data resulted from students’ absence in certain sessions, technical issues, or students’ accidental deletion of log data.

## Data analysis

We followed Howard and colleagues’ (2008) framework  to classify the 79 types of actions into five cate gories: observation, formulation, revision, redesign, and analysis. These five categories of actions represent critical stages of engineering design, including exploring, optimiz ing, and evaluating designs. We then used d3.js por tal (https://d3js.org/) to visualize patterns of interactions with arc di agrams. Then we discussed and compared patterns of interactions in weekly meetings. In par ticular, we examined students’ final artifacts when comparing patterns of interactions to understand students’ design preferences and strategies. The emerging themes from this phase of analysis are described in the following section.

## Preliminary findings

Based on the analysis of patterns of interactions, we found three engineering design patterns: criteria-oriented design, practice-oriented design, and lacking design strategies. The three de sign patterns show that students had various strategies when constructed design solution s with practical constraints: sixteen students had goals of increasing the energy output and reducing payback p eriod (criteria-oriented design), five students paid close attention to design constraints (practice-oriented design), while sixteen students did not have clear strategies  but applying trial and error strategies for problem solving (lacking design strategies).

## Discussion and implications

Our preliminary findings presented significant impl ications on how seventh graders negotiate between criteria and constraints. The findings illustrated three dif ferent profiles in terms of how learners acted upon  the interplay between criteria and constraints in an authentic ep istemic practice, including criteria-oriented design, practiceoriented design, and lacking design strategies. For the participan ts who followed criteria-oriented design, they carefully designed the solar panel plan in accordance with the criteria-1) solar offset and 2) return on investment but overlooked the existing constraints such as the size of the school campus and some areas that we cannot place the solar panel (e.g., the area occupied). Accordin gly, their interaction patterns in Aladdin features iterations of movements among observation, formulation, and revis ion. In contrast, those who followed a practice-oriented design pattern prioritized the considerations of practical constraints by iteratively undergoing movements among observation, formulation, revision, and analysis to  reach a trade-off between criteria and constraints in their proposed solutions. We also speculate that the analysis action in engineering design plays an important role in supporting learner effort to balance criteria and c onstraints in such an authentic epistemic practice. Given the current preliminary findings, we cannot make conclu sive argument on the generalizability of those three profiles or what factors affect how seventh-grade students proceeded with problem solving. Future research may recruit a larger sample of students with a larger volume of time-logged data about students’ actions in the platform.
