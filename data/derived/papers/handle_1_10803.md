# Challenging the Logic of Education Policy Design: A Complex Systems Study of Afterschool Continuous Quality Improvement

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
California created a policy requiring grantee organizations responsible for the state’s more than 4,500 publicly funded afterschool programs to implement continuous quality improvement (CQI) focused on quality standards for expanded learning developed in support of CQI. We conducted case studies of grantee organizations and afterschool sites across California to investigate how the CQI policy has been interpreted and implemented. Preliminary findings show that CQI planning, practice, and knowledge are shaped by the education system’s hierarchical structures and that social dynamics and organizational cultures shape CQI interpretations and implementation in ways that counteract policy designs. We use complex systems concepts to analyze and explain patterns in CQI policy interpretations and implementation. We contrast common education policy assumptions with the complexities in education systems and address the role of complex systems science in education policy research.

## Introduction

Education policies, like most public policies, are often designed, authorized, and financed by actors operating far from the context in which the policy is meant to be  implemented. Policy compliance is often enforced t hrough incentives or negative consequences or some combination of the two (National Research Council, 2011). We investigated the implementation of California’s 2015 continuous quality improvement (CQI) policy for the state’s more than 4,500 publicly funded aft erschool programs. The policy was based on  state legislation passed in 2014 that requires the organizations rece iving federal and state grants to operate afterscho ol programs (grantees) to develop and implement CQI plans for e ach individual afterschool site (S.B. 1221, 2014). We conducted case studies of 11 afterschool sites and their grantees to investigate how actors at different levels of the system made sense of and implemented the CQI policy. We use sensemaking theory (Weick, 2009) and complex adaptive systems theory (Holland, 1995) to analyze our data and explain patterns in our findings. Analysis is still ongoing, and this paper describes initial findings.

## Conceptual framework

We drew from two theoretical traditions in this study. We used s ensemaking theory (Weick, 2009) to help us understand how afterschool actors interpreted and acted on CQI ide as and practices. And, recognizing that organizational contexts in which sensemaking occurs  are fundamentally complex systems (Stacey, 1996), we connected sensemaking to complex adaptive systems (CAS) concepts (Holland, 1995). Sensemaking is a process in which people in an organization make meaning of and respond to changes in their organizational environment (Weick, 2009). It is also a continuous process in which people make sense of their experiences and the world (Klein et al, 2006). People engage in sensemaking both individually and collectively when something that needs explanation occurs, in an ongoing process of meaning-making that both responds to and influences the environment in which people operate (Turner et al, 2023). Complex adaptive systems are made up of agents (e.g., people) that process information about their environment through interactions with the environme nt and with other agents in it, they adapt, changing their behaviors (Holland, 199 5). As agents interact  with each other and the environment, they self-organize in ways that lead tonew structures with new, emergent properties (de Haan, 2006). The se emergent structures (e.g., language, social practices, sociocultural concepts) in turn shape and constrain the behaviors of individual agents, which called downward causation (Sawyer, 2005).

In connecting sensemaking theory to CAS theory, we conceptualized people as adaptive agents and their sensemaking as adaptative behavior. Sensemaking processes are rooted in social interactions and negotiations (Coburn, 2001), which we conceptualized as self-organizing behaviors within a CAS. The resources people draw from include social resources (Allen & Heredia, 2021), which correspond to the critical role of social networks in CAS (Benham-Hutchins & Clancy, 2010); as well as cultural resou rces (Weick, 2009), which correspond  to emergent structures (Sawyer, 2005). Sensemaking leads tonew or changed ideas and practices (Coburn, 2001), which also constitute  emergent structures  that in turn become part of the organizational environment that continually exert influence on people people’s thoughts, behavi ors, and sensemaking practices (Turner et al, 2023). In other words, people adapt to the social structur es that shape and constrain their behaviors, while simultaneously reproducing and changing those struc tures (de Haan, 2006), an interdependency that’s al so characteristic of the sensemaking process (Cristofaro, 2022). C onnecting these two theoretical frameworks helps generate explanations for CQI policy implementation that speak to both individual and organizational factors as well as the dynamics between them. In our study, we focused particularly on the CAS features of adaptat ion (sensemaking as social adaptation), emergent st ructures (social structures and cultural concepts), and social networks (sources of meaning, information, and resources) to explain findings about CQI implementation.

## Research context

The analysis described in this paper is part of a 5-year study investigating the system in which the CQI policy was implemented and how people at different system levels interpreted and implemented the policy. The study included, in addition to the case studies we report  on here, interviews with system actors at local, r egional, and state levels, and two statewide surveys about CQI implementation (Lundh, et al, in press). The afterschool CQI policy originated with legislat ion passed in 2014 that requires publicly funded afterschool sites and their grantees  (local education agencies and school districts that  receive state and federal grants to run afterschool programs) to develop and implement CQI plans for each afterschool site. Sites, working with their grantees, are free to choose which of the state’s 12 Quality Standards for Expanded Learning (California Afterschool Network, 2014) to focus on and what types of assessments to use to optimize improvement based on local contexts. CQI processes engage system actors in focusing on a specific problem of practice through a series of iterative cycles: brainstorming change ideas, testing the changes, gathering data about the effects of the changes, and studying the potential influence of these changes on outcomes of interest (Bryk et al., 2015).

## Methods

Process We conducted case studies (Yin, 2017) to investigate how CQI was interpreted and enacted at 11 afterschool sites across California. To select sites, we took a critical case sampling approach (Flyvbjerg, 2006) to identify grantees with a strong record of CQI implementation with the help of consultants with extensive knowledge of California’s programs and sites. We identified six grantees, two sites for each of five grantees, and one site for one grantee, for a total of 11 sites. Sites were in rural, small-town, suburban, and urban environments  across the state.  We interviewed staff in each grantee organization. At each site, we interviewed the site coordinator, fro ntline staff, and, as needed, other staff with knowledge of the site’s CQI practices. We interviewed a total of 50 adults. Analysis The study team coded interview data both deductively, to identify constructs related to CQI policy interpretations and implementation (e.g., CQI-related terms, concepts, practices, organizational contexts, information sources, supports, constraints, resources), and inductively, to identify sensemaking components (e.g., conflicts, social and cultural resources, mental models, connections) (Saldaña, 2016). To explain CQI implementation patterns, we are analyzing people’s sensemaking narratives to infer the emergent structures interviewees adapted to, as well those that informed their sensemaking processes. We are a lso using qualitative network analysis (QNA) to map  the social networks related to interviewees’ sensemaking processes (Ahrens, 2018).

## Findings

Preliminary findings suggest three CQI implementation themes: 1) Grantees design CQI plans with minimal input from the ir sites; 2) CQI implementations emphasize standardized, compliance-oriented procedures over local customization; and 3) CQI knowledge  primarily resides  with grantees (i.e., grant managers and program directors), while site coordinators and site staff  have minimal knowledge. These themes correlate with findings from our systems interviews and statewide surveys (Lundh et al, in press). Grantee control of CQI planning Grant managers provided rich descriptions of their C QI plans for their sites, demonstrating relatively broad CQI knowledge, which they learned in trainings and through connections to other regional and state organizations. All but one grant manager said CQI was valuable and imp ortant. All six grantee organizations developed CQI  plans for all their sites to use, but mostly without input or participation from site coordinators or site staff. CQI plans and enactment The CQI plans grantees developed were largely not enacted according to policy descriptions or guidance. Grantees developed standardized plans and templates for site s. Sites could select which expanded learning standards to focus on, but received little support for translating standards into site-relevant improvement goals. Most s ite coordinators used data collected by the grantee or collected dat a directed by the grantee. But beyond  brief CQI trainings, most had no support or were fully empowered to take ownership of CQI, saying they followed directions from the grantee. Interviews with site coordinators and site staff suggested that CQI-specific practices, mindsets, and concepts were, at best, marginally present in everyday practice. Some saw CQI as a matter of compliance and unaligned with the ethos of their youth development work. One site coordinator said: “To me, it means a checkoff list that I need to do for the state” (Site coordinator, April 2023). CQI knowledge and awareness CQI knowledge and awareness tend ed to be concentrated at the grantee or program level s. For example, at one site, the grant manager provided a detailed description of their sites’ CQI plans, saying, “The site should have at least these monthly meetings with their staff to ta lk about the specific quality indicator that we’re going to work on that month” (Grant manager, June 2023). In contrast, a site coordinator for one of the grantee’s sites said they were not familiar with CQI or ideas of quality impr ovement and was only able to refer to one of the assessment tools used in the CQI process: “I've never heard of that, CQI… I know we have [Assessment Tool]… I feel like it's mostly just admin stuff (Site coordinator, April 2023). Complex adaptive system interpretations In our ongoing analysis, we have begun to generate explanations focusing on three CAS features: adaptation, emergent structures, and networks. Early insights from system actors’ sensemaking narratives suggest that the different meanings interviewees attributed to CQI r eflected adaptive responses contingent on the struc tures, practices, and concepts circumscribing their work. For example, one site coordinator was constantly ad apting to the demands and challenges of working with youth, managing staff, and addressing ongoing resource challenges. Youth development values and ideas informed her wor k and she perceived CQI as irrelevant. Her grant manager greatly valued CQI, but in practice created and sup ported CQI as  a standardized compliance process. Very different emergent structures characterized grantee  organizations and afterschool sites. They each had  their own social and cultural structures, emergent from people’s self-organizing behavior within their environment, i.e., how they interacted with each other and with resources, goals, and idea s unique to their organization’s position in the overall system. Finally, the networks people were part of appeared to significantly influence  knowledge of CQI at different levels in the system. For example, one grant manager, highly knowledgeable of CQI, received CQI support and information via formal and informal con nections to local, regional and state organizations, and participated CQI-related conversations and meetings with colleagues at different levels. In contrast, a site coordinator at one of th e grant manager’s sites was only connected to the program director in the grantee office, from whom she received instructions and information for CQI implementation. She was not privy to CQI-related information and resources available in the larger s ystem. Staff in the grantee office did, because the ir networks were more extensive as a function of their organizational context.

## Significance

The implementation logic of most e ducation policies is characterized by “hard systems” ideas, such as positivist epistemology, prediction, control, and linear thinking (Banathy & Jenlink, 2003). At the same time, scholars have long found that education policies are not implemen ted as intended because of the gap between policyma kers’ assumptions and the lived reality of policy impleme nters (McDonald, 20 20). Organizational theories, such as loose coupling,  have provided some explanations for unrealized policy goals  (Fusarelli, 2002). More recently, insights from complex systems science have begun to  shed more light on  the study of education systems and policies (Honig, 2006).

Education systems are fundamentally social systems t hat are neither linear nor mechanistic (Mason, 2008). Social systems are instead characterized by dynamic interactions, non-linearity, feedback loops, emergent phenomena, and adaptive behaviors (Holland, 1995).  Our study’s demonstration of how complex system dynamics shape education policy implementation contributes both findings and methodological innovations to the study of education policy and education systems.  Recognizing the complexity of social organizations cautions against policy implementation explanations that attribute causes of policy success or failure merely to “properties” of people (e.g., beliefs, skills, “buy-in”) or organizations (e.g., capacity, culture, leadership).
