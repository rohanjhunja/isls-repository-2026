# Semi-Parametric Non-Linear Modeling of Student-Centered Learning Challenges

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Student-centered learning (SCL) emphasizes learners’ autonomy, engagement, and responsibility for constructing knowledge. Yet when external supports such as scaffolding, guidance, and authentic audiences are limited, learners may encounter heightened unce rtainty and strain. This study uses Electrodermal Activity (EDA) as a real-time indicator of physiological arousal associated with task engagement in unsupported SCL-like tasks and applies semi-parametric spline modeling to characterize temporal dynamics i n the signal. Results indicate recurring arousal patterns that, in relation to task context and theory, are interpreted as possible episodes of heightened challenge, brief recovery, and later stabilization. By relating these patterns to the OLSit (Own it, Learn it, Share it) design framework, the paper highlights the importance of balancing autonomy with timely support. The study contributes both a methodological approach for modeling EDA time series and design hypotheses for supporting productive affective engagement.

Student-centered learning (SCL) empowers learners to take responsibility for their own learning processes (Hannafin et al., 2014). Rather than passively receiving information, students are expected to generate learning opportunities, set goals, and reconstruct knowledge dynamically within open-ended learning environments (Harel & Papert, 1991). Prior research has shown that SCL can enhance disciplinary understanding, foster deeper engagement, and develop critical thinking skills (Brush & Saye, 2000; Fredricks et al., 2004). Nevertheless, these benefits often depend on how well students can navigate the uncertainties of less structured settings, as well as the kinds of supports provided by teachers and the broader environment. Central to SCL is a shift in responsibility: learners become the owners of their decisions, goals, and strategies, while instructors adopt more facilitative roles. In theory, this shift promotes autonomy, self-regulation, and agency (Zimmerman, 2002). However, in practice, students often struggle to adapt to these expectations. For example, without clear guidance, scaffolding, or feedback, they may experience confusion and heightened stress when confronted with open-ended tasks. This challenge reflects what  Lee and Hannafin (2016) describe as the three key constructs of engagement in SCL: autonomy, scaffolding, and authentic audiences. Autonomy fosters motivation and ownership but can overwhelm students when exercised in isolation. Work on minimally guided instruction and cognitive load similarly suggests that open-ended learning without timely support can overburden learners when task demands are high or solution paths are ambiguous (Kirschner et al., 2006; Sweller et al., 2011). In minimally scaffolded SCL, instructors therefore lack reliable, real-time indicators of when autonomy tips into overload. Self-reports are retrospective and biased, and observations often miss moment-to-moment shifts in affective arousal.

To investigate these affective dynamics, this study employs physiological measures that capture students’ emotional responses in real time. Electrodermal activity (EDA), fluctuations in skin conductance driven by sympathetic activation, has been widely used as an index of arousal and stress in psychophysiology (Boucsein, 2012; Dawson et al., 2007). In this study, we treat EDA as a process-level indicator of sympathetic arousal that may accompany epistemic affect, rather than as a direct measure of discrete states such as uncertainty, anxiety, or relief. Jaber and Hammer (2016) theorize that affect is constitutive—not peripheral —of disciplinary engagement; building on this framework, we use EDA to track moment-to-moment dynamics that can be interpreted alongside task context and prior theory. Both positive and negative affects, such as satisfaction, curiosity, frustration, or doubt, are essential components of authentic inquiry. Therefore, tracing these fluctuations can help clarify how students experience unsupported SCL-like tasks.

Because EDA is sampled at high frequency, it yields a noisy time series that demands careful modeling. Pointwise or purely linear approaches miss the nonlinear, nonstationary structure of physiological signals. We therefore model EDA with natural cubic spline smoothing (a penalized NCS basis), which joins cubic pieces at knots and imposes zero second derivative at the boundaries to prevent unrealistic end behavior. Rather than exact interpolation, smoothing attenuates sensor noise while preserving salient peaks and recoveries, producing a 707 continuous trajectory of arousal over time. From this representation we derive interpretable features, event-aligned peaks/onsets, slopes and curvature, and duration/area, that quantify dynamic affective engagement. These indicators support inferences about epistemic affect and how supports (e.g., scaffolds, authentic audiences) shape engagement in SCL.

The purpose of this study, therefore, is twofold: methodologically, it demonstrates how non-linear modeling can provide nuanced insights into affective engagement from physiological data; pedagogically, it illuminates the challenges students face when navi gating unsupported SCL. Specifically, our research is guided by two questions: What do affective patterns look like in unsupported SCL-like tasks without scaffolding? How do affective engagement dynamics vary across two unsupported SCL-like tasks with different challenge profiles?

By integrating EDA measurement with spline-based analysis, we aim to contribute both methodological tools for educational research and design insights for improving SCL. Our findings are intended as evidence from controlled, unsupported SCL-like tasks rather thandirect claims about all classroom settings. Student-centered learning framework Student-centered learning (SCL) shifts the roles of both students and instructors, transforming the learning environment from one dominated by the delivery of content to one in which students actively construct knowledge (Glasgow, 1997). Instead of being passive recipients of information, students are expected to generate questions, make decisions about their own learning goals, and take responsibility for their progress. Instructors, in turn, must learn to facilitate rather thandictate, guiding students toward resources and strategies without assuming complete control over the process (Brush & Saye, 2000). This pedagogical shift reflects a broader trend in education toward constructivist and inquiry-based models, which emphasize autonomy, motivation, and authentic engagement. Lee and Hannafin (2016) identify three essential elements for effective SCL environments: autonomy, scaffolding, and authentic audiences. Autonomy, as conceptualized by Deci and Ryan’s (2000) self-determination theory, refers to the learner’s ability to make choices aligned with their own values and interests. Autonomy is not the same as independence; rather, it is the endorsement of one’s actions as self-directed. In educational contexts, autonomy supports intrinsic motivation, fostering satisfaction, persistence, and overall wellbeing. However, autonomy without guidance can overwhelm learners, leading to anxiety or disengagement, particularly when tasks are ambiguous or demanding.

Scaffolding provides the structured support necessary for learners to accomplish tasks they could not complete independently. Rooted in Vygotsky’s zone of proximal development and articulated by Wood, Bruner, and Ross (1976), scaffolding allows instructors to gradually transfer responsibility to students as their competence grows. This may take the form of prompts, modeling, feedback, or structured activities. Effective scaffolding not only aids cognitive development but also supports affective engagement, helping learners build confidence in navigating complex problems. Conversely, the absence of scaffolding often results in confusion, frustration, or disengagement (Vygotsky, 1978). In this sense, the present study does not treat autonomy and support as opposites; rather, it examines what learners’ arousal dynamics look like when support is intentionally minimized during open-ended challenge.

Natural cubic splines for time-series analysis Alongside these theoretical considerations, the methodological dimension of this study relies on NCS for analyzing physiological time-series data. Splines are piecewise polynomial functions that interpolate smooth curves through a sequence of data points, or knots, while minimizing abrupt changes in slope (de Boor, 2001; Hastie et al., 2009). Splines are widely used in computer graphics, statistical modeling, engineering, and increasingly in educational research, where researchers seek to represent non-linear processes such as learning progress or physiological responses.

Cubic splines, a common variant, model each interval between knots with a cubic polynomial. They are particularly useful because they ensure continuity not only of the function itself but also of its first and second derivatives (Eilers & Marx, 1996). This  guarantees that the interpolated curve is smooth and avoids the jagged transitions that occur with simpler interpolation methods. Such continuity is critical when representing phenomena like EDA signals, which may fluctuate rapidly but do so in patterned ways that require nuanced modeling. NCS impose an additional boundary condition: the second derivative at the endpoints of the interval is constrained to equal zero (Ramsay & Silverman, 2005). This condition prevents the spline from exhibiting artificial curvature or oscillation at the bound aries, thereby producing a “flatter” and more stable representation 708 near the edges of the dataset. For time-series analysis, this feature is especially advantageous, since physiological processes like skin conductance are not expected to produce extreme inflections at the beginning or end of a measurement window.

In the context of educational research, NCS provide a methodological advantage for modeling students’ affective dynamics. Unlike linear or simple polynomial regressions, splines can capture subtle shifts in engagement and stress that unfold across time. For example, a student’s EDA signal during problem-solving may show gradual increases in arousal, sudden peaks of frustration, and eventual stabilization as they adapt to the task. By applying spline interpolation, researchers can visualize these trajectories with greater fidelity, identifying periods of heightened challenge or relief.

Moreover, spline modeling integrates well with probabilistic approaches such as Bayesian analysis, which allows for estimation of uncertainty and variability across individuals. This is particularly relevant for education, where learners’ responses to SCL environments are heterogeneous. By smoothing noisy physiological signals while preserving their non-linear structure, NCS enable researchers to analyze affective data in a way that aligns with the complex, dynamic nature of learning itself.

## Participants and context

The study recruited twenty undergraduate students from a Midwestern U.S. university. Participants were drawn from diverse majors to reduce bias associated with disciplinary expertise and were recruited through email announcements, course flyers, and voluntary sign-up sheets. All participants reported no prior experience with physiological measurement devices or advanced training in the tasks selected, which ensured a relatively comparable baseline in task familiarity. Informed consent was obtained prior to participation, following Institutional Review Board (IRB) protocols, and participants were compensated with a $20 gift card. Each participant completed the experiment individually in a controlled laboratory environment designed to minimize external distractions. The laboratory setting ensured consistency across sessions while isolating participants from peer or instructor support, thereby simulating unsupported SCL-like conditions under controlled constraints.

## Devices and data collection

Electrodermal Activity (EDA) was measured using the Empatica E4 wristband, a widely adopted wearable physiological sensor. The E4 records skin conductance at a frequency of 4 Hz, capturing subtle fluctuations in sweat gland activity driven by the sympathetic nervous system. Increases in skin conductance are associated with heightened physiological arousal during task engagement; accordingly, EDA was treated here as an index of sympathetic arousal rather than a one-to-one proxy for specific epistemic states (Cain & Lee, 2016). In addition to skin conductance, the E4 has the capacity to measure accelerometer data and temperature; however, in this study, only EDA signals were analyzed. Participants wore the wristband on their non-dominant hand to minimize movement artifacts during task performance. Raw signals were transmitted wirelessly to a computer for storage, and quality checks were conducted to ensure stable sensor contact. Tasks Two cognitive tasks were chosen to elicit affective responses under unsupported conditions: 2-4-6 Task (Wason, 1960).  This classic experiment on confirmation bias requires participants to infer a hidden numerical rule based on feedback (Klayman & Ha, 1987). It challenges learners to test hypotheses beyond their initial assumptions and is well known for eliciting frustration, persistence, and relief (Nickerson, 1998). The task was selected because it mirrors the kind of open-ended inquiry often encountered in SCL, where learners must tolerate uncertainty and risk failure without explicit guidance. Rubik’s Cube Algorithm Task.  In this task, participants observed and attempted to replicate the Right-Hand Algorithm (R U R' U R U2 R') for solving the last layer of a Rubik’s Cube. Unlike the abstract reasoning of the 2-4-6 task, this activity required motor coordination, working memory, and repeated practice. Its inclusion ensured that the study captured affective responses across both cognitive and procedural domains. Each participant was given six opportunities to successfully execute the algorithm, providing multiple points of engagement, error, and potential frustration.

Together, these two tasks were selected to balance abstract reasoning with embodied problem-solving, ensuring a richer dataset of affective dynamics under unsupported SCL-like conditions. Procedures 709 Each experimental session lasted approximately 45-60 minutes. After signing consent forms, participants were fitted with the E4 wristband and provided with an overview of the experimental structure. The order of tasks was randomized across participants to control for sequence effects. During the 2-4-6 task, participants were presented with the sequence “2, 4, 6” and instructed to propose additional sequences that would be evaluated as either conforming or not conforming to the hidden rule. The experimenter’s responses were intentionally minimal (e.g., “That is not the rule”) to simulate unsupported inquiry. In the Rubik’s Cube task, the interviewer demonstrated the Right-Hand Algorithm once, after which participants attempted to reproduce it independently. No corrective feedback or hints were provided, even in cases of repeated failure. This absence of scaffo lding reinforced the study’s aim to observe affective responses in unsupported contexts. Between tasks, participants were given brief breaks to reduce carryover arousal. At the receiving compensation.

## Data analysis

Preprocessing EDA signals were down-sampled from 4 Hz to 1 Hz by averaging every four data points, which reduced noise and simplified temporal interpretation. Data segments containing abrupt spikes unrelated to task engagement (e.g., due to sensor adjustment or excessive movement) were identified using threshold-based filters and removed (Benedek & Kaernbach, 2010). The processed dataset provided a cleaner representation of second-by-second physiological arousal throughout task performance.

Interpreting the arousal trajectories in unsupported SCL-like tasks (RQ1) Our spline-modeled EDA trajectories suggest a recurring pattern that we interpret as heightened challenge → brief recovery → stabilization in unsupported student-centered learning-like tasks. The data directly support recurring arousal dynamics; labels such as frustration, relief, and stabilization are interpretive shorthand grounded in task context and prior theory rather thandirect physiological readouts. Early in the tasks (roughly 50-225 s), most students exhibited heightened arousal, consistent with the discomfort of hypothesis generation under uncertainty or repeated motor errors during the algorithmic task. These elevations were often followed by short troughs corresponding to partial successes or moments of clarity, before converging toward a more s table state later in the session. Crucially, stabilization was heterogeneous: for some students it reflected adaptation (sustained engagement with lower arousal), while for others it suggested disengagement (flat, unresponsive patterns after extended frustration). This cyclical profile aligns with accounts of epistemic affect as integral to inquiry; affect is not just noise but part of how learners organize activity over time (Kapur, 2008; Bjork & Bjork, 2011). Challenges and arousal dynamics across tasks (RQ2) The findings also help differentiate how engagement dynamics varied across the two tasks. Descriptively, the 24-6 task elicited spikier early arousal and slower return to baseline, whereas the Rubik’s Cube algorithm produced steadier moderate arousal and earlier stabilization. At the same time, the evidence for a between-task difference in peak frequency was weak, so claims about more or less frequent surges should be treated cautiously. In both cases, challenge functioned as a double-edged sword: it could support productive struggle and persistence for some learners yet also coincide with sustained high arousal and eventual withdrawal for others (Sweller et al., 2011). Without timely supports, frustration could accumulate, underscoring the need to dose cha llenge and time support to sustain inquiry.

Synthesizing with the SCL Engagement Triad The observed patterns sharpen the functional roles of SCL’s three design levers: autonomy, scaffolding, and authentic audiences. Autonomy invites ownership but, in isolation, can amplify uncertainty and stress, as shown by extended early-stage arousal (Kirschner et al., 2006). Scaffolding, when minimally provided, was the missing buffer against cumulative strain, helping explain prolonged elevations and irregular recovery troughs (Vygotsky, 1978; Hattie & Timperley, 2007; Nicol & Macfarlane-Dick, 2006). Aut hentic audiences, largely absent in our laboratory setting, might have transformed partial successes into socially meaningful milestones, potentially deepening recovery phases and re-energizing engagement cycles rather than letting arousal collapse into disengagement. Collectively, the results suggest the value of calibrated autonomy supported by just-in-time scaffolds and authentic purpose.

A Design Heuristic: The “Productive Struggle Window” A practical implication is the identification of a productive struggle window early in task engagement. In our data, heightened arousal clustered between ~50-225 seconds; within such windows, learners are actively testing hypotheses or refining procedures.  Rather than eliminating struggle, designs might treat struggle as a signal and offer micro-prompts, resource cues, or reflective pauses after a detected run of failed attempts —support that acknowledges effort while preserving agency (Chi & Wylie, 2014; Fi orella & Mayer, 2015). When recovery troughs fail to appear following extended peaks, it may be a cue to shift from passive facilitation to targeted, temporary scaffolding (e.g., strategy hints, constraint relaxation) to prevent affect from tipping into wi thdrawal. The damping patterns in Figures 1-2 suggest a short time horizon in which struggle is most productive: early spikes should be met with minimal, well-timed cues that help learners pivot strategies without removing ownership. This aligns with research on productive failure and desirable difficulties, which argues that wellscaffolded challenge can enhance long-term learning even if it elevates effort in the short term (Kapur, 2008; Bjork & Bjork, 2011).

Task Ecology Matters: Abstract Reasoning vs. Embodied Learning Challenges 713 Contrasting the tasks clarifies how cognitive vs. procedural demands may shape arousal. The 2-4-6 task’s abstract uncertainty was associated with spikier early arousal as learners revised hypotheses. The Rubik’s Cube task, by contrast, concentrated arousal around execution bottlenecks (e.g., mis-sequencing), generating more plateau-like trajectories with discrete peaks. This pattern suggests that different supports may be optimal: hypothesis-space expansion prompts and counter-example generation for the former; chunking, rhythm scaffolds, or slowed demonstrations for the latter. Matching scaffolds to a task’s affective signature is therefore a design hypothesis to be tested in future work rather than a settled prescription. Taken together with Figures 1-2, these task signatures imply different profiles: abstract hypothesis testing taxes intrinsic and germane load in ways that call for prompts expanding the hypothesis space, whereas embodied sequencing imposes procedural load better addressed through chunking and pacing. This interpretation is consistent with cognitive load theory, four-component instructional design, and generative learning accounts of strategy externalization and worked-example support (Sweller et al., 2011; van Merriënboer & Kirschner, 2018; Fiorella & Mayer, 2015).

## Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that qualify the interpretation and generalizability of the findings. First, individual differences in physiology (e.g., skin conductance reactivity), prior experience with inquiry tasks, tolerance for uncertainty, and unreported demographic characteristics may have contributed to variability in the EDA trajectories beyond what our models captured. Given the intensive protocol and sample size (N = 20), the study should be read as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Second, the research took place in a controlled laboratory without social interaction or classroom contingencies. While this setting helped isolate unsupported SCL-like dynamics, it also removed authentic peer collaboration, instructor discourse, and audience e ffects that typically shape engagement in real classrooms. Third, we focused on a single physiological channel (EDA). Although EDA is a sensitive index of arousal, it does not disambiguate cognitive load from emotional valence or pinpoint the sources of engagement. Finally, although participants completed a brief debriefing survey, those data were not systematically analyzed for triangulation in the present paper. Methodologically, our analytical choices also introduce constraints. We down-sampled from 4 Hz to 1 Hz and applied natural cubic splines to smooth trajectories; different smoothing parameters, knot placements, or alternative time-series models (e.g., GAMs,  Gaussian processes, state-space/HMMs, cvxEDA for tonic– phasic decomposition) could yield different emphases on peaks vs. plateaus. While the Bayesian framework afforded uncertainty quantification, we did not implement a fully hierarchical model that pools  information across participants and tasks; such a model might better characterize between-person variability. Finally, our design did not manipulate scaffolding or authentic audiences; therefore, inferences about design levers are suggestive rather than causal.

Future work should address these limitations in several ways. First, expand samples across institutions, courses, and learner profiles (e.g., novices vs. experts) to test robustness and boundary conditions. Second, move to ecologically valid classroom settings where autonomy, scaffolding, and audiences co-evolve, and include peer collaboration to examine how social dynamics modulate the frustration– relief–stabilization cycle. Third, add multimodal data–HRV for regulatory effort, pupillometry for cognitive load, behavioral logs for strategy shifts – and integrate short self-reports or micro-reflections for triangulation. Fourth, compare design conditions that systematically vary the timing and granularity of scaffolds (e.g., no scaffold, micro-prompts, worked-example snippets) and the presence of authentic audiences (e.g., peer critique, public showcases). Fifth, explore real-time analytics that update spline fits online and trigger just-in-time supports within the “productive struggle window.” Finally, develop ethical guidelines for affect-aware SCL (consent, transparency, data minimization) to ensure that measurement augments learner agency rather than surveils it. Collectively, these directions would strengthen causal claims and translate methodological advances into actionable classroom design. This study modeled second-by-second EDA with NCS and interpreted patterns in a Bayesian framework across two laboratory tasks —abstract hypothesis testing (2-4-6) and embodied execution (Rubik’s algorithm) —that functioned as unsupported SCL-like challenges.  The analyses suggest recurring arousal dynamics that can be interpreted as heightened challenge, brief recovery, and later stabilization, with notable individual and taskspecific differences. Unsupported autonomy often coincided with early, prolonged arousal, while later stabilization appeared to reflect either adaptation or disengagement. Methodologically, NCS offered a noise-robust way to visualize non-linear arousal dynamics, and the Bayesian framework made uncertainty explicit. Pedagogically, the findings generate design hypotheses about how SCL’s engagement triad might be operationalized: preserve autonomy while adding just-in-time micro-scaffolds 714 and authentic audiences that convert effort into meaningful recovery points.
