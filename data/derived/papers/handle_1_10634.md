# “I’m one of the Kids who is Perceived as Smart”: Exploring Student Experiences in a Fifth-Grade Science Classroom

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study seeks to understand children’s experiences in a fifth-grade science classroom designed to incorporate the principles of collective activity, heterogeneity, and epistemic agency. Using a semi-structured interview approach, children were asked to describe their experiences within their class community. The findings reveal that the inequitable distribution of status in the classroom affected students’ engagement with science practice as well as the risks they perceived in participating in collective learning. This study provides student perspectives on what it means to engage in the practices of science and has implications for how educators and researchers attend to student experience when designing and analyzing learning environments.

## Introduction

The field of science education is currently driven by a vision of “science-as-practice”, in which children engage in the types of activities that scientists use to construct understandings of concepts and phenomena. To enact this vision, teachers must foster opportunities for stud ents to engage with each other’s ideas  in service of collective development of scientific knowledge. While this is crucial for children’s epistemic development, it can also create challenges as teachers and students figure out how to navigate the power dynamics and unequal distribution of status that frequently pervade classroom interaction (Agarwal & Sengupta-Irving, 2019; Hand et al., 2012). Students may feel that their participation in scien ce work comes with risk, such as having their ideas  negatively evaluated by their peers and/or teacher, which can have consequences such  as disciplinary action or social marginalization (Wright et al., 2018). However, eliciting and using students’ ideas in classroom activity also provides opportunities for learners to demonstrate care for the disciplinary work of  science activity and for their peers’ learning and thinking (Krist & Suárez, 2018).

Although the field has made progress on understanding the vision of science-as-practice and developing strategies for instantiat ing it, few studies have sought to understand students’ p erspectives and experience s, specifically in relation to the ways that they feel able to exercise agency and how they experience the distribution of status in their class community. In this study, I conducted interviews with fifth-grade students (aged 10-12) to learn more about their learning experiences. By interviewing thes e children and analyzing their responses, we share student perspectives on their opportunities t o exercise epistemic agency, their ability to parti cipate in classroom activities and discussions, and what they felt was cared about in their classroom community. The paper addresses the following research questions:  In this fifth-grade classroom,  1) How d o students describe their experiences in a science class designed to emphasiz e collective activity, heterogeneity, and agency? 2) How do students use the constructs of status, risk, and care to explain their experiences?

## Conceptual framework

The co-design research team engaged in this work positions science as a collective activity, in that knowledge is constructed through social interactions and defined by what a community takes as useful and warranted (Longino, 1994). Therefore, we seek to invite students into  a process  of collective sensemaking  that is taken up in a community of practice that has shared purposes for learning. The outcomes of this collective activity rely on the heterogeneity of students’ ideas and ways of expression being elicited and highlighted during the learning process. In addition, the concept of epistemic agency is an established principle in the field that we draw from in our work. For this paper, I take up Miller et al.’s (2018) definition of epistemic agency: “students being pos itioned with, perceiving, and acting on opportunities to shape th e knowledge building work in their classroom commun ities” (p. 1058).

Researchers and educators seeking to instantiate the principles of collective activity, heterogeneit y, and epistemic agency have drawn attention to the social norms and practi ces for doing this kind of work, as well as how localized norms and practices are influenced by both societal power dynamics and the construction of status in individual classrooms (Agarwal & Sengupta-Irving, 2019; Hand et al., 2012). However, it is less understood how elementary-aged children perceive and describe the ways status is distributed in their learning environments, as well as how the construction of status impacts students’ ability to engage in collective activity or be positioned with epistemic agency. From this perspective, e ngaging in the practices of science can be laden wi th risk for children. Designs that emphasize collective activity and epis temic agency typically rel y on opportunities for students to work together to evaluate and revise ideas to explain a phenomenon. While this process is a powerful tool for student learning, it can also be interpret ed by students as risky. For example, students may feel worried that their contributions in class discussions may b e considered “wrong” or that they will be positioned by others to have lower academic and/or social status because  of  these answers.  This can be a particularly vulnerable experience for students whose identities have historically been marginalized in educational spaces (Wright et al., 2018).

One way to mitigate the risk that children experien ce in their classrooms is to consider how care is instantiated in these spaces. For this paper, I take up Antrop-González & De Jesús’s (2006) theory of critical care, which privileges interpersonal relationships betwee n students and their teachers  and values the heterogeneous experiences children bring with them to school. However, school settings typically expect students and teachers to demonstrate care for abstract ideas, which can l ead to acts of resistance or demoralization when ch ildren’s understanding of these constructs differs from that of their teachers (Antrop‐González & De Jesús, 2006). Instead, when learning environments shift to prioritize  students’ heterogeneous experiences and knowledge,  there is a potential to disrupt hegemonic structures that perp etuate an inequitable distribution of status. There is emerging work in this area to understand how care is concept ualized and enacted in schooling (Krist & Suárez, 2 018; McKinney de Royston et al., 2021), but it is still unknown how children experience and describe care i n their classrooms and how this impacts their engagement with other principles such as collective activity, heterogeneity, and epistemic agency.

## Methods

This study was conducted in a fifth-grade classroom in an urban public school in Massac husetts as part of a co-design partnership  exploring investigation and modeling in elementary science classrooms.  The class of 25 students was taught by “Ms. Shaw”, who had been a p art of the co-design team for two years. I attended classes multiple times a week during the 2021-2022 school year as an observer, thought partner, and occasional co-teacher during lessons designed to center uncertainty as a support for emergent science practice (Manz, 2018). As our codesign work unfolded, Ms. Shaw and I became increasingly curious about students’ experiences in the classroom and how these affected the ways students positioned themselves and others during science act ivity. One way we sought to understand these experiences was by inter viewing a selection of students at the end of the s chool year. For this study, I focus primarily on students’ resp onses to interview questions related to class norms. Students were asked if each norm described their experience or not to give examples to support their response. These norms were 1) Students help figure stuff out, 2) All ideas are valued, 3) It’s okay to not know something or to be unsure, and 4) Students learn from each other’s ideas.

I interviewed eight students, working with Ms. Shaw to choose students who represented a diverse range of demographics and identities. We also chose students who we suspected had a wide range of experiences in the classroom to help us determine how the construct of  “status” might be experienced by students. This wa s based on our observations of the frequency of students’ participation in class discussions and how their ideas were taken up by other students. Two students were used to tes t and revise the interview protocol, and six studen ts were interviewed for the analytic work in this study. These students’ pseudonyms are Alan, Damandi, Ethan, Henrique, Rafael, and Samantha. Analysis of the interview transcripts involved several rounds of qualitative thematic coding (Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006). First, I coded the transcripts for instances related to the constructs of agency, risk, and care. I then systematically organized students’ responses to the questions about the four norms of study. This allowed me to see where student perspectives overlapped and diverged, as well as  whose perspectives diverged from the majority, and I engaged in memo writing to  explore how student experienced differed across the interviews. I then used these themes to develop a set of conjectures about student experiences. In my final round of analysis, I found evidence that supported these conjectures and allowed me to refine them into fina l claims.

## Findings

Students had varying perceptions about how heterogeneous ideas were valued, and these perceptions affected their engagement with science practice. Students had mixed responses to the question asking  if  their class was a place where “all ideas are valued.” Typically, the students who said that this was true  were also ones that participated most frequently in class discussions. These students stated that it was valuable to hear diverse ideas because the y could then be used to co-construct a collective understanding. For example, Henrique said, “You have to share your ideas with other people. And they get to share their ideas with you. Like you can use their idea and your idea and make it like it’s both of your idea”. However, other students felt that hearing a variety of ideas was not valued in their classroom. When asked if she felt that all ideas were valued in this class, Damandi said that she wasn’t sure because students will “put people down” by saying “that their ideas are better, or that they’re doing better, or that they are better”. It seems that while other students viewed disagreem ents as indications of diverse ideas and as a vital  part of the learning process, others felt that when people disagreed with them it sent the message that their ideas (or perhaps, even their personhood) were not valuable because th ey did not align with the dominant ideas of the cla ss community.

Students who felt heterogeneity was valued also felt that their class was a place where students engaged deeply with science practice. Henrique, Samantha, Rafael, and Alan agreed that their classroom could be described by the statement “students help figure stuff out”, and they shared specific examples of times when they engaged in practices such as planning investigations and analyzing data. Damandi and Ethan, however, seemed to feel that they were not as able to engage in these practices and that their ideas were not honored by their peers and teacher. For example, Ethandescribed the class as “boring” because “we d on’t get to do anything… When I tried to do something Ms. Shaw was like, no, you can’t do that”. From hearing perspectives from students who felt heterogeneity was valued and from students who did not feel this was true, we see that students also had varying opportunities to feel that they were able to exercise epistemic agency through engagement with science practice. Students perceived an inequitable distribution of status in their classroom. When describing their experiences with heterogeneity and epistemic agency, all but one student referred to an unequal distribution of status in their class community. Students generally talked about their classmates fal ling into one of two categories: A group of students who  were active participants and authors of knowledge,  and a group of students who took on a more passive role. For example, Alan said that some students in the class “actually figure stuff out”, while others “don’t do anything in class. Th ey’re just not that into it”. Damandi categorized students in a similar way by saying, “ some people are like, the stars of the classroom, and th en other people are like Pluto”. These responses indicate that students are aware of the ways that different students are positioned by their classmates and teachers, and that they have beliefs about how these positionings affect students’ participation and learning.

Students’ perceived labels impacted the risk they felt in sharing their ideas. The categorizations the students described  above seem to impact the level of risk that they felt in sharing their ideas with their classmates and teacher. For example, Rafael said that he is comfortable sharing his ideas “because I’m one of the kids who is perceived as smart”, and he suggests that other students may not participate  because they worry that the “smart people” will judge their ideas. Ethan’s response for why he disagreed with the idea of “all ideas are valued” supports Rafael’s  suggestion: he said that he did not think this was true because when he shared ideas “people weren’t listening to me, and they always lau gh at me for some reason”. This dichotomy suggests that the students who have the label of “s mart” or “the stars” feel less risk in participatin g, while those who do not perceive themselves as part of those gro ups may feel that their participation comes with a risk of judgement or even ridicule. These labels then influenced students’ comfort in s haring ideas and their perception of whether all ideas were valued.

## Discussion

Students’ responses paint a picture of two distinct learning experiences occurring in Ms. Shaw’s classroom – one in which some students engaged deeply in science practice to collectively construct understandings, and another in which students saw science as something that happened to them or around them rather than an activity in which their participation was seen as authentic and valua ble. Other literature has suggested that redesigning curriculum to incorporate students’ epistemic agency requires an intentional shift in power from adults to children (Ko & Krist, 2019), and this study expands on this work by illuminating the need for teachers to consider how this power shift may be unequally distributed  amongst students and continue to perpetuate an inequitable distribution of status in the classroom.

Students’ experiences were linked to the way that they were positioned by their peers, teacher, and themselves. This positioning led to a construction of status in the classroom in which some students were seen as having more authority  than others. Prior research has shown how teachers notice and re spond to this unequal distribution of status (Baldinger, 2017), and this study adds to this body of work by explo ring how children perceive this inequity and how it affects their learning experiences. Students who were typically positioned with lower status (in particular, Ethan and Damandi) expressed feeling greater risk in participating in scientific activity. Both Ethan and Damandi referred to feeling worried that their ideas would be evaluated by their peers and teacher, which could cause them to feel embarrassed or even ridiculed. In contrast, students typically positioned with higher status did not seem to have these concerns, instead explaining that disagreement or sharing an incorrect idea provided opportunities for collective knowledg e development. This finding supports Wright  et al.’s (2018) suggestion that  students’ feelings about their abilities  impact the risk they feel in participating in disciplinary practice, and that these risks are particularly acute when students feel that the purpose of participation is to “get the right answer” rather than to engage in collective knowledge development.

## Implications and further research

This paper contributes to the field’s understanding  of how children experience science learning, parti cularly by showing that elementary-aged students are able to  articulate the ways that scientific activity is imp acted by the unequal distribution of status in their classrooms. If designers seek to take up principles like collec tive activity, heterogeneity, and epistemic agency without conside ring how these intersect with the construction of s tatus in a classroom, their efforts may serve to only support some students, thereby reifying power structures that perpetuate inequity. A continuing question this study leaves unanswered is what aspects of the learning environment caused the unequal distribution of status to be constructed in this classroom. It is unclear if Ms. Shaw and the researchers acted in particular ways that positioned some students to have more authority than others, as well as how students’ interactions in previous school years impacted how they related to each other in fifth grade (an idea that both Rafael and Alan referred to in their interviews). Developing a better understanding of these causes w ould allow for future work designing to disrupt patterns of status distribution to foster more equitable learning environments.
