# Exploring Critical and Conceptual Data Literacy in Upper Elementary School

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Practicing conceptual and critical data literacy is important for civic participation. Emergent research has thus examined how to teach data literacy skills in K-12 classrooms. We report on a sixth-grade lesson sequence focused on promoting data literacy within outdoors recreation contexts. The lessons provide a place-based context for students to connect personal data, observations of natural phenomena, and outdoor recreation infrastructure. We analyze student artifacts, classroom observations, and interviews to find evidence of emerging conceptual and critical data literacy. While we find instances of both data literacy aspects, we highlight the importance of additional task structures to prompt students to reflect on and compare their data work to that of their peers, to deepen data literacy discourse.

## Introduction

Engagement in data literacy to understand, interpre t, and create data is crucial for civic participation (D’Ignazio, 2017). Conceptual data literacy —the application of technical data skills —should be balanced with critical data literacy that considers data topics through societa l lens (Lee et al., 2022; Louie, 2022; Noushad et a l., 2022). Embracing conceptual and critical data literacy involves the skills to ask data questions, collect data, analyze, and disseminate data, in ways that recognize the underl ying power and hierarchy behind these processes (Lee et al., 2022). Emergent research has considered how data literacy can be facilitated in K-12 classrooms, with particular focus on the alignment between the design of learning activities and evidence of student learning (Noushad et al., 2022). In this work, we report on a data literacy lesson sequence in sixth grades. The lessons are grounded in the context of recreation decision-making, with the aim to help students connect perso nal data (recreation activities) with large-scale data about wildlife habitat and recreation in frastructure. We analyze student data artifacts, classroom observations, and student interviews to answer the following questions: RQ1: What aspects of conceptual and critical data literacy emerge in students’ data reasoning? RQ2: How might we revise the learning design to support student learning?

## Background

Conceptual data literacy is the technical competency to engage in the analyses and interpretations of quantitative and qualitative data (Cui et al., 2023; Gebre, 2022). It encompasses general knowledg e about data and specific skills to use data tools and analyses (Jiang et al., 2022a). Rese archers have investigated the ability to generate hypotheses and questions, strategize data collectio n, implement analyses, and understand data variabil ity (Cui et al., 2023). While K-12 curricular initiatives have examined ways to tea ch conceptual data literacy (Lehrer & Schauble, 2017), students may find it difficult to personally connect with data (Bowler et al., 2017).  One way to help students build connections with data is to introduce them to the critical and authentic aspects of data curation, analysis, and representation (Jiang et al., 2022b; Lee et al., 2022; Louie, 2022; Schultheis & Kjelvik, 2020). Critical data literacy  attends to understanding of data as related to lived experiences (Louie, 2022). Frameworks such as racial data literacy emphasize “ the ability to individually and collectively read t he world … toward racial equity and justice” (Philip et al., 2016, p. 366). Meanwhile, the Data Feminism framework highlights that data is not objective (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020). The fra mework includes several principles to critique data work, including Elevating emotions  (recognizing the emotions and opinions behind data analyses and visualizations), Rethink binaries and hierarchies (consider how classification and hierarchy can incl ude or exclude participant groups), and Consider context (understanding data curation and questions in conte xt). Educators can introduce students to critical data l iteracy, by allowing students to practice agency in  selecting problems to address, asking data questions, engagin g in authentic, first-hand data coll ection, and reflecting on race, class, spatial, and social patterns underlying the data (Louie, 2022; Noushad et al., 2022).

## Methodology

Data came from the first year of a design-based implementation research project (Fishman et al., 2013) to design a data literacy curriculum for sixth graders. The r esearch took place at a  K-6 elementary charter school in the Western United States (86.2% White, 6.5% Hispanic/Latino; 4% two or more races; 1% Asian or Pacific Islander, 1% Black). Participants include d 53 sixth graders and two teachers, Mary (pseudonym), who taught math and science, and Alex (pseudonym), who taught English Language Arts and social studies. The school has a strong focus on experiential learning, and students join several unique fieldtrip experiences throughout the school year. Our work in this paper is centered around a local 6-mile hike at the beginning of the Fall semester. Th e teachers and researchers collaboratively designed opportunities to engage with natural phenomena before, during, and after the hike. Prior to the trip,  Mary introduced students to the cultural, historic, and scientific phenomena they might encounter on the hike, while Alex prompted students to discuss their favorite recreat ion activities. Students went on the hike in groups of 10-12 over several days. During the hike, the teachers invited students to take pictures of interesting phenomena they encount ered, such as a natural rock amphitheater or differ ent plant species. Teachers prompted students to document their emotions (e.g., “Are you feeling happy, sad, or tired?”) in video forms. The following week, students wrote a data report of their experience in their science lessons. Students identified three phenomena  from the ir pictures, wrote qualitative and quantitative questions to e xamine the phenomena, and graphed their emotions during the hike  using pie charts. Before letting students work independently, Mary facilitated class discussions about  what made a good phenomenon  (students: “something you observe and is interesting”) and good statistical questions (students: “having a variety of answers”; “not just yes or no”). Mary invited students to come up with qualitative questions and reiterated that the questions reflected characteristics (“ why and how”), whereas quantitative questions began with “about how many”.  S he also demonstrated how to read pie charts and encouraged students to create different categories for their charts. ## Findings

Emerging conceptual data literacy Our results suggested variation in the quality of data questions students came up with. Analyses of the questions in students’ data reports showed that most of the quantitative questions correctly targeted frequency and quantity (e.g., "about how many rocks are there”). When it c ame to qualitative questions, however, while studen ts used “why” and “how” as question stems, the questions were not always oriented toward data collection. For example, questions such as "Why does the mountain look like a dinosaur spine?” or “How did the rock look like s teps?” posed a scientific inquiry, rather thandata collection to understand qualitative characteristics and nuances.

We find that students naturally attended to how they would collect data from their questions and showed familiarity with quantitative sampling methods. For  instance, when asked how they might answer the pro posed question, “How many acres of land did trees take up, because it spanned across the whole bank of the w ater”, Margot (pseudonym) stated that “You could zoom in [the pic ture] and then draw a box and see how many acres it takes up in that one square and then multiply it  or something.” We did not find m any instances of qualitative data collection strategies, such as observations, interviews, and document analysis. Finally, we found evidence of awareness of data variability in students’ discussion of their graphs, where they plotted emotions of the classes during the hike. Students s uccessfully made observation about the variable inferences lined to data contexts, such as the timing and weather of the hike (Table 1). Emerging critical data literacy We found several instances of overlapping aspects of critical data literacy, namely elevating emotions, considering hierarchies, and considering context, in students’ reflections on their visualizations. Although simplistic, students recognized emotions as a form of data. They  further reflected on data categories and contexts, as they built on their experiences to devise the categories for thei r visualizations. We t hus included additional task and participant structures for students to create questions, share narratives of data investigation, and compare their work to that of their peers, to engage with data hierarchies and contexts.

## Discussion and conjectures for future work

While the current work is limited to our data conte xts, findings illustrate the utility of attending t o multifaceted data literacy in designing learning activities and examining students’ work. Students showcase both co nceptual (asking data questions, arti culating data collection methods, understanding var iability) and critical data literacy (elevating emotions, considering hierarchies, consi dering context). Situating the data literacy unit w ithin placebased phenomena —inviting students to make observations  and collect data during a local hike —supports awareness of data context and recognition of emotio ns as data (Lee et al., 2022; Noushad et al., 2022). We conjecture about opportunities for explicit scaffol ds in qualitative data practices and reflection to share and compare data narratives. Future work candocument d ifficulties with qualitative data ideas and explore  how prompting strategies may deepen conceptual and crit ical data literacy.
