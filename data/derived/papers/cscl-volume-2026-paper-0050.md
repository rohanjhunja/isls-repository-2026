# Combining Collaborative and Adaptive Learning: Constructive–Interactive Engagement through Concept Maps, Teachable Agents, and an Adaptive Tutor

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Integrating intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) with computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) may support deeper learning by combining adaptive feedback with collaborative knowledge construction. This study examined whether integrating adaptive and collaborative supports promotes constructive –interactive engagement. Seventy-four university students participated in an experiment comparing three conditions: ITS-only (adaptive), concept map construction with a teachable agent (collaborative), and a hybrid condition combining both supports. Learning was measured using pre-and posttests, and collaborative processes were examined through concept maps and verbal interaction measures. Results showed that the hybrid condition achieved the highest learning gains, while adaptive support alone produced higher comprehension than collaborative support alone. Process measures showed no significant differences across conditions, suggesting that the hybrid condition maintained comparable levels of collaboration despite additional individual tasks. These findings suggest that integrating adaptive and collaborative supports can effectively support deeper learning in CSCL environments.

Balancing individualized support and collaborative learning has become an important challenge in educational practice. Intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) provide adaptive feedback and mastery-based progression tailored to learners’ understanding (Anderson et al., 1995; Koedinger & Aleven, 2007; VanLehn, 2011). Such adaptive support has proven effective for improving learning efficiency, for example through cognitive-model–based hint generation and mastery learning in systems such as the Open Learning Initiative(OLI) and Cognitive Tutor (Koedinger et al., 2013). However, ITS activities such as problem solving and response manipulation often remain at the active level of engagement, providing limited opportunities for learners to generate new interpretations or engage in reciprocal explanation (Graesser et al., 2014). In contrast, computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) emphasizes peer interaction and knowledge co-construction through dialogue and shared artifacts (Roschelle & Teasley, 1995; Stahl et al., 2006). According to the ICAP framework (Chi & Wylie, 2014; Chi & Boucher, 2023), activities such as explanation, concept mapping, and collaborative reasoning correspond to constructive  and interactive engagement, which are associated with deeper learning. Prior studies have shown that self-explanation facilitates deeper understanding (Chi et al., 1994), while peer explanation and questioning can promote reasoning and knowledge refinement (Miyake, 1986; Webb, 2009). Collaborative environments often incorporate externalization tools such as concept maps (Novak, 1990) and scripts or awareness systems that structure discussions and support knowledge building (Nesbit & Adesope, 2006; Fischer et al., 2013; Hayashi, 2020). Nevertheless, when learners differ in their level of understanding, some may dominate while others remain passive (Barron, 2003), and collaborative activities do not always lead to deep learning (Kirschner et al., 2006). These limitations partly stem from the limited capacity of CSCL environments to provide individualized adaptive support (Holmes et al., 2019; Roll & Winne, 2015). Overall, ITS and CSCL exhibit complementary strengths and weaknesses. ITS provides individualized adaptive guidance but often elicits relatively shallow engagement, whereas CSCL promotes deeper constructive and interactive activities but lacks fine-grained adaptivity. Integrating these approaches may therefore support both individualized guidance and deeper collaborative engagement (Holmes et al., 2019; Roll & Winne, 2015). This study examines whether combining adaptive support from ITS with collaborative activities supported by concept maps and a teachable agent can promote deeper learning. We conducted a controlled experiment comparing three conditions: (1) ITS-based adaptive learning, (2) collaborative learning using concept maps and a teachable agent (Hayashi et al., 2025), and (3) a hybrid environment integrating both supports. Learning outcomes were measured using pre –post comprehension tests, while collaborative process es were examined through concept maps and verbal interaction measures. 349

## Participants

Seventy-four first-and second-year undergraduate students enrolled in an introductory psychology-related course at a comprehensive university in Japan participated in the study as part of a class activity. After receiving an explanation of the study and providing informed consent, participants were randomly assigned to one of three conditions: adaptive (n = 24), collaborative (n = 24), and hybrid (n = 26). Learning environment The learning environment combined the collaborative concept map system CoCot (Hayashi et al., 2025) with adaptive learning support from the OLI. The instructional materials addressed attribution theory in cognitive psychology (internal–external, stable–unstable, controllable–uncontrollable). The integrated system used in this study is illustrated in Figure 1.

Figure 1 Example of an Integrated Hybrid System CoCot allows learners to construct and revise concept maps while interacting with peers and a teachable agent. Learners teach the agent using their concept maps and receive a generated map as feedback, encouraging constructive and interactive activities. However, CoCot alone does not provide individualized adaptive feedback. To provide adaptive guidance, the system was integrated with OLI, which delivers mastery-based learning support through immediate feedback and graduated hints based on learner responses (Koedinger et al., 2013). This integration enabled adaptive feedback within a collaborative learning environment. Experimental design Three conditions were implemented by activating different system functions:

1. Adaptive condition: learners worked individually using only OLI’s adaptive support.

2. Collaborative condition: learners worked in pairs using CoCot to construct concept maps and interact

with the teachable agent without adaptive feedback.

3. Hybrid condition: learners worked in pairs using both CoCot and OLI, combining collaborative

interaction with adaptive feedback.

Dependent variable Learning outcomes were measured using pre –post comprehension tests scored on a five-point rubric (Hayashi, 2020). Two coders independently evaluated responses (Krippendorff’s α = 0.81).  Collaborative processes were analyzed using two indicators. First, concept map construction activities were categorized into six operations: Create Node, Update Node, Delete Node, Create Link, Update Link, and Delete Link. Second, the total number of verbal utterances during collaborative activities was used as a quantitative indicator of interaction. 351 This study demonstrated the theoretical potential and practical significance of integrating ITS and CSCL to enhance learning outcomes. The hybrid learning environment that combined the OLI with collaborative concept mapping produced the highest comprehension gains, significantly outperforming both adaptive support alone and collaborative learning support alone. These findings suggest that individualized adaptive support stabilizes learners’ foundational understanding of the content, which can then be further refined through collaborative activities. In addition, the higher performance of the adaptive condition compared with the collaboration-only condition indicates that adaptive guidance can effectively support initial knowledge acquisition before collaborative knowledge construction. Although the sequencing of adaptive and collaborative activities was not explicitly controlled in the hybrid condition, future research should examine how different sequencing strategies may further enhance learning outcomes.

Analyses of concept map operations and verbal utterances revealed no statistically significant differences between the collaborative and hybrid conditions, although the hybrid condition showed a slight tendency toward higher concept map activity. Important ly, even though learners in the hybrid condition engaged in additional adaptive problem-solving tasks, their concept map activity and verbal interaction levels remained comparable to those in the collaboration-only condition. This suggests that the added adaptive support did not reduce collaborative engagement and may have supported productive interaction. Future work should therefore examine collaborative processes over longer learning sessions and investigate qualitative aspects of dialogue and knowledge construction.

These findings also have implications for educational practice. Simply introducing collaborative learning activities may not always produce strong learning effects; however, combining collaboration with individualized adaptive feedback may enhance learning  outcomes. The higher learning gains observed in the hybrid condition suggest that adaptive feedback may help learners correct misconceptions and better participate in collaborative knowledge construction. Such integrated support may be particularly useful  in learning contexts where learners’ levels of prior knowledge vary widely, such as first-year university education and large-scale online courses. Nevertheless, several limitations should be noted. First, the measures of collaboration used in this study—concept map operations and utterance counts —capture only limited aspects of collaborative activity and do not directly reflect the quality of learners’ interactions. Second, multiple instructional supports varied across experimental conditions, which limits the ability to isolate specific causal mechanisms underlying the observed effects. Third, the participants were limited to Japanese university stud ents in a short-term experimental setting, and the generalizability of the findings to other contexts remains uncertain. Future research should therefore investigate the qualitative features of collaborative interaction and their relationship to individualized adaptive support. In particular, discourse analysis grounded in the ICAP framework (Chi & Wylie, 2014) could help cl arify how learners engage in active, constructive, and interactive activities during hybrid learning processes. Combining such analyses with learning analytics and AI-based modeling may further enable the design of real-time support that adapts to learners’ collaborative processes. Overall, the integrated condition was the most effective in improving learning comprehension, demonstrating the complementary roles of individualized adaptive support and collaborative interaction. Rather than treating ITS and CSCL as separate approaches, this study suggests that their integration can provide a productive framework for supporting deeper learning.

By combining adaptive feedback from ITS with knowledge externalization and interaction in CSCL environments, this study highlights how hybrid learning environments can support deeper understanding. These guidance with collaborative knowledge construction.

Future studies should continue to investigate how hybrid environments influence learners’ activity levels and collaborative processes, particularly through qualitative analyses grounded in the ICAP framework. Such work may contribute to bridging the ITS and CSCL research traditions and advancing integrated learning-support frameworks.
