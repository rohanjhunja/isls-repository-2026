# Toward a Collective Understanding of Designing STEM Learning for Equity and Justice

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Due to assumptions of “cultural neutrality,” STEM education has neglected designing for equity and justice, especially in higher education. This study investigates how a group of learning scientists, STEM faculty, and a computer scientist engaged in collective knowledge building to develop a common understanding of designing for equity and justice in STEM. Findings demonstrate how critical readings and discussions supported building a common foundation of what designing for equity and justice means across STEM disciplines.

## Introduction

Despite STEM teaching and learning foregrounding th e disciplines as tools for societal advancement, ST EM learning spaces, especially in higher education, co ntinue to ignore the inherently sociopolitical nature of STEM disciplines (McKinney de Roysten & Sengupta-Irving, 2019). Eurocentric ideologies of neutrality make invisible the need to attune to equity and justice in STEM (M cGee, 2020), especially when considering curricular  design. With research calling for the disciplines to more critically account for the sociopolitical and racialized aspects of STEM learning (Lee & Grapin, 2022), there is a need to understand through what means, and to what ends, STEM educators come to understand how to design for equity and justice. Collective knowledge building and decision making Co-design, as a methodological approach, has been used  to draw upon the diverse and varied knowledge s of all stakeholders in the design process to engage in col lective design and decision-making (Gomez et al. 2018). As such, engaging in co-design practices necessitates collaborative knowledge building toward a common foundation to inform how we “define the problems that need to be addressed and propose solutions to those problem s, with important consequences for present and future learning opportunities,” (NASEM, 2024 p. 138). Informed by these ideas, this paper describes how a co-design team engaged in collaborative knowledge building to develop a shared understanding of equity and justice in STEM, and us ed that shared understanding to inform decision-making around designing STEM learning experiences for equi ty and justice. In this paper, we highlight our co-design practice of collective critical reading and discussion as a strategy for cross-disciplinary learning. We ask: How do a team of STEM faculty and learning scientists engage in coll ective knowledge building to inform decision-making around designing for equity and justice?

## Context, methodology and methods

Data for this study were derived between 2021-2023 as part of a larger, multi-year participatory design-based research study (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) focused on the co-design of a two-quarter undergraduate STEM course for preservice elementary education majors at a large public university in the Pacific Northwest, and three online professional learning courses for pre-service and in-service teachers, teacher leaders and informal educators. Our co-design team consisted of four women of color learning scientists, one woman of color computer scientist and four white (three women and one man) STEM faculty members from physics, chemistry, environmental science, and biology, respectively. Our co-design space served as an opportunity to not only d esign the course sequence, but, also, for co-design members to reflect on our own pedagogical and curricular design practices. Data for this study included shared critical reading notes of 40 research and practice articles centered on equity and justice in STEM, recordings and transcri pts of co-design sessions (~150 hours), and three yearly interviews with each of the four STEM faculty membe rs (~15 hours). Our theoretical and analytical  framing utilized the Science and Engineering in Preschool Through Elemen tary Grades: The Brilliance of Children and Strength of Educators (NASEM 2022) report’s four “approaches to equity.” Adapted from Philip and Azevedo’s

(2017) original paper, these four approaches highlight the range of ways education sy stems and educators can

work towards equity  and justice, and include: (1) increasing opportunity and acces s to high-quality science and engineering, (2) emphasizing increased achievement,  representation and identification with science and engineering, (3) expanding what constitutes science and engineering, and (4) seeing science and engineering as a part of justice movements. We used these frames to understand how co-design team members collectively came to understand equity and justice in STEM learning. We, then, use the Equity in K-12 STEM Education: Framing Decisions for the Futures  report’s (NASEM 2024) five “making decisions for equity” frames to understand the ways our team built upon this collective understanding to engage in knowledge building that informed curricular design decision-making. These equity decision-making frames include: (1) reducing gaps between gr oups, (2) expanding opportunity and access, (3) embracing het erogeneity in STEM classrooms, (4) lear ning and using STEM to promote justice, and (5) envisioning sustai nable futures through STEM. Finally, we triangulated our findings across the data  sources to determine how critical readings and discussions supported collective knowledge building to inform decision-making in designing for equity and justice in STEM.

## Findings

We found that critical readings and discussion  became an essential tool for co-design team members to collaboratively engage in knowledge construction to ward a more critical understanding of equity and ju stice in STEM. Early in the first year  of the project, conversations around equity and jus tice were situated within more normative approaches to STEM teaching and learning  that centered on increasing opportunity and access (NASEM, 2021 frame 1) and achievement, representati on and identification (NASEM, 2021  frame 2). More specifically, conceptions of STEM teaching and learning were focused on providing opportunities for minoritized students by reducing barriers in STEM (frame 1) and positioning students to see themselve s as scientists (frame 2). For example,  in initial interviews with faculty members, they si tuated equity as “starting first with creating resources for everyone to access” (Faculty 2) and “ every individual believing they belonged in my clas sroom,” (Faculty 4). These same sentiments were reflected in the first critical reading discussion where, when conversing around a reading on STEM curricular pitfalls, co-design members made comments such as we must “acknowledge all the hard work over years that communities have done,” (Faculty 2)  situating equity and justice within the acknowledgement of community contributions to STEM.

After a year of engaging in critical readings and discussions, co-design members’ approach to equity and justice shifted to seeing science as a part of justice movements (N ASEM, 2021 frame 4). For example, when discussing a reading from Octavia’s Brood (2015), one faculty brought up the idea of speculation as a practice of “decolonization of the imagination [as] a subversiv e form.” Th e resulting discussion  prompted a  collective understanding of the role of science fiction and sp eculative storytelling as an equitable science prac tice that supports students in imagining a more just future, and knowledge building around  situating speculation in our curricular design to help students use STEM to think about “what is and what could be.” This collective knowledge building around the role of speculation informed the design of a final project focused on speculative storytelling and robotics as a justice-centered STEM practice. More specifically, curricul ar design decision-making centered on embracing heterogeneity to disrupt how STEM fields represent and design for diverse sensemaking repertoires (NASEM, 202 4 frame 3), as well as  to situate how  STEM can be used to promote justice by engaging in speculation as a practice of futures world building to remediate community injustices (NASEM, 2024 frame 4). Overall, faculty found value in critical readings and discussion as they “provided a launching pad for us to talk” (Faculty 4), “challenged a lot of my persp ectives and challenged a lot of my own core beliefs ” (Faculty 2), and “[helped us in] reaching a shared understanding…something that I wo uldn’t do in my normal academic life,” (Faculty 1). In sum, critical readings and discussions provided opportunities to “crack open an idea, or a theory, or something that was chewy enough for us to talk about as it applied to our own work and capacities…I think this would have been a different experience if we didn’t have those opportunities to read other people’s work who are thinking about this, and bring those into our own conversations,” (Faculty 3).

## Significance

Findings from this paper demonstrate that the co-design practice of collective critical readings and discussion can serve as a powerful space to  develop more critical, complex and nuanced conceptions of equity and justice in STEM, and support decision-making through collective knowledge building toward designing STEM learning for equity and justice.  In higher education, STEM curricular design and instruction often fail to address the sociopolitical, racialized, and neutralized  aspects of the discipline. While not presented in this paper, co-design members, including STEM faculty, operationalized these cri tical conceptions of equity and justice in their ow n curricular and pedagogical practices, demonstrating the value of collaborative, participatory approache s to supporting researchers and educators across STEM disciplines to promote equity and justice.
