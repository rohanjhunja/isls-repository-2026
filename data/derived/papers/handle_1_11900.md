# Exploring GenAI Technologies within Collaborative Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Since the release of OpenAI ChatGPT along with other Large Language Models (LLMs), a surge of AI tools has been offered to teachers and students. Beyond LLMs’ common uses as efficiency tools and 1:1 tutors, we contend that AI advancements provide opportunities to support robust student learning in other approaches, specifically in collaborative learning groups. In this symposium, we present the ways in which generative AI can be used to facilitate jigsaw work, promote discussion, identify confusions and conflicts, and create new knowledge with student groups. Interactive demonstration of the tools is built into the symposium so together as a group, we can learn more about how to use Gen AI to facilitate and promote group learning.

Objectives of the session The advancement in generative AI (genAI) technology in the past couple years has pushed education innovations, expanding possibilities to support student learning. Unfortunately, most use cases of the emerging tec hnologies still center around decades old problems, for example, one-on-one tutoring and interactions. Much less is known in the underexplored space of genAI in collaborative learning. In this symposium, we seek to start the conversation investigating the state of the field when it comes to collaborative AI in the classroom. Particularly, what do people mean by collaborative AI within learning environments? Where and how are people exploring collaboration? How does AI fit into collaboration with humans in the classroom settings?

## Overview of the presentation

The papers in this symposium cover a wide range of ways for supporting collaboration with AI in diverse learning environments.

To answer our research questions, we present a rang e of approaches to collaborative AI starting with more conceptual and moving towards evidence-based papers.  For example, paper 1 addresses RQ1 by presenting how generative AI and humans can jointly regulate collaborative learning in a new model, while paper 6 addresses it by showcasing promising evidence for genAI to pr ovide support for human collaborative knowledge bui lding. ## Scholarly significance

Group work is inherently challenging. The “triple p roblem solving space” framework posits that group w ork involves cognitive, social, and affective component s that requires real-time calibration (Nieswandt et al., 2020). Cognitive, task-related challenges can sometimes lead to interpersonal conflicts, especially when students’ social skills may still be developing, derailing the colla borative learning goals (Lee et al., 2015). Therefo re, facilitating meaningful group work and ensuring all students hav e opportunities to participate can be difficult, and teac hers often feel underprepared to address group work (Bennet et al., 2010). GenAI tools can potentially alleviate these problem s. In a literature review of the roles of collabora tive AI, Tan and colleagues (2022) identified four types  of AI support for collaborative learning: descript ive, diagnostic, predictive, and prescriptive. In these papers we reveal ways of going beyond these four categories. The symposium contributes to existing literature by  exploring the breadth of ideas for supporting collaborative learning with AI. We dig into the dynamics and challenges of a group collaboration that varies based on the learning context, learning objectives, and l earning design and ask how and how well GenAI can h elp address these concerns.

Structure This face to face symposium will be organized as a structured poster session. Each presenter will begi n with a brief elevator pitch (no more than one minute) to i ntroduce their project. Presenters will then move t o their respective stations, where participants can view posters, watch demonstrations, and engage in discussions. Remote presenters will provide video walkthroughs of their  posters and be available on Zoom to answer questio ns from participants.

Shared hybrid intelligent regulation of collaborative learning model Andy Nguyen The swift advancements in Artificial Intelligence (AI), particularly with Generative AI (GenAI), have substantially impacted numerous sectors, reshaping both professional and educational environments (Giannakos, 2024). These shifts highlight an urgent need to reimagine and reform educational practices while also emphasizing the importance of reinforcing human skills, such as  collaboration and self-regulation. Collaborative learning has long been recognized as a highly effective approach  to cultivate these competencies and Socially Share d Regulation of Learning (SSRL) theory offers a useful framework to examine how learners co-regulate and manage their learning efforts in collaborative settings (J ärvelä  et al., 2023). Recent models, including Jär velä and Hadwin’s (2024) trigger framework and Singh and Muis’s (2024) integrated SSRL model, offer advanced insights into SSRL, enhancing our ability to support collaborative learning. While these models mark significant progress in SSRL research, they have yet incorporated th e potential role of GenAI as an active partner in c ollaborative learning.

GenAI differs from prior technologies through its c apacity for human-like interactions across various modalities (Nguyen et al., 2024). Furthermore, with  machine reinforcement learning, GenAI can have the  ability to continuously improve based on human interactions  (Counts et al., 2024). However, there remains an o ngoing debate regarding the extent to which learners should develop skills that allow them to perform tasks independently of AI support (Giannakos, 2024; Nguyen, 2025). Give n that technology such as GenAI is deeply embedded in modern society and workplaces, many tasks now rely on an interplay between human and technological capabilities. Socio-technical theory emphasizes that any organizational  system’s effectiveness relies on an integrated view of both 'social' and 'technical' dimensions as interdependent parts of a complex system. Recently, the concept of hybrid intelligence has emerged, aim ing to merge human and artificial intelligence in w ays that yield outcomes unattainable by either entity alone (Nguyen et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2025). Within this hybrid intelligence paradigm, human-AI shared regulation is seen as vital (Järvelä et a l., 2023; Nguyen, 2025). Taking a socio-technical system approach and building up from previous SSRL frameworks (Järvelä et al., 2023; Järvelä & Hadwin, 2024; Sing h & Muis, 2024), this study proposes the model for Shared Hybrid Intelligent Regulation of Collaborative Learning (SHIRCL) (as shown in Figure 1). Figure 1 Shared Hybrid Intelligent Regulation of Collaborative Learning (SHIRCL) Model The framework comprises both individual-level conditions and group-level dynamics in human –AI teaming for learning. At the individual level, huma n conditions include cognitive, affective, motivati onal, and metacognitive factors, while AI-related conditions refer to its technological affordances, learning capabilities, and underlying knowledge bases. At the group level, the  framework conceptualizes AI  as an active, adaptive entity that interacts dynamically with human learners thro ugh sensing, sense-making and adaptation. The collaboration is situated within defined task conditions, highlighting the interdependence between human and AI agents in joint learning activities as well as in understanding each other. The SHIRCL framework seeks to not only provide insi ghts into the dynamics of SSRL in collaborative learning involving multiple learners and GenAI agents but also guide the design, development, and evaluation of learning tasks and GenAI agents that can effe ctively engage in shared regulation with human lear ners in collaborative learning contexts.

Responsible innovation for AI partners in jigsaw classrooms Emily Doherty, Leanne Hirshfield The Jigsaw Classroom, first implemented in desegreg ated classrooms, was a pivotal innovation in educat ional techniques aimed at fostering collaborative learnin g and social unity (Perkins and Tagler 2011; Aronso n 1978). The impact of the Jigsaw Classroom continues to make it a well-established and common collaborative technique in today’s classrooms. However, with increasingly m ore students in classrooms, it is often difficult f or teachers to closely monitor many small group collaborations at once. This presents an opportunity to leverage the growing toolbox of Generative AI (GenAI) resources to support collaboration during jigsaw activities. One way to leverage GenAI tools to support small gr oup collaboration is through conversational agents powered by Large Language Models (LLMs), due to their ability to understand human language and produce realtime, intelligent interventions. In this comme ntary, we build upon the empirical findings explori ng the human-centered design and evaluation of an LLM-based jigsaw partner to support small group collaboration (Doherty et al., 2025). Fifty-eight groups of dyads and triads (145 participants), aged 12-17,collaborated in a jigsaw activity and were assigned to be assisted by our agent or not. The results showed decreased self-reported ratings of social loafing and increased use of  language related to respectful collaboration in interactions with the agent compared to those without. Our studies demons trate both the potential and the risks of integrati ng LLMs into collaborative learning settings: while they can scaffold group learning and encourage inclusive dialogue, they can also reinforce undesired social dynamics if not carefully designed. Thus, the use of LLMs as educational supports requires a responsible innovation approach (Stilgoe et al., 2013). Concerns around model selection, transparenc y, and limitations of out-of-the-box LLMs require careful attention (Williams et al., 2023). We advocate for a human-centered design process that incorporates educator and learner perspectives throughout development, protot yping, and iterative evaluation (Amershi et al., 20 14, Wang et al., 2021).

In this talk, we detail the opportunities, challeng es, and implications of designing GenAI tools for collaborative learning. In particular, we focus on the importance of human-centered design and evaluation practices to ensure that such tools promote equity, lift underrepresented voices, and stay aligned with the original social goals of the Jigsaw Classroom.

From learning about to figuring out: AI-partner embedded curricula for meaningful collaboration and understanding Mon-Lin Monica Ko, Peter Foltz We describe the development of an AI-partner embedded-curriculum unit, the AI-Games Unit, and the opportunities that it provides for middle school students to work collaboratively and figure out how A I systems work. We have iteratively developed and refined an AI partner that supports collaboration and a curric ular unit that addresses how AI systems are used to moderate online communities. We argue that by 1) engaging students in a unit about moderation and 2) giving students a chance to experience and exam ine an AI moderation tool enables students to co-construct ideas about how AI can be used to foster equitable online communities. Incorporating AI partners integrated with curricula  addresses the challenge of how to promote deep conceptual learning via rich socio-collaborative learning experiences for all students. While the benefits of collaborative learning are well-known, contemporary classrooms still face challenge s in implementing collaborative learning experiences (D’Mello et al.,  2024).  Teachers face the challenge of orchestrati ng rich, collaborative experiences in their classroom (Chin, 2007). Students mus t also attend to the  relational aspects of attending to and building off of one another’s ideas (Chowning et al., 2023). Our approach to supporting collaboration involves t he development of an LLM-based AI partner that is meaningfully integrated into a curricular unit that addresses key ideas in the AI4K12 framework (AI4K12, 2024). The Community Builder (CoBI) AI Partner supports the relational dimension of collaboration. Students use CoBi to define a set of “community agreements” or norms of behavior (e.g., being equitable, respectful, and  moving thinking forward). After this criteria is establish ed, CoBi listens in on student talk to identify exa mples of these agreements during small group activity to identify how students are engaging in collaboration. Classro oms can review CoBi’s outputs and reflect on their joint co llaboration and also discuss the strength, limitati ons and opportunities for improvement. Throughout CoBI, mul tiple AI-based approaches are incorporated to best characterize student collaboration. CoBi is used wi thin a Storyline unit (Reiser et al., 2021) where s tudents take the lead on working together to build an explanation of how AI systems are used to moderate online communities. CoBi is used at multiple points throughout the unit  to support students in examining how AI-and human-moderation systems work.

Our preliminary analysis of use in an 8th grade ELA  classroom suggests that the AI-partner embedded unit creates a rich learning environment for engagi ng with AI systems in ways that provide students wi th opportunities to “look under the hood” and facilita te more meaningful collaborations. For example, a t eacher reported that using CoBi supported students in gene rating ideas about specific prompts that reflected different dimensions of collaboration. Moreover, reflections on CoBi’s output also allowed students to engage in conversations about how different perspectives and design decisions underlying AI systems can lead to bias in its output.

In this talk, we will demonstrate the integration o f AI partners into curricula and discuss the novel opportunities for AI-embedded curricular units for supporting students in meaningful collaborative learning about AI systems.

## Designing chatbots to enrich discussions and promote systems thinking

Ha Nguyen, Victoria Nguyen, Sara Ludovise, Rosella Santagata Objectives. We will describe the development process of different AI chatbots (using GPT-4 as the base) and their integration into collaborative learning in a high s chool environment science curriculum. We co-create five chatbots—demonstrating varied perspectives around climate change’s impact on a local marine ecosystem—with students, teachers, and environmental educators. The chatbots role-play as a fisherman, a college student, a social media influencer, a kelp researcher, and a civil engineer. Following the jigsaw method (Aronson, 1978), students in groups of four are given individual tasks to converse with different chatbots and collect information. Then, they share the information with groupmates to collective ly refine their models of climate change’s root cau ses and causal relationships. Interactions with the chatbot s prompt students to consider factors often overloo ked in environmental education, including climate change's  impacts over time and space and across communities. We ask: How are the chatbots developed  to integrate community perspectives? How are they i ntegrated into smallgroup discussions to facilitate systems understanding? Perspectives. In prior virtual learning environments, pedagogical  agents often had limited, pre-defined dialogues. Leveraging the advances of Large Languag e Models (LLMs) to facilitate human-like dialogues, we design our chatbots to not just provide information, but also elicit students’ ideas, link to multifaceted data sources (e.g., scientific sources, personal stories), and d eliver feedback on students’ models. To design the chatbots’ dialogues, we build on the ICAP framework (Chi & Wy lie, 2014), which p ositions interactive and constructive activities as the most conducive to learning, compared to active and passive engagement. The chatbots encourage students to explain their understanding (active), while building on students’ ideas (interactive). Methods. We co-created the different chatbots’ roles, backgrounds,  and dialogues with high school students, undergraduates, teachers, and environment educators in eight sessions (17 hours). To build the chatbots’ knowledge base, we collected semi-structured intervi ews with community-based individuals. Finally, we collaborated with high school teachers and environment educators to develop the curricular activities. Results. Our presentation will demonstrate: (1) the chatbots ’ development workflow, and (2) examples of co-designed learning activities. We will discuss our h uman-in-the-loop approach to iteratively evaluate and refine the chatbots. An early observation is that the chatbots’ responses were often surface-level. To address this, we connected each chatbot to an external knowledge base curated from interviews and evidence-based resources (using Retrieval-Augmented Generation). Another observation from chatbot evaluation is that students might take the responses as facts. We developed collaboration scripts to facilitate the student-chatbot conversations and jigsaw model-building activities to reflect on responses’ accuracy and depth. Significance. We illustrate how to integrate AI into collaborative discussions in environmental education, while attending to how the technology can represent locally grounded, multifaceted, and accurate perspectives. Enhancing knowledge building discourse in online communities through AI-generated rating and feedback Lydia Cao, Marlene Scardamalia, Dina Soliman, Monic a Resendes, Ahmad Khanlari, Stacy Costa, Carol K. K. Chan Knowledge Building integrates theory, practice, and  technology to foster knowledge creation and contin ual improvement of ideas  (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). While Knowledge Forum technology supports collaborative knowledge advancement through various  analytics tools  (Chen & Hong, 2016; Chen & Zhang, 2016), providing timely, theoretically-grounded assessment and feedback remains a signific ant challenge, especially for analyzing complex collaborative discourse in real-time. This study explores a human-AI partnership approach by investigating how Large Language Models  (LLMs) can augment collaborative knowledge work through automated assessment and real-time feedback within Knowledge Forum. In this study, we developed and validated a theoret ically-grounded six-level rating scale capturing both idea improvement and collaborative knowledge advancement (Chan et al., 1992) through iterative refinement with five Knowledge Building researchers. Fiv e raters achieved inter-rater reliability (QWK ranging from 0.667 to 0.860) when assessing 79 student notes from a gradu ate-level course on Knowledge Building theory and technology. We then conducted systematic testing of  GPT-4o using varied prompting strategies, including zer oshot, few-shot, chain-of-thought, and combined approaches with different temperature settings. Results showed moderate success in automated rating. The combined approach of chain-of-thought and few-shot demonstrated the best overall performance (tem perature=0), showing a moderately strong positive correlation (Spearman's rho = 0.5402) and moderate agreement (QWK = 0.46) with human ratings, with the lowest average deviation of 0.6887 (RMSE). This indicates general alignment with human judgment but with room  for improved accuracy. Furthermore, we observed consistent LLM overestimation compared to human ratings, which unexpectedly revealed potential human biases regard ing note length (human ratings showed stronger corr elation with word count, r=0.569, compared to LLM ratings, r=0.392). Results showed promise in AI-generated feedback, with 83.54% of generated feedback meeting all four quality criteria (clarity, specificity, relevance, and potential for idea improvement). Qualitative an alysis revealed areas for improvement in feedback actionability and contextual understanding. This exploratory study is limited by small and skewed dataset (79 notes predominantly at levels 2-3) and testing with only one LLM model. Despite the limitations,  this study highlights both the potential and limitations of using LLM as an initial step tow ard concurrent, embedded, and transformative assess ment for Knowledge Building. While the LLM's capability to evaluate th e quality of student discourse remains inconclusive  and requires further experimentation, its strong perfor mance in feedback generation  warrants classroom testing. We are going to implement the LLM feedback tool in an ongoing graduate-level Knowledge Building course to examine how students engage with AI-generated feedback, their perceptions of its useful ness, and its impact on improving their Knowledge Building discourse quality.
