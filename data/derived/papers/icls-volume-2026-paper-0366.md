# What Changes with a Bit More Data in Learning Experience Network Analysis

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examine s how patterns shift as more data accumulates. Expanding a dataset from 13 to 18 student reflections, learning experience network analysis (LENA) investigated what worked, struggles, and learning theory alignments. The addition of five reflections led to a structural shift from five clusters to three broader, more integrative ecologies. Design implications include more nuanced support for feedback processing, time management, resource navigation, and identity building.

This study builds upon previous research analyzing student reflections in a graduate-level instructional design course (Do et al., 2025). The initial analysis, based on 13 reflection papers, revealed five distinct learning ecologies. This follow-up study incorporates an additional five reflection papers, expanding the dataset to 18, to further refine our understanding of student learning experiences within the course. Utilizing learning experience network analysis (LENA; Donaldson et al., 2024), this analysis aims to observe how the network map evolves with the addition of more data, potentially revealing broader or more refined patterns of student experiences. This study is grounded in a socio-constructivist framework, guided by cognitive constructivism (Piaget, 1952), social constructivism (Vygotsky, 1978), situated learning theory (Lave & Wenger, 1991), constructionism (Papert & Harel, 1991), transformative learning theory (Mezirow, 2018), self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2017), and the dynamic systems model of role identity (Kaplan & Garner, 2017). These theories informed how we interpreted student reflections in terms of interdependent aspects regarding collaboration, identity, feedback, and meaning-making in the learning process. This aligns with a complex systems perspective where learning experiences are not discrete events but are dynamically coupled and emergent phenomena (Jacobson, 2020). Part of a  design-based research project (Barab, 2022), this study used learning experience network analysis (LENA; Donaldson et al., 2024). Initial analysis (presented elsewhere: Do et al., 2025) was conducted on 13 reflection papers. For this deeper analysis, five additional reflection papers were collected and coded. The assignment for these reflection papers asked students to reflect on what worked particularly well, what they struggled with, and what the learning experience meant to them. An iterative coding process categorized learning experiences into three primary categories of what worked well, what students struggled with, and alignment of student experiences with principles derived from learning theories. We met regularly tonegotiate coding, resulting in 66 codes covering 382 coded segments. C orrelation analysis was conducted, identifying codes that frequently appeared together in the same reflection papers. C orrelations at p  <.01 were used to construct a network map, and cluster analysis (Girvan & Newman, 2002) was applied. The modularity index of the new map was Q = 0.506. While the three-cluster model is more robust with the additional data, the initial five-cluster model captured some finer-grained, valid distinctions within a smaller sample. The most immediate observation in the new map was the reduction from five clusters to three clusters. This suggested that with more data, some distinctions between the previous five learning ecologies became less pronounced, leading to broader, more encompassing clusters where certain previously separated themes now cooccur frequently enough to be grouped together.  In the initial analysis, Management & Collaboration cluster experiences involved logistical and emotional hurdles with teamwork and time manage ment, though clear assignment outcomes helped; Engagement & Identity  (red circles) cluster experiences reflected intellectual and emotional connection to the course with personal growth and professional development, sometimes accompanied by feelings of being underprepared; Personal Growth & Transformation cluster experiences centered on developing self-awareness and critical reflection while struggling with online networking; Cognitive & Metacognitive Processes  cluster experiences involved deep thinking and reflection opportunities alongside difficulties applying abstract concepts; and Practical & Relational Coursework  cluster experiences included struggles with ambiguous instructions but benefits from structured assignments and peer dialogue. Each type of 2524 experience connected to established learning theories like constructivism and transformative learning, leading to targeted design recommendations such as staggered deadlines, orientation sessions, community-building activities, resubmission opportunities, and clearer group organization processes. In the revised analysis with new data, t he new Holistic Engagement and Identity Formation cluster combines the prior Engagement & Identity  cluster with new elements related to consistency in positive engagement, resource utilization, identity development (DSMRI), constructionism, social constructivism, and self-determination. The inclusion of a situated learning theory principle of communication in a community is now more closely associated with broader aspects of engagement, identity formation, and collaborative learning. However, the remaining struggle around collaboration in a team could be seen as part of the process of engagement and identity work, rather than just logistical hurdles. The persistence of struggles that come from a lack of experience and background knowledge reinforces the need for foundational support. Students reported high motivation, resource engagement, community participation, and identity growth, while also recognizing teamwork struggles as part of professional development. Support for collaboration might need to be framed not just as solving logistical problems, but as integral to meaningful engagement and peer learning.  The new Managing Process through Reflective Practice  cluster retains the core of the old Management & Collaboration cluster, focusing on struggles with project management, time management, and procrastination while keeping the positive aspects related to helpful examples. The inclusion of reflections and development of critical thinking skills, both previously in the old Engagement & Identity cluster, suggests that students experiencing management and timing struggles are also frequently engaged in self-reflection and critical thinking development. The emergence of focus on process, not product or result, is a powerful new addition, indicating that students struggling with management might be adopting a more process-oriented mindset as a coping strategy or learning outcome. Struggles with teamwork meetings, collaboration, teamwork time management, and team procrastination remained. Sequential due dates for discussion replies were still very relevant. Design moves could now explicitly link time/project management support with prompts for self-reflection on those processes and encourage a "focus on process" to alleviate p ressure about perfect outcomes amidst these struggles. The new Navigating Course Mechanics and Interpersonal Complexity  (black squares) cluster combines the old Cognitive & Metacognitive Processes, Practical & Relational Coursework, and Personal Growth & T ransformation clusters. Here students struggled with ambiguity, feedback, communication, and comprehension but also found growth in peer discussions, clarity of assignments, and identity exploration. Struggles with resources will need specific attention, including exploration of how resources can be made more accessible or scaffolded, relevant to assignments, and manageable to complete. Struggles with ambiguity and instructions reinforce the need for clarity and multiple explanations for this cluster, and supporting peer discussion and scaffolding will remain crucial.

## Implications and conclusions

Moving from five clusters to three broader ecologies suggests that aspects of learning experiences co-occur in complex interdependent ways. The new map provided a broader picture with fewer clusters, pointing to wider overlap among experiences. Teamwork is  no longer isolated as a “management” issue. General collaboration issues are now integrated with professional identity in the Holistic Engagement and Identity Formation cluster, while time-management related teamwork issues remain with the Managing Process through Reflective Practice cluster. Logistical and communication-specific teamwork struggles are found in the Navigating Course Mechanics and Interpersonal Complexity  cluster. This split reveals a nuanced insight into teamwork issues. Feedback appears in both positive and negative dimensions within the same clusters, highlighting its pivotal role in learning dynamics. When given opportunities to reflect, revise, and resubmit, students engaged in deeper learning; conversely, unclear or inconsistent feedback led to confusion. Feedback is understood as a communication signal about the learning process. Resources, once mostly viewed as helpful (as seen in the old Engagement & Identity cluster and new Holistic Engagement and Identity Formation  cluster), now emerge as a source of struggle for some students in the Black Square cluster. This could be due to increased cognitive load or a lack of scaffolding for this group. These shifts in patterns found when adding more data to our analysis reinforced the need to design courses not around isolated problems but around holistic ecologies that support motivation, collaboration, structure, and meaning-making simultaneously. The new map suggests fewer, but perhaps more deeply entrenched and multifaceted, patterns of student experience. The Holistic Engagement and Identity Formation cluster themes appear quite stable, while the Managing Process through Reflective Practice  cluster themes are now more explicitly linked with reflection, critical thinking, and a process orientation. The biggest change is the consolidation of cognitive, practical, relational, and transformative aspects into one large Navigating Course Mechanics and Interpersonal Complexity cluster, suggesting these elements are highly codependent for a large group of students.
