# Looking for the Right Blend: Comparing Different Assessment Scenarios in a Blended Course

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents a study comparing different ways of orchestrating blended learning activities in a university course. As the main instrument, the study used Quizitor-a quiz platform that supports both in-class and at-home assessment. The data analysis shows that the presence of in-class assessment has a significant positive impact on students’ engagement with at-home quiz material. There is also strong evidence that the blended scenario, in which in-class assessment is administered as a pop-up quiz in the beginning of the next lecture, is more advantageous compared to the scenario scheduling in-class assessment as a post-lecture quiz.

## Introduction

Blended learning is a combination of  face-to-face and online learning activities. During face-to-face classes, students can engage in discussions and receive feedback from a teacher, while online they can enjoy the flexibility of self-paced learning. There are many different ways to orchestrate learning activities in a blended course, which makes the discovery of an optimal “blend” of such activities an interesting and important did actical challenge (Graham, 2006). Previous studies have proposed two main types of  sequencing strategies for  blended learning activities. Commonly, a blended course would start with a face-to-face meeting, followed by online activities. Particularly at the beginning of the course, this a pproach helps students to get more comfortable with  online learning activities (Hoic-Bozic et al., 2009). Conversely, an alternative approach is to start the course with online activities and supplement them with face-to-face sessions. This can be found, for example, in a  study by (Ebner et al., 2017) where the online course was interrupted by several face-to-face meetings with the purpose of reducing the dropout rate. This study aims to  compare the effectiveness of two competing approaches to sequencing activities in a blended course, especially, from the point of their impact on students’ engagement.

## Method

This study used Quizitor. It is a quiz platform designed to support assessment in both classroom and online settings (Hamzah & Sosnovsky, 202 3). In the in-class mode, Quizitor functions as a personal response system (Gauci et al., 2009)  or a voting tool (Draper & Brown, 2004). In the at-home mode, it is similar to many typical self-assessment platforms (see, e.g., (Brusilovsky & Sosnovsky, 2005)). Both types of assessments are important and can be interrelated. For instance, students experiencing c hallenges in-class may utilize at-home quizzes for additional practice. Conversely, students who work at-home quizzes can better prepare for the in-class activity. An experiment was conducted in a Web Technology cou rse for undergraduate students taught during the fa ll semester of 2023 in the Universitas Islam Indonesia. 217 students enrolled in the course; 174 of them used Quizitor both in class and at home. There course was divided  into six groups with three instructors  (one per two groups). The groups were combined into three conditions (balancing instructors across conditions) corresponding to three scenarios. Students in Scenario 1 had an in-class quiz at the end of every lecture, after which the at-home quiz on the same topic was made available. The idea behind this scenario was to provide students with immediate feedback on their understanding after the lecture, enabling them to assess how much additional work they needed  to do during the at-home session. In this scenario, students tended to use the self-reflect phase in the  self-regulated learning framework. Students in Scenario 2 had an in-class quiz in the beginning of the next lecture – after they had a chance to work with the at-home quiz for a week. This scenario was designed to allow students to sel fassess their knowledge at-home and to determine how much additional work they need ed to prepare for the in-class assessment, which was scheduled for the subsequent lecture that served as a knowledge check to assess their learning during at-home sessions. The third group served as the control group and received only at-home quizzes.

## Results and conclusion

This study sought to explore the impact of differen t sequencing approaches for blended learning on stu dents’ engagement with learning material.  Specifically, it focused on the two research questions. RQ1: How does in-class assessment impact students’ engagement with t he at-home activities? RQ2: Which sequence  of blended assessment is more effective for enhancing students’ engagement with the at-home activities? Table 1 helps answering RQ1. It presents the results of comparing parameters charactering the amount, the regularity, and the persistence of students’ at-home work with Quizitor between Scenario 3 and a combination of Scenarios 1 and 2. For all parameters, the two “blended” conditions combined s ignificantly outperform the nonblended scenario. This means that the general presence of in-class assessment has a significant positive impact on students’ motivation to practice at home. Table 2 c ompares the two bl ended scenarios on the same set of parameters. There is a strong general trend in favor of Scenario 2; for most of them it is significant. These results strongly suggest that sequencing in-class assessment as a pop-up quiz in the beginning of the next lecture can be more beneficial than scheduling at the end of the c urrent lecture. Students tend to engage with at-home practice more, possibly, because they have an additional event that can trigger such engagement: for every topic, a lecture and an in-class assessment are separated by time for Scenario 2 compared to Scenario 1. We plan to continue this line of investigation to obtain more definitive results.
