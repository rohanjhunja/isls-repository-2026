# Playing With Probability: How Brownie Scouts Earned Their Gaming Badge Through Board Game Design

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This practice-oriented paper presents a design workshop developed in partnership with a Brownie Scouts troop in the San Francisco Bay Area. The two-part workshop engaged seven-to nine-year-old girls (N= 9) in earning their Game Design Badge through board game design using simple, low-cost materials. Learnings from the workshop included children’s partial but functional understanding of probability, improved design confidence after analyzing other games, sense of pride and ownership about self-designed games, peer-to-peer learning opportunities through playtesting, and remixing basic game templates based on personal interests. The project demonstrates how complex topics can be made approachable through characters and storylines. The workshop flow, moving from guided play to independent design, can be adapted in classrooms, libraries, or after-school programs to make complex mathematical concepts more approachable through play and creativity.

This project was developed in collaboration with a Brownie Scouts troop in the San Francisco Bay Area for girls ages seven to nine. Their troop leader invited me to design a two-part workshop that would help the girls earn their Game Design Badge. We agreed to focus on board game design because it was  accessible, affordable, and offered hands-on exploration of game design concepts. The workshop aimed to help children understand how games are structured both conceptually and tangible design elements, and how design choices influence gameplay experience. By making and testing their own games, the girls could observe probability in action. The sessions were organized around simple materials like paper, dice, and game tokens, encouraging the idea that meaningful design does not require advanced technology or expensive materials. Through this process, the workshop sought to introduce early concepts of probability through play. The girls were encouraged to notice patterns, adjust their design and rules, and reflect on how changes affected gameplay. The overall goal of the workshop was to improve children’s understanding of probability through designing and playtesting their own board games, within the broader goal of earning their game design badge. For prior troops, game design badges were earned through other forms of games such as racing remote controlled toys through a cardboard maze, scavenger hunts, or modifying rules for an existing sport. I chose board games as the activity because, (1) it offered opportunities to combine art, storytelling, game mechanics with a special focus on probability, (2) it created artifacts that could be enjoyed beyond the workshop, such as children playing their games with family and friends, and (3) it offered balance between creative and educational novelty to the troop and making use of my experience as a designer. The data presented in this study are based on observations, field, notes, and reflections by researcher/facilitator and troop leader.

Pre-workshop activities design Before beginning the board game workshop, children  participated in two brief outdoor activities centered on Maplo, a friendly maple-leaf character from a fall-themed story world. The first activity, Maplo’s Treasure Hunt, invited the children to explore the private outdoor area and collect natural items such as acorns, leaves, and rocks. This activity encouraged them to observe and work as a team, while appreciating the transition from summer to autumn. Next, the group created Maplo’s Dollhouse, a cozy paper-template I designed featuring Maplo in a wooden cabin – where he could “rest” after the treasure hunt in a bed or “play” in a tire-swing. This simple crafting activity helped children visualize Maplo as a character who existing in both exploratory and playful activities. These pre-workshop activities offered a gentle transition from outdoor  to indoor environment of the workshop using the character and story elements as a bridge.

Constructionism (Papert & Harel, 1991) emphasize s that learners build knowledge most effectively when creating public artifacts that represent their thinking. This insight shaped the workshop goal of having each child design a board game as a visible expression of their  ideas about chance or likelihood (a simpler explanation of probability). Kafai (2006) highlighted that designing games helps learners connect play with conceptual understanding. Her work guided the structure of alternating play and creation, so the girls could play a given board game and then reflect on how their design decisions may affect the experience of others. Cochran (2005) demonstrated that a board game can integrate multiple probability ideas through direct engagement with randomness and outcomes. This finding supported my decision to use a simple turn-based board game like ‘Chutes and Ladders’ where landing on certain sections may help the player progress forward or backward on the game board – making probability observable through play. Falk, Falk, and Levin (1980) showed that young children can reason about likelihood when given concrete and repeated experiences. Their work informed my focus on visible game elements, such as two characters representing plus and minus moves, instead of abstract math. Heim and Holt (2021) emphasized that student-driven game design supports creative learning in accessible, collaborative ways. Their study encouraged me to incorporate  peer feedback through playtesting sessions.

Playtesting facilitator-designed board games In the first session, scouts were told a facilitator-designed short story to visually represent addition and subtraction. Maplo (a maple-leaf character) as a hero character who gets closer to his treasure every time a player lands on his block, whereas Nutley (an acorn with a m ustache character) as a villain character who wants to block Maplo’s treasure and requires players to step back when they land on his block. This story was followed by three versions of board game featuring Maplo (+1) and Nutley (–1). Each version varied in the number and placement of 2181 add/subtract blocks, influencing probability of the players towards the finish line. After playing, the group discussed which versions felt too fast, slow, or enjoyable speed. The design of this board game that assigns numerical positive or negative value to positive or negative traits of the characters, was inspired by the board game ‘Bricksters’ that had hallowen tricks and treats themed characters posing addition/subtraction prompts (Tiwari, 2025). Children’s comments reflected emerging understanding of probability, expressed in intuitive ways such as “it will probably take longer if there are more minus ones.”, “It’s frustrating when you almost win but have to go back to the start, but it’s also exciting”, “Not knowing who is going to win makes the game fun!” These reflections helped identify how variations in board game layout influenced the pace and excitement of gameplay, summarized in Table 2.

Playtesting child-designed board games In the second workshop, six out of nine girls brought the board games they had developed during the week between our meetings. The workshop began with each child walking the group through their board game concept and design process. Using common art supplies such as cardboard, dice, markers, paint, and simple game tokens, they had experimented with different layouts, rules, and themes. Some designs featured fantasy and nature-based designs, such as a “Candy Box” game with a foldable path, and another with a paper “mud pocket” holding worms that players could collect. Four of the six games incorporated K-pop demon hunter characters, showing how popular media influences children’s creative choices. During playtesting, the girls exchanged games in groups of 3, shared feedback, and asked peers, “What did you think of my game?” or “Should I add more bonus cards?” This phase encouraged iteration, collaboration, and confidence as the girls became both designers and play testers. While all games included instruction cards, children were eager to intervene when a peer had any confusion about the rules or gameplay. This broader sequence of starting with a story followed by a game design was rooted in Transmedia Making (Tiwari, 2020), an approach that combines children’s media with maker activities. Learnings from this work include: Partial but functional understanding of probability While the girls did not use formal mathematical vocabulary, their reasoning showed emerging grasp of probability. Comments such as “it will probably take longer if there are more minus ones” or “it’s fair if both players have the same chance” demonstrated recognition of design variation and its relationship with probability. Improved design confidence after analyzing other games When invited to design original games, children began by recalling favorite commercial titles and discussing what made them enjoyable. They identified recognizable features  of their favorite board games such as ‘Candy Land’ and ‘Outfoxed’: boards, dice, rules, and cards, and quickly understood the gameplay of the provided board games without requiring detailed instruction. This showed their ability to deconstruct and reconstruct game systems. Sense of pride and ownership Children carried a posture and expression of pride as they presented their board games to the troop, “I worked hard on this”, “I added some extra things to my board and my dice is also made of cardboard with pictures on each side” They gladly intervened to help if playtesting groups found their instructions confusing, “If you’re confused about anything just let me know!” Peer-to-peer learning The playtesting experience allowed children to review and offer feedback on each other’s games. Some younger children of the group were critiqued for the spelling errors in their instructions while others were given feedback that their game “ended very quickly because there are way too many negative blocks of-4 at a time.” Remixing basic game templates based on personal interests While the demonstrated game was autumn-themed and had a simpler gameplay, all children added their creative spins on the basic gameplay of adding and subtracting upon landing on a particular spot on the game board. Some added pop-culture references such as the characters Rumi, Mira, Zoey from K-Pop Demon Hunters appearing as the addition blocks whereas Saja boys (an evil boy band) representing subtraction blocks. These learnings from the workshop show  how even young children can explore advanced (or assumed advanced) mathematical ideas such as probability through game design – particularly when they are given the freedom to design their own games using themes (such as movies, nature, candies etc.) that are of interest to them. Relevance for others This workshop model demonstrates how educators and community facilitators can make complex learning topics approachable and enjoyable through simple, low-cost design activities. Some relevant takeaways for the broader informal learning community:

• Triangulating children’s interests (K-Pop, Nature, Candies etc), facilitators’ design skills (Character

design, game design), and an organizational reward system (Brownie Scouts, Game Design Badge) may serve as a strong foundation of purposeful partnerships combining intrinsic and extrinsic motivations.

• Complex themes such as probability can be made to look less intimidating through cute characters and

storylines (Maplo and Nutley fighting over treasure).

• The workshop flow may ease children into learning through design:  starting with a gentle introduction

to the story/characters through outdoor activity,  guided exploration of given board games as a group, then independent design time at home, and finally presenting their designs for playtesting by peers. This flow of activities can be adapted in classrooms, libraries, or after-school programs to teach iterative design and reflection based on their designs in action.

• Giving children the sandbox to explore complex topics first through pre-designed objects, and the

platform to showcase their original designs built on this understanding-may help them gain confidence and may shift the identity from player to designer.

Overall, this project showed how children can explore probability through creative, hands-on design. The Brownie Scouts demonstrated that when given the freedom to design and test their own games, they begin to understand probability tangibly. Children’s participation in this workshop showed that learning can emerge through playing intentionally to explore how design changes correlate with play experience, and how mathematical concepts shape these designs.
