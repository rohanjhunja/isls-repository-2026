# A Systematic Literature Review of Large Language Model Applications in the Algebra Domain

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Algebra is often challenging to teach and learn. While large language models (LLMs) are gaining attention in education, their role in algebra remains unclear. This systematic review, guided by the PRISMA framework, analyzed 21 studies from 532 records. Findings revealed that LLMs were mostly used on secondary data, for assessment, with ChatGPT, using zero-shot prompts but struggled with complex reasoning. This review offers theoretical insights and practical guidance for effectively applying LLMs in algebra education.

## Introduction

As a bridging subject in mathematics, algebra has g arnered much attention due to the ongoing challenges in its teaching and learning (Pardos & Bhandari, 2024). Students often struggle to understand algebraic symb ols and operations, process unfamiliar contexts, and transi tion from arithmetic to algebra (Ying et al., 2020). Large language models (LLMs) are widely used in education to generate automated feedback, support content learning, and create teaching plans (Hu et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024; Matelsky et al., 2023; Pardos & Bhandari, 2024). However, LLMs can generate incorrect, misleading, or even fabricated information (Pardos & Bhandari, 2024). Studies have also shown  that LLMs performed poorly on specific mathematics tasks compared to the average graduate student (Plevris et al., 2023) or specialized algorithms (Soygazi & Oguz, 2023). Thus, the overarching goal of this  research is to ascertain  how to apply LLMs in algebra to enhance student learning processes and improve learning out comes. A systematic literature review is needed to answer these questions and deepen our understanding of sta te-of-the-art LLM applications in the algebra domain. Previous reviews on LLMs have often focused on a single model (e.g., ChatGPT) or general education rather than on specific domains. This review aims to answer the following research questions to address a gap in research on the performance of LLMs in algebra: 1) What are the primary applications and purposes of L LMs in algebra? 2) What benefits and challenges arise when LLMs are applied to algebra? and 3)What aspects were evaluated when assessing LLM performance in algebra, and how?

## Methodology

“Large language model”, LLM, algebra, and education were u sed as keywords. The final search string, (“large language model” OR LLM) AND algebra AND education, was searc hed across six databases: ACM Digital Library, ERIC, IEEE Xplore, PsycINFO, Scopus, and Web of Science. The search was conducted across all fields on August 19, 2024, and limited to academic peer-reviewed literature, yielding 532 records. The screening was conducted in  Covidence through multiple rounds of exclusion based on prede fined inclusion and exclusion criteria. This review included only empirical, peer-reviewed literature written in English that focused  on algebra and used at least one LLM in their methods. In the first round, two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts, resulting in 59 studies. In the second round, all reviewers examined the full texts for final eligibility. In the end, 21 records were included for data extraction for each research question. First, this review extracted the educational level, application setting, data source, user type, LLM name, and LLM purpose from the included studies.  Then, thematic content analysis was used to identify, analyze, and summarize the benefits and challenges of applying LLMs in the algebra domain. Finally, it examined four aspects:

1) evaluation methods  used to assess the LLM performance; 2) prompting engineering method  used to guide

LLMs (Jia et al., 2024); 3) whether the LLM performance in algebra was compared to  other domains  (e.g., geometry); and 4) whether different LLMs were compared on the same algebraic tasks.

## Results and discussion

What are the primary applications and purposes of LLMs in algebra? Around half of the 21 included studies used secondary data to explore LLM performance in algebra. For studies reporting educational levels, the distribution was evenly split  between K-12 and higher education. The most common application setting was learning (n = 6). Although ChatGPT was the most frequently employed LLM in algebra, LLMs were often employed to answer algebra questions (n = 11). Researchers were the primary users of LLMs in 17 studies. Most included studies were exploratory, as most of them 1) used secondary data, 2) did not specify an educational level, 3) lacked a focused application setting, and 4) focused on solving algebra problems using LLMs. This reflects the recent surge in LLM development, especially since  the release of ChatGPT in November 2022 (Plevris et al., 2023). Question-answering and virtual assistance were the most common purposes for using LLMs, aligned with  previous findings  (e.g., LLMs generat ing automated feedback for open-ended questions; Matelsky et al., 2023).

What benefits and challenges arise when LLMs are applied to algebra? Consistent with the findings on LLM purposes, most reviewed studies highlighted the benefits of using LLMs to automatically solve algebra problems (n = 8). For example, OpenAI’s Cod ex could automatically solve 81% of the provided university-level mathematics problems (Drori et al., 2022). Personalized learning was another frequently identified benefit (n = 6), supported by LLMs’ abilities to provide context-specific hints and feedback. For example, students showed significant learning gains when usi ng ChatGPT while learning algebra compared to the control group (Pardos & Bhandari, 2024). Other benefits include enhancing user engagement, s upporting instructional design and teaching, and facilitating  automated grading (Hu et al., 2024).  Most studies focused on automatic algebra problem-solving, aligning with the most frequently reported benefit. A comparison of 12 LLMs suggested that GPT-4V achieved the highest accuracy (49.9%) on a math data set, attributed to its reasoning capabilities (Lu et al., 2024).  However, this review found that  LLMs struggled with  certain mathematical reasoning tasks, especially requiring proofs or complex computation (Drori et al., 2022); were often unreliable and inconsistent  (e.g., GPT-3.5 generated misleading or incorrect feedback for 32% of problem s; Pardos & Bhandari, 2024); and had low accuracy on some algebra problems, with ChatGPT performing below the level of an average graduate student (Frieder et al., 2024).

What aspects were evaluated when assessing LLM performance in algebra, and how?

Both quantitative and qualitative methods evaluated LLM performance in algebra, with four studies combining them. In qualitative evaluations, studies often created task-specific rubrics. For instance, some focused on aligning generated teaching plans with the technological pedagogical content knowledge (TPACK) theory (Hu et al., 2024), whereas others focused on self-regulation processes (Rigaud Téllez et al., 2024). However, the diverse evaluations made it difficult to compare LLM performance across stud ies. Zero-shot prompting was the most frequently used prompting method (n = 11), suggesting that LLMs were often used without task-related examples (Jia et al., 2024). However, providing task-related information improved LLM performance by reducing failure rates from 84% to 20% in prior studies (Plevris et al., 2 023), suggesting that LLM applications could consider alternatives to  zero-shot prompting.  LLMs performed worse in algebra than in  statistics (Hu et al., 2024), elementary arithmetic (Frieder et al., 2024), and scientific or statistical reasoning (Lu et al., 2024).

## Contributions and implications

Theoretically, this review summarized the benefits and challenges of LLM applications in algebra and suggested several research directions. First, a comprehensive evaluation framework is needed to evaluate LLM performance consistently to support better comparison and synthesis across studies. Second, further theoretical work is needed to understand how LLMs can be adapted to the specific characteristics of algebra. Third, the differential prompting method performance suggests the need for a deeper understanding of how LLMs respond to different input types. Practically, this review suggests adopting tailored prompting methods to improve  LLM performance. As many studies adopted secondary data, future research should target other education levels to enhance generalizability. Educators and researchers could consider LLMs to facilitate learning and teac hing, given the ir benefits in automated problem-solving and virtual assistance. Addressing the identified challenges with LLMs in algebra could further enhance the effectiveness of LLMs in algebra education, such as difficulties in complex reasoning.
