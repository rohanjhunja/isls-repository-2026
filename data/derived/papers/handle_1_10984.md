# Seeing is Believing? Students’ Evaluation of Visual Misinformation

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As misinformation spreads online, discernment of reliable information is a crucial skill to make informed decisions. This study explores middle-school students’ abilities to evaluate fabricated and manipulated visual representations, and which strategies they use to evaluate visual misinformation. Findings indicate that students partially identified visual misinformation and employed various strategies including visual evaluation, content evaluation, corroboration, and source evaluation.

## Theoretical background

With the spread of social networks and the accessibility of artificial intelligence (AI) tools, there is an increase in the quantity of misleading and false information (Kidd & Birhane, 2023). Students often use the infor mation on the Internet without evaluating its reliability (Breakstone et al., 2021). Students may ignore, superficially review, not consider the source of information, rely solely on surface features such as the website 's name or author, or judge the content based on personal opinion (Bråten et al., 2 018). Students also have limited familiarity with strategies for evaluating information sources (Abed & Barzilai, 2023), often relying on evaluation of surface information features, not searching the web to find  information about the source or to corroborate cla ims (Breakstone et al., 2021).

Most studies have focused on how learners evaluate textual information. Few studies examined how students evaluate visual information (e.g., Instagr am images or TikTok videos), which are predominant sources of information among youth. Images and videos can present learners with unique evaluation challenges. Abed and Barzilai (2023) found that learners focused mostly on visual quality and content when evaluating YouTube videos. While visual quality is important, it is not necess arily a good indicator of the reliability of online  information. Many studies examined reliable or semi-reliable visual information. Few studies have exami ned how school students evaluate visual misinformation such as manipulated videos or images  (Breakstone et al., 2021). Thus, the aim of this study was to examine how students evaluate manipulated and fabricated online videos and images. We asked: How successfully do students evaluate fab ricated and manipulated photos and videos? And whic h strategies do they use to evaluate these photos and videos?

## Methods

The participants were 193 8 th & 9th-grade students from a large comprehensive public school in Israel. The students were randomly assigned by class to evaluat e one of two sets of visual items including one vid eo, one manipulated image, and one AI-generated image. Students  were asked to rate the extent to  which each item is trustworthy from 1 (not trustworthy at all) to 7 (very highly trustworthy) and to explain their answer in terms of trustworthiness and how they evaluated the items. Students could search the Internet if they wanted to. ## Findings

Students were partially successful in identifying visual misinformation items as untrustworthy: most students successfully identified AI-generated images (78.2%), more than half identified manipulated videos (62.7%), and about half of the students successfully pointed to the la ck of authenticity  of manipulated images (51.9%). In applying evaluation strategies, most of the students relied on visual appearance evaluation such as noticing signs of manipulation (e.g., “The video looks like they used  a filter"), paying attention to (im)plausible deta ils (e.g., “The man has three hands”), or simply identifying t he work of AI (e.g., “It looks like AI”). Less than half of the students used content evaluation strategies based o n prior knowledge (e.g., “It’s impossible to see th e moon and sun together”) or plausibility (e.g., "Maybe it rea lly happened”). Across all image types, only a small percentage of the students employed source evaluation (e.g., “ There is no information about this source”), however, this strategy was more often used when faced with manipulated images, perhaps because these were harder to judge. Lastly, corroboration (e.g., “I searched other webs ites") and social validation (e.g., “I looked in th e comments") were rarely used for evaluating visual items (see percentages in Table 1).

## Discussion

Our findings show how students evaluate misleading or fabricated visual information. A key finding is students’ tendency not to evaluate source reliability (e.g., publishers and platform s) or to corroborate information. This echoes previous research, emphasizing the prevalence of judgments based on personal opinion, prior knowledge, or visual appearance rather than systematic sourcing or corroboration  (Abed & Barzilai, 2023). On the positive side, our study demonstrated that already at middle school, students have resources and strategies that can h elp them resist visual misinformation, which stems from their awareness of how images and videos can b e produced and manipulated. However, not all students were sim ilarly able to identify visual misinformation. These gaps point to the need to address challenges in evaluating visual misinformation and to raise awareness regarding how visual information can be manipulated and how sourcing and corroboration can help evaluate images and videos. This work contributes to advancing the nascent research into students’ evaluation of visual misinformation.
