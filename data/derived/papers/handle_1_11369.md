# Good Vibrations: Feeling and Interpreting Haptic Feedback

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We contribute to the field of embodied technology by designing a Multiple External Representation (MER) learning environment that integrates gesture-guided haptic feedback with an interactive computer visualization. A think-aloud protocol was conducted to determine how students use the MER environment to reason about a complex system in biochemistry. We assessed how students interpret MERs and found that haptic feedback supported students’ learning by (1) complementing the visualization, or (2) introducing conceptual confusion. In a few cases, however, the haptic feedback left students’ interpretations unchanged. Our case analysis highlights how the introduction of haptic feedback transformed students' interpretations of the visualization, prompting them to recognize previously overlooked influences.

## Introduction

Haptic technology in STEM education STEM education tasks students to understand natural  dynamic systems through visual-spatial representations (e.g., computer simulations). Visualization technol ogies illustrate some quantities well (e.g., number) but often fail to make others effectively sal ient (e.g., forces). Prior studies demonstrate that  students do not adequately attend to these physical forces when visual feature s prove salient (Lira, 2020). This renders visualizations less effective than they might be if students’ visual perception was guided by modes that convey physical forces. Actions upon physical objects (e.g., plastic models) provide an enticing solution by grounding students’ understandings of visualizations in the body’s sens orimotor system. In our previous study, we found th at after manipulating haptic molecular models (i.e., magnets), students re-represented intermolecular forces through gestures (Tang et.al., 2023).  However, this tangible feedback approach  also present distracting features such as color or texture (Uttal et al., 2009).

Haptic innovations provide embodied solutions for c oupling visualization and motor action through tactile input. Equally critical, haptics afford stu dents opportunities to make symbolic movements in t he form of gestures as opposed to concrete action upon objects. Combining haptics with visualizations therefore a ddresses the limitations of visualizations or concrete manip ulations. By integrating design elements from the abovementioned approaches, we contribute to research on embodied technology in ST EM education by creating a hybrid that offers students an immersive experience with minimal constraints on bodily engagement (Figure 2). Learning with multiple external representations (MERs) Haptic feedback differs from touching everyday objects in that it functions as a form of representation, conveying meaning beyond the tactile sensation itself  (Palmer, 1977). The combination of haptic feedback and computer visualization creates a learning environment with m ultiple external representations (MERs).  Ainsworth’s (2006) DeFT framework (Design, Function, Task) provides co nstructs that highlight the affordances and constra ints within MER environments and identifies design principles for pairing visualizations with haptic feedback. Ainsworth (1999) suggested that there are three key functions of MERs: complementary, constraint, and construct. The complementary function refers to how each representation enhances  the others by presenting unique information. In our design, the haptic techn ology complements the molecular visualization: alth ough the visualization represents individual entities and th eir aggregate mo vement patterns, haptic feedback represents invisible attraction forces. The constraining function occurs when one representat ion limits or directs the interpretation of another. In our design, we examin ed whether haptic feedback and computer visualizati on could mutually constrain each other to shift students' in terpretations of both representations.  Last, the constructing function refers to how MERs support developing a de eper understanding by encouraging learners to integ rate information across representations. In our investig ation, haptic feedback augments the visualization b y allowing students to experience tactile forces, which enables students to construct an understanding of dynamic equilibrium as a moment whereby multiple forces still operate.

## Investigation context––Resting Membrane Potential

We introduce a complex system situated at the inter section of life and physical sciences: the Resting Membrane Potential (RMP). The term "resting" denotes a state  of dynamic equilibrium, where two opposing process es–– electrical and chemical potential––occur at equal rates but in opposite directions. Th e challenge for students lies in recognizing two processes operating at both the individual and aggregate levels within the system. Electrical potential is a physical attraction force, while chemical potential is ion’s diffusion due to their random movement. Specifically, the electrical potential prevents ions from diffusing until they reach equal concentrations. Although computational visualizations effectively represent the random movement of ions, it falls short in representing the attraction force between ions that simultaneously affects their motion. Therefore, we aimed to extend the computational visualization by introducing a haptic glove that simulates the electrical attractive force. Design principles and specifications Molecular visualization and Gestalt principles The computer visualization models how a cell generates RMP (Figure 1). In the simulation, the most important feature is allowing the learner to embody a designated ion 𝐾+ with their hand (𝐾+). Hand tracking is accomplished with the Leap Motion controller which is placed on a desk facing upward below the learner’s hand. It is noteworthy that, as illustrated in Figure 1c, we specifically demonstrated the attractive force between oppositely charged ions by aligning the positively charged K⁺ ions along the extr acellular side of the membrane and the negatively charged Cl⁻ ions along the intracellular side. This visual design is supported by Gestalt Theory, which suggests that individuals perceive visual ele ments as unified wholes rather than as separate com ponents, thereby enhancing the visual salience of complex interactions (Guberman, 2017). Wearable haptic technology and gesture-guidance We designed a wearable haptic glove with Eccentric Rotating Mass haptic actuators. Rotation of the motor causes the housing to vibrate, producing omnidirectional w aves that propagate throughout the device, stimulat ing the skin (Wee et al., 2021). Pairing a glove for visual ization, we used the grid-based approach to simu late haptic feedback. These grids are equally spaced and mapped onto glove, with increasing intensities towards the channel, starting with a minimum of 20% of intensities at th e leftmost and rightmost grids of visualization. As  the user moves their hand from Intracellular or Extracellular towards the chann el, the vibrational feedback increases and reaches maximum intensity, and likewise, it decreases haptic intensity as the hand moves away from the channel. Figure 2 illustrates how the combination of a gesture-based computer simulation and wearable haptic technology enables students to immerse themselves i n the simulated environment while receiving guided haptic feedback. To determine how students’ interpretations of repre sentations change when new representations, such as haptic feedback, are introduced, and how their u nderstanding of RMP evolves as they interact with t his MER learning environment, we asked:

1. How does haptic feedback shift students’ interpretation of the visualization?

2. How does adding haptic feedback to a computational visualization influence students’ reasoning about

multiple simultaneous influences affecting the RMP?

## Method

This study is part of an ongoing larger investigati on. Twenty undergraduate students majoring in Biology and Psychology at a research-intensive university in the United States were recruited through a university mass email. After providing informed consent, participants comp leted a pretest of seven multiple-choice questions assessing prior knowledge of RMP. They then engaged in a think-aloud protocol with an instructor, designed to learn RMP with MERs. The think-aloud protocol consists of two sequential parts: (a) exploring and interpreting the computer visualization alone, and (b) exploring and interpreting the computer visualization while wearing the haptic glove. In part (a), participants were asked to make predictions and explain the visualization without haptic glove. In part (b), participants interacted with the visualization  using the haptic glove, answered the same question s, and additionally reflected on the meaning of the haptic  feedback.  Afterward, participants completed a posttest, identical to the pretest, and were compensated $30. The entire experiment lasted approximately one hour. We conducted a quantitative analysis of students' pre-and post-test scores using an unconditional growth model to assess changes over time. Additionally, a qualitative analysis was performed on students' thi nk-aloud protocols, focusing on their reasoning about RMP and interpretation of the computer visualization. Specifically, we coded and analyzed students’ respo nses to the question: “What does the line-up of charges mean?” before and during the haptic experie nce. This question was selected as it indicates whe ther students recognize the second influence —the electrical potential—responsible for the alignment of charges (as illustrated in Figure  1c). Open coding yielded three categories among students'  responses––attraction, no idea, and others. Students' responses were coded as attraction if they indicated the line-up resulted from attraction between positive and negative charges, no idea if they stated uncertainty about the meaning of the  line-up, and others for any alternative interpretations. Then, we selected an exemplary case that illustrate s a students’ shift in reason about RMP after learning with haptic technology.

## Results

An unconditional growth model was conducted to examine the change in students' scores from pretest to posttest. The fixed effect of time was significant, b=3.0, SE =0.53, t(19)=5.68, p<.001, indicating that students ’ scores increased by an average of 3 points from pretest to posttest. Students’ used haptic feedback to re-interpretate the molecular visualization During the think-aloud protocol, we found that at the initial state of the visualization (Figure 1a), before students saw the alignment of charges, 16 out of 20 students noticed only the chemical potential in the visualization. Thus, they failed to see the electrical potential that operates between charged particles. Then, after observing the Gestalt in the visualization, most of the students recognized the "attraction" between the ions. We then compared students' interpretations  of the “lineup”  while interacting with the computer visualization only versus with the visualization and glove. Figure 3 illustrates the distribution of students' interpretations across the three categories in each  learning phase. Two main patterns emerged in students' explanations. First, some students maintained a con sistent interpretation, identifying the line-up as "attraction" across both learning phases. This suggests that adding haptic feedback did not complement or constrain these students’ interpretation s. In contrast, other students exhibited shifts in int erpretation. Specifically, all students who initially reported "no idea" about the line-up's meaning changed their interpretation during ha ptic learning, with most reinterpreting it as "attraction" and one  offering an alternative explanation. This pattern suggests that, for some students, haptic feedback complements their interpretation of the phenomenon by making le ss obvious aspects, such as the electrical driving force, more conceptually salient (Rau, 2020). Additionally, a small number of students who initially interpreted the line-up as "attraction" shifted to "no idea" during hapt ic learning, suggesting that the haptic feedback introduced some confusion as students attempted to interpret the “represented world” of MERs.

When Penny (pseudonym) interacted with the computer visualization without the haptic glove, the instructor prompted her to imagine what she would feel as a K+ ion moving across the cell membrane: Penny: “I'm not sure. I think it would go a lot slower.  It’s going to be bouncing a little bit slower. And if it was potassium, it would slow down and then join onto the membrane.” In this exchange, she intuitively suggested ions wo uld move "a lot slower," focusing solely on motion. However, she also described ions "joining" the membrane without specifying a cause. As Penny put on the glove and felt the haptic feedback, she began to recognize a second influence. When the instructor asked if she “noticed any kind of potential,” Penny responded: Penny: "There's a lot more vibrations. I almost wonder if i t's... nice around ions, to be in the intracellular fluid. If it's so... buzzy in the extracellular fluid, it could be because there are fewer chloride ions. But I think they'd want to go back to the intracell ular fluid, so there's a lower concentration, or they'd go to the membrane to try and get as close as possible." This exchange illustrates the complementary roles of haptics and visualizations. Penny builds upon naïve intuitions (e.g., “nice around ions”), but in a resourceful wa y. She immediately recognizes the intensity of the vibrations and reflects on the meaning of the haptic feedback in relation to the visualization. In the visualization, she noticed differences in ion concentrations. The haptic feedback complemented this by directing her attention specifically to chloride ions, which she previously  overlooked. Additionally, experiencing stronger vi brations instead of a slower sensation in extracellular fluid created a conceptual conflict, enabling her to construct a deeper understanding by associating the "buzz" with chlori de ions. This emerging shift reflects Penny’s constructing a new understanding, one grounded in how haptic feedback represents distinct forces within the visualization.

## Discussion

We contribute to embodied technology in learning sciences by developing an embodied learning environment that incorporates MERs, allowing students to immerse themselves in a biochemistry phenomenon––RMP. We found that (1) visually representing the attraction force following Gestalt Theory prompts students to notice the influence that they previously ignored. (2) Moreover, within a MER system, haptic feedback and computational visualization mutually constrain and complement one  another, allowing students to construct deeper understanding of RMP. Our findings can be described with the Knowledge-in-Pieces (KiP) framework (diSessa, 1993), which models knowledge as a complex system of interconnected elements (Smith III et al., 1994). Students exhibited both challenges and resources in coordina ting their understanding of RMP with the representa tional resources available to them. By modeling students' knowledge as a system of dynamically interacting el ements that cue and assemble in context, we observed how s tudents connect productive intuitions (tacit knowledge) and heuristics (mental shortcuts) to features of extern al representations such as haptic feedback and comp utational visualizations (Parnafes, 2007).
