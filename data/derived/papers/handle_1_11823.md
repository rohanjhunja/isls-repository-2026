# How Attending STEM Camps for Multiple Years Impacts Students’ Science Identity

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research shows that science identity plays a crucial role in shaping students’ inclination in pursuing advanced STEM degrees and careers. Individuals from historically underserved populations often struggle to see themselves as a ‘science person’ and tend to receive less recognition in such roles from teachers, family members, and peers. This study investigates how persistent participation in out-of-school STEM camps impacts science identity development among high school students. We found that returning participants, progressing from campers to mentors and leaders over the years, exhibited enhanced levels of science identity. Statistical analyses and student experiences from their interviews reveal that long-term engagement, coupled with evolving roles and opportunities to explore novel STEM activities, motivates continued participation and fosters stronger science identities. This persistent participation plays a crucial role in the development and reinforcement of a robust science identity, offering valuable insights for designing programs that can enhance it, particularly for underrepresented groups.

## Introduction

The persistent underrepresentation of students from  historically marginalized groups in higher educati on and career paths within the STEM fields represents a significant and ongoing challenge. According to a 2023 National Center for Science and Engineering  Statistics report, demographic disparities persist within STEM employment sectors. The report highlights that Hispanic and Black individuals are underrepresented in STEM jobs, comprising 20% and 18% of their respective workforce, respecti vely. In contrast, White and Asian populations hold  a more substantial presence, representing 25% and 39%, res pectively. Furthermore, the gender distribution wit hin these fields remains uneven. For example, out of all men, 29% are in the STEM workforce, and out of all women, 19% are in the STEM workforce.  Many factors contribute to this phenomenon, and a lack of perceived science identity among students has been found to be one of the primary factors. For example, Stets et al. (2017) demonstrated in a study that lower levels of science identity among undergraduate students can adversely affect their career choices in STEM fields. Unfortunately, in many instances, t raditional schools cannot offer adequate opportunit ies for students to engage meaningfully with science and technology in ways that can capture their interest and attention. Additionally, students often lack sufficient access  to advanced technological tools, restricting their  opportunities for hands-on experiences in STEM-related domains. In this context, out-of-school-time STEM programs, such as summer camps, can serve as valuable alternatives, of fering essential avenues for practical engagement t hat can enrich students’ experience and involvement in these fields. Out-of-school-time STEM learning experiences have been found to b e effective at supporting youth in improving their STEM identity over short periods of time (Riedinger & Taylor, 2016; Vennix et al., 2018; Baran et al., 2019). Unfortunately, most OST research rep orts on interventions of short duration (National R esearch Council, 2009), yet for many underrepresented groups, who already may possess moderate-to-low STEM identity to start with, it often decreases over time (Robins on et al., 2018). Staus et al. (2021) concluded that short-term interventions may not often have enough impact on the students, but they suggested that if a student goes through a series of experiences subsequent to their initial  educational engagement, it can have a more robust effect. A similar suggestion was provided by Archer et al. (20 20), claiming that programs with a deeper interacti on with young learners that can influence them over signifi cant periods of time, such as for multiple years, m ay prove to be more effective for them in developing resilient science identities. These types of out-of-school experiences are particularly crucial for students from underreprese nted backgrounds, as they often have fewer opportun ities to engage with science through other avenues  during their earlier life stages. Increased amount of  participation of these youths from these populations not only will foster new innovations in STEM or improve their life standards in the future,  but it can also cultivate  a positive sense of self-belief among future generations, inspiring more young people from these backgrounds to pursue STEM fields. In this study, we investigated the impact of multip le attendances at a STEM-related camp on students’ science identity. Following the framework established by Carlone and Johnson (2007), we examined the three interconnected components of science identity: comp etence, performance, and recognition. Our study aims to illustrate how persistent participation in such STEM camps enhances students ’ perceptions of themselves as science persons, as well as shapes their perceived recognition by their peers and mentors around them. Throughout this research, we examined the effects of affording  students’ roles as mentors, teachers, and leaders, as well as opportunities to operate advanced technological equ ipment like 3D printers and laser cutters/engravers, on their development of science identity.

## Research question

In this study, we explore these two questions:

1. How does persistent participation at STEM-related camps influence the development of science identity

in students, compared to those who attend only once?

2. What factors are critical in fostering the development of science identity among students participating in

the camp for multiple times?

## Research context: Out-of-school collaboratory space

Having a dedicated space equipped with various tech nological tools and allowing students the freedom t o experiment with these instruments can have a huge i mpact on the development of science identity. In 20 22, in collaboration with a local history museum and the l ocal school system, we co-established the Mill City Collaboratory (pseudonym), situated within the cent rally located history museum. The MCC is a youth-led makerspace where youth can engage in STEM learning experiences throughout their middle and high school years. The MCC is the first of its kind space in the area in that it serves as a youth-led learning environment where youth learn how to build, design, and make objects (i.e., 3D print, laser engrave, automated farming r obots, automated embroidery, etc.) for themselves, their f amily, and the community. That is, rather than bein g gatekept by adults, which can be seen in many makerspaces, youth are the leaders of the space with adults as empowering agents. Two major programs we run each year are the Februar y Vacation Camp (one week)  and the Summer Camp (two weeks), both of which have been ongoing for over five years. Before establishing this dedicated space, materials and equipment were transported and utilized at the local middle school. Working with the English Language Learning Departme nt and Newcomer Academy program with our partner school district, we strive to recruit youth  who may not necessarily view themselves as ‘STEM-oriented’ individuals. We recruit from Mill City schools that enroll nearly 5,700 students with 60% of the studen t body representing ethnic minorities with Latino/a (45%),  African (Ugandan  mostly) and African American (12%) making up the largest groups. Further, according to  the state Department of Education, 59.9% of  students in the district have a first language that is not English,  62% are considered high needs  (i.e., students with disabilities), and 49% of the student population is considered low income. The recruitment of the campers prioritizes students from traditionally underrepresented groups in STEM fields. For both camps, high school students prima rily take on  mentor/teacher roles and lead some sessions showcasing various technologies, including coding with microcontrollers (i.e., Micro:bit), using 3D printers, laser cutters/engravers, and other technologies  available in the collab space. In both camps, middle school campers typically attend each camp for one week. The main d ifference between the two camps is the preparation stage: high school students undergo a shorter training period before the February vacation camp, whereas for the summer camp, they receive a week of training before taking on leadership roles as teachers/mentors the following week.

## Literature review

The concept of science identity derives from social  identity theory, which is defined as an individual ’s awareness and sense of belonging to a specific social group or category (Hogg & Abrams, 1988, as cited in Stets & Burke, 2000). In the context of science identity,  it can be defined as the perception of an individu al about themselves, as well as recognition by that individu al’s meaningful others as a “science person” (Carlone & Johnson, 2007). A student’s personal experiences wi thin educational settings, at home, among peers, and in the broader societal context contribute to the formatio n of their science identity (Aschbacher et al., 201 0). Gender, race/ethnicity, socioeconomic background, early education, and many other factors significantly influe nce the intertwined personal and social processes involved in the construction of science identity (Carlone & Johnson, 2007; Chen et al. 2021; Dou & Cian, 2021).

Science identity, increasingly referred to as STEM identity, has emerged as a critical factor influenc ing students not only in achieving academic success in science (Chen et al., 2021) but also in shaping the ir broader life trajectories and the career paths they aspire to pursue (Stets et al., 2017). Singer et al. (2020) highlighted that engaging students in collaborative group work withi n authentic learning environments can significantly  help in fostering their STEM ide ntity. Places like MCC can offer students valuable opportunities to be involved in exploring new science concepts and technologies whi le collaborating with their peers, thus contributin g to the growth of their science identities. Rahm and Moore (2016) emphasized that such programs should be strategically designed to offer dynamic opportunities that adapt to participants’ growth, fostering sustained engage ment over time. In our camps, students begin as novice participants and, as they gain experience, take on roles such as mentors, teachers, apprentices, and leaders, provid ing them with progressively evolving forms of parti cipation. Atkins et al. (2018) also reported that mentorship played an important role in forming science identity among the students. Encouragement from family members and fri ends plays a significant role, as documented by Dou  and Cian (2021). In some cases, older siblings introduce younger siblings to such programs, while long-term participation fosters the formation of new friendsh ips among peers. These relationships, in turn, have  an indirect but meaningful impact on the development of students’ science identity. Students from underrepresented minority groups demo nstrate unique and complex trajectories in developing and maintaining their science identities. Typically, students enter middle school interested in STEM but opt out over time or lose their self-esteem toward STEM, and as a result end up not seei ng themselves as STEM-oriented individuals (Staus et al., 2020). Similarly, research has shown  it is not uncommon for youth to avoid or switch out of certain fields due to feelin gs of exclusion and discrimination —or even just anticipated feelings of exclusion, not belonging, and potential discrimination in a field (Astorne-Figari & Speer, 2018). These feelings often deteriorate any positive sense of ST EM identity and, as a result, diminish youth’s pers istence and resilience to pursue a STEM career. However, researc h has also found that a youth’s sense of positive S TEM identity is critical, particularly for an individual from an underrepresented group, who must navigate the inherently unfriendly STEM career trajector y that they may face as they proceed through their career and acade mic life (Chen et al., 2021). Therefore, careful considerati on and intentional design are essential when creati ng STEMfocused camps, particularly for programs primarily serving students from underrepresented families.

## Theoretical framework

For our analysis of students’ science identity, we aligned it with the framework presented by Carlone and Johnson

(2007) for science identity. According to this fram ework, science identity can be described as a dynam ic social

construct, influenced by contextual factors such as  gender or race, and can be characterized by three interrelated and overlapping dimensions. These are: how competent someone is as a ‘science person’ (Competence), whether that person can perform as a ‘science person’ (Perf ormance), and whether someone can recognize herself, and gets recognized by peers and mentors as a ‘science perso n’ (Recognition). Their model is built upon the social ly constructed nature of identity, where family members, teachers, friends, classmates, all contribute to the development of someone’s science identity.

Among the three dimensions, the ‘recognition’ aspect has been identified as a principal component of an individual’s science identity by some of the resear chers in the field. In a study on STEM identities, Rodriguez et al. (2017) focused on asking questions in the inter views based on the recognition component of the fra mework. In another study, Roberts & Hughes (2022) focused o n building external recognition of girls to strengthen their science identity. Avraamidou (2020) asserts in a conceptual paper that both recognition and emotion play crucial roles in the formation of science identity. In a st udy on students’ science identity, Stets et al. (20 17) examined both how students perceive themselves as science pe rsons and how close people in their lives, includin g family members and friends, perceive them in similar terms. This approach effectively taps into the ‘recognition’ aspect of science identity. These studies encouraged us to focus on the ‘recognition’ aspect when designing survey items for the science identity scale. However, in analyzi ng the interviews, we tried to incorporate all thre e aspects of science identity outlined by Carlone and Johnson (2 007), while also integrating the dimension of ‘emotion’ into our analysis from the Avraamidou (2020) paper.

It was noted by Carlone & Johnson (2007) that there can be multiple ways of defining a ‘science person’ and selecting a particular definition may constrain  our understanding of what constitutes a science pe rson. However, the structured nature of an organized fram ework offers a valuable analytical opportunity, whi ch encouraged us to use it in measuring the science identity of students.

## Participants

From Summer 2021 to Summer 2024, students from an u rban school district in the Northeastern United Sta tes participated in our camp programs (each lasting one to two weeks). Of approximately 250 attendees, 227 students completed at least one pre-or post-survey during their initial or subsequent camp sess ions. Among them, 49 students completed at least one survey during both their first-and second-time attendances. Eighteen of these students completed all four surveys: both pre-and post-surveys during their first-and second-time camps. Among those who completed at least one survey in multiple  camp sessions (n = 49), only 16 students were inte rviewed multiple times either before or after the camps. We  looked at the scie nce identity score of those 16 students and found that seven students showed an upward trend du ring their second-time camp attendance compared to their initial participation. The interviews conducted with these seven students were subsequently selected for qualitative analysis in this study to answer the second research question (see above). All of them were females and identified as people of color (See Table 1).

## Research design

This study employs a mixed-methods approach, beginning with statistical analysis of the pre-and post-surveys completed by students to assess changes in science identity scores between first-time and second-time attendees. After identifying the students who attended the cam p more than once, were interviewed during multiple camps, and demonstrated growth in their science identity s core through survey responses, we focused on analyz ing all available interviews of these students to explore further. Data sources Design of survey items The survey items were designed to measure the stude nts’ perceptions of their science identity from mul tiple perspectives—perceived personal identity, perceived recognition by others, and desired recognition. The science identity scale comprised eight items, categorized as follows: ● Perceived personal identity: This was measured by the question, “I think of myself as a science person,” which directly addresses the participants’ self-identification as a science person. ● Perceived recognition by others: This dimension was evaluated through questions about how significant figures in the students’ lives perceive their scientific identity. For instance, “My science teachers/ family members think of me as a science person” assesses e xternal perceptions from educational and family perspectives.

● Desired recognition: Further questions such as “I want my science teachers/ classmates/ friends/ family/ non-family adults to see me as a science person” explore the participants' aspirations regarding how they wish to be viewed by their social and educational circles. The survey items (see Table 2) were informed by the science identity Scale proposed by Vincent-Ruz & Schunn (2018), which contributed to the items in th e first two categories: perceived personal identity  and perceived recognition by others (items 1-3). Additionally, the paper by Stets et al. (2017) influenced selecting our remaining items on desired recognition. We adapted their item, “How do you think that your family members rate you as a student?” to more specifically align with our focus on science iden tity, into items such as “I want my family members to see me as a science person.” (ite ms 4-8). The scale consisted of eight items, with respon ses ranging from “Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree ” on a Likert scale from 1 to 5. This survey formed  part of a broader questionnaire assessing additional constr ucts such as students’ interests, competency belief s, and anxiety. Selection of interview questions From Summer 2021 to Summer 2024, our interview prot ocols evolved as we adapt ed and refined the questions during various camps to better explore different aspects of science identity. Initially, we inquired about students’ engagement in behaviors typical of scientists or en gineers, aiming to examine the ‘performance’ dimens ion of science identity as described by Carlone and Johnson (2007). Additionally, we explored students’ aspirations towards STEM-related careers, which aligns with findings by Stets et al. (2017) regarding the correlation between science identity and the pursuit of science occupat ions. In later stages, our interviews included dire ct inquiries about students’ self-perception as STEM individuals, incorporating the c oncept of self-categorization discussed in the works of Stets et al. (2017) and Vincent-Ruz and Schunn (2018). The questions related to science ide ntity that we asked the students during the interviews were as follows:

1. Do you ever think or act like a scientist or an engineer in your everyday life?

2. Do you imagine yourself being in a job related to STEM?

3. Do you think you are a STEM person?

## Quantitative analysis

The analysis started with independent t-tests to compare pre-and post-camp scores on science identity between the first-and second-time campers (pre-first-time vs pre-second-time and post-first-time vs post-second-time). Given the significant difference in sample sizes be tween first-time campers and second-time campers (200 vs. 40), we then conducted pair wise t-tests to compare pre-and post-camp scores on science identity for these two groups (first-and second-time). To gain a more comprehensive insight into th e development of science identity scores across students’ two camp attendances, we conducted a repeated measures ANOVA. This analysis included 18 students, each of them providing four data points: pre-and post-surveys for both first-and second-time camp.

## Qualitative analysis

Among the 16 students who participated in pre-and post-interviews across multiple camps, we conducted a preliminary analysis of their science identity scor es from survey responses. Seven  students showed an upward trend from their initial pre-camp survey to their second-time post-camp survey. Interviews of these seven students were chosen for in-depth analysis because we were interested in understanding how their ongoing pa rticipation in the STEM camps influences the development of their science identity and what prog ram components serve as prominent factors influencing this. We extracted excerpts from the interviews related to science identity and then analyzed their responses to understand how their an swers evolved over time. The interview questions co uld be answered with yes/no responses, which made the init ial categorization of the data relatively straightf orward. However, in cases of ambivalent responses, these were classified as positive or negative through consensus among the research team members. Fol lowing this categorization, an inductive approach w as employed to identify common themes across the responses. Subsequently, t hese themes were aligned with the theoretical frame work proposed by Carlone and Johnson (2007), which artic ulates three core components of science identity: competence, performance, and recognition.

## Findings and discussion

Statistical analyses From the independent t-tests on pre-and post-scores between first-time and second-time campers, we found statistically significant differences between the s cores. The meandifference between the pre-scores of first-and second-time campers was 0.37 (p < 0.05). On the other hand, the meandifference between the post-scores of first and second-time campers was 0.50 (p < 0.01) (see Figure 1). It is worth noting that the  difference between pre-and post-scores is smaller for first-time campers compared to second-time campers (0.13 vs. 0.26). However, both the pre-and post-scores for second-time campers are much higher than those for first-time campers (3.59 and 3.85, respectively).

However, the sample sizes for first-time and second-time campers differ significant ly, as many students did not return. This raises th e question of whether students with initially higher science identity lev els are more likely to attend a second time, potent ially skewing the results. To address this, we conducted pairwise t-tests on the pre-and post-scores for both groups. While comparing pre-camp scores using a pairwise t-test, a statistically significant meandifference o f 0.39 (p <.001) was observed between first-and second-time campers, showing a medium to large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.66). Similarly, the post-camp scores analysis revealed a statistically signi ficant meandifference of 0.29 (p <.05), showing a small to medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.41) (see Figure 2). The meandifference between the pr e-first-time camp score and post-second-time camp score was 0.47, which was statistically significant (p <.01). The analysis of the graphical data reveals a clear incre ase in science identity scores over time. Insights from the interviews Upon preliminary analysis, we found that three students’ interviews showed a noteworthy increase in their science identity among the seven students analyzed. Another  three students displayed somewhat positive orienta tions towards developing science identity. Conversely, on e student expressed a somewhat negative perception of her science identity, particularly in her final interview.

Two principal themes emerged from their interviews.  The first theme centered on the students having opportunities to engage with new technologies and learn new concepts within the STEM fields. The second theme was about students’ enjoyment and finding inte rest in participating in STEM-related activities. These themes highlight key aspects of motivational factors that contribute to a growing science identity among thes e students. In the following section, we present selected quota tions from the participants to illustrate these themes more vividly. All names mentioned are pseudonyms to ensure the confidentiality of the participants. For instance, Ananya highlighted the influence of e xposure to STEM activities on her future career considerations. When prompted about her potential i nterest in a STEM-related job, she noted, “Probably, yeah, because I think this is the place where I get exposed to a lot of STEM-related stuff, so this would probably play a big part in what I decide!” (Post-summer camp 2024). Similarly, Venessa expressed a growing self-identification with the STEM community, stating, “I feel like I’m getting to that point where I consider myself a STEM person with all these new things that I’m learning, and they seem really interesting!” (Post-February vacation camp 2024) Another participant, Melanie, reported during her initial interview that she never acted as a scientist or an engineer in her everyday life. However, after returning to t he camp multiple times, she expressed a growing int erest in pursuing a career focused on coding and building so lutions to help others. She also mentioned that she  enjoyed engaging in these ac tivities (post-February vacation camp 2023). Moreover, Alex shared  a transformative perspective on her engagement with STEM, reflecting  on her growing confidence and interest while answe ring the question whether she thinks herself to be a STEM person: “I used to say no because I didn’t think I was smar t enough to consider myself one [STEM person]. But I would say yes because I actually lik e learning about all new science stuff, and even though I do struggle, it kind of makes me want  to understand it more and go further into it. So, I think so. Yeah.” (post-February vacation camp 2024) One student, Amelia, who said that she does not consider herself as a STEM person in her final interview with us (post-February vacation camp 2024), came as a bit of a su rprise for us. We tracked her score from the surveys and found out that during her second-time attendance, her post-survey score for science identity was 3 (out of 5). But on the presurvey of her third time participation (during the time she was last interviewed), her score for science identity plummeted to 1.25. For the que stions such as “I think of myself as a science person.” or “I want my science teachers to see me as a science per son.”, she responded with “Disagree”, but otherwise  she responded with “Strongly Disagree” for all other it ems. In a personal discussion with us, Amelia menti oned that through her career discernment process and family discussions she had decided to study law as her career choice, which no doubt influenced her STEM identity. Dou & Clan (2021) highlighted the significant role of ‘sc ience talk’ among close family members in shaping the STE M identity of individuals from marginalized d emographic groups. This finding aligns with the case of Amelia, whose science identity initially increased but su bsequently declined. This incident was not necessarily a bad one, and we believe that Amelia’s continued participation might have contributed positively to her long-term career considerations, perhaps igniting future interest or curiosity in STEM-related opportunities.

Another interesting observation was that several students expressed interest in pursuing careers in STEM fields that were not directly related to the activi ties conducted at the camp. For example, Jocelyn, A nanya, Venessa, and Layla indicated a desire to enter the medical (e.g., doctor) or mental health (e.g., psychiatrist) sectors, despite the lack of any medical science-related activities within our camp’s activities. Th is suggests that participation in the camp may have fostered broader  STEM-related confidence among the students, encouraging them to consider educational pathways in varied STEM disciplines, including medical science. These students participated in the camps over a lon g period, with two joining in 2021, two in 2022, and three in 2023. This aligns with the assertion of Ar cher et al. (2020) that prolonged engagement in educational programs benefits students. Initially, at tendees often engage as passive learners, and we se e minimal growth in science identity during the camp time. However, upo n returning, they took on greater responsibilities, such as being mentors or leaders, and explored new technologies, which likely encouraged their continued participation. Applying the framework proposed by Carlone and John son (2007), the theme related to learning new STEM-related stuff aligns with the ‘competence’ aspect of science identity. As students developed proficiency in utilizing the technologies available at the ca mp, this competence seemed to have a positive influence on their science identity. Similarly, the second theme, which emphasizes enjoyment in STEM-related activities, also plays a crucial role in science identity development as students can find doing science activities fun. These two themes also align with the suggested core features of scie nce identity, ‘recognition’ and ‘emotions’, by Avra amidou (2020). Although the interviews with the seven sele cted participants did not explicitly mention how as suming roles such as mentor or teacher during their subsequ ent camp attendances might have helped in developin g their science identity, that aspect of their second or th ird-time camp may have played a subconscious role, whic h reflects the ‘performance’ aspect of s cience identity. In conclusion, our findings from t he selected interviews suggest engaging students in programs that introduce new STEM-related concepts and activities  helps in developing their self-recognition and external recognition, while making sure students enjoy doing such activities. This can significantly influence the development of their science identity over a longer period.

## Implications and future directions

The findings highlight the importance of designing STEM-related programs for students that prioritize susta ined engagement over extended periods through offering e volving roles and opportunities to explore new and stimulating content. We recommend incorporating dynamic participation struc tures alongside the regular introduction of engaging and innovative STEM activities to enhance student retention. Such approaches can have a profound impact on the development of science ide ntities, particularly among underrepresented minori ty populations, thereby providing them with greater opportunities to access STEM education and career pathways. In the future, we wish to continue investigating th e long-term impact of our programs on the students and hope to find how these programs impact these students’ future career choices and trajectories in STEM fields. Additionally, we aim to explore how these programs can be adapted for broader contexts and diverse populations, ensuring their effectiveness across diverse educational and social settings. These initiatives have the potential to make important contributions to advancing equity and diversity within STEM fields.
