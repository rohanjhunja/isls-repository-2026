# “That should make the student uncomfortable”: Affective Aspects of Disciplinary Expert Attention to Student Thinking

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The Listening Project (TLP) offered professional development in attention to student thinking for university faculty. It was based on K-12 teacher education to support responsive teaching, but with two key differences: the participants in TLP worked in multi-disciplinary groups, and they all had significant expertise and identity in their respective fields. Here we examine affective dynamics in one session, which we chose for its challenging complexity, focused on a student’s quiz from a participant’s course in mathematical proof. We highlight evidence of affect as part of the mathematician’s expertise as well as in the collaborative interactions with two faculty from other disciplines. We present the case, and we discuss considerations for university-level PD in responsive teaching.

There is a long-standing emphasis in STEM education on placing student thinking at the center of classroom instruction. This goal has influenced K-12 teacher professional development (PD) to support practices of noticing and engaging with student thinking in meaningful and constructive ways (Sherin & Han, 2004; Hammer & van Zee, 2006; Sherin, Jacobs, and Phillipp, 2011). Similar interest in student-centered pedagogy has arisen at college levels (Gehrtz et al., 202 2). As in K-12, this interest has begun with a focus on curricula and strategies for elicitation. However, there is further need to understand how faculty listen to the thinking they draw out: When and how do faculty listen interpretively, seeking to understand rather than to evaluate student ideas (Appleby et al., 2024)?

We have begun to study college faculty engagement in The Listening Project (TLP), a PD program designed based on K-12 efforts (Hammer & Schifter, 2001; Sherin & Han, 2004). We have found that the dynamics of TLP sessions can be quite different from those in K –12 projects, apparently for two main reasons. First, unlike most K –12 teachers, most college faculty bring deep expertise and strong professional identity in their respective fields. This can help them recog nize productive beginnings in student thinking, but it can also challenge them to navigate tensions, when student thinking contrasts with disciplinary norms and practices. Second, and in consideration of the first, TLP involved faculty collaborating in multi-disciplinary teams. This allowed experts in one discipline to benefit from the perspectives of colleagues out-of-field (Ajilore et al., 2024; Perl-Nussbaum, Schwartz, & Yerushalmi, 2025).

The nature of faculty expertise is not only epistemic but also affective, in emotional aspects of disciplinary inquiry (Jaber & Hammer, 2016ab). There has been considerable work to examine affective aspects of learning, such as how emotion shapes what learners attend to, how they interpret classroom interactions and how they engage with challenging ideas (Schutz & Pekrun, 2007; Pekrun et al., 2002; Meyer & Turner, 2006). Research into the affective dynamics of teacher learning, however, remains relatively limited. In their review of research on teacher’s emotions in PD learning contexts, Ehlert and colleagues (2025) found that only 28 studie s out of 408 (about 7%) were eligible for their inclusion requirements: that study participants were teachers, the setting was PD, and the study design included data on teachers’ emotions. Some research has begun to address this gap, studying for instance the comfort-building moves teachers offer one another in PD settings (Mahmood et al., 2024); how intense emotions in teacher-coach learning relationships catalyze instructional change (Schneeberger et al., 2023) or how generic praise and the desire to avoid the discomfort of criticism limits the usefulness of peer feedback (Wu & Yezierski, 2023).  Although some work has explored college STEM faculty emotions in the classroom (e.g. Trigwell, 2012; Ramlo, 2025), studies focusing specifically on faculty affect in contexts of their learning are rare. Theoretical perspective: Background and framework Responsive teaching in higher education Research in responsive teaching has argued first for centering student thinking in instructional practice and, second, that to center student thinking, it is crucial to help faculty learn to recognize and engage with it (Robertson, 253 Scherr, & Hammer, 2015). While there is now significant attention in higher education to promoting “active learning” pedagogies (Wright, 2011), including at centers for teaching and learning providing PD in “best practices,” there is limited research exploring how faculty listen to the student ideas they elicit and how they learn to listen in interpretive ways through professional development. Our prior work begins to address this gap by examining how faculty learn to listen to student thinking in constructive ways (Ajilore et al., 2024) and the unique challenges they encounter in learning to listen within multidisciplinary PD contexts (Ajilore et al., 2025). It is in continuation of this research effort that we explore how affect shows up for faculty within these listening PD settings.

Affect in teacher learning Research has established the significant role that affect plays in learning. Danielak, Gupta and Elby (2010) considered affect to mark transitions in the epistemological and conceptual thinking of an engineering student, revealing that patterns in the affective response of the student to their learning corresponded with epistemological shifts. Similarly, Radoff, Jaber and Hammer (2019) identified affective pointers to trace the journey of a student’s relationship with her physics course from feeling anxious  and focused on correctness, at the beginning of the course, to feeling enjoyment and focused on sense-making at the end. They argued that her affective shift was entangled with her shift of epistemological framing.

DeBellis & Goldin (2006), working specifically in mathematics and citing earlier work (Goldin, 1998; McLeod and Adams, 1989 Goldin and Kaput, 1996), conceptualized affect as an “internal representational system” (p.131) that includes “changing states of em otional feeling” (p. 133) as well as attitudes, beliefs and values/morals/ethics. Emotions, in this view, are transient states of feelings; affect is a larger and, in some respects, more stable system. They present this view as a conjecture: “Roughly speak ing, it means that the states of emotional feeling carry meanings for the individual.” For this study, we adopt their view, taking signs of emotions in moments as evidence of larger systems of affect.

In teacher education, research has argued for the critical roles of the affective aspects of pedagogical content knowledge (PCK). Zembyl as (2007) argued broadly that there are three planes of emotional knowledge within teachers’ PCK: an individual plane describes connections to the subject matter; a relational plane describes affiliations with students; and a socio-political plane describes teachers’ emotional characterization of their institutional/cultural context. Other researchers have argued more speci fically that emotions influence teacher’s attention, motivations, behaviors and identity, which ultimately influence their teaching practices (Day and Leitch, 2001; Winograd 2003, Sutton and Wheatley 2003, van Veen & Lasky 2005). More recent works have shown that emotions also influence how teachers frame and participate in collaborative professional development (e.g. Mahmood et al., 2024).  Reviewing twenty-five studies assessing teacher emotions in PD contexts, Ehlert et al., (2025) found that emotions in PD contexts are centered on a range of objects including the PD focus topic, PD implementation strategies, their learning experiences within the PD, and their general job experiences.   Notably, only one of the twenty-five teacher-centered studies focused on college faculty. This is evident of the low attention given to research on how affect shows up in faculty PD contexts.  This study aims to begin to fill this gap.

Ehlert et al.’s (2025) review further revealed a preponderance of qualitative methods in teacher emotion research with affect marked by explicit emotional expressions or implicit emotional cues, verbal (e.g. Yoo and Carter, 2017) and non-verbal (e.g. Chang et al., 2018), in PD learning spaces or in interviews.   This qualitative predominance is also seen in other research on affect beyond the scope of the authors’ review. For instance, Radoff, Jaber and Hammer (2019) tracked the student’s use of emotional expressions (e.g. excited, frustrated) in relation to transitional markers (e.g. at first) as evidence of the nature and factors contributing to her affective transition in her learning. Our work builds on this qualitative foundation. We consider a case of one faculty member in a PD context, using the emotional indicators in his verbal and non-verbal expressions to understand the nature of his affect and its influence on his PD learning.

## Project context

The Listening Project (TLP) ran for six years with funding by the Inclusive Excellence Initiative at the Howard Hughes Medical Institute. It provided faculty PD in listening to student thinking. In TLP, listening means working to understand the meaning students are constructing, generally in contrast to evaluating or directing student thinking. Listening in this sense aligns with “noticing” (van Es & Sherin, 2002), with an emphasis on epistemic empathy (Jaber, Southerland, & Dake, 2018), “understanding and appreciating the cognitive and e motional experience within an epistemic activity” (p. 14). Listening, or noticing, is integral to responsive teaching— 254 practices of encouraging student thinking, engaging with it, and integrating it into collective instruction (Robertson, Scherr and Hammer, 2015).

TLP focused on supporting faculty to engage with student thinking in ways that center on the productive ideas in their thinking, using an interpretive stance focused on understanding students' sensemaking, rather than an evaluative stance focused on assessing its correctness. TLP sessions occurred both in small groups, with 2 faculty participants and a faculty “coach” who served as facilitator, and in large groups, with up to 10 participants and often more than one facilitator. In each session, participants  collaboratively analyzed student thinking from one faculty member’s course, in written work or video recordings. The explicit task was to explore the question, “What are the students thinking?” As needed, the facilitator or coach guided the discussion back to that question, when, as often happened, the focus shifted away from that, for example into assessing student thinking for correctness or discussing more general pedagogical matters. The data for this study comes from a small group TLP session that took place through Zoom. It included 2 faculty participants (Anton and Klaus, both pseudonyms) and one facilitator (the second author). The group was working with written students' responses to quiz questions shown in Figure 1 from an introductory Algebra class taught by Anton. The study focal moment comes from the time when the group was discussing student Charlie’s response shown in Figure 2.

Our analysis began with multiple viewings of the session video, attending closely to both verbal and non-verbal interactions among participants. The first author developed interpretive narratives based on these observations, which the research team came to gether to challenge and refine. While our initial goal was to examine how participants framed their actions at different moments, we noticed the affective dimensions of Anton’s participation. This prompted a deeper focus on how affect showed up in his inte ractions. We concentrated on a focal moment in which Anton’s affect was especially salient in his interactions with the group and with the student’s work, evidenced by his explicit use of emotionally charged language (e.g., “uncomfortable,” “terrible”) and by non-verbal cues such as prosody and body language. To better understand this moment, we analyzed both its immediate interactional context and broader PD context to develop conjectures about the nature of Anton’s affect: why he might be feeling that way; how it shaped his engagement in the listening session, and how it might be shaped by the multidisciplinary PD design.

The affective nature of Anton’s interpretation of Charlie’s reasoning The quiz question and Charlie’s response came from a course Anton described as focused on students’ learning to write proofs around a sampling of topics from multiple areas of mathematics. For much of the session, Anton explained the question for Klaus, whose expertise is in physics and Julia, whose expertise is in science education and biology. After about 25 minutes, the group started to consider Charlie’s answer. Looking first at his answer to question 1, Anton started: Anton: There’s all sorts of issues with (chuckles), like this is, (hesitates) this is…that's terrible notation. What they just did is incorrect. Julia: Okay, tell me more about that. Anton: Well, this symbol should not be, like you shouldn't have this symbol, followed by an equal symbol, followed by the symbol, followed by a cross like that. That’s not…it’s like (hesitates)  it’s like writing (Julia: Okay wait, wait, wait)…It’s bad sentence (unclear words) … Julia: Okay, cool. So now we're gonna go from saying that it's bad to try to understand why they did it like, what are they, what could they be thinking?

Anton thought that the misuse of language could be evidence that the student might not have sufficient mathematical understanding. His statement also contained emotional content —that such misappropriation of symbols “should make people very uncomfortable.” Anton’s reaction to the student work communicated his own discomfort with the student’s notation, evident in both his calling it “terrible” and his tone and pace of voice. He also expressed that the student should feel that discomfort. That Charlie seemed comfortable is what was “raising red flags.” Anton continued to respond with emotionally charged language both to the student’s work and to the group’s discussion of the work.  For instance, in further describing the student’s use of notation to the group, he called it an “abuse.” When the facilitator seemed to be trying to shift him from focusing on the flaw of the notation use in the student’s answer, he defended his stance citing that the “symbol carries heavy meaning.” Later, he reiterated his concern that Charlie was “using notation incorrectly,”  and elaborated, explaining to non-mathematicians: "it makes it sound trivial when you say ‘notation incorrectly,’ but it’s not.” Responding to question 2, Charlie had written “S 0={(0,0), (1,1), (2,2)},” as though S 0 is a finite set comprised of three points. The facilitator heard that as the core of Anton’s concern, that the student did not understand S 0 is comprised of infinite points in that pattern. When she pointed out that the student’s answer to the third question, which showed more than three points in S0 was evidence they may have understood S0 to have an arbitrary number of points, Anton remained concerned: Anton: Yeah. So maybe S₀ having more than 3 should clarify that for me. But it also should, well (chuckles), yes, it also should make them feel like they shouldn't have written what they wrote. But that's okay. 257 The essence of Anton’s concern was not that the student understood the set S 0; it was that the student was not using the mathematical notation to be precise. That the student may have known that S 0 has more than three elements made their writing “S 0 = {(0,0), (1,1), (2,2)}” all the more problematic: This was a course on writing mathematical proofs, in which precision of expression is paramount. Again, he expressed his concern over what the student should feel, if the student were taking up the mathematical practices of proof. The affective nature of Anton’s stance towards the student work and participation in the TLP session is evident in multiple ways. He expressed his concern over the student’s use of notation with emotionally charged language, that it was “terrible,” and an “abuse of notation.” He seemed troubled that the student did not experience discomfort with their use of notation, suggesting a view of affective responses —such as feeling unsettled by unconventional use of notation—as integral parts of disciplinary practice. That is, Anton seems to be communicating that being uncomfortable about certain ways of using notations is part of what it means to be a good mathematician.

In this we recognize a kind of conflict of intellectual practices. TLP emphasizes working to understand the meaning a student is trying to convey. If a student uses a word in a wrong way, for example (Ajilore et al., 2024), listening well means working to understand the meaning they had in mind. Mathematical proof, however, is centrally concerned with notation and terminology having precise meaning. It may seem “trivial” to care so much about notation, but for Anton as a mathematician, it is not. For him, it is at the core of the practice. Anton was noticing that the student was working at odds with the core mathematical practices he was trying to promote. And, we could see, Anton was experiencing difficulty explaining the practice to non-mathematicians. We also recognize an affective conflict, between Anton’s care for mathematical practices and TLP’s striving for “epistemic empathy,” that instructors work to put themselves in the position of students to understand what they are thinking and feeling. With his concern that the student should be "uncomfortable” with his use of notation, Anton had shifted from that empathy to an assessment of the student’s reasoning with respect to a different sort of correctness. To be clear, as with other matters of correctness, this is not to suggest instructors should simply accept what students say and do. It is, rathe r, to suggest that there is essential value in understanding. Anton was recognizing an aspect of Charlie’s engagement that he felt needed constructive instructional attention.

Anton also showed affect in his attempts to explain mathematical practices to non-mathematicians, especially to the facilitator, an education researcher who did not seem to be as troubled about the student misuse of mathematical notation as he was. He soug ht diverse ways to help her see and possibly share his concern, including trying to get her to acknowledge that his concern was not trivial. This case opens up a crucial conversation about how deeply intertwined faculty affect is with their disciplinary expertise. This is in line with prior research (Zembylas, 2007) that has argued a teacher's “emotional ecology” includes the individual plane which involves their “emotional connections to their subject matters” (pp. 358), along with relational and sociopolitical planes that deal with relationships and institutional structures. Anton’s case study gives a view of how this plays out for college faculty. In prior work, teachers’ affect is often discussed in terms of its relational dynamics (e.g. Hargreaves, 2000). Anton’s example highlights a disciplinary aspect, connected to his expertise and identi ty as a mathematician. We anticipate similar dynamics for faculty in other disciplines. While some work in K-12 settings have linked teacher affect to disciplinary expertise (e.g. Mahmood et al., 2024), the research focuses on how teachers’ affect interacts with a relative lack of expertise (i.e. how less experienced teachers navigate the feeling of discomfort in PD spaces). By contrast, Anton’s case highlights how affect reflects and emerges from deep disciplinary expertise.

We argue that in higher education, faculty’ affect is bound up with their deep disciplinary identity and expertise. This insight is essential for teacher education research focused on professional development (PD) for faculty. It is especially critical in interdisciplinary PD settings, which can activate for college faculty a strong sense of disciplinary fidelity (Ajilore et al., 2025). That is, they may see themselves as accountable to the norms and knowledge practices of their discipline, with affordances for what they notice and how they interpret student thinking. Anton’s affective actions were not just personal reactions; they were expressions of his disciplinary commitments. Anton’s attempt to stay true to his discipline’s ways of knowing, and language  standards— especially in the face of the student’s misappropriation of mathematical language and the facilitator’s seemingly low level of concern—shone through his emotionally charged language and visible displeasure. His affect can be seen as a tool for enacting disciplinary fidelity, allowing him to take up the role of the good mathematician in the group.

258 The challenge for responsive teaching is in coordinating disciplinary fidelity with listening and epistemic empathy. In this episode, we see an affective dimension of the challenge. Anton’s mathematical expertise sensitized him to affective aspects of Charlie’s engagement, while at the same time it seemed to challenge him to consider that engagement from Charlie’s perspective. That is, in this moment, his taking the role of the good mathematician seemed in conflict with his taking the role of listener. This tension also carries consequences beyond this moment. Enforcing the norms of good mathematics can also be seen as expressing the “settled expectations” of disciplines, which can function to control and restrict meaning-making practices for students, particularly those from nondominant communities (Bang et al., 2013).  In mathematics instruction, negative affect expressed as dissatisfaction or dismissal of students' ideas can reinforce gendered and racialized hierarchies and contribute to student marginalization (Leyva et al., 2021). Understanding affect as both entangled with Anton’s identity and as having the potential to perpetuate inequities creates tensions for facilitation.  Seeing Anton’s emotionally charged responses not just as resistance against the goals of the PD, but also as expressions of disciplinary expertise is to recognize complexity in the moment. There is tension for Anton, and in other moments other participants, betwe en disciplinary fidelity and appreciating student thinking (Ajilore et al., 2025). This perspective frames his language choice and non-verbal cues as means of expressing his commitment to disciplinary integrity while also illuminating the possible tensions he might be navigating between pursuing his pedagogical goal of correct notation and engaging in TLP ’s goal of attentively and respectfully listening to student thinking. At the same time, there is tension for the facilitator between promoting caring and respect for student thinking, on the one hand, and enacting care and respect for faculty thinking—faculty in this context as learners themselves. This tension is further complicated by differences in power. While the facilitator has some power in a PD setting to set goals and to attempt to steer the conversation, faculty also have the standing and expertise to push back with minimal risk. The power differences are greater in classrooms where a mathematics professor can communicate which forms of thinking and practicing mathematics are valued and which are not.

This case raises questions about how facilitators can navigate faculty affect in PD settings. Perhaps PD facilitators candraw faculty attention to the affective dimensions of disciplinary practices. Noticing the emotional nature of their own disciplinary practice could help them attune to the experiences of students. In previous work (Ajilore, et al, 2024), we documented how a chemist, during a TLP session, leveraged the group’s confusion about a chemistry problem to highlight the disciplinary value in a student’s reasoning. She used the group’s collective affective response to prompt their shift toward greater epistemic empathy towards the student.  Faculty affect, in this way, becomes a powerful resource for helping instructors see the deep disciplinary value in how students construct, communicate, and critique knowledge —even when students’ approaches don’t align neatly with disciplinary expectations. Anton’s case study also exemplifies the challenges of navigating emotionally charged moments. As mathematician, Anton noticed aspects of the student’s work in ways that seemed to be difficult for the non-mathematician facilitator to appreciate. This created a challenge for the facilitator to listen to and understand Anton’s perspective. At the same time, the facilitator recognized Anton’s reaction as aligning with marginalizing discourses she felt compelled to disrupt. Part of the challenge was that the design of this PD, while emphasizing “listening,” did not directly engage with the often-hidden dynamics of power, culture, language, and identities (Warren & Rosebery, 2001). While Anton and Julia negotiated what counted as listening to student thinking, more complex questions about why certain practices are considered good mathematics were left unexplored. This suggests, that in addition to a focus on listening, university faculty may benefit from spaces in which they can more explicitly grapple with the possibility of power hierarchies in their disciplines — conversations that will require careful attention to affective dynamics. This study explores the connection between faculty affect and their disciplinary expertise, especially as it shows up professional development spaces. Using this case study, we argue that faculty participation in professional development can be both cognitive and affective, and that their affect can be an integral part of their disciplinary identity, shaping how they enact their disciplinary practice and commitment. Understanding this connection can help facilitators better understand the complexities of navigating affect in professional development contexts and raises questions about how to design PD that can engage with that complexity.
