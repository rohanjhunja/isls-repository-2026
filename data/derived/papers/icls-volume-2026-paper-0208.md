# The Significance of Narrative Creation for Culturally Sustaining Pedagogy: A Tale of Redesigning the Game Forbidden Island

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how junior high students engage in narrative creation through analog game redesign. Using narrative analysis, we analyze two student-redesigned games to explore how storytelling fosters cultural expression, ethical reflection, and curricular connection. Findings suggest that student-led game redesign supports culturally sustaining pedagogy by positioning youth as creators of meaning. Narrative analysis reveals how collaborative tensions, thematic choices, and design decisions r eflect deeper engagement with identity, community, and academic content.

This study explores how junior high students create stories and narratives in a tabletop game redesign project to engage with academic content and express their cultural identities. Grounded in culturally sustaining pedagogy (Paris, 2012, 2021; Paris & Alim, 2017), we examine how students’ stories  and narratives in their redesigned games serve as vehicles for cultural expression. Stories are self-contained units with a beginning, middle, and end while narrative is the broader framework that connects and gives meaning to stories through structure and context (Alleyne, 2015; Culler, 2011; Halverson et al., 2011). Narrative inquiry concerns itself with  questions of how identity, knowledge, and meaning develop over time and in context. Using narrative inquiry (Bruner, 1983, 1985, 2002; Hamilton et al., 2008; Polkinghorne, 1988, 1991, 1995), we distinguish between narrative analysis – examining individual story construction and its role in shaping narrative – and paradigmatic analysis – identifying patterns across narratives – to compare two student groups that are seemingly similar in composition and backstory.

In this paper, we analyze their game backstories and final designs as narrative artifacts to reveal how youth reinterpret cultural, historical, and ethical themes through storied game co-creation and play. Narrative creation and games research Story is a method by which people “construct reality and make sense of the world around them”  (Young et al., 2015, p. 199), which means stories and narratives are powerful pedagogical tools. While much research explores the importance of storytelling in digital games for learning  (Lim et al., 2014; Naul & Liu, 2020; Young et al., 2015), less attention has been paid to analog games  (e.g., Tabletop Role Playing Games) and even less to analog game redesign projects. Moreover, most educational game studies focus on designing games to deliver specific content, often overlooking the educative potential of student-led game redesign as a site for narrative construction and cultural expression. This is a missed opportunity: storytelling is not only a proven learning modality (Baldasaro et al., 2014; Phillips & Nguyen, 2021), but also a means for students to surface lived experiences, explore identity, and engage critically with curricular content.

## Research design and significance

In our six-month study, diverse students from Canadian classrooms in a mid-sized urban junior high redesigned the cooperative board game Forbidden Island (Leacock, 2010) to incorporate themes relevant to their curriculum, lives, and communities. Regarding curriculum  inclusion, instructions were left undetailed so students would choose how and with which  curricular topics from their classes  to engage. Though our goal was to explore the effects of culturally sustaining pedagogy on a diverse group of students  (Paris, 2012, 2021; Paris & Alim, 2017), we found that story and narrative creation  significantly enhanced student engagement in the project. Students followed a tabletop game redesign process consisting of playing, ideation, and redesign, supported by their teachers and an accompanying workbook (Play, Design, and Learn, 2025). Researchers recorded student interviews. This paper compares the experiences of two groups that created similar games  set in countries where inhabitants must overcome disasters.

## Participant groups and their games

The two groups analyzed in this study, Survival Islands and Escape the Quake, named after the titles of the games they created, were selected because of key similarities in their games and mechanics. These groups were made up of girls who centered their games around real countries (historical and modern respectively) as settings, featured natural disasters as the primary driver of gameplay, and maintained the original Forbidden Island objective of escaping environmental devastation to win.

Escape the Quake  consisted of three girls: Sheera, Samantha, and Ellie. None of the three expressed having familial or friendship ties outside of Canada. Each member actively participated throughout the redesign sessions. Ellie naturally emerged as a leader although decisions were generally made collaboratively. Their game features modern countries represented by tiles marked with national flags (Figure 1a). The central conflict in Escape the Quake  is an impending disaster. To win, players must cooperate to collect all the necessary parts to build a submarine and escape to the water before an earthquake. Survival Islands was made up of three girls named Grace, Saabira, and Shaza, with robust familial and friendship ties to Cameroon, Lebanon, and Sudan respectively. Grace and Shaza were most often present for redesign sessions, while Saabira was frequently absent  (Poppendorf et al., 2024). Their game features historical countries, represented by tiles with iconic landmarks (Figure 1b). The central conflict driving gameplay involves heat waves caused by vengeful, anti-colonial spirits inhabiting these countries. Players participate as the game’s colonizers, working to escape the escalating danger. Grace most often led playtesting and design sessions. ## Research questions and data analysis

Several aspects of the two student groups’ experiences emerged as uniquely significant when viewed through a narrative lens. Initially, we were interested in how the personal stories students shared during the creation process intersected with the narrativ e backstories embedded in their game designs. As our analysis deepened, our focus expanded to explore the critical themes that surfaced not only through students’ storytelling about game creation, but also within the games’ backstories, how those stories s hape gameplay narratives, and the narrative elements embedded in the design process. This led us to ask: How does narrative emerge from story through game redesign, and what might be significant about this process for students? To answer this question, we focus on two types of data: (1) the written game backstories created by each group and (2) the games as narrative artifacts. First, we compared the backstories to understand how students used storytelling to frame gameplay and convey meaning. These narrati ves served as foundations for and products of the redesigned games while also becoming windows into students’ creative thinking, cultural references, and thematic priorities. We then analyzed the games as narrative artifacts, examining stories embodying cultural, historical, and ethical themes to better understand how storytelling supports deeper cultural engagement. Throughout the process, we discussed key findings in weekly meetings of our research team, with senior members providing deeper analytical input  before deciding collaboratively which findings warranted deeper analysis in preparation for this submission.

(1) Understanding narrative elements of game backstories

Our narrative analysis began by summarizing each backstory, focusing on the main story arcs, key characters, the setting, and the central conflict driving the plot. From there, we identified underlying themes, asking what values or ideas were emphasized in the finished narratives, such as survival, community, and resilience. We also looked for evidence of cultural, historical, and personal influences. Concurrently we  considered how the backstories reflected students’ cultural backgrounds or personal experiences, noting overlaps and distinctions in the ways each group integrated these elements. Finally, we examined power dynamics and critical themes, paying particular attention to how challenges were resolved and what these resolutions revealed about students’ understandings of equity, collaboration, and agency. This layered approach provided a rich framework for interpreting the backstories as both creative expressions and pedagogical artifacts. The Escape the Quake backstory (Figure 2a) centers on seismologists who detect signs of an impending earthquake. To survive, players must collaborate across countries to gather parts for a submarine, which will allow them to ride out the imminent natural disaster. Specifically, as the tremors begin, players must, build a submarine, but “[s]ome countries had the [finest] metal in the world, in order to get to that metal [players] need to travel and explore each and every country and build the submarine” to lead everyone to safety. The Survival Island backstory (Figure 2b) follows British “colonizers” in the “80s” who settle on a mysterious island south of Egypt only to uncover a hidden tunnel leading to another world filled with vengeful danger and secrets. Players must “face the consequences of their  actions,” and navigate the island’s challenges, collect hidden treasures, and survive extreme conditions like rising temperatures and drought before escaping. Narrative elements of game backstories We found that narrative analysis of Escape the Quake  and Survival Islands  backstories reveals both shared thematic foundations and distinct storytelling approaches. Both backstories center on high-stakes survival scenarios driven by escalating threats, natural disaster in one and supernatural disappearance coupled with disaster in the other. Each narrative emphasizes exploration, resource extraction, and the urgency of overcoming environmental or existential challenges. Their differences, however, are equally telling. Escape the Quake unfolds in a modern, science-driven context, where global collaboration and scientific ingenuity are mobilized to escape disaster. In contrast, Survival Islands  is rooted in our colonial past, where the protagonists, British colonizers, face the consequences of their exploitative actions, blending supernatural elements with moral reckoning. While Escape the Quake  presents a linear, action-oriented structure focused on collective problem-solving, Survival Islands  offers a layered, introspect ive narrative that critiques power and explores the psychological toll of colonialism.

These differences also reflect varying cultural influences: the former leans toward optimism, with universal themes of teamwork and resilience, while the latter subtly echoes the students’ self-disclosed personal or ancestral experiences with displacement, colonization, and survival  (Poppendorf et al., 2024). Together, these narratives illustrate how students used storytelling not only to design engaging gameplay but also to explore complex social, historical, and ethical dimensions, with Survival Islands tackling deep themes of colonial legacies and lasting harms while Escape the Quake focuses more on unity and science as humanity’s saviors. 1524

(2) Game pieces and game design as narrative artifacts

Our second focus was analyzing how story and overall narrative design manifested in game pieces and rules. We considered the physical game design, the visual design of their rulebook, the function of game rules, gameplay, and how to win.  Both Escape the Quake  and Survival Islands used national symbols, flags and landmarks, to represent different countries. While both games involved movement across locations, Survival Islands embedded geography into its rules, requiring travel from Britain to Egypt  and back, whereas Escape the Quake  focused on mechanical progression, collecting submarine parts without narrative context that situates the significance of place. This contrast extended to their rulebooks: Survival Islands framed players as colonizers with a clear narrative goal and an “Objects and Skills” section, while Escape the Quake emphasized how to lose, lacking a story or narrativedriven objective (Figure 3). These differences reflect distinct narrative worlds: Survival Islands  constructs a historical adventure with moral undertones and supernatural elements, demanding more elaborative context, while Escape the Quake offers a flatter, contemporary survival scenario. Both games use story to shape experience, but Survival Islands more deeply integrates narrative into gameplay and design, bridging a more distant historical setting with imaginative play.

The results suggest that Survival Islands, which developed more detailed and culturally grounded narratives, formed deeper connections to curriculum content about colonial histories. In contrast, Escape the Quake, which placed less emphasis on storytelling  and more focus on game mechanics, demonstrated more surface-level engagement with story as a meaning-making device. Narrative analysis revealed how storytelling and co-created narratives surfaced cultural, historical, and ethical themes in students’ design choices. These stories offer insight into culturally sustaining pedagogy, showing how students reflect on  lived experiences, negotiate meaning, and engage critically with social contexts. In exit interviews, Escape the Quake students described their game as “the best in the class,” citing clear alignment with assignment goals, the speed of their work, and minimal challenges. In contrast, Survival Islands  students reported teamwork struggles and uneven workload, yet expressed pride in creating a game about colonialism. These complexities underscore how storytelling shapes content and reflect the relational and cultural dimensions of learning.

Culturally sustaining pedagogy is “not about offering access to the system as it is, but about transforming spaces toward what they could and should be” (Paris, 2021, p. 373). In this endeavor, storytelling is a powerful mechanism for transforming spaces because it calls on us to consider our collective humanity, relationships, and obligations (Phillips & Nguyen, 2021). Although the power of story has been explored in learning sciences  (see Bruner, 1983, 1985, 2002), our findings invite questions about how learning occurs through storytelling and implications for learning theories focused on motivation, meaning-making, and identity. This study demonstrates how analog game redesign can serve as a powerful site for narrative expression, cultural engagement, and collaborative learning. By adopting a narrative lens, we uncovered how students used storytelling not only to shape their games  but also to reflect personal, cultural, and ethical dimensions of their lived experiences. The contrast between the two groups highlights the pedagogical value of positioning students as creators rather than consumers of educational content.
