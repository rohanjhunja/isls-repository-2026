# Navigating Local versus Colonial Languages of Instruction in Out-of-School Contexts: Insights from a Randomized Controlled Trial in Uganda

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Colonial and local languages shape the complex linguistic environment in most African countries, creating misalignments between language policy goals, local perceptions, and the realities of learning in rural contexts. To understand these differences, we studied a randomized controlled trial (RCT) in a rural region within Northern Uganda, comparing a local versus colonial language as a medium of instruction in an engineering course for out-of-school learners. Key findings showed that both instructors and students perceived students in the local language condition as having faster comprehension of STEM course content, while students from both language conditions encountered literacy challenges and valued cultural cohesion, balancing the desire to appear knowledgeable in a colonial language with the need to respect and contribute to their community through their local language.

## Introduction and background

The linguistic landscape of most African countries is shaped by both colonial and a myriad of local la nguages (Obeng, 2002). However, colonial influences remain dominant in most African  educational experiences. Prior studies investigating local and colonial languages in education show that many students, families, and  educators believe in the connection between colonial language s and higher privilege (Trudell, 2007; Gumbi & Ndim andeHlongwa, 2015; Madaio et al., 2019; Erling et al., 2021; Kukulska-Hulme et al., 2023). Conversely, a frequently shared perspective among students and parents is th at local languages are for intimate use within  communities and home environments, closely tying them to local culture, knowledge, and identity (Trudell, 2007; Wi ldsmithCromarty et al., 2023). As proficiency in colonial languages is generally f ar lower, educational opportunities in colonial languages are not immediately accessible to marginalized regions (Kukulska-Hulme et al., 2023). However, prior studies investigating languages of instruction (LOIs) in African communities and educational systems have shown the potential for students to learn effectively thr ough their local language (Nomlomo, 2007; Piper et al., 2016; Erling et al., 2021). For instance, Nomlomo (2007) looked at classrooms learning in isiXhosa, a South African indigenous language, and English as LOIs in teachin g science to students in grades 4-6 in two primary schools located in the Western Cape. The study showed that learners taught in isiXhosa demonstrated confidence, creativity, positive attitudes, and an understanding of the science content, unlike learners taught in English (Nomlomo, 2007). Furthermore, teaching in local lan guages can improve literacy, even for local languag es without traditional written forms. For example, a  medium-scale randomized controlled trial (RCT) in Kenya showed that including mother tongue interventions in a primary math and reading program significantly improved literacy outcomes for children in their mother tong ue compared to the control group taught in only Eng lish and Kiswahili, particularly in oral fluency and reading  comprehension (Piper et al., 2016). However, paren ts, instructors, and community stakeholders may object to using local languages in learning, perceiving th em as limiting and, hence, offering no meaningful future opportunities (Muthwii, 2004; Trudell, 2007). Alternatively, while there is a continual preference for colonial languages in education amongst students, their families, and instructors, prior work in South Africa has uncovered parent concerns that students, especially children, may develop arrogant attitudes when learn ing in a colonial language (Saneka & de Witt, 2019). These attitudes may connect to linguicism, a concept that refers to the association between power and the language used by a dominant social class, leading to a dichotomy between attitudes towards colonial and local langua ges in which colonial languages are favored in social, educational, and economic contexts (Skutnabb-Kangas, 2013; Saneka & de Witt, 2019). Younger students learning in a colonial language may develop such linguicist tendencies, including a lack of respect for their pare nts and older family members, who may lack educatio nal experience. For instance, Saneka and de Witt (2019) interviewed parents from a low-income area in South Africa to explore their involvement in maintaining the use  of their local language and understand the challen ges young learners face learning in a second language. Several parents reported that their children preferred to communicate in English with them due to exposure at schools, on e parent noting that their children only listened t o their reprimands in English, reporting its association with power (Saneka & de Witt, 2019). Other studies conducted in rural and minority communities in Cameroon, Mali, a nd Zambia have found that some parents fear that fo rmal schooling may alienate their children from their co mmunity, posing a threat to their cultural heritage and pulling their children away into a “superior” world (Trudell, 2007). The literature to date demonstrates misalignments between language policy goals, local perceptions, and realities of learning in a local versus colonial la nguage. Most of these prior studies have investigat ed differences in instructional languages within i n-school populations. Out-of-school learners in rural settings, however, continue to receive insufficient attention given th at their educational experiences are limited due to  lack of educational infrastructure and resources. Within the context of a small-scale RCT in a rural region within Northern Uganda, we compare the learning experiences and att itudes of out-of-school learners enrolled in an engineering education course by analyzing facilitator observati ons and participant interviews. The trial compared  learning with instruction in Leb-Lango, a predominant local language used by the par ticipants of this study, to English, one of the official languages of Uganda. We aim to address the following research questions: RQ1) What are the differences in engaging  with STEM education among out-of-school rural learners learning through a local versus colonial language? RQ2) How do out-of-school rural learners view the practical implicatio ns of learning with a local versus colonial language in their community? The main contribution of this research is to provid e insight into integrating local versus colonial instructional languages for out-of-school rural learners. Findings from facilitator ob servations and student interviews showed that students in the Leb-Lango course were perceived as having faster comprehension of course content, while students from both language conditio ns encountered literacy challenges and valued cultu ral cohesion, balancing the advantages of appearing knowledgeable in a language like English and the need to respect and contribute to their community through the use of their local language.

## Methods and study design

This work is part of a multi-year collaboration with an educational technology company in Northern Uganda. The authors of the paper were not involved in the recruitment, consent, or data collection processes, all of which were conducted by our collaborators in Uganda. The study was approved by a Ugandan ethics committee. With our collaborators, we conducted a randomized controlled trial (RCT) to compare students' learning experiences between instruction in a local language, Leb-Lango, and English, with each language group comprising 20 participants. The RCT took place in a classroom setting accessible by community members and was delivered in person. Students in each language group were split into a morning or afternoon sessio n, each facilitated by one of two facilitators. The course  curriculum guided all students to create  a solar-powered food dehydrator following the engineering design process. All participants were recruited from one district in Northern Uganda, whose population is predominantly of Lango ethnic groups who speak Leb-Lango at home. We recruited and received  written consent from youth and their parents for co urse participation from local community organizations, such as churches. Recruited particip ants were youth aged approximately  12 to 18 who were not currently attending school and who reported that th ey were comfortable in English and Leb-Lango. Participants were then randomly assigned to the Leb-Lango or English-instructed course. Our study required a second roun d of recruitment for the Leb-Lango course, as attendance among registered studen ts during the first week of the course was unexpectedly low. In order to keep the r andomized controlled trial intact, we re-recruited additional participants and randomly assigned the new participants to the Leb-Lango condition. Facilitators documented observations and recorded d aily debriefs on student behaviors, reactions to lesson activities, and levels of engagement in each language course. Facilitators also conducted post-course semistructured interviews with students in each languag e group (English: n = 19, Leb-Lango: n = 20) about their language preferences, beliefs, and course experience. We then conducted a consensus-based analysis (Hammer & Berland, 2014) using an open-ended iterative approach (Corbin & Strauss, 1990) o n written observation notes, transcripts of facilitator debriefs, and English-translated interviews from both language groups. Our research team developed and refined significant themes and insigh ts from these data sources, which we present below.  As we acknowledge that our individual backgrounds as rese archers may influence the outcomes of this study, w e held weekly online meetings with our collaborators and c ourse facilitators in Uganda, sharing updates on fi ndings, gathering feedback, and co-developing data collection materials.

## Findings

This section presents a select number of key insigh ts addressing challenges in the course experience a nd the importance of balancing appearance with knowledge i n English and cultural cohesion with the local lang uage, drawn from a larger set of findings. Quotes from student responses are labeled with their respective I Ds: “E” for students in the English course and “L” for those in the Leb-Lango course. Functional and academic literacy contribute to STEM education In general, both facilitators and students in the L eb-Lango course perceived that Leb-Lango learners had a faster and better understanding of the course content, attributed to students learning in a language most familiar to them. Several students in the Leb-Lango course reported that it was easy to understand course content “because you are listening to a language you easily understand” (L8). For instance, L49 reported a term, “airflow syste m”, that would have been difficult to comprehend in English, but was easily understood when said in Leb-Lango. Conversely, several students in the English group r eported that, at times, they relied on translations  in Leb-Lango from both instructors and their peers to unde rstand STEM concepts and course instructions. This suggests difficulties in separating linguist ic challenges from learning STEM as noted by Tikly et al. (2018). Facilitators noted that students’ self-assessment of their English skills may diverge from  the proficiency level needed to effectively engage with the STEM course. Facilitators discussed how  though students are knowledgeable in basic conversational English, they  struggled when confronted with terms and course co ncepts in English, particularly related to different food fermentation methods, having difficulty understandi ng abstract ideas such as the disadvantages of these methods, especially compared to students in the Leb-Lango course. This shows that while students may have developed basic communication skills in English, they may find diff iculty with cognitive academic literacy (Cummins, 1979), especially in STEM subjects. Apart from listening comprehension, students in both courses faced challenges with reading and writing. Several students in the English course reported challenges keeping pace with the delivery of course content, which highly impacted their understanding, reading, and writing skills. Some students in the Leb-Lango course expressed some fear of learning in Leb-Lango, with one student sharing that they felt disc ouraged to learn in their local language and anxious as they were not proficient in  reading and writing in Leb-Lango. In fact, several students from the English course highlighted that it is easier to read and write in English, noting that these skills are more challenging in Leb-Lango. Throughout the course, facilitators observed students relying on peers more proficient in reading and writing in Leb-Lango to record notes from course lessons. However,  this student and others from the Leb-Lango course indicated that they improved their rea ding and writing in Leb-Lango without the help of their peers by the end of the course.

Self-image: Balancing knowledge and cultural cohesion through language To address RQ2, we investigated student responses t o questions about their general views of English and LebLango, whether science can be taught in the local language, how their respective instructional language can impact their future, and their preferred LOI in a future STEM course. Students in both English and Leb-Lango courses reported the need to use Leb-Lango to contribute to their community, enabling them to be effective inst ructors and contributors in their community. Studen ts from both language courses associated their STEM course experience with how they can teach village farmers through their local language, who, according to students, g enerally are not proficient in English. They found Leb-Lango to be more accessible for teaching solar food drying techniques and relevant skills to farmers in their community. When asked if science can be taught in Leb-Lango, E47 reported that since “most farmers use Leb-Lango,” it can facilitate effective teaching about solar food drye rs to farmers. L76 reported that “[science] can als o be taught to farmers who don’t understand English” as they also “need to be taught how to dry food.” However, students in the English course emphasized English as a language that conveys intelligence and prestige. When asked about the general importance o f English, most students associated English with li fe advantages beyond their community, consistent with prior literature. These advantages involved the nee d for English to communicate outside their community, ran ging from leaving their village for errands and goi ng to Kampala (the capital of Uganda) to interacting with foreign workers and attaining important future opportunities. When asked how learning in English would impact the ir future, students generally associated English wi th privileged opportunities related to public work and  jobs, entrepreneurship, mentorship, engagement wit h people from a wider school and town network, and sponsorships for future educational opportunities. Nonetheless, several students in both language grou ps also reported that speaking English in front of their community members is considered arrogant and rude, especially in front of elders. Students in both language courses indicated deliberately negotiating linguicism, balancing the need to appear knowledgeable in English and maintaining respect and responsibilities in their h ouseholds. For instance, when asked about the gener al importance of Leb-Lango, E5 emphasized that they use Leb-Lango with community elders, particularly their grandparents, and cautioned about the use of Englis h in front of elders as they perceive speaking Engl ish as “showing off”. This may imply that students feel ob ligated to use Leb-Lango as a means to position themselves in the middle, mediating the desire to appear knowl edgeable and the need to respect their family and c ommunity members who may have limited to no schooling experience. This is further supported by L76, E10, and E37, who reported the importance of using Leb-Lango to communicate with village members who lack schooling experience, elderly members, grandparents, and children. Another factor contributing to students' negotiation of linguicism is the need to support their households and families. Several students from the Leb-Lango course reported the financial support the Leb-Lango course can provide for their families in the fu ture. For instance, out-of-school learner L67 reported that their father supported their participation as it offered experie nce in taking a course with a non-government organization (NGO), considering this course as “the other form o f studying” that offers “technical knowledge.” The student’s father associated learning such technical skills with the youth financially supporting their home in the future when the father is physically weaker and less able to co ntribute. L76 reported that they could learn to “m ake a technology” through Leb-Lango to support their parents in the future and to  teach their future children and grandchildren how to write in Leb-Lango. Nevertheless, knowledge of the English langu age itself may also help support family and community responsibilities. E25 shared a personal experience highlighting the impor tance of English in supporting their household, where they h ad to communicate with government census enumerator s in English at their home as their mother was not proficient in English. Finally, we asked students in both groups about their language preferences for a similar future course. In the English course, 53% of students interviewed cho se English as a future language of instruction, 37%  chose both English and Leb-Lango, and 11% chose only Leb-Lango. In the Leb-Lango course, only 5% chose English, while 60% indicated both languages and 35% chose Leb-Lango only.

## Discussion and conclusion

There is an evident discrepancy between the ideal o bjectives of national language policies in Africa a nd the practical realities of out-of-school learners. Uganda is one of many countries with national language-in-education policies that incorporate local languages in the primary years of education (Chumbo w, 2005), as local languages can be more effective to learn content. In fact, students from this Leb-Lango course appeared to understand the STEM course content better and more quickly than students from the English course. However, previous w ork exploring local versus colonial languages has shown that many households continually prefer colonial languages as a primary medium of instruction as they are asso ciated with future opportunities and power. From th is study, students in the English course did associate the En glish language with prestigious future opportunitie s beyond their community and a knowledgeable appearance. Rel atedly, previous literature has articulated concern s about young learners developing arrogant attitudes when learning in a colonial language. However, students in this RCT carefully negotiated linguicism in their lives. Ins tead, based on their experience from the course, students expressed the importance of cultural cohesion, indicating the need to balance appearing knowledgeable in English, a language linked with higher intelligence, and showing respect and contributing to their community through the use of their local language, which also connects students more closely to their community by bringing knowledge and literacy well within reach (Trudell, 2009). Non etheless, other findings suggest that there are sig nificant challenges in learning with a local language. Many students in the Leb-Lango course initially struggled with reading and writing, which may stem from Leb-Lango being one of many pre-colonial languages in Sub-Saharan Africa which were traditionally oral and not written (Abdi, 2007). In fact, in our study, the professional translators disagreed about how to express course conten t in Leb-Lango, with some advocating language for easier stu dent understanding and others preferring literal translations, highlighting the challenges of interpreting STEM content in a local language.

Creating equitable national language policies for e ducation that effectively address the needs of all learners—especially out-of-school learners —remains a challenge. Educational technology compani es are similarly confronted with difficult decisions about instructional language use, weighing the learning su pport that local languages provide with the higher costs and l imited scalability of local languages. While prior literature articulates tensions between views of local languag es being limited to the community and colonial lang uages providing privileged future opportunities, learners in this RCT had a more negotiated approach to the complexities of language use than what previous literature has shown. Importantly, exposure to Leb-Lango as a language for a higher-level STEM course in this RCT may have influenced l earners to view it as a more appropriate language for future courses. However, this does not imply th at the English course was ineffective for learning.  Possibly, the faster learning support that Leb-Lango provides can complement the accessibility of literacy skills in English. Given this tension, prospective out-of-school learners may struggle to decide which language is most appropriate for their educational experience. Based on our find ings, we suggest a community outreach campaign disseminating the values, benefits, and challenges of learning in a local or colonial language to lear ners and community members.
