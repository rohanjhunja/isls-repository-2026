# Asking people to “think again” can have unexpected consequences

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Asking people to “think again” can have unexpected consequences.

Meeting People Where They Are Can Alter Where They Are Sybille Bruun, Caroline Geithner, & Deanna Kuhn ksb2180@tc.columbia.edu, ccg2155@tc.columbia.edu, kuhn@tc.columbia.edu Teachers College, Columbia University Abstract: Asking people to “think again” can have unexpected consequences. A polarized society has become an ever-greater con cern. As people continue to confine their interacti on to the like-minded, all outside their chosen circle are seen as increasingly more distant, less understood, and less worthy of consideration. Exposure to views of the differently minded would seem the remedy, but it is at its best only modestly successful and risks further polarization, excessiv e certainty and confidence, and reduced complexity of an individual’s thinking about the issue (Fernbach & V an Boven, 2021; Kuhn, 2020; Kugler & Coleman, 2020), in particular the tendency to account for complex phen omena in terms of a single factor (Johnson, Merchan t, & Keil, 2020; Kuhn & Modrek, 2018, 2021; Lombrozo, 2007), “explaining away” others if they are considered at all (Lagnado, 2021; Tešić et al., 2020). A more recent approach s uggests “meeting people where they are,” by engagin g them in discussion of their own view on an issue, by reflecting on, exploring, and clarifying this view. Or, as Adam Grant puts it in a New York Times Op-ed, to Think Again, the title of his book advocating the approach. It e mphasizes careful listening and questioning, getting a person to talk  about the specifics and the implications of their position. The objective is to lead them to a more nuanced, potentially less hardened view. We wanted to try Grant’s recommendations for ourse lves, hoping to make those we talked to less certain in their commitment to their position and more open to  further examining it and considering alternatives,  potentially leading to enriched, more multi-faceted thinking about the issue.  We sought an issue that did not sub mit readily to easy, single-factor solutions and chose this: “What should be done about the problem of young people brought to the US illegally as children; should they be allowed to  stay or sent back ? Law-and-order hard-liners can easily justify their Send-back position based on the single factor of upholding law.  Are progressives more open to thinking further? The very term ‘progressive’ suggests so. Progressiv es favoring the Stay position must work harder to justify this position, and therefore we chose to talk to this group (who dominated their politically progressive urban locale). Stay proponents must bring together two competing sets of considerations –those of the society and its laws and those of an individual who did not knowingly violate them. S ingle-factor thinking that ignores one of these has  not fully addressed the issue. Addressing it requires the richer, more nuanced thinking about a difficult sociopolitical issue that we hoped discussion would encourage. We were therefore surprised by our findings. Participants were 61 community adults approached i n various public places in a large US city. Genders were equally represented and 64% reported having graduat ed from college. We asked individuals to explain th eir Stay position, encouraging them to elaborate, and included these follow-up questions: What about their parents who brought them to the US?  Should they be allowed to stay? What about grandparents who want to keep the family united? What about aunts and uncles and other relatives who would like to stay too? What about others not related but close to the family?

For half the sample (condition E2), these questions were asked first, before seeking explanations. All were also asked how strongly they felt about the issue and how certain they were regarding this position, each on a scale 1-10. Contrary to our expectations, these individuals di d not tackle the complexity these questions invoke, to arrive at a more nuanced, richer position. Instead, they simplified their Stay position. Over 90% favored extending residency to grandparents, over 80% to aunts and uncles, and roughly half to unrelated persons close to the family (Table 1). A majority (73%) offered only a single factor or c onsideration (e.g., “they’ve worked hard”) to support their position. Rather than becoming elaborated and enric hed, their thinking became simplified and single-di mensional. Strength and certainty ratings were uniformly high in both Conditions E1 and E2, with median ratings o f 10 and negligible change following interviewing.

Two additional groups of 50 participants served as  control groups, one from the same community population as the main sample and the other solicited from Mec hanical Turk. Both were similar demographically to the main sample, with genders equally represented and 64% and 56% respectively reporting having graduated from college. Control conditions differed from the experimental conditions in two ways. First, participants were ask ed to indicate ICLS2022 Proceedings 1868 © ISLS their position but were at no point asked to justify or explain it in any way, on either the main question or the followup questions. Second, they had no interpersonal interaction with an interviewer.  In control condition 1, a sheet was handed to passersby in the same locations as those in the experimental condition, asking them to indicate their answers on the sheet and then deposit it in a box that sat nearby. Why did a majority assert that anyone from the aunts and uncles of undocumented youth to anyone else who knew them be granted residence, a view at striking odds with US immigration policy as well as public opinion? “Did our participants really believe what they were saying?” we wondered. Coordinating the perspectives of stakeholders who have competing positions on an issue is a cognitive ly demanding task. In the case of this issue, law-and-order conservatives understandably might be less inclined, but who better than those who champion progress would be mo re up to taking it on? Yet our politically progress ive sample most often took the cognitively easier path of simp ly extending their Stay position to broader and broader groups, rather than invest the mental effort that constructing and justifying distinctions requires.  One explanation is cognitive laziness (Stanovich, 2011). Another is self-present ation. Having taken a position and elaborated on wh y it was the right one, one ought not appear uncommitted or inconsistent, especially to someone unknown. Our intention had been to enrich more than change people’s views. Should our findings be taken as discrediting the strategy of meeting people where they are? We maintain not. Where better to meet them? But we may need to develop more nuanced approaches to the discussions we engage them in. Our findings also have implications for public opinion assessment methods, which may need to become more cautious than is typical.
