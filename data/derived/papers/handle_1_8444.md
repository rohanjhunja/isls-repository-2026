# Learning to Do Substantive Relational Work in Research Practice Partnerships: An Apprenticed Autoethnography

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Relationships are at the core of doing community-based research that works and matters. However, the process through which university researchers learn to engage in relational work is often presumed to be understood rather than explicitly articulated. Building on approaches that conceptualize relationality and critical historicity as central to the success of equity-oriented approaches in education, we present a polyvocal autoethnography where researchers in a RPP featuring YPAR elements learned to engage in what we call substantive relational work (relational work with commitment to caretaking relations and critical bifocality) with community partners. By analyzing everyday moments, we highlight the invisible learning process of a scholar-in-training working alongside a university researcher, program staff, and youth leads at a non-profit organization. We discuss how relational work is both work necessary to move equity-oriented community-based research forward, as well as work that matters in itself for researchers to reconceptualize discursively and materially.

## Introduction

Scholars studying research practice partnerships (RPP) have written on the importance of building relationships with community partners (Coburn et al., 2016) in do ing “work that matters” (Penuel et al., 2015) towar d educational improvement. However, given the often-e xtractive history of research within communities, R PP scholars face the multifaceted, abstract task of “avoid[ing] perpetuating existing systems of injustice” in not only their research foci, but also their relational appr oach with communities (Denner et al., 2019, p. 2). We hope to expand on RPP scholarship centering relational work  as key to doing community-based research that disr upts systems of inequity on practical and structural lev els. We examine two otherwise “invisible” (Jurow, 2 016) elements of relational work: building substantive relationships, and learning how to do so effectively within the academy. We build on the insights of participatory design research, which “attends to the ways in which (a) critical historicity, (b) power, and (c) relational dynamics shape processes of partnering and the possible forms of learning that emerge in and through them” (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016, p. 173-174).

In this paper, we present a scholar-in-training’s a pprenticed learning around what we refer to as substantive relational work. To define this concept, we draw on critical bifocality (Weis & Fine, 2012), and caretaking relations (TallBear, 2019) from critical and decolonial pers pectives. Rooted in indigenous ways of knowing, caretaking relations represents a narrativ e based in “relations and obligations in the here a nd now” (TallBear, 2019, p. 25)—alternate to a settler-base d society that “objectifies the land and water and other-thanhuman beings as potentially owned resources'' (p. 3 2) for relentless “Americandreaming” of production  and progress (p. 24). At the same time, critical bifocality requires researchers’ responsiveness to the ways higher-level structures and inequities hold “attendant consequen ces for the local level with regard to lived-out so cial and economic practices of individuals and collectivitie s'' (Weis & Fine, 2012, p. 3). At the core of subst antive relational work is therefore the idea that centerin g caretaking relations in research is not an abstra ct point of sentimentalism detached from real world concerns—it  is a quotidian political stance centering connecti on and wellbeing in the face of the demands of White supremacy and exploitative capitalist structures. We situate our study within the first cycle of a yo uth participatory action research (YPAR) project in a two-year RPP with a non-profit organization. The RPP goal is to understand through a YPAR approach—which positions youth as researchers with expertise/agenc y (Caraballo et al., 2017)—how youth voice shapes improvement of out-of-school career pathway programs. Authors 1 and 2 joined as adult co-researchers supporting youth leads in this project. We approached our inquiry with the exploratory question: What does it look like in practice for a scholar-in-training researcher to le arn to engage in substantive relational work alongs ide university researchers, youth, and program staff in a YPAR-infused RPP? Overall, we hope to contribute to ICLS2022 Proceedings 1197 © ISLS scholarship which highlights relational work as an “expert educational practice” with “unarticulated assumptions'' to be honored, made explicit, and mentored (Bevan & Penuel, 2017, p. xxii).

## Methods

Distinct from conventional autoethnographies, this apprenticed autoethnography is a polyvocal piece that weaves between first and third person perspectives to capture personal and shared learning reflections. Through the lens of the researcher as an instrument of data collection (Denzin & Lincoln, 2003), we collected field notes and memos from Authors 1 and 2 throughout the 14-week YPAR project cycle to capture everyday interactions and learning (Holmes & Hall, 2020). Authors 1 and 2 wrote field notes/memos during and following each session with each other, program staff (Chloe and Khat), and youth le ads (Emily and Michelle). Memos were guided by the following prompts: What’s coming up for me after th is [event, session, meeting etc.]?; Are there any highlights/healthy tensions?; What am I noticing ab out the relationship or the project this week?  The final data corpus consisted of 32 single-spaced pages of university researcher field notes and memos. Throughout a month-long series of discussion, Authors 1 and 2 analyzed these documents and identified pivotal points of relational work. Using these mome nts as a way “in” to the relationships, we coded fo r critical bifocality and caretaking relations among the selected excerpts and wrote analytic memos to demonstrate how the learning of substantive relational work mapped onto everyday moments. All partners’ names have been replaced with pseudonyms.

## Findings

In this section, we shift to a first-person perspective to walk through reflective vignettes which guided my (Author 1’s) learning of substantive relational work as apprenticed by community practice experts. Our findings 1) show the interconnection of critical bifocality and caretaking relations in the quotidian learning of substantive relational work and 2) illustrate the importance of language, informality, and recommitment as areas with the potential to rupture bifurcated roles and asymmetry in power. We  emboldened segments within each vignette which demonstrated researchers’ consciousness of critical bifocality (in blue) and caretaking relations (in red). At the start: Learning to navigate language, history, and power in research We demonstrate how navigating historicized dynamics through language served as an opportunity for relational learning in the initial phases of the RPP. The following excerpt reflects on an initial meeting where program staff and researchers introduced themselves and their rol es in the project. My (Author 1’s) use of technical  domainspecific language in explaining university research ers’ roles and inviting our partners to engage as c oresearchers—especially when understood alongside ex tractive histories of research in communities—visib ly confused and unsettled program staff members Khat and Chloe. Through mentorship from Author 2, I reflected on the role of language and immediacy in relational work: Talking to Chloe I realized that I was talking [like] how I'm used t o talking in academia. [...] Like Author 2 told me [...] it’s important to know who we are talking with and how the language we're using can affect the way that the people we're trying to build relationships can feel …especially since Khat said  this was the first time they were working with researchers who wanted to learn more about what the y’re doing and actually try to integrate into it. [Author 2 and I] both stayed on after Chloe left (I  think he could tell I was feeling some sort of way about the exchange) and we talked it out. I appreciate [Author 2] being there. I’m starting to see how this big deal in the litera ture sometimes really can be as simple as noticing how someone’s feeling and staying after to talk. Working through these tensions with Author 2 provided me with the space to practice critical bifocality by processing and reconciling into local context th e structural and historicized implications of resea rch language—namely how it has been used to reinforce h ierarchies of expertise and stifle non-researcher v oices (Bevan & Penuel, 2017). As aware of these tensions as I was in the moment, I found myself unable to escape or resolve them. Through discussion and reflection, I came to see the nuanced perspective-taking and awareness of structural dynamics required to navigate how I posi tion myself in relationships with partners through quotidian means like language. Additionally, the immediacy wi th which Author 2 addressed these tensions demonstr ated elements of caretaking relations. Through this inte raction, I realized that in addition to prioritizin g relational obligations in the “here and now” (TallBear, 2019, p. 25), it was of equal importance to consciously expand who I considered as part of my spatial web in relational work. Until this point, I had conceptualized relational work in RPPs as an endeavor directed toward community partners, not fellow university researchers. Author 2 showed me ICLS2022 Proceedings 1198 © ISLS that substantive relationality is an ongoing stance —regardless of roles within a project—that pushes b ack on exploitative rhythms and hierarchies of work.

In the thick of it: Learning to cultivate affective connections through informality RPP scholars detail the importance of establishing collaborative ways of work to advance relationships, but are less explicit about how this process might unfold. In the following excerpt, I (Author 1) reflected on  the importance of informal interactions between program staff and youth leads in fostering collaborative routines: Emily, a youth lead, sat in [a YPAR session plannin g meeting] (totally of her volition after a check-in with Chloe where Chloe casually invited her to the meeting) and gave great feedback. It’s because of those moments of connection between  people outside of meetings that so much is shared, and new doors and routines open which further cement the heart of the work we’re trying to do—in this case, incorporating youth input in more of the planning. I learned that Chloe met with youth leads and participants individually outside of sessions to hear youths’ personal updates and programmatic input, positioning them as fellow contributors and stakeholders. Just as Author 2’s choice to attend those around him was rooted in critical bifocality, Chloe’s commitment to caretaking relations “outside of meetings” created informal spaces for a ffective connection to intentionally rupture conven tional hierarchies between youth and adults. This vignette  demonstrates how informal moments—despite often be ing under-documented, undervalued, and invisible, espec ially in task-oriented environments—can be critical  to establishing deeper routines of collaboration within RPPs. Seeing how committing time to informality and affec tive connection could transform relationships, I texted Michelle, the youth lead I was paired with, to ask if she was free in the upcoming week to check in. This one-off meeting quickly became joyful weekly workin g sessions where we shared life updates and spirite d suggestions for project directions. Throughout our relationship, I saw Michelle step into her role as a caring and passionate team lead who energized and drew out the strengths of our group members to craft an inspired youthled project. From a critical bifocal lens, I saw how taking initiative to carve out informal, affective spaces in the RPP served as a local opportunity to push back on s tructures that neglect the voices of marginalized y outh in social change (Caraballo et al., 2017). This instance highlights how seemingly small actions rooted in caretaking, such as reaching out and cultivating spaces for aff ective connection, is relational work which can tra nsform relationships and make more democratized, synergistic work in RPPs possible. Reprise: Learning to recommit to substantive relational work as a political stance Part of the challenge of relational work is its nonlinear nature, which requires re-commitments and renegotiations between competing priorities and challenges. In this section, we draw attention to an everyday moment of tension between sanctioned, often rewarded markers of progr ess, and invisible relational work. Although not mu tually exclusive, these moments of tension can serve as opportunities to engage in the unlearning and relearning required to make intentional decisions that align with who we aspire to be as researchers—amidst the backdrop of settler notions of production/progress (TallBear, 2019) which have infused social science research. As the end of the cycle drew near, university researchers and youth leads grew increasingly conscious of the tensions between the varied academic and domestic commitments of marginalized youth and the time needed to execute their ambitious YPAR projects. Following discussion, youth leads gave their teams the option to scale down their project deliverables (i.e., fewer interviews and smaller scale surveys). In the following reflection which was crucial to my own learning regarding relational  work, Author 2 reflected on his relational recommi tments throughout the last few weeks of the cycle, how youth lead Emily’s approach catalyzed this process, and how this decision led tonew possibilities in the YPAR project space: I feel like I’ve let go of most of the impulses around scientific validity that I was struggling with in past weeks and have tried to focus on the interpersonal and politi cal foundations of participatory research. Being paired with Emily, who is a college student and a youth lead, I’ve appreciated their approach to prioritizing rel ationship building by building in substantial check-ins during our breakout sessions.  A few weeks ago, I suggested integrating appreciations at the end of our group meetings as a way to build community amongst our groups. I feel like these small gestures have helped to facilitate more engagement from everyone, but especially the younger and more quiet high schoolers in our group. Currently, we are planning the presentation. [...] There seems to be some energy around designing a presentation that isn’t an “academic” presentation, but one that integrates a ICLS2022 Proceedings 1199 © ISLS bit more creativity and interactivity since we have several artists in the group. I think it’s a great way for our group to continue to unsettle t he professionalized and “scholarly” modes of research.

Through critical bifocality, Author 2 sensed the ta cit obligation to commoditize the group’s time and effort toward fulfilling the project to an intangib le standard reflective of larger settler notions of  “Americandreaming” ingrained in our systems and psyches (Tal lBear, 2019). Reinvigorated by Emily’s commitment t o caretaking relations, Author 2’s suggestions demons trate a conscious decision to subvert settler minds ets of productivity (TallBear, 2019). Furthermore, this vignette demonstrated how recommitting to relationships created moments of rupture that further rebalanced asymmetr ies in power while pushing RPP work in innovative directions. Through deep discussion with Author 2, I realized that choosing to balance on the one hand, project deliverables and the dreams of progress they represent, and on the other, relational work with the people in front of me—despite the time commitments, emotional labor, and discomfort that came with it—was one way to unlearn these notions and disrupt systems of inequity on local and structural levels.

## Conclusion, limitations, and implications

By foregrounding substantive relational work and it s learning, we emphasize the importance of not redu cing relationality to a broad discussion about methods, partners, or milestones— “but rather [seeing it as]  a way of living and interacting with the world” (Ritterbusch 2019, p. 1300) that dictates and enables the diffe rent forms, methodologies, solidarities, intimacies, and trajectories social change projects might take. For RPP r esearchers committed to addressing complex issues of equity, substantive relational work in daily practice is critical to doing community-based research that both works and matters—as well as work that matters in itself. A limitation of this paper is its focus on researc her learning. Our insights were possible in large part to the relational sensibilities each of our partners came into the space with, likely influenced by their prior work and community organizing experiences. A future direction is the amplification of community partners’ rich expertise surrounding the dynamics of language, informality, and recommitment in substantive relational work. Additionally, we acknowledge that structural dynamics within the academy may incentivize particular timelines and interpretations of community-based research tha t may dilute substantive engagement with communitie s, encouraging “lite pedagogies” of relationality (Ritterbusch, 2019). As such, our insights prompt a push to design RPPs around relationally sensitive timelines and epistemologies, mentorship, and reflection opportunities to allow for substantive relational work and its learning. As equity-oriented educational improvement continues, we look to perspectives from the margins to deepen our commitments to such work with communities.
