# How the Learning Sciences Can Help Improve Corporate Training

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This conceptual paper describes contemporary issues in one corporate professional development program. Culture, practices, modes of delivery, and external influences are all factors that contribute to the lack of innovation in corporate training. Although the learning sciences community does address learning in many contexts, including corporate training, this area is also underserved in research and the literature. This paper describes how the learning sciences might have a greater impact on corporate training by preparing learning and development practitioners to create more effective learning experiences and communicate more effectively about those experiences. Instructional designers in the corporate world must be able to efficiently create technology-enhanced learning experiences, while still advocating for the adoption and inclusion of relevant theories of how people learn.

## Introduction

Many businesses in the United States have an employee training and development program. Typical categories of training include new employee orientation, complian ce or regulatory training, job-specific skills impr ovement, and “soft skills” training (Colman, 2020). Research has shown a connection between employees’ satisfaction with training and their overall job satisfaction (Jawors ki et. al., 2018; Schmidt, 2007). However, these st udies also found that employees are often dissatisfied with tr aining options and companies can be reluctant to of fer any training to part-time employees or those who are lower in the corporate hierarchy. Although learning scientists have long advocated for a shift in how businesses view training (Barab & Plucker, 2002; Schank et. al., 1999) to focus more on contextualized experiential learning, much of the work in this area continues to be centered on schools or programs that are education-adjacent, such as university-businesspartnership training programs (Bergner & Farad, 2021; Mambrey et. al., 2002; Raes et. al, 2017). This conceptual paper, using an example from my exp erience as an instructional designer at a clinical service organization, argues that the learning sciences can help instructional designers and other corporate training practitioners to re-envision training in the workpl ace and create better learning experiences for all employees, resulting in greater job satisfaction and more fulfilled workers.

## Theoretical Perspectives

Broadly, I am arguing for the learning sciences to examine how we can prepare instructional designers to work in corporate, non-educational settings. Learning scien tists could apply principled practical knowledge, i n those settings, to create “a ladder leading to sometimes radical design improvement” (Bereiter, 2014, p. 4). In addition, I am suggesting that we need to train instructional  designers who are also “learning sciences communic ators,” similar to the Data Communicators proposed by Wolff  et al. (2016), to broadcast the benefits of LS the ories of learning and design frameworks throughout the corporate world. To make this argument less abstract, I will discuss  a specific case using the Social Infrastructure Framework developed by Bielaczyc (2006, 2013) to both design learning experiences and evaluate those learning experiences. The SIF focuses on four dynamic and inter-related elements: culture, practices, socio-techno-spatial relations, and interaction with the outside world (Figure 1). Previously, this framework was used in a  single instructional design (ID) project for corporate training at a service organization for individuals with moderate to severe autism (Duvall, 2020). Although the SIF was originally focused on schools, the elements can be repurposed and refined to provide an approach to improving corporate training experiences as well.

## Methodology

In this conceptual paper, I seek to address the que stion “How can perspectives from the learning scien ces help instructional designers in corporate training programs? To do this, I provide an example of an organization where I worked as an instructional designer. Since 2004, X Autism Services (XAS), located in the northeastern United States, has served individuals with moderate to severe autism. The business’s programs include early intervention for children from one to six years old, an educationally integrated behavioral services program for K-12 students who cannot be adequately served in the public schoo l system, outreach programs embedded in public scho ols, and community integration and employment services for adults. XAS uses an applied behavior analysis (A BA) approach for treatment. In the last five years, the  company’s number of employees has more thandouble d, resulting in an initiative to reframe the corporate  training program. The goals for restructuring were  to improve ICLS2022 Proceedings 917 © ISLS training in terms of resources needed, availability of courses, and impact on employee and business performance. In the next section, I will explain how I applied the SIF to the company’s training program.

## Analyzing the context using the Social Infrastructure Framework

Cultural beliefs Corporate learning programs often include discussions of adult learning theories (Colman, 2021; Gutierrez, 2021), modes of delivery (Masalimova et.al, 2016), and effectiveness (Strother, 2002). XAS is no exception. However, in practice there was little connection between the ories of learning and practical implementation. In 2020, the business purchased and implemented a new learning management system (LMS) after evaluating several options. All of the systems touted individualized learning, peer-to-peer collaboration, and achievement recognition, but there was no discussion in the promotional materials about how these connected to various paradigms of learning. At XAS, as in many corporate training programs, the re was not a well-defined overall paradigm for lear ning— especially surprising, given that the activities of the organization are centered on a behavioral approach to learning. Often, training design and development requestors had little idea of which learners should be included or how learning should be assessed. There were also  different categories of training: regulatory requi rements, knowledge acquisition or “best practices,” practica l knowledge for specific jobs, and so-called “soft skills” training around interpersonal interactions.

Despite opportunities for innovation, the corporate training program at XAS often ended up focusing on things such as completion tracking, particularly fo r regulatory requirements, and return-on-investment  versus costs (Rothman, 2002). There was also a focus on knowledge/learning as a product that could be purchased from others or, if developed internally, sold (Campbell, 2010; Taneja & Goel, 2014). With the introduction of a Quality, Training, and Research (QTR) department in 2018, the organization indicated that it was attempting to shift its culture and training practices. Practices Because XAS began as a small company with fewer than 100 employees, corporate training most often occurred through direct instruction – lectures to all employees that were run by higher level employees in the organization. Typical materials included PowerPoint presentations and paper handouts. More informal training occurred on the job, in the moment and in context.

The goal of the QTR department was to implement an LMS and provide more streamlined instruction, including computer-based, self-guided training. However, early efforts merely replicated the existing practices, with videos of lecturers presenting PowerPoints.

Socio-techno-spatial relations The practices of instructor-led training caused an issue as XAS grew. All-employee trainings for a com pany of 100 staff members require much fewer logistics and resources than for a company of almost 400. But ini tially there was no LMS to offer electronic delivery of tr aining, which meant that other technology had to be  adapted and repurposed. At the same time, the idea of “instructorless” training was such a cultural and practical shift that it impacted how employees, and executive leadership, viewed the relationship between learners/instructors/computers. Many discussions centered on how to ensure that learners were actually “doing” the learning. The most common questions were about how to prevent them from skipping ahead in videos o r looking up answers for assessments.

Interactions with the outside world The population that XAS serves, and the services it  provides, means that there are significant regulat ions, particularly from the state government, that impact  how its training programs operate. Yearly audits o f each program require evidence of achieving training requ irements. XAS also has consultants from private ABA practice and a graduate program at a local university who conduct professional development opportunities. These are primarily, but not singularly, offered to clinicians as opposed to direct support professionals or other support staff.

Instructional design using the Social Infrastructure Framework Bielaczyc (2006) describes the elements of the SIF as interconnected, with the culture serving as the “substrate” (p. 306). That is, culture is infused throughout the practices, socio-techno-spatial relations, and interactions with ICLS2022 Proceedings 918 © ISLS the outside world. Given this, to make significant changes in designing learning experiences, the cult ure alsoneeds to change. There is a challenge here, because changing the culture requires both some form of intervention and the participation/acceptance of the members of the culture. It is a careful balancing act between pushing too far, and not pushing far enough, and requires commu nication tailored to the culture. Hallmarks of the learning sciences such as application of principled practica l knowledge (Bereiter, 2014) and an iterative desig n-based approach can ameliorate these issues.

Next, I will discuss two ID projects and how they c onnect with the elements of the SIF as well as how the broad, corporate level understanding of the SIF elements, especially culture, informed these specific examples. Software simulation training In 2018, XAS adopted a new electronic health record software system. Executive leadership wanted to conduct training for all employees who would be entering client demographic and service records in the system. The original request was for an instructor-led session with an “I do – We do – You do” approach. I suggested an automated step-by-step simulator training, given th at individuals often only need such training resour ces when they are actually using the system as novices. However, as a non-profit with limited financial resources, the only technology available to implement this training des ign was PowerPoint. Nevertheless, both the learners  and executive leadership viewed the resulting “training module” favorably. This project involved and impacted culture (expectations of what comprised training), practice  (method of training), socio-techno-spatial relatio ns (use of software and how/when/where learners used it), and interaction with the outside world (knowledge of how to use PowerPoint to create a software simulator). As a result, it made a small but noticeable shift in how the company viewed training and helped make the case for invest ment in more advanced ID software. At the same time, an early suggestion that we needed no training at all – that users could learn how to use the system on their own from other employees and the materials available online from the software company itself—was unacceptable. Because of the ABA background of the company and its executive leadership, not having some XAS-developed form of a “task analysis” describing, step-by-step, how to complete data entry into the system was not viewed as a tenable solution.

Compliance and ethics training One regulatory requirement was for employees to receive compliance and ethics training. While this is a pro-forma expectation at many companies, XAS wanted to customize its training due to the company’s cultural precedents, as well as the unique population it served. The initial prototype was a brief welcome video from our compliance officer, followed by a recording of a PowerPoint presentation with a voice-over narration. The stated objective for the training was primarily to make em ployees aware of the compliance program and comfort able with identifying and reporting possible issues. I developed a second version of the training, a board game adapted from the classic Clue game. Learners would consider  ethical principles through the lens of employees f rom the various program lines, generate ideas about what unethical behavior might look like, and discuss ways that they could report lapses (phone, email to compliance officer, website, and so on). In a trial of the two approaches, there was a statistically significant difference between the end-of-course knowledge assessments, with those  who played the game performing better. In this case, one interesting feature was the socio-techno-spatial relations. The original request envisioned employees completing th e training on their own with a computer, a departur e from the corporate training culture. However, the game d esign allowed for sharing of multiple perspectives and discussions about ethics, which was more in line with the training’s actual objectives. Another factor, related to interaction with the outside world, was that the co mpliance officer was in an external mentorship prog ram on corporate compliance, which encouraged businesses to adopt novel approaches for their training programs. Finally, this case demonstrates how even “check the box” reg ulatory trainings can become more meaningful learni ng experiences if businesses want them to be.

## Discussion

The SIF provides one example of how the learning sc iences can improve corporate training. There are other theories (such as activity systems) or specific frameworks that could also be applied. Bereiter (2014) argued for the importance of principled practical knowledge in improving education. With corporate ID, practice is often emphasized, while principles – the theories underly ing learning – are overlooked. Regardless of the ae sthetic quality or technical aspects of corporate instructional materials, there is little connection between learning theory and practice.

At the same time, corporate training programs are a  significant source of adult learning. They require businesses to invest resources and hopefully lead to more fulfilled and more competent employees, which benefits ICLS2022 Proceedings 919 © ISLS both the employees and the company. However, corporate training often views learning as a product and focuses on metrics like time spent in training or multiple-choice questions answered correctly. The learning sciences has much to offer corporation s to improve learning. To achieve this potential, though, we must pay careful attention to factors that influence the acceptance of different approaches to learning. We must also equip practitioners, such as instructional designers, with research-based approaches to design that can facilitate their work. Most importantly, we mus t prepare them with the communication skills necess ary to advocate for the use of learning theories and desig n frameworks that reflect what we know about how pe ople learn.
