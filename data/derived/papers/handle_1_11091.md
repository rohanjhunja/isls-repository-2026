## Background

Increased attention has been paid in recent years to learning opportunities afforded by rapidly developing immersive simulated environments. In teacher education, the use of simulations is gaining traction as a way to support the professional learning of pre-service and in-service teachers in various disciplines. Researchers have examined how simulated digital environments can offer spaces for teachers to develop knowledge  about instructional practices and to practice implementing discipline-specific skills without negatively impacting real students’ learning experiences (e.g., Bondie et al., 2020; Cohen et al., 2020; Higgs et al., 2021; Mikeska & Howell, 2020). The provision of low-stakes opportunities for teachers to  explore and practice meeting instructional challenges has also led some technology developers, researchers, and teachers to position immersive simulations as potentially transformative tools that can help address educational inequities, particularly those experienced by culturally and linguistically diverse students. The assumption is that simulations provide harm-free opportunities to engage with authentic challenges experienced by teachers in culturally and linguistically diverse classrooms. However, as we contend in this paper, immersive sim ulations used for teacher education are far from harm-free. In particular, we are concerned by the ethical issu es raised by mixed-reality teaching simulations that require a single human actor to enact all student avatars in a simulation, regardless of the actor’s and students’ racial and ethnic identities – performances that critical race and digital studies scholars have condemned as forms of digital blackface (e.g., McClean, 2020). Drawing on scholarship that identifies how inequities and bias can be embedded in and perpetuated by digital technologies  (e.g., Benjamin, 2019; Noble, 2018), this paper reports on emergent findings from an ongoing study that examines how different stakeholders think about representations of and simulations of racial and ethnic identity in materials designed for teacher learning. In this paper, we examine one sub-question: How do actors playing students in an immersive teaching simulation think about tensions related to acting as a person of a different race?

## Method

This study draws on data from a multi-year research project that aimed to help new teache rs develop critical understandings of their classroom interactions, par ticularly in relation to language, race, and power.  One aspect of this project was the design and implementation of mixed-reality teaching simulations to support new teacher learning. While we selected the simulation technology specifically for the availability of diverse student avatars that reflect students in culturally and linguistica lly diverse classrooms, we noted that the technical design–which requires one actor to enact all of the student avatars –suggests a colorblind logic that deems unproblemati c any performances of ethnicities to which actors do not belong. To gain a deeper understanding of ho w different stakeholders make sense of representations and performances of racial and ethnic identities in immersive teaching simulations, we conducted semi-structured interviews with two simulation actors and a think-aloud session with a third simulation actor who was not available for an interview. One interviewed actor identified as a white woman (Anna) and the other interviewed actor identified as a His panic man  (Alejandro). The actor with whom we conducted the think-aloud identified as a white woman  (Briana). The actors were all hired for our project at different points, and our team developed friendly professional relationships with them through our collaborations. The semi-structured i nterviews with the actors were approximately one hour long and recorded with permission. Both interviews were conducted over Zoom. The think-aloud session, conducted in-person, was approximately 45-minutes long and recorded with permission. While this paper focuses on the perspectives of the actors, our team will also conduct interviews with teachers and teacher educators  to learn how they think about these topics, and, if accepted, we anticipate being able to share findings from those data at the conference. To answer our research question  (How do actors playing students in an immersive teac hing simulation think about tensions related to acting as a person of a different race?), we analyzed the interview and think-aloud transcripts. For our first cycle of coding, our authorship team collectively devised descriptive codes to summarize basic topics of data passages, focusing on one transcript at a time. We further reduced the data through pattern codes that identified themes related to how each actor understood representation and performance of races and ethnicities different from their own, as well as tensions or dilemmas that the actors identified. Following this, we compared patterns across the three actors and coded  for points of overlap and divergence  in their thinking/understandings.

## Results

Findings highlighted a variety of identified tensio ns and challenges raised by the three actors. For example, both Anna and Briana expressed that they felt  uneasy about performing identities that did not fit  their own lived experiences and, moreover, causing unintentional harm with their performances. As Anna shared, But the more that I do this work over a long period of time, I think that sim specialists and those who partner with sim specialists need to think long er and harder about what it means to do no harm because the learner doesn't know who's on the other side.  They don't know how they present, how they identify, what their lived experience is. Briana similarly noted that trying to imagine and then enact a student avatar who was an English language learner surfaced tensions for her related to her decision-making about how the student would present in teaching simulations and how her decisions might not only elicit particular actions from teachers engaging the simulations but also shape what teachers thought about language learners. There were stark differences in identified tensions, some surprising. Alejandro shared that while he experienced some tensions as a simulation actor, he located th ose tensions in technological constraints, stating that performing multiple identities was inevitable due t o the technical design. He contended that  “it’s physically impossible” to create a simulation in which every student will be played by a different actor with similar identities and life experiences. When asked his opinion about simulation performance as a form of digital blackface, he was resisted that characterization of his activity, stating that he was not participating in digital blackface but was rather doing his job by “being an actor – we’re actors, we act.” The variability in the actors’ thinking about and experiences with the simulations suggests the complexity of employing technologies to authentically represent the diversity of teaching and learning in today’s classrooms.

## Implications

As immersive simulations gain popularity in teacher  education, more attention must be paid to the limitations and potential harms of having one actor enact and animate culturally and linguistically diverse student avatars. This study aims to trouble assumptions that these technologies are inherently harm-free tools that support equitable and ethical teaching and learning. In doing so, it also aims to push researchers, developers, and educators to consider how simulations might be intentionally designed and implemented to serve these critical goals.
