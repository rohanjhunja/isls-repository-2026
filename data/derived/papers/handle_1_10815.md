# “An act of a revolution”: Critical Learning Trajectories in a Pre-service Teacher Computer Science Course

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To better understand pre-service teachers' computer science learning, a critical computer science education course was developed for pre-service teachers in the final summer of a credential program at a large American University. In this study, I examine general trends in how pre-service teachers navigate a critical perspective of computer science and how they operationalize their understanding into their practice. Analyzing data gathered from weekly reflections, lesson plans, unit plans, and teaching philosophies, this study found that the ways that teachers incorporate critically conscious computer science into their practice were highly varied. This study presents a deeper understanding of the changes in how teachers learn to incorporate critical consciousness into their curriculum.

## Introduction

As several Learning Scientists have established, the Computer Science (CS) Education that pre-service teachers in the United States receive often lacks a broad di scussion of societal impacts of CS (Yadav et al., 2 022). Typically, programs leave discussions of social jus tice to the general teaching license program and on ly ask students to apply these principles to CS education (Yadav et al., 2022). However, as students navigate  a deeply inequitable world with technology at the forefront, teachers will need t o be equipped with the skills to engage students in discussions that critically examine technology and computing.

In this paper, I analyze the data from a course designed to introduce pre-service teachers (PTs) to critical orientations to CS. The research question guiding t his work is: How do pre-service K-12 teachers navigate the connections between critical consciousness, and computing in a critically oriented computer science course? As established (Philip, 2011), PTs all started with ge neral commitments to critically conscious CS but de veloped more specific visions as the course progressed. Building on theoretical framings of critical consciousness (Freire,

1970) and CS teacher learning (Vakil, 2018), this s tudy seeks to explore the differing dimensions in h ow PTs in

a critically conscious CS curricula conceptualize this way of thinking in their practice.

## Background

While attention to teacher learning regarding the r ole of justice and criticality in CS is relatively new (Ko et al., 2020; Everson, 2023), exploring how teachers learn to think and teach critically in STEM fields is mor e established. Here I briefly review such work and relate it to recent calls for critical CS teaching and learning. Science education has a rich tradition of critical perspectives informing pedago gy and curriculum (Morales-Doyle, 2017; Sengupta et al., 2019). STEM Teacher e ducation programs that involve critically conscious pedagogies have been shown to be benefici al for teacher development yet dif ficult to implement (Crabtree & Stephan, 2022). Other scholars have fou nd that science PTs develop greater agency in disru pting systems of power by investigating how racism and oppression shape society (Morales-Doyle, 2020). This suggests that when PTs engage in discovering the legacy of oppression, their ability to teach critically and engage students in similar discussions is heightened. With CS being  a known inequitable space, this finding provides a  further impetus for understanding emerging critical models of CS education. Computer Science education is theorized as a rich problem space for critical perspectives (Vak il, 2018; Ko, 2020). In response, CS credentialing programs have started to include critical consciousness in t heir curricula, indicating th at this is a new research area (Ko et al., 2023). PTs enrolled in a justice-centered CS course navigated a diverse set of tensi ons between their roles as teachers, the field of CS, and critical interrogations of CS. As a result, some students expressed pessimism, while others expressed increased interest  (Ko et al., 2023). Ko et al.’s early findings show that the trajecto ry to developing proficiency in teaching computing in critically conscious ways needs to be better understood (2023).

## Theoretical framework

To understand emerging and shifting teacher critica l learning trajectories, I bring together theories of critical consciousness (Freire, 1970) and Vakil’s justice-centered approach (2018). Combining these theories with teacher learning further supports the  understanding of how teachers take existing concept s about social justice and pedagogy and transfer (or not) them to a novel context of CS teaching. This study is framed by Paulo Freire’s concept of c ritical consciousness (1970). Freire contends that critical consciousness is a way of interrogating th e power dynamics of the world and then, using that new understanding to take action toward liberation agai nst oppressive forces (1970). Scholars have begun t o apply Freire’s ideas to technology and CS education (Ko, 2020; Vakil, 2018). Vakil (2018) calls for a critic al CS education framework, arguing that it is paramount t hat researchers “develop theories a nd frameworks that challenge dominant trends in this nascent field and  to articulate our own visions rooted in commitment s toward freedom, humandignity, and justice” (p. 47). Answe ring this call, this study will start to illuminate  how justice, critical consciousness and CS intersect and how this framing changes how students learn CS. These dimensions were at the core of this study’s research and cours e design, as well as the analysis of pedagogical do cuments. Teachers readily make broad commitments to social justice, but the ways that they enact that commitment in their practice are nuanced (Philip, 2011). The context of this study involves PTs at the end of a credentialing program, which emphasized social justice as a core value. We can recognize that PTs may complete their teacher preparation programs in their main discipline with pre-existing conceptions of social justice. CS, however, presents a unique and novel context for the PTs where these mental models of social justice may need adaptation or restructuring. The course: Critically conscious python computing Critically Conscious Python Computing is the final course in a supplemental CS authorization sequence within a graduate-level teaching credential and master’s program at a n American university. PTs had previously taken a block-based programming course and an introduction to CS teaching in. The course that is the focus of this study was eight weeks long and introduced students to text-based programming and advanced CS teaching methods. The PTs first learned CS concepts by using Python c oding labs and then, in line with a Freirean perspective (1970), engaged in a critical dialogue about the power dynamics embedded in computing. To facilitate this, PTs read texts from Science and Technolog y Studies as well as Critically Conscious Computing (Ko et al., 2023), which served as the main pedagogical text. F or the final, PTs developed a unit plan for their school’s curriculum, which included a coding lab and a descr iption of the critically consci ous elements. PTs were also asked to write a CS teaching philosophy to describe their vision of CS education. ## Methods

Seven students were enrolled in the course, and all  seven consented to participate in this study. Five  of the participants were elementary school PTs; one was a secondary ELA PT, and one was a secondary STEM PT. All PTs were enrolled in the credential and master’s program at a large West Coast American university. For this study, a multiple-case approach was used to highlight the nuanced perspectives and experiences of PTs as they engaged with the course (Yin, 2014).  This approach allows for an in-depth exploration of the dimensions of criticality that emerge throughout the course. The data came from all coursework, as detai led in Table 1. All student work was inductively coded (Mi les & Huberman, 1994) for themes, with special atte ntion given to teachers’ engagement with computing concepts, how those concepts intersect with issues of justice, and how those intersections manifested (or did not) thr ough the design of specific lessons and instruction al approaches. The first round of coding resulted in preliminary themes of the varying dimensions of criticality that PTs used in their pedagogical designs and vision of  CS teaching. These themes were refined with furthe r rounds of coding that focused on how critical consciousnes s was being concep tualized in focal assignments. The first author, who was the creator of the course, conducte d multiple rounds of interpretative coding while se eking feedback on the coding process from colleagues and participated in consultations with a faculty member to revisit the validity of codes.

## Findings

By analyzing the data that was collected throughout  the course, a fuller picture of how teachers conce ptualize critically conscious teaching was developed. The ge neral findings are only from assignments where PTs were asked to synthesize course content into a lesson pl an, unit plan, or teaching philosophy. All PTs star ted the year with commitments to social justice in their practic e. This was expected as all the PTs had just comple ted their credentialing program where social justice is cente red. What was novel was that PTs varied considerably in how they thought about critical consciousness and CS. R esponses in final assignments ranged from identity and representation issues to examining unintended conse quences of technological development. All of which are centered on cultivating students’ critical consciou sness through a variety of lesson content and desig ns. Here, I describe emergent themes stemming from the data analysis with examples from PT’s submitted work. Un(Intended) consequences of technology Five of the students resonated with the idea that s eemingly benevolent technologies have harmful effec ts on people. In Amelia’s lesson, she guided students through a discussion of the history of emojis and engaged students in questioning whom emojis were designed for and who was not considered (i.e., skin tones were not added until 2015). After viewing Joy Buolamwini’s (2018) poem, “AI, Ain’t I a Women”, and reading about her documentary Coded Bias, Celeste suggested that her elementary s chool could host a showing of the documentary and i nvite parents into a deeper discussion about how AI and t echnology has affected their lives. This theme rela tes to Freire’s critical consciousness (1970) where PT’s lessons questioned implicit assumptions about technology. This theme also demonstrated the variance in how students envision a CS pedagogy where “technology and computing have social and political implications” (Vakil, 2018, p. 37). CS as a tool of oppression A second theme that emerged was how PTs conceptualized CS’s role in contributing to oppression. Four PTs felt that this needed to be at the core of their pedagogy and incorporated this idea into their design or philosophy. For example, Phillis’ teaching philosophy was anchored in recognizing that technology’s “negative potential extends to exploiting organizational self-interest, leveraging race-based algorithms for personal gain, and capitalizin g on the monetization of racial groups” (Teaching Philos ophy, 2023). Similarly, in her teaching philosophy,  Celeste focused on the role young students will play in building the technological landscape of tomorrow. She argued that teaching CS in a way that empowers students to be critical consumers (and creators) of technology is “more than an act o f resistance…it's an act of a revolution” (Teaching  Philosophy, 2023). In line with a justice-centered approach, PTs who centered on this theme found new ways for their students to develop a “critique of u nethical abuses of technological power” (Vakil, 2018, p. 37).

CS history and CS futures Five of the teachers highlighted the promise of sit uating CS in historical context in their midterm or  final assignments. When introducing technology to her fourth-grade students, Amelia sought to “prioritize the or igin of how things came to be, and the intentionality and ethics behind it” (Teaching Philosophy, 2023). An other student, Zara, contended that it was “essential that students understand to think independently, and when they do, they start to dream. And not only dream but want to  solve problems”. For Zara, this was the power and promise of CS and fostering classroom spaces where students  can be the creators of tomorrow (Teaching Philosop hy, 2023). Phillis highlighted the transformative power of CS and resonated with Code for What (Lee & Soep, 2023), where students develop critical computational liter acies to disrupt systems of oppression (Teaching Ph ilosophy, 2023). CD’s final unit plan involved their fourth-grade students reading biographies of computer scie ntists who have resisted oppression and used technology to disrupt inequities (i.e., Joy Buolamwini, Margaret Hamilton). Community and identity Six of the PTs conceptualized ‘community’ in distinct but related ways. Amelia expressed concern about the “CS Culture” (Lee and Soep, 2023) that is often patriarchal and exclusionary. Celeste and Zara saw the need to create CS communities in their distric ts so that underrepresented students would be able to identify with CS and see themselves in the field. CD emphasized that their p ractice is predicated on building a classroom cultu re where students “love their multiple identities and cultur es” and make CS “more accessible to individuals like them” (Teaching Philosophy, 2023). Building on this, Celeste saw CS as a way to build community for their kindergarten students by connecting CS algorithms to classroom rules and practices of collaboration to their classroom values (Teaching Philosophy, 2023).

## Conclusion and discussion

A Freirean approach to CS is predicated on engaging  in dialogue about computing and asking, “for whom”  and “by whom” in order to bring hidden power dynamics i nto the light (1970). PTs incorporated this idea in to their lesson/unit design in complex and unique ways. As C S credential programs add discussions of the social implications of computing into their curricula, understanding how critical orientations manifest in their pedagogy will be vital as this study shows the variance between PTs in their conceptualization of CS. Future work will include a full analysis of all PTs ' critical learning trajectories, how conceptions o f critical CS change over time, and how moments throu ghout the course alter their visions of computer sc ience practice. These findings can inform the emer ging critical CS teacher education field and furthe r build learning theories of how PTs change and navigate critically conscious CS teacher education programs.
