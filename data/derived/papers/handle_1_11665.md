# Pursuing Equity for Youth of Color through Informal Science Programming: A Situative Motivation Study

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Acceptable modes of engagement and knowledge legitimation processes in science classrooms can alienate racially-minoritized students. We employ a situative motivation framework to conduct qualitative analyses of ethnographic interviews from partnership work with a teacher of color and 7 students participating in an informal science program. We discuss connections between teachers’ workplace experiences, teachers’ and students’ equity frames, teachers’ motivation, and students’ science motivation. We illustrate links between participation, racialized identities, and power in science motivation.

## Introduction and theoretical framework

Science education reforms such as the Next Generati on Science Standards (NGSS) aim to provide learners  more opportunities to engage in the actual practices of science and ensure equitable learning opportunities to youth from all backgrounds (NRC, 2012). Yet, the acceptable wa ys of sharing and understanding ideas and what coun ts as legitimate knowledge in science  classrooms may be experienced as alienating or dism issive for students from racially-and/or ethnically-minoritized groups (Agarwal & Sengupta-Irving, 2019; Bang et al., 2012; Newberry & Trujillo, 2018; Tuhiwai-Smith, 2016). Given the current shortcoming s of formal science classrooms, some practitioners divert their efforts toward building informal science learning programs aimed at better serving youth of color. Research on informal STEM learning contexts indicates that informal STEM learning supports students’ interest in STEM, supports students’ understanding of STEM, facilitates connections with supportive ad ult role models, and positively impacts achievement in ways that reduce disparities in STEM learning outcomes based on income (See NRC, 2015 for recommended practices). How different parties involved define equity is important for guiding decision making and further program dev elopment: NASEM’s (2024) report outlines equity frames/approaches in STEM education. These frames h elp illustrate five conceptions of equity and their corresponding views on problems to be solved, proposed solutions, rationales for ethical action, orientation toward history, understanding of power, and possible futur es implicit in each approach. Accordingly, we exten d our previous work (Adams-Wiggins & Tierney, 2021), we offer an introductory situative motivational analysis with the following question guiding our analysis: How do  modes of participation, identities, and relations of power inform how motivation is constructed in an informal  science learning program serving primarily youth o f color? We distinguish our approach from the predominantly sociocognitive oriented motivation research literature, which we argue typically employs methods that analytically split individual from context (Nolen et al., 2015; see Stahl, 2016  regarding unit of analysis; also see Vander Veer & Valsiner, 2012  and Valsiner, 1994 on sociogenesis of individual-level phenomena).

## Method

The present study involved an informal science prog ram in which the equity frame was not clearly artic ulated, although the program primarily targeted students of  color, aimed to help students of color see themsel ves in science, and engaged students in acti vities like identifying topics and materials that w ould be of interest to other high schoolers of color learning science. Through e thnographic observation and qualitative interviewin g, we conducted a situative analysis of motivation in you th of color in an  informal science program led by a Black/African-American teacher. A theory-based sampling strategy (Patton, 2015) was applied to better understand how to support motivation and engagement  in youth of color through an informal science educ ation program housed at a suburban high school in the U.S. Pacific Northwest. Participants included 1 teache r (Mr. Franklin) and 7 students (Jazelle, Madeline, Olivia, Zakariah, Hassan, Adrian, and Brendan) who were i nvolved in the program during the 2022-2023 school year.  Research team members were trained to conduct whole-day ethnographic field observations (Emerson et al., 1995) and conduct in-depth qualitative semi-structured interviews (Patton, 2015) with the teacher and individual stud ents,   we also conducted 2 focus  groups with students. Field observations were conducted four times on random da ys of the program within the last two months of the  school year. During observations, 1-2 research team members made fieldnote “jottings” during classroom activities to be typed into formal field notes at the end of the day.

## Results

Initially, we examined how equity goals were define d within the program. Importantly, our field observ ations of the program showed no explicit discussion of what “ equity” meant aside from distinguishing equity from “equality” using a cartoon to illustrate the differ ence. Accordingly, we expected equity frames in thi s project to remain implicit and evidenced in the teacher’s and students’ stated understandings of their experience s with the program. Both students and their teacher articulate d a need for increased representation of people of color in STEM fields. For example, a Hispanic/Latino boy nam ed Adrian and a Pacific Islander girl name Jazelle were unaware of any representation of people like themse lves in STEM field. Both also indicated that the sc ience curriculum’s disconnection from their own interests as students of color, lack of opportunities for learner agency and student authority, and limited messaging that t hese students were capable all constituted failures  to provide students of color equal opportunities for learning.  Yet, the teacher and students both also referenced  deficit-oriented explanations for the differences in outcomes for students of color. This was in partial contradiction to an extended exchange between two students in which the y articulated a critical view of economic opportunity and schooling given their experiences as children of Ea st and Central African immigrant parents. Next, we identified affordances and constraints in how the community of  learners was able to achieve equitable outcomes. We identified three affordances. First, the program wa s designed to normalize students as local authoriti es, which students found important for their  connection to science and motivation to participate. Second, racial/ethnic representation is also a hallmark of the program, w ith students of color being the main participants, although students from other marginalized or underrepresented groups also were present. Mr. Franklin also had experience as a Black STEM professional prior to becoming a physics teacher. He developed activities where students created materials for existing high school science lessons to make them more interesting to youth from  program participants’ own cultural backgrounds. He also aim ed to increase program participants’ awareness of p eople of color in STEM by having them investigate whether people from their own cultural backgrounds were working in STEM fields. Third, the program provided opportunit ies to safely discuss racialized experiences with s chool; importantly, this had spillover effects on students’ assertiveness in the face of negative racialized experiences in school more generally. Mr. Franklin noted that he d rew on his own experiences to support students’ handling of racism. Finally, we identified two challenges in the prog ram context. First, school-level and workplace factors limited the teacher’s received support. For example, Mr. Franklin described having his teaching succes ses dismissed rather than provoking colleagues’ curiosities, making it  burdensome for Mr.  Franklin to integrate the informal program’s features into his school day science class activities. Second, the teacher’s own understanding of equity at times limited opportunities to engage learners’ more critical perspectives, engage gendered inequities more deeply, and challenge learners’ deficit-thinking. (e.g. challenges recruiting girls, limited particip ation from enrolled girls). While some students also voiced vague or deficit explanat ions for other people of color’s STEM participation, this may have been due to the lack of a clearly arti culated equity frame and the corresponding lack of structured opportunities for students to develop their understandings of how social identities inform the cultural norms of STEM fields.

## Discussion and conclusions

Findings highlight how workplace factors affecting the lead teacher’s own motivations, though invisibl e to students, nonetheless impacted students’ own developing motivation for science learning. The teacher’s first-hand experiences with racism and concern  over the lack of engagement with students’ cultural  identities in the school more broadly led him to design a specific program i ntended to meet those needs, which enabled students  to appreciate the program as responsive to their own n eeds, goals, and valu es. Yet, tensions between the implicit equity frame guiding the teacher’s actions, the subsequent resources offered for students’ meaning mak ing about science as a discipline, and students’ lived experi ences with racialized transnational systems of powe r. Students’ comments suggest they were interested in more in-depth discussion of the culture of science and how it informs which questions and problems are deemed scientifica lly relevant. Learning about the sociopolitical con text of science and technology could have helped students g rasp the social dynamics influencing how scientific  ideas operate in the real world, as well as  clarify stud ents’ ideas about what people from their own cultur es might prioritize in their science education curriculum. T hese tensions limited students’ abilities to connec t science to everyday life for the pursuit of more just futures,  even if students indeed articulated ideas moving t hat direction. In sum, a  this lens on  motivation enabled examination of  relationships between individual and group level processes, including  individuals’ contextualized intentions, goal direct edness, values, cultures, and construals regarding activity in the learning environments as important contributors to forms of participation.
