# Adolescents’ Interdisciplinary Learning in Self-Driven Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Increasingly complex societal and environmental issues demand interdisciplinary solutions. However, there are barriers to implementing interdisciplinary learning, especially in the K-12 context. This study explored whether adolescents (i.e., mainly high-school-age students) chose to pursue interdisciplinary learning topics when they could choose learning topics. Through a content analysis of the application materials of 800 adolescents (Mage =16.59) participating GripTape – a 10-week self-driven learning program, we found that 44% of learners chose interdisciplinary learning topics. The results suggest that adolescents care about complex societal issues and are willing to tackle them using interdisciplinary knowledge in self-driven learning.

## Introduction

Tackling complex real-world issues usually requires  interdisciplinary thinking and knowledge. There is  a need to foster interdisciplinary learning that is concerned with learners integrating knowledge derived from multiple disciplines to deal with complex problems, situatio ns, and themes (Ivanitskaya et al., 2002). The Next  Generation Science Standards suggest that subjects such as sci ence, engineering, and technology are interdependen t, mutually supportive, and together affect human society and natural environments (NRC, 2012). However, it is challenging to implement interdisciplinary learning in the school context considering the organization of current curricula, students lacking competencies to navigate across disciplines, and teachers’ limited experiences and knowledge in supporting students’ interdisciplinary learning. These challen ges make us wonder whether self-driven learning, wh ich usually happens outside of school, promotes interdisciplinary learning topics. Self-driven learning emphasizes students’ own responsibility, agency, and motivations for pursuing meaningful goals (Ferlazzo, 2015). This study aimed to explore whether youth chose interdisciplinary learning topics in a self-driven learning project, GripTape, in which they candetermine the learning challenges.

## Method

The participants were 800 youth (58.25% female, Mage=16.59) accepted by GripTape from 2016 to 2020. The project offers youth between 15-and 19-years old opportunities to pursue learning challenges that they are passionate about. Among the participants, 20.75% identified themselves as Hispanic or Latino; 19.63% as Black or African American; 11.88% as White, Non-Hispanic; 7.88% as Asian/Asian  American; 2.75% as American Indian or Alaska Nativ e; 0.75% as Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander.  The other participants did not report their race i nformation or reported “other”. The data source was participants’  textual responses to the questions “ What topic or skills are you planning to explore during this Challenge”. Guided by the common subject classifications and inductively added new categories based on the data, we included the following categories in the final coding book when analyzing participants’ determined topics: Arts & Humanities, Business & Ec onomics, Clinical, Pre-Clinical & Health, Computer Science, Engineering & Technology, Life Sciences, Physical S ciences, Social Sciences and Law (Times Higher Educ ation, 2020). Two raters independently coded 258 (31.73%) youth responses and achieved a substantial average agreement in all the categories (Cohen's kappa=0.62, Landis & Koch, 1977). The rest of the data were split and coded by the two raters. The learning topics involving one topic area were labeled as single-subject topics while the ones involving two or more topic areas were labeled as interdisciplinary topics.

The following is an example of a single-subject topic that was coded as Arts & Humanities because the participant mainly aimed to explore music and singing in the learning challenge. “I plan on exploring music and singing during this challenge. I want to buy music equipment so that I can record the songs that I have written (and hopefully post videos on Youtube). I am passionate about this because music is all that I know. I have been singing and writing songs since I was a little girl, about 6 or 7 years old, I really want to make it into the entertainment field…” The following example was coded as both Arts & Huma nities and Psychology, and therefore, was identified as an interdisciplinary topic. This participant not only wanted to pursue photography but integrated it with youth self-esteem. “I hope to pursue study in photography and the increase of self-esteem of youth that come from the inner city (using cameras and images), specifically at James Hillhouse school. I’m passionate about this because I was once bullied and didn't have the person in my life besides my mother, (who was alive at the time) to help me build that inner confidence with this project I hope to alleviate some of that pain that students like myself have faced.” This study found that 44% of youth chose interdisci plinary learning topics when they were not constrai ned by curriculum structures and were empowered to make de cisions. Real community or society issues tend to b e authentic and complex and may require the participants to acquire related knowledge, resources, and approaches from several disciplines to make sense of (You, 2017). Interdisciplinary learning benefits learners’ deep understanding, positive affect, and creation. It can provide “relevant, challenging, and enjoyable learning experiences” to learners (Scottish Government, 2008, p. 21) and contexts for creativity to arise (Madden et al., 2013). Schools and positive youth development organizations can consider how to enhance youth’s self-driven learning by incorporating their agency and voice in learning.
