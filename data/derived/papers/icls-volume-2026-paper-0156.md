# Designing Teacher Curricular Customization Tools to Enhance Elementary Students’ Language Use for Scientific Sensemaking

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Elementary classrooms are a critical space for engaging students in expansive science instruction. High quality instructional materials can support teachers in shifting and transforming how science is taught at the elementary grades. However, teachers need to customize the curriculum for their specific contexts. By curricular customizations, we mean adaptations or changes teachers make to personalize or localize the materials. Specifically, we are interested in supporting teachers in making principled customizations to support their multilingual learners (MLs). For this project, we ground our design work in a language for science perspective in which we center the diverse and wide-ranging language resources MLs bring to the classroom. In this paper, we share the designs of two curricular customization tools aimed at increasing multimodality during discussions, and an example 4th grade customization that illustrates MLs’ expansive language use for scientific sensemaking. Problem and importance The elementary grades are critical learning spaces for supporting students’ early experiences with science, which includes developing their expansive understandings around what science is, what the discipline entails, who does (and can) do science, and for fostering students’ science identities (NASEM, 2022). Despite the importance of elementary science, relatively little instructional time is devoted to this subject in US schools; on average 17 minutes per day in K-2 and 23 minutes per day in 3-5 (Plumley, 2019). Compounding this issue is the lack of high quality curricular materials (Davis et al., 2016; NASEM, 2022) and the limited professional learning provided around elementary science instruction (Plumley, 2019), all of which influence the extent to which elementary teachers feel prepared and able to teach science. However, the growing number of open-source reform-oriented curricular materials, many of which are educative in nature (i.e., include features aimed to support teacher learning), offers a potential solution to this pressing area of concern. Yet, research shows that teachers make sense of and take up educative, reform-oriented science curriculum in varied ways (Davis et al., 2017). This variation is influenced by teachers’ instructional contexts, which includes time allotted for science, availability of materials for science instruction and professional learning around how to implement these materials, and the students that teachers serve (Bennion et al., 2024). Furthermore, when considering minoritized student groups, teachers’ curricular decisions can be impacted by ideas they hold around students’ resources and perceived needs (Katsh-Singer et al., 2016). For multilingual learners (MLs) in particular, teachers’ curricular decisions are often influenced by intertwined beliefs around these students' language-related abilities and needs, and how language is used in science to make meaning and communicate science ideas (González‐Howard & Suárez, 2021). Critically, these beliefs can result in curricular customizations that either expand or narrow MLs’ science learning, particularly in relation to the extent students might use their full language repertoire (i.e., linguistic and multimodal resources; Pierson & Grapin 2021) for sensemaking. Combining curriculum with high quality professional learning can support more expansive customizations (McNeill et al, 2026). Thus, what is urgently needed is curriculum-based professional learning materials and resources that can be implemented in ways that correspond to elementary teachers’ varied contexts, and that target teachers’ understandings and abilities to customize science curriculum to enhance, not minimize, MLs’ sensemaking experiences. Design-theoretical basis of customization tools By curricular customization we mean the adaptations or changes that teachers design to personalize and localize the materials for particular students and settings (McNeill et al., 2026; Gerard et al., 2022). Specifically, we are interested in the principled customizations teachers make to expand and support how MLs in their class use language to engage in rich sensemaking about phenomena (McNeill et al., 2026). Grounding professional learning in practice based tools is critical, because “Teaching occurs i n particulars – particular students interacting with particular teachers over particular ideas in particular circumstances” (Ball & Cohen, 1999, p. 10). Partaking in this customization work requires practice based tools and collaborative professional learning to reimagine and incorporate language inclusive and expansive practices to support MLs in science. 1262 In addition to grounding the tools in the classroom practice of enacting curriculum, we also draw on translanguaging research (e.g., Otheguy et al., 2015) to support MLs in science. Specifically, this project is grounded in a language for science perspective (González- Howard et al, 2024) in that we center the diverse and wide-ranging language-related resources that students already have, might adopt and could draw upon to engage in knowledge construction work (Warren et al., 2001). For MLs, this includes the linguistic (e.g., written and spoken named languages) and multimodal (e.g., gestures, drawings, graphing) resources students might mobili ze to sensemake (Pierson & Grapin, 2021). Recent research has shown that not only does such a view provide a fuller picture of how students’ figure out scientific phenomena, but it also has the potential to transform MLs’ science learning in more equitable ways (Suárez & Otero, 2023). As such, our design of customization tools for MLs aims to support teachers in fostering opportunities for students to translanguage, enhancing sensemaking moments so that students use all their linguistic and multimodal resources. Design-examples of customization tools for elementary MLs We present two elementary curricular customization tools for illustration. First, the Evaluating Existing Language Opportunities Tool (Figure 1) supports teachers in examining a science lesson plan to identify and unpack which language resources the lesson currently encourages students to use for sensemaking. This tool helps teachers evaluate these existing opportunities in relation to the lesson level learning goals, and then to consider the extent to which the lesson may privilege certain language resources over others. Figure 1 includes a sample completed tool using a lesson from the OpenSciEd Elementary fourth grade unit 4.4 Structure & Function (OpenSciEd, 2024) in which students figure out the overarching question-How do the structure and function of traits of living things benefit them? Although we use the OpenSciEd curriculum as an example here, the tools can be used across different science curricula. Specifically, in this case the tool illustrates that while the lesson has many opportunities for students to share their observations of the focal phenomena, most of those opportunities take the form of students communicating via talk and writing, and presumably in English. Figure 1: The Evaluating Existing Language Opportunities Tool The second curricular customization tool is the Enhancing Sensemaking Discussions Tool (see Figure 2). This tool supports teachers in enhancing the multimodality of sensemaking discussions through strategies such as adding drawings, inviting and using multiple named languages (e.g., Spanish, English), using gestures and incorporating sensemaking displays; strategies that open up the classroom space to broaden what students can figure out and how they can express their evolving scientific ideas (González‐Howard & Suárez, 2021). Figure 2 includes a sample completed tool used in the context of the previously described lesson. The mock-up of the 1263 completed customization tool shows potential customizations to this lesson for multilingual learners. Specifically, this includes translating the Notice & Wonder handout to add Spanish and encouraging students to use the named language of their choice to capture their ideas and questions around the focal phenomena (Poza, 2018). In addition, the plan includes a focus on using gestures to communicate and engage in sensemaking (Suárez, 2020) as well as utilizing lesson materials such as squirrel images and information cards.
