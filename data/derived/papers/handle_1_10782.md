# Co-Designing an Adaptable Curriculum with Teachers: Affordances and Tensions in Flexible Partnership and Collaboration

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Together with a team of middle school teachers, we have been co-designing flexible, interdisciplinary curriculum focused on the interwoven concepts of personal, community, and ecological resilience. In this paper, we look at teachers’ perspectives on their experiences participating in a series of professional learning workshops and implementing co-designed curriculum in their classrooms. We focus empirical analysis on identifying affordances of this projects’ approach to co-design. We look at the role that flexibility in the co-design process and curricular enactment played in shaping these affordances. We also discuss the emergent tensions tied to this adaptable approach in regard to research and designing high quality curriculum.

## Introduction

Together with a team of middle school teachers, we have been co-designing a flexible, interdisciplinary curriculum focused on the interwoven concepts of personal, community, and ecological resilience. Co-design can support teachers’ agency, build on teachers’ knowledge, and provide new learning opportunities, but often entails challenges related to competing goals, needs, and limited resources. In this paper, we look at teachers’ perspectives on participating in professional learning workshops  and implement ing co-designed curriculum. We focus our empirical analysis on identifying affordances of th is projects’ approach to co-design and the role that flexibility played in shaping these affordances. Several key themes have emerged, aligning with and expanding on learning sciences research on co-design (e.g., Severance et al., 2016). We also disc uss the emergent tensions tied to this adaptable approach in regard to  research and designing high quality curriculum, pro viding insights into how we might maximize the benefits of co-design for both teachers and learners. Co-designing curriculum with teachers Educational co-design is a process where teachers and researchers collaboratively design learning environments, often focusing on the design of curriculum for part icipating teachers to use in their own classrooms. During codesign, a team addresses a concrete educational cha llenge and co-creates a product that takes unique classroom contexts into account (Roschelle et al., 2006). Whe n given a prescribed curriculum, teachers rarely im plement it as designed; they instead adapt it based on their understanding of their students and classrooms, using knowledge of their students’ interests and funds of knowledge  to make adaptations (e.g., Talbert & McLaughlin, 2002). The co-design process can allow for this adaptability while supporting teachers’ agency (Severance et al., 2016). When teams with diverse knowledge and skill sets engage in co-design, the process can support content and pedagogical content knowledge (Leary et al., 2016).

Co-designing curriculum has its challenges. Co-design can be time and resource intensive, and teac hers may not feel they have the capacity to fully partic ipate. Further, co-designing a final product involves complex social dynamics and negotiation of diffe rent norms and priorities (Roschelle et al., 2006).  For example, teachers and researchers may have competing curricular output goals, with teache rs sometimes perceiving researchers’ goals as impractical for their classrooms (Roschelle et al., 2006). Similarly, when teachers from diverse disciplines and teaching contexts come together to engage in de sign, it can be hard to address everyone’s needs. T aking a praxis approach, we emphasized flexibility in our c o-design project in response to these challenges do cumented in the literature and echoed among our community partners (see below).

## Co-designed curriculum project context

This project involves a large design team consistin g of coastal North Carolina middle school teachers (English language arts, social studies, science), education researchers, scientists, outreach leaders, undergra duate and graduate students, and practitioners of resilience. The design team meets for full day workshops several times per year, and subgroup meetings occur at schools throughout the year. Aligning with similar projects by learning sciences researchers, we are working to address an educational challenge and co-create product(s) for unique contexts (Roschelle et  al., 2006). Specifically, we have been co-designing a curriculum interweaving concepts of per sonal, community, and ecological resilience; focusi ng on local resilience challenges. Definitions of resilie nce vary across fields from ecology to psychology (e.g., Krasny & Tidball, 2009) but generally, the project approac hes resilience as the  ability of an individual, community, or ecosystem to recover from or adapt to challenging c ircumstances. In the context of returning to school following the height of COVID-19, one resilience challenge that partner teachers identified was their perception that students were experiencing social and emotional challenges at an unprecedented scale. They also resonated with emerging and overwhelming evidence of high rates of teacher burn-out associated with teaching during the pandemic (Presley, 2021). Another resilience challenge specific to the local area is frequent coastal storms and hurricanes, two of which had devastating impacts on the community in 2018 and 2019. This project’s approach to co-design possibly offers something unique in terms of flexibility for teacher engagement. Whereas similar co-design projects commonly ask teachers for firm comm itments in terms of time spent designing and implementing a curriculum, we h ave provided flexibility on both points, foreground ing it in recruitment efforts, and emphasizing it throughout the co-design process. Though flexibility is an aspect fou nd commonly in co-design (e.g., Ko et al., 2022), we see this heavy e mphasis as somewhat unique, driving our line of inquiry. Specifically, we sought to understand how emphasizing flexibility might both entice teachers to engage in the co-design process as well as address some challenges associated with co-design documented in the literature (e.g., time constraints, competing objectives among team members: Roschelle et al., 2006). Stemming from this approach, teachers have worked w ith one or two partners from their school at a depth and pace that works for them. The research and outreach strands proposed resilience content, tea chers adapted that for use in classrooms, and the outreach team offered polishing support (e.g., formatting, building out resources). As it stands, the curriculum includes four layers of engagement: fables that feature resilience in nature, short activities supporting individual resilience p ractices (e.g., somatic mindfulness activities), co ntent-specific and standards-aligned units, and student-led community events to engage the broader communit y in resilience conversations, typically through art or performance. Teachers participated in the design and implement ation processes in ways that felt sustainable and appropriate for their own needs and contexts.

## Methods

This case study of one co-design project involved middle school teachers in coastal North Carolina. We conducted semi-structured interviews with six teachers who participated in a co-design process during the 2022-2023 school year individually or in pairs, representing each of  the four participating scho ols (four other teachers were unavailable). Interviews were audio recorded and tr anscribed verbatim for analysis. Supplementary data  sources include fieldnotes of professional learning worksho ps and artifacts produced by teachers at these work shops. Analysis of teachers’ experiences was part of an it erative design cycle in which we tailor the worksho ps and project materials to better support teachers. We an alyzed data using an inductive approach (Strauss & Corbin, 1994), and we informally member-checked preliminary findings with participating tea chers who attended the most recent workshop in Summer 2023. As flexibility  quickly emerged as a pervasive theme, we analyzed data through this lens. In addition to the  small number of teachers interviewed, another limit ation is that members of the research and outreach strand central to facilit ating the project were the interviewers, and this s ocial dynamic may have influenced teachers’ responses.

## Findings: Teachers’ perspectives on affordances provided by flexibility

Teachers had generally positive things to say about  this particular experience with curriculum design and enactment, as a whole. As one example, a teacher shared, “I've been a curriculum development writer for the state, which I thought was wonderful. And now this. This i s the top of it all.” Teachers talked specifically about how they appreciated the fluid nature of the curriculum design process and the flexibility of the curriculum itself, which they depicted as crucial for allowing them to engage more fully in the project. Looking across teachers’ depictions of the affordances of this curricular co-design and enactment experience, with an eye toward s flexibility, we identified three emerging themes that we introduce below: (1) creativity, (2) agency, ownership, and professional respect, and (3) partnership and community.

Creativity Several teachers described how this particular co-design experience provided them with an opportunity  to be creative. According to one teacher, this approach m ade them feel like there were “no boundaries” impos ed on their creative process: “it's very fluid, which is good, because if you allow us to not have boundarie s then there [are] no boundaries, you know what I mean? Cause wh en creating curriculum, if its step-by-step, step-by-step, then everybody's going to get fed [up] with it.” Similarly, another teacher said: “[this project] just let us run with creative thought, and we just started that way. And  you can see it at [each] school. What’s really int eresting is how it’s developed differently.” Flexibility, we argue, helped facilitate teachers in building and expanding on the concept of resiliency in more unique ways across sc hools and classrooms. It not only supported individual creativity by helping teachers feel like they could make the curriculum their own, but it also allowed for more of a diversity of examples from which teachers could draw inspiration. Agency, ownership, and professional respect Relatedly, teachers talked about a sense of agency in this project and feeling of ownership over the c urriculum. Many commented that most professional development w orkshops they had attended in the past without such flexible design process made them feel a s though the training were uni-directional, leading to what teachers described as lower levels of investment in the curriculum. In contrast, teachers relayed that being a central player in the design work provided a different scenario: “[This experience is] very different in that we've actually sat and worked together. We've actually developed it…It was something that we were actually working together to write and it gave us like more investment in it, which gives us the power. And I w as able to, we were able to, make it work with our curriculum. Which was just phenomenal. We weren't t old exactly how to how to do it, what to do with it. We were given the goals and what you wanted us ultimately to accomplish, and we worked together to come up with it.” We argue this lack of constraints, and ensuring teachers were in the driver’s seat of making curricular choices for their own classroom, contributed to fostering this sense of agency and ownership, as teachers were encouraged to really make the curriculum their own.

A closely related concept was a sense of profession al respect. A flexible co-design and implementation process facilitated teachers feeling agency and own ership, which in turn made them feel respected as professionals. They appreciated recognition of having important ideas to contribute and relevant knowledge about their students and classrooms. Particularly in the context of teacher burn-out, this feeling of respect was highly valued by teachers. Teachers used language like “re spected,” “valued,” and “listened to.” One teacher said that they do not typically feel valued or respected in t he context of working as a teacher, especially post  pandemic, making this professional learning experience possibly more valuable. Teachers talked about feeling like their time was valued (e.g., teachers were financially compens ated for their time) and their ideas were valued. T eachers shared a sense that their input was being actively sought and incorporated into the workshops and cu rriculum by other team members. We argue t hat providing teachers with flexibility to approach  the curriculum how they felt was best, while not necessary for engendering profe ssional respect, did promote an environment where t eachers were positioned as respected experts.

Partnership and community A key part of the built-in flexibility to the co-design process was the degree of choice teachers ha d in working with partners at their schools or across schools. A s the project has grown, they are asked to invite o ther teachers of their choosing to the workshops, prioritizing wo rking relationships and interest over requirements associated with discipline or grade level. Similarly, teachers  are supported, but not required, to work with teac hers at other schools. The value of these collaborations was an a ffordance that emerged across teacher interviews and i n the professional learning workshops. Teachers indicated  that working in partnership helped them successful ly navigate challenging times during the school year or feelings of overwhelm with the curriculum. One teacher told their partner teacher in the interview, “If you wer en't here, [this project would] be too overwhelming  for me and I'd bow out, I would, because I've got so many other [things going on].” Teachers in these partnerships represented various combinations of three disciplines (e.g., social studies, science, and English language arts). Teachers could collaboratively design or work on shared projects a cross disciplines, but were not expected to do the same thing in their classrooms. We argue tha t the flexible approach to the collaborations among  teachers afforded teachers to prioritize working with others by choice. The va riety of combinations also provided a wide diversit y of examples of projects that were both discipline-specific as well as interdisciplinary. More broadly, teachers appreciated being part of what one teacher called a “like-minded community” of teachers from other schools and design partners acr oss other organizations, such as researchers and ou treach leaders. Teachers mentioned liking different things about this experience: one teacher appreciated learning about grant opportunities from university partners, anoth er talked about the benefit of getting access to gu est speakers for their classrooms, others mentioned the support that team members fro m the research and outreach strand provided during resilience community events. We argue that the open-endedness of the design work helped many different people come together to generate and share ideas, new teaching practices, and resources.

## Findings: Emerging tensions in flexible co-design and enactment

Though teachers appreciated this flexible approach,  it has created several research challenges. These include understanding students’ learning experience s beyond one individual classroom, comparing teachi ng practices and implementation across classrooms, a nd keeping track of what precisely has occurred in each classroom. Members of the research and outreach str and are actively grappling with how to continue to engage with teachers in flexible design and implementation, while also accomplishing broader research goals, especially those focused on student learning.

On the design side, flexibility has placed some limitations on our ability to collaboratively develop high quality products that push all of our thinking. Ros chelle et al. (2006) argued that a key feature of c o-design is “central accountability for the quality of the prod ucts of co-design” (p. 2). By mixing and matching curricular resources and taking the themes of resilience in so  many different directions, there has been less cen tral accountability and collective grappling with pedago gical decisions. As one example, some members of the research and outreach strand have been talking abou t how to build more opportunities for conversations  around ecological resilience more aligned with Traditional  Ecological Knowledge, such as how collaboration, community, and interwovenness exists within ecosystems (e.g., Kimmerer, 2013). Teacher priorities have tended towards personal and community resilience as they have seen this as most critical for supporting students socially and emotionally following the height of COVI D-19. We anticipate discussions around further integr ation of ecological resilience will come with time, but reco gnize that intentional surfacing of priorities and potential tensions in values would likely result in deeper discussions about collectively improving and integrating resilience teaching practices and content.

## Conclusions

Given the degree to which teachers identified flexibility as both requisite of their participation as well as a driving force behind the benefits they felt, we argue that flexibility is a key aspect that may greatly contri bute to success of co-design projects in a post-pandemic context. This emphasis, however, has created trade-offs in terms of how this team has conducted research, prompting us to reimagine methods that can be more inductive and exploratory, timelines that are more generous, and research desi gns that are more responsive to teacher needs and p riorities. As flexibility itself is a key attribute of resilie nce, this approach may support adaptive and resilient cu rriculum design that responds to emergent challenges in the educational landscape, such as future pandemics or other forces shaping the learning and teaching contexts broadly.
