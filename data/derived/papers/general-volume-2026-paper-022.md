# From Thought to Action: Designing AI Coaching Support for Time Management with Cognitive Behavioral and Self-Regulated Learning Strategies

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract

This demonstration introduces TimeWise, a conversational agent that integrates self-regulated learning (SRL) and cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) to provide personalized, context-aware coaching for time management in online learning. The system leverages learning management system data to visualize study patterns and initiate reflective dialogue grounded in students’ actual learning activity. Through guided interaction, TimeWise engages students in examining their thoughts, identifying irrational beliefs, and reconstructing more adaptive perspectives to restore task-oriented behavior. We present the design principles and illustrative interaction cases that demonstrate how the system operationalizes SRL and CBT integration. This work highlights the potential of combining these frameworks to promote sustained academic regulation in online learning contexts. As online learning modalities gain increasing prominence within higher education, students are given greater control over when, where, and how they engage with academic tasks. While this flexibility promotes access and autonomy, it also requires learners to take a more active role in regulating and sustaining academic effort. Among the various self-regulatory skills needed in online environments, time management has been identified as one of the most critical self-regulatory skills influencing students’ learning engagement and academic performance (Wolters et al., 2025). However, many students still struggle to manage their time effectively and delay academic tasks even when they intend to complete them. Two major approaches have guided efforts to improve procrastination and time management in learning: self-regulated learning (SRL) and cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT). SRL-based interventions support learners in planning, monitoring, and evaluating their study behaviors through techniques like goal setting and task organization (Zimmerman, 2000). Digital tools such as mobile planners and productivity apps (e.g., Xie et al.,

2024) aim to scaffold scheduling and help students break down large tasks. More recently, conversational agents

like StudyBuddy (Tian et al., 2021) have applied SRL principles to support pre-task planning through fixed dialogue flows. While these systems improve task visibility, they often assume that upfront planning is sufficient and provide limited support during task execution or post-task reflection. As such, many tools fall short during moments when self-regulation breaks down, underscoring the need for adaptive systems that can intervene during the actual regulation of academic behavior. In contrast, CBT-based approaches address the cognitive and emotional barriers underlying procrastination by targeting maladaptive thought patterns (Lukas & Berking, 2018). While therapeutic interventions consistently show strong effects in reducing academic procrastination (van Eerde & Klingsieck, 2018), they are often time-and resource-intensive, limiting their scalability. Digital implementations of CBT offer a more accessible alternative, yet they typically rely on static web-embedded content, reducing their ability to adapt to learners' real-time academic contexts. Although some systems attempt to integrate CBT with task management features, for example, GanttBot (Pereira & Díaz, 2021), these implementations typically concentrate on project-level planning and timeline adjustments rather than addressing the in-the-moment disruptions that prevent learners from engaging with their work. Despite their distinct origins, SRL and CBT offer highly complementary perspectives for addressing academic procrastination. SRL-based strategies emphasize planning, monitoring, and behavioral regulation, while CBT approaches focus on identifying and restructuring the emotional and cognitive triggers that drive avoidance. Given that procrastination involves both breakdowns in task regulation and maladaptive responses to discomfort or self-doubt, an integrated approach has the potential to support students across both procedural and psychological dimensions of time management. To address these limitations, we designed TimeWise, a large language model (LLM)-based conversational agent that integrates principles from both self-regulated learning and cognitive-behavioral therapy to support students in managing their time more effectively in online learning. D esign principles We grounded our design in theoretical insights from SRL and CBT principles (see Figure 1). TimeWise is designed to provide adaptive support across the full cycle of SRL: forethought, performance, and self-reflection. In the forethought phase, TimeWise assists students with goal setting and planning, aligning with functions seen in earlier SRL-based tools. The system's central contribution lies in its support during the performance phase, where time management often breaks down. TimeWise uses behavioral indicators from LMS data to identify patterns that may signal task avoidance. They are introduced into the conversation to help students raise awareness of the study behaviors, setting the stage for deeper dialogue by anchoring the coaching in concrete behavioral moments. TimeWise then engages the learner at the moment of struggle, initiating the Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT) process to identify and transform the underlying beliefs related to the procrastination behaviors (Dryden and Neenan, 2004). This process is informed by the CBT ABCDE model, which includes the Activating event, the Beliefs that arise in response to that event, the emotional and behavioral Consequences of those beliefs, the Disputation of irrational beliefs, and the development of more Effective beliefs. In the performance stage, it helps learners identify the activating event that triggered the difficulty, such as encountering a demanding task or feeling overwhelmed, and then guides them to articulate the beliefs shaping their reactions. The system also helps them recognize how those beliefs contribute to emotional responses. This allows the system to intervene precisely when procrastination is unfolding, offering real-time dialogue that helps learners reframe unhelpful thoughts and re-engage with the task. In the reflection phase, TimeWise guides users through disputation of maladaptive beliefs and supports the formulation of more effective, adaptive beliefs that can strengthen future self-regulation. By embedding REBT techniques into each phase of SRL, the system moves beyond surface-level prompting to deliver emotionally responsive coaching that aligns with each learner’s moment-to-moment cognitive and affective state. After the learner forms a study plan in the forethought phase, TimeWise tracks learning behavior in the LMS and uses these data to support the next stage of the process. In the performance phase, TimeWise presents LMS-based indicators and visualized activity patterns to help learners monitor how their engagement unfolds in practice. The learner reviews recent study patterns and acknowledges that starting assignments late is not effective for learning, while also noting that some study habits only work in some classes (see Figure 2). TimeWise extends this phase by exploring thoughts, emotions, and beliefs related to learners’ behaviors. It guides the learner translate a general intention into a concrete study event, then identifies likely distractions and emotional reactions, such as feeling overwhelmed and pressed for time, that may disrupt task completion (see Figure 3a). Figures 3b to 3d further illustrate how breakdowns during performance are shaped by students’ irrational beliefs. One common thinking pattern was the use of strict "must" or "should" statements, also known as musterbation in REBT. They often interpreted difficulties through inflexible expectations about how they should perform, like “I must do well,” “I must finish perfectly,” or “I shouldn’t fall behind.” These demands increased pressure during task engagement and often contributed to avoidance, distress, and harsh self-evaluation. In Figure 3b, must/should thinking appears together with catastrophizing and self-downing. The learner interprets falling behind as something that “ruins” the whole week and then begins to view the setback as evidence of personal failure. In this case, the difficulty is not only the missed progress itself, but also the belief that such setbacks are unacceptable and reflect something negative about the self. In Figure 3c, must/should thinking takes a more clearly perfectionistic form. The learner says, “I must do well” and “I can’t stand the idea of doing something halfway,” which reflects both rigid internal demands and low frustration tolerance. The learner also suggests that if the work is not done properly, then “everything I did was pointless,” showing all-or-nothing thinking in which anything less than perfect feels worthless. The barrier is not only high standards, but also the b elief that imperfect work is intolerable. In Figure 3d, the same perfectionistic pattern remains visible. The learner still starts from a perfectionistic and all-or-nothing position, assuming that work needs to be done properly in order to count. This also reflects low frustration tolerance, because incomplete or unpolished work still feels difficult to accept. But the interaction begins to show the softening of that belief. The learner gradually begins to accept that progress does not have to be perfect to be meaningful. In the reflection phase, TimeWise helps students recognize, dispute, and reconstruct irrational beliefs about time management. As shown in Figures 4a–4b, when a learner expresses self-downing — interpreting missed deadlines as proof of being "a failure" — TimeWise uses gentle questioning and reflective prompts to challenge rigid self-talk and encourage perspective-taking. Students begin to shift toward more compassionate thinking, saying things like "I'll plan small goals" or "Maybe I'm just still learning." Similarly, when a learner describes distraction as "too much to deal with," TimeWise reframes the task as manageable, helping them move from "I can't do this right now" to tackling it in smaller steps. In Figure 4c, the reflective work extends into action, as the learner connects procrastination to unhelpful habits and works with TimeWise to establish a concrete study plan as a first step toward changing their time management behaviors. During the interactive demonstration, participants will explore selected dialogue flows that illustrate how TimeWise supports students using principles from REBT and SRL. They will have the opportunity to engage with the chatbot directly by entering common student concerns and observing how the system responds. Through this hands-on experience, participants can see how TimeWise identifies irrational beliefs, challenges rigid thinking, and scaffolds the development of adaptive plans, helping students shift from emotional reactivity to proactive time management.
