# Learning to Use Students’ Ideas in Instruction in Teacher Preparation Science Methods Courses

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study addresses the need for responsive teaching in light of current science education reforms. Responsive teaching involves recognizing and building on students' ideas, adapting instruction, and engaging with their thinking (Robertson et al., 2016; Thompson et al., 2016). However, this approach is challenging for pre-service teachers (Haverly et al., 2020). This qualitative study investigates how pre-service secondary science teachers (PSSTs) learn to use students’ ideas in instruction during their teacher preparation science methods (TPSM) course as we adopt the Ambitious Science Teaching (AST) conceptual framework (Windschitl et al., 2018). Interviews with four PSSTs and their instructor identify the perceived course features that support PSSTs in learning to use students’ ideas, including class community, peer teaching, and the problem-based unit plan assignment. We examine the alignment between the instructor’s and PSSTs’ perceived course features and provide recommendations for teacher education programs to support PSSTs in developing responsive teaching practices.

## Background

Current efforts towards improving K-12 science education advocate deep content learning via students’ participation in scientific investigation and sensemaking practices (NGSS, 2013; NRC, 2012). Hence, it is critical to have future science teachers who can support thi s kind of learning. Such teachers must attend to students’ thinking and reasoning about the natural world rather than merely attending to the correctne ss of content and leverage these ideas to continuously adapt instruction (Kang & Anderson, 2015; Luna, 2018; Watkins et al., 2020). Responsive teaching is an effective way of meeting those demands. R esponsive teaching is a pedagogical approach that includes eliciting students’ ideas (Hammer et al., 2012), centering and engaging with the intellectual resources that students bring (Robertson et al., 20 16; Thompson et al., 2016), noticing  and attend ing to the substance of students’ ideas, pursuing that thinking, and responding by supporting students in their reasoning and adjusting the flow of instruction as needed (Kang &  Anderson, 2015; Maskiewicz, 2015; Robertson et al., 2016; Thompson et al., 2016; van Es & Sherin, 2008; Watkins et al., 2020). Given its complexity, developing responsive teaching is challenging, especially for pre-service teachers (PSTs; Haverly et al., 2020). The teacher preparation years are one of the most challenging yet significant periods a teacher goes through in their development and tea cher education (Saka et al., 2013; Tschida, 2009). Therefore, understanding the teacher preparation period is cru cial to supporting pre-service science teachers’ (PSSTs) learning of responsive teaching (Harris et al., 2012; Luft et al., 2011; Saka et al., 2013). This study contributes to the field’s understanding of PSSTs’ learning to be responsive through learning to use students’ ideas. Specifically, we examine ho w the teacher preparation science methods (TPSM) courses prepare PSSTs to learn to use students’ ideas in in struction. We examine the alignment between the instructor’s design reflections and the PSSTs’ perspectives on w hat supported them in learning to use students’ ide as. Understanding this will help both teacher educators and researchers better understand the uptake and adoption of the practice of using student ideas, and how teachers learn to be responsive in teacher preparation programs.

## Theoretical framework

This study theorizes “learning to use students’ ideas” using the lens of situated learning (Lave & Wenger, 1991).

Using a situated model of responsive teaching views teaching as embedded w ithin a broader system of social interactions. However, while this approach captures the variability and adaptability of teaching, it does not provide a structured framework for what responsive practices look like in action. To operationalize the concept of "using students' ideas," this study adopts the Ambitious Science Teaching (AST) framework (Windschitl et al., 2018) as a conceptual framework. While situated learning focuses on the process of learning in social and cultural contexts, AST provides the practical scaffolding for how this learning manifests in the classroom through specific practices. This allows the research to move from understanding the variability of teachers’ responsiveness to identifying the design features in teacher preparation science meth ods (TPSM) courses that best support PSSTs in devel oping responsive teaching skills. This framework emphasizes the planning and enactment of instructional strategies that help us conceptualize the principles of “using students’ ideas in instruction” (see Table 1). ## Research question

How do teacher preparation science methods (TP SM) courses prepare pre-service secondary science teachers (PSSTs) to learn to use student ideas in science instruction?

## Participants

The participants were four PSSTs enrolled in a seco ndary teacher licensure program at a large Midweste rn university in the US, student teaching in Spring 20 24: Clyde, Raymond, Susan, and Adeline. Their TPSM Fall 2023 course instructor, Lila, also participated. Th is group was chosen because secondary teachers, unl ike elementary educators, focus specifically on science  education and have received less attention in rese arch compared to elementary science teachers (e.g., Kang & Anderson, 2015; Richards & Robertson, 2016).

## Data collection

This study is part of a larger longitudinal interview dataset in which each PSST was interviewed three times during their teacher induction: during their TPSM course (Fall 2023), at the start of student teaching (early Spring 2024), and after student teach ing (end of Spring 2024). This study focuses on dat a from the second interview, where PSSTs reflected on course features that helped them  learn to use student ideas. Interviews were conducted via Zoom and lasted for 60 minutes. To address  the research question, an additional interview was conducted with the instructor to discuss the course design and reflections on supporting PSSTs in using student ideas.

## Context

PSSTs were recruited from their TPSM course  which was integrated with a high school field experience  and focused on the curriculum and teaching philosophy f or high school science. This course, led by Lila, w as the second in a science methods sequence, following a m ore theoretical first course that emphasized using students' ideas through the Ambitious Science Teaching framework. In contrast, the focal second course shifted to practical teaching activities. Lila stated that it was not the original intent of her planning  to support PSSTs in learning to use students’ ideas in instruction.

## Data analysis

We used a grounded approach to analysis (Charmaz, 2014), refining Zoom-generated transcripts to separate turns by speaker utterance. We conducted several rounds of qualitative coding using MAXQDA, guided by Framework Analysis (Ritchie & Spencer, 2002), which involves five stages: Familiarization, Identifying a Themati c Framework, Indexing, Charting, and Mapping and Inte rpretation. The first author fa miliarized themselves with the data by reading transcripts and noting recurring themes on how the course supported PSSTs in using students’ ideas. Next, we developed an initial coding scheme based on the data, research questions, and the TPSM syllabus, using the AST framework (Windschitl et al., 2018) a s a sensitizing framework for conceptualizing the principles of “using students’ ideas in instruction” and decid ing what counts as talk about that concept (see Tab le 1). The first author then conducted open coding followed by axial coding (Cha rmaz, 2014), developing a coding scheme that captured all course features discussed by participants. Third, after refining the coding scheme, all interviews were coded for perceived course features that supported PSSTs in learning to use students’ ideas and categorized by who mentioned these features. Fourth, we summarized the data from both the instructor and PSSTs’ into a matrix based on the course features and looked for patterns in alignment and non-alignment between instructor perceptions and PSSTs’ perceptions. Inter-rater reliability was 95%, with differences resolved through consensus.

## Findings

To better understand the alignment between the instructor and the PSSTs’ perceived course features for supporting PSSTs in using students’ ideas, we summarized the data from both the instructor and the PSSTs to help us identify the patterns to better understand what course features were perceived to support PSSTs in learning to use students’ ideas (see Table 2 below). We compared the course f eatures between the perspectives of the instructor and the perspectives of the PSSTs and identified the similarities and differences in perceived course features. In her interview, Lila, the instructor, identified several course features as helping PSSTs in learnin g to use students’ ideas including in-class activities, specific course topics or materials, and specific assignments (see Table 2). A big area of focus for Lila  was the approaches for teaching high school science  as course topics or materials in her course. She stated that both modeling instruction and project and problem-based learning were connected to using students’ ideas and gave PSSTs a  way to use students’ ideas in instruction. For example, Lila stated that in modeling instruction as an approach for teaching high school science, “the students are modeling various phenomenon and developing models that get c loser and closer to the current thinking”. In that approach, “there's a focus and emphasis on how scientists dev elop those models, and the students develop them as well”. Lila described the connections between modeling instruction and using students’ ideas in instruction in that a “big part of modeling instruction is [having] students share their ideas” where the teacher starts by “visually or orally presenting the topic and then the students actually make observations”. Lila highlighted that modeling instruction as a topic allowed PSSTs to learn to elicit students’ ideas, support ongoing changes in students' thinking, and help students draw together evidence-based explanations.

However, we noticed that there were course features that only P SSTs discussed as supporting them in their learning  to use students’ ideas and were not mentioned by the course instructor when asked about perceived features that support PSSTs. These course features were the class culture and community, a modeling instruction resource: American Modeling Teachers Association, the school context assignment, and the reflections on lesson plan assignments. Finally, both parties highlighted a common challenge when it came to using students’ ideas in instruction. Both parties reflected on how learning to use students’ ideas in instruction was challenging when the cooperating teachers in their course placements did not practice that ap proach. PSSTs had a hard time seeing good examples of practice in regard to using students’ ideas, and  the instructor stated that the approaches of teaching discussed in the course had to be “a small step from what PSSTs are seeing in their placements” and it’s hard to emphasize using students’ ideas in instruction without having PSSTs see a good example of it in their placements.

## Discussion and implications

The interviews with PSSTs revealed that the most impactful course features for learning to use students’ ideas in instruction were those that involved active engagem ent, such as peer teaching, modeling instruction, problembased learning, planning lessons, and reflecting on teaching practices. These features allowed PSSTs to construct meaning through hands-on experiences, reflection, and experimentation, al igning with constructivist (Vygotsky, 1978), experiential learning (Kolb, 1984), and reflective practice (Schön, 1992) theories. In contrast, features like lecturing, modeling instruction  without active engagement, PSST presentations, and discussing the Danielson Framework were perceived as less effective, despite the instructor's belief in their relevance.

The study highlights the importance of intentionall y incorporating opportunities to use students’ idea s into course features rather than assuming they will emerge naturally. Instructors need to recognize that what they perceive as supportive may not align with how PSSTs experience these activities. Additio nally, the analysis underscores the challenge of bridging theory and pr actice, as course features that blended both dimens ions most effectively supported PSSTs in developing responsiv e teaching practices. Our findings suggest that met hods course instructors should thoughtfully design learn ing experiences that actively engage PSSTs in plann ing, implementing, and reflecting on instruction. This a lignment between theoretical understanding and prac tical application was critical to fostering responsive teaching skills. Ultimately, this study offers practical guidance for professional development facilitators and teacher e ducators on designing impactful learning experience s that prepare beginning teachers to use students’ ideas in instruction, supporting them in their journey toward becoming responsive educators.
