# The Multicompetent Learners

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Engineering and science are meant to develop solutions and knowledge for a diverse population, yet Black and Brown communities often remain excluded from these intellectual enterprises. This work builds on prior literature in the learning sciences, sociolinguistics as well as science and engineering education to improve access for culturally and linguistically diverse communities. It aims at creating socially just learning spaces where individuals can bring their whole selves through introducing the notion of multicompetent learners described in terms of their language practices, cultural understandings and cognitive resources.

## Introduction

American society has traditionally viewed the practices of non-dominant communities as problems to overcome (Moschkovich, 2012) and has dealt with people who h ave any cultural or linguistic variability by attem pting to transform them into monocultural monolinguals. Whil e there is an implicit embrace of diversity in the melting pot metaphor, in fact the expectation has been that  people of color should gravitate to the culturally-dominant model (Ortega, 2020). These societal expectations are inextricably linked with notions of learning as they shape what counts as knowing and speaking, perpetuating structures of power and domination and ignoring the sociohistorical contexts that created them (Ladson-Billings, 2006; Lee et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2020; Philip & Sengupta, 2021). Such an approach ignores the strengths that different populations bring to the table – especially in science and engineering (Hofstra et al., 2020; Layne & Aren berg, 2002). This paper puts forward the notion of multicompetent learners to honor and center the cognitive, linguistic and cultural practices of Black and Brown communities that too often are made invisible in the technical and scientific spheres. Multicompetent learners People who see the world through different cultural, cognitive and linguistic lenses are multicompetent learners. They come from diverse backgrounds, which equip the m with the potential to draw on their experiences a nd knowledge, including the language practices, cultural understandings and cognitive resources of home and their local environments (Lee, 2003). When provided with opportunities to learn that draw on their background, these individuals are “transformative intellectuals” with  multifaceted understandings of social justice issu es and disciplinary knowledge with commitments and credibility in their local contexts (Morales-Doyle, 2017). Although they have dynamic and rich strengths, multicompetent learners are often seen through a deficit perspective as lacking language and understanding o f the world around them (Nasir et al., 2006). Label s like English Language Learners (ELLs) or at-risk student s limit the potential of these learners by focusing  on the quantification of languages and ways of knowing, taking as a standard the dominant society instead of mobilizing the intellectual resources students develop in thei r lived realities through which they index via lang uage (both cognitively and culturally). But even in instances where the ways of knowing and speaking of non-domin ant groups are valued, it is often with the purpose of ushering their resources into dominant, Westernized, and monolithic conceptions of human identity around language, histories, racial and ethnic groups, class and gender (Bang & Medin, 2010). From this perspective, divers ity is addressed through helping multicompetent lea rners become more like dominant learners (Nasir et al., 2006). In truth, multicompetent learners have many experie nces developed through navigating different audiences, which requires a great deal of sophistication in tailoring speech, particularly when it comes to technical and scientific talk. Through the dynamic activity o f language choice, multicompetent individuals acqui re experiences working with people from a wide range o f backgrounds. As compared to monolinguals and monoculturals, multicompetent learners have also lived many realities. While monolinguals in engineering may produce a single exclusionary technical-based culture, the kinds of viewpoints multicompetent learners bring to the table derive from diverse lived experiences and  direct interaction with the distributed intelligen ce in their communities, physical and social world. The opportu nities to interact with different kinds of people (e.g., engineers, parents, professors), in various cultural and linguistic settings (e.g., school, home, work) and through different discursive practices equip them with a wide range of valuable perspectives, repertoires of practices and various forms of acting and doing in the world (García & Wei, 2015; Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003). Language practices One of the means by which people draw on their strengths is through language. By looking at how language works, we can identify ways in which humans can tap the knowledge that resides in their differences from the dominant ICLS2022 Proceedings 1677 © ISLS culture and language (Blommaert, 2013). The experie nces people bring from living between languages and cultures equip them with the dexterity to navigate different contexts, such as engaging in specific ways of speaking and literacies (Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003). Researchers in translanguaging see language as associated with peoples’ ways of “acting, being, knowing, and doing” (García  & Wei, 2015). Thus, learning is enriched when it o ccurs within culturally and linguistically sustaining contexts that mobilize students’ intellectual resources. Translanguaging scholars have advocated for pedagogies that grant license for learners to draw on their full repertoire (García & Kano, 2014; Otheguy et al., 2015). However, the reality in learning environm ents is plagued with social norms that restrict students from drawing on their ways of speaking that feel authentic to them. For instance, industry settings tend to see languag es other than English as unprofessional (Ruben, 202 0). But linguistically-inclusive environments ensure students develop a deeper understanding and connection with ideas. In these contexts, students focus on cultivating ways of knowing instead of worrying about sounding right. Cultural understandings Learning is also cultural. Scholars have described cultural understandings as the plethora of practices developed through social interaction and the historicity and context of communities, which are ever-evolving for the purposes valued in these groups (Nasir et al., 2006). In thi nking about the need for “dignity-conferring” inter actions for belonging, Espinoza and Vossoughi (2014) invite us to think about how to open spaces for the multiplic ity of voices in society to expand the discursive opportun ities available to communities for the social futur es they imagined to live (Costanza-Chock, 2020). For example, Lee (1995) described the role of wordplay characterized by “ironic, metaphoric, satirical, and figurative l anguage” (p. 360) in learning as a practice from th e African American community. Although African American stude nts showed poor performance on standardized test assessments, Lee showed that the assessment instruments failed to capture the linguistic dexterity of these students and led to them not bringing the practices of their  communities for learning. She argued for valuing s tudents' cultural and linguistic multicompetences and welcoming them in the classroom as an asset for learning. Cognitive resources In her work with Latinx learners, Moschkovich (2012) focuses on the funds of cognitive resources that students bring to support their learning pathways. Instead o f seeing their differences as deficits, she follows  Vygotsky

(1978) in centering the “potential for progress in what learners already know and do” (p.91). Summariz ing

scholarship in psycholinguistics related to mathema tics, Moschkovich (2007) described bilinguals “sele ctive attention” or the ability to focus on important inf ormation and reduce noise from non-relevant materia l. These multicompetent learners also have “translation skills” as they navigate the ways of speaking in their local contexts and those of the broader society (p.128). When enga ging in different cognitive tasks, linguistically d iverse students should be able to choose the ways of speaking that feel better to them for accomplishing the goals of the activity. The focus on students’ backgrounds as a resource for learning transforms the power dynamics regarding who is heard and is allowed to contribute in the classroom (Bell, 2017). Moving forward Being a multicompetent learner in engineering and s cience today typically means having a predominantly monolingual teacher population whose cultural and l inguistic backgrounds often differ from oneself. Students have teachers without the training to bridge community knowledge and practices into their pedagogies. And when students do bring the languages and experiences of their communities into making sense of scientific i deas and solving problems, they are seen as lacking objectivity and professionalism in the scientific and technical subjects, diminishing their sense of belonging. The lack of d iversity in who teaches combined with assessments c reated without diverse populations in mind results in multicompetent learners with low academic performance scores. Changing this dynamic and creating learning environ ments that see the potential in students as multicompetent learners involves considering their ways of speaking (language practices), what they know to be true in their communities (cultural understandings) and the knowledge and experiences associated with their lived realities (cognitive resources) as crucial for the world of engineering and science. A world that embr aces this perspective requires seeing the communities where students come from not as discrete and essentialized groups in their language, culture and experience (e.g., La tinx people only as Spanish speakers). Instead, it involves embracing the nuances of how time and place influen ce their practices to incorporate who they are in t he production of knowledge and problem-solving. It also dictates a teacher population that mirrors the student body, incorporates elements of students’ lived realities in the education of future teachers, and investigates the role of the teacher’s background and training in creating a  culture of belonging in the disciplines. Educators  in such a world would focus on developing conceptual understa nding and practical experiences rather than grammar  or ICLS2022 Proceedings 1678 © ISLS vocabulary (Warren et al., 1994) while acknowledging the multiple factors (e.g., audience, language boundaries, topics, places, social and political realities, time) that shape the opportunities available in learning environments. In short, there is no silver bullet that will addre ss the needs of multicompetent learners to be welcomed into the science and engineering community, but there are concrete steps that can be taken to address this issue head-on. Truly valuing these learners means creating spaces that send messages of belonging and reflect what they know to be true in their community while equipping students with the tools to navigate other realities  without necessarily having as a goal to leave behind who th ey are. Students should be able to become proficien t in the knowledge and discursive practices of the disciplines while also being able to communicate such expertise to the groups where they claim membership. They should be provided ample opportunities to make sense of scientific phenomena in their communities to understand the potential and limitations of scientific ideas in context (e.g., as shown in the scholarship of Morales‐Doyle (2017) on chemistry in urban neighborhoods). Multicompetent students should not have to pay the steep price of being seen as less knowledgeable or their contributions de-valued when using Spanish or  speaking discursively. Ideas should be judged not on the sophistication of the vocabulary nor the language u sed to communicate them, but on students’ conceptua l understanding of real-world phenomena. If the goal is learning science and engineering, teachers must allow students to express their ideas in the ways that su pport their sense-making and honor those ways of speaking as valuable resources for communicating knowledge.

## Conclusion

Up until now, much of the empirical and theoretical work on the role of language and cultural understandings has investigated the role of linguistic resources in discursive practices associated with science and engineering (Brown et al., 2005; Lee & Roth, 2002; Millar, 2006) or in social justice movements (without a focus on the disciplines) with the goal to liberate linguistically diverse le arners from monoglossic expectations (Creese & Blac kledge, 2015; García & Wei, 2015; Otheguy et al., 2015). Su ch studies have conceptualized language primarily a s a medium for instruction. In contrast, this paper puts forward the idea of the multicompetent learners to shed light on how the diversity of experiences in Black and Brown communities (which are indexed through language) equip them to bring a different set of perspectives to le arning. It challenges scholars to expand their unde rstanding of bringing diverse perspectives into learning not sim ply as a matter of investigating the boundaries bet ween languages but as an issue of whose linguistic practices are valued in society. As suggested by Moschkovich (2007), scholarship should focus not on comparing multicomp etent learners with the dominant groups. Instead, t he learning sciences should pay attention to the ways Black and Brown students make sense of, communicate  and engage with different forms of knowledge within meaningful sociocultural and historical contexts. This paper joins other calls to retire deficit-orie nted perspectives toward culturally and linguistica lly diverse populations (González‐Howard & Suárez, 2021), inviting us to embrace asset-based frameworks that see students as multicompetent learners, speakers and doers. The relevance of this work resides in understanding the nature of multicompetent individuals (Cook, 2016; Ortega, 2016) and how we could draw on the assets of these groups for equitable learning. This work advocates for expanding research endeavors that build on the creative insights diverse students bring to the scientific and engineering enterprise. Justice in science and engineering as well as in society demands we invite differences into learning environments and welcome individuals to come as their whole selves into the process of figuring out  problems as well as producing, assessing, and crea ting new human realities (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020; Dym & Little, 2003; Sheppard, 2003).
