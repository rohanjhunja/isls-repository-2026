# Practitioner Transitions: Role of Materials, Mentoring, and Staged Participation

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines boundary crossing and identity expansion within the Practitioner Research Idea Summit (PRISm), a cohort-based research–practice partnership for educators. Using a case study research design, we analyse two participants’ trajectories from Learner toward mentoring roles (Learning Buddy; Research Buddy) based on semi-structured interviews and programme documents. The analysis revealed transitions of participa nts within and across batches of PRISm, and the mediating role of boundary objects such as templates, checklists, and rubrics, in the transition. Findings indicate that research identity emerges through ongoing engagement with research-oriented thinking, r eflective practice, and application in professional contexts, rather than solely through tangible outcomes like publication. Boundary objects, guided mentorship, and explicit expectations facilitated productive transitions, while role ambiguity hindered them despite motivation. The study underscores that structured participation, recognition, and transfer of learning are essential for fostering research agency and practitioner-led inquiry.

Imagine a teacher experimenting with a pedagogical change in a classroom, such as a new question sequence, a revised feedback routine, or an EdTech tool, and wondering whether that classroom insight could evolve into researchable knowledge. The path from s uch everyday experimentation to a publishable research study is rarely straightforward. It depends not only on teachers’ motivation and commitment but also on the availability of structured spaces, scaffolds, and mentorship that help them systematically document and communicate their innovations as research (Ni, 2024; Tarrayo et al., 2021).  Coburn et al. (2013) and Farrell et al. (2021) describe how Research Practice Partnerships (RPPs) emphasise long-term collaborations between practitioners and researchers, involving interdisciplinary actors in collaborative research processes. Higher education institution s have increasingly implemented continuing professional development (CPD) initiatives designed to bridge this gap by building research capacity. A notable trend in these programmes is a shift from purely theoretical workshops towards hands-on, project-based learning where participants are guided through the entire research process (Chavda et al., 2021; Quezada-Espinoza et al., 2024).

Despite decades of attention, the research–practice gap in education persists (Schlicht-Schmälzle, 2024). While researchers generate theoretical and empirical insights, these sometimes remain detached from classroom realities (McKenney & Reeves, 2018). Literature suggests that the integration of teaching and research enables educators to systematically address pedagogical challenges and reflect on their practice  (Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Hartmann & Kunter, 2022). Rycroft-Smith (2022) argues that the assumption that research publication and improved dissemination alone will lead to its classroom uptake has proven insufficient to bridge the research-practice divide. Bridging this divide requires enabling practitioners to participate meaningfully in knowledge production. This requires a shift in idea from thinking of teachers as implementers of research to co-investigators and contributors to educational research.

For many teachers, transitioning into educational research remains a difficult journey. Institutional mechanisms to build research capacity among practitioners are limited, often fragmented, and rarely sustained beyond short-term workshops or isolated training events (Ahmed & Pervin, 2015). Teachers lack access to scaffolded pathways, mentoring relationships, or examples that can help them transform classroom innovations into systematic inquiries. Furthermore, existing professional development models in the  global south frequently overlook the expertise teachers already possess (Revina et al., 2023). Capacity building requires collaborative 1048 frameworks that respect teachers’ practitioner identities while supporting the gradual development of research-oriented ways of thinking, reasoning, and communicating. The challenge, therefore, lies in designing environments that enable teachers to engage with research without alienating their professional selves. Mentorship from experienced researchers is consistently identified as a cornerstone of effective CPDs  (Chavda et al., 2021; Sjölund et al., 2023; Quezada-Espinoza et al., 2024). The presence of a dedicated mentor improves research productivity and helps novice researchers overcome common challenges (Quezada-Espinoza et al., 2024).  Studies suggest that identity transformation depends not only on skill acquisition but also on a shift in agency authorship within collaborative structures and local constraints (Korhonen et al., 2024; Warriem et al., 2017). However, it remains unclear how the mentoring relationship specifically nurtures these qualities. The programme featured in this article-Practitioner Research Idea Summit (PRISm), was conceptualised to bridge the research-practice gap in the Indian context (Pathan et al., 2025). This programme aims to build practitioner communities that advance the s cholarship of teaching and learning, while supporting their transition into EdTech research. The first iteration of the PRISm was conducted in 2013 (Warriem et al., 2013). Insights from this experience highlighted the need to build teachers’ competencies in pedagogical practices and the effective integration of educational technology into their teaching. To address this, prerequisite workshops were organised in 2014, 2015, and 2016 (Murthy et al., 2015). The second iteration of the PRISm was subsequently conducted in 2016 (Warriem et al., 2017). Across these offerings, from 2013 to 2016, a total of approximately 7132 practitioners were trained in technology integration and exposed to educational technology research. Due to logistical and budgetary constraints, the programme could not be continued between 2016 and 2022. The programme was relaunched in 2023 with a restructured curriculum, a tiered mentorship model, and redesigned learning pathways aligned with emerging needs in the field of educational research. In its renewed format, 20 practitioners from engineering, liberal arts, school education, and the EdTech industry completed the programme in 2023 under the guidance of 24 research buddies (mentors). Participation expanded significantly in subsequent years, with 49 learners (practitioners) and 36 research buddies in 2024, and 52 learners, 6 learning buddies (mentors), and 57 research buddies in 2025. As a culmination of the PRISm, learners with the guidance of their mentors have published their research in international conferences, with accepted publications increasing from 7 in 2023 to 19 in 2024 and 21 in 2025, spanning diverse formats such as full papers, short papers, and experience reports. As a capacity-building initiative, the PRISm provides practitioners with a structured pathway from classroom-based innovations to research publication. It brings together a diverse community of participants, ranging from first-time practitioner-researchers to alumni who return as mentors. Key features of this programme include:

1. Structured progression: A sequence of iterative checkpoints that guide participants step by step. These

checkpoints include research idea submissions, the Idea Proposal Template (IPT), the Study Planning Template (SPT), data collection, the first draft, and the final manuscript.

2. Scaffolded learning materials: Tools like the IPT and SPT enable teachers to articulate and refine their

ideas, making tacit classroom insights researchable.

3. Tiered mentorship: Participants receive guidance through a three-level model comprising Learning

Buddies (LB), Research Buddies (RB), and an orchestration team. This ensures both peer support and expert feedback.

4. Sustained participation: Many graduates rejoin the programme as mentors. This creates a recursive

learning ecosystem that sustains community knowledge and evolving professional identities. This programme has shown promising outcomes in promoting practitioner-led inquiry and fostering a culture of practitioner research (Pathan et al., 2025). This study, therefore, takes the PRISm as a setting to investigate the research questions: RQ1: How do practitioners enrolled in PRISm navigate the transition to the roles of researchers and mentors? RQ2: How do learning materials, mentoring, and staged participation scaffold practitioner transitions?

## Theoretical framework

This study draws on a sociocultural understanding of learning and identity formation. The framework integrates insights from Boundary-crossing Theory (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011), Transfer of Ownership (Warriem et al., 2017), and Legitimate Peripheral Participation (Lave & Wenger, 1991) to interpret the transitions and identity expansion experienced by practitioners participating in the PRISm. During the learning phase of PRISm, practitioners engage with new epistemic, cultural, and institutional practices of research that differ from their prior professional contexts. Fundamentals of boundary crossing are the interactions and transitions as individuals make movements across landscapes and domains of knowledge and practices. Akkerman and Bakker (2011) defined 1049 boundaries as “sociocultural differences that lead to discontinuities in action or interaction”. However, when these differences are navigated through collaboration and reflection, they can become sites of rich learning. Wi th educators transitioning from practitioners to research, Boundary Objects (Star, 1989) in the form of tools, learning materials, and other knowledge pieces facilitate the ongoing dialogues for capacity building and sustained partnerships among the researchers and practitioners.

As practitioners navigate such movements from traditional roles to researchers, the Transfer of Ownership (Warriem et al., 2017) manifests with distributed modes of leadership and professional agency where practitioners recognise themselves as drivers of educational research inquiry and innovation. As mentioned previously, sustainable research-driven interventions take shape as practitioners develop research competencies and express ownership of educational reforms.

To explore how participants transitioned from being learners to mentors (LB or RB) in the subsequent PRISm cohort, this study draws on Legitimate Peripheral Participation (Lave & Wenger, 1991). This framework conceptualises learning as a process of participation in shared practice, where newcomers move toward fuller involvement through observation, modelling, guided participation, and gradual assumption of responsibility. The peripheral participation of practitioners in terms of research is augmented with structured scaffolding and mentorship with a close association with the researchers. In other words, practitioners take part in peripheral activities of understanding foundations of educational research, problem identification, problem formulation, etc., and gradually engage in developing methodological approaches, collecting and analysing data, writing academic research papers, and later guiding other community members on their research journey. Key to such sustained movements are the authenticity, meaningfulness, and relationality of participants’ real-world teaching contexts (Cochran‑Smith & Lytle, 2009).

Study details The context for this study is that of the programme-Practitioner Research Idea Summit (PRISm), conceived to address a persistent capacity gap in Indian higher education: educators frequently innovate in their classrooms yet lack structured pathways, mentorship, and communal infrastructures to transform those efforts into rigorous, publishable educational research. The programme’s point of departure is that teaching knowledge should be rendered community property through systematic inquiry, documentation, and dissemination, rather than remaining private and tacit within individual classrooms. Designed for first-time practitioner-researchers across school and higher-education settings, PRISm scaffolds the full research cycle, from problem framing to manuscript submission through a 12-14 week blend of asynchronous learning and live mentoring organised in four phases: building foundations in educational research, conducting context-embedded studies, writing and submission, and reflective closure. Delivery follo ws a Learner-Centric MOOC architecture that couples concise concept modules with Learning-by-Doing activities, weekly interactive sessions, and optional extension trajectories. A tiered mentorship ecosystem undergirds the programme: Research Buddies (exper ienced researchers) provide methodological guidance; Learning Buddies (alumni) offer peer-level scaffolding and genre socialisation; and a central orchestration team coordinates pairing, progress monitoring, and academic quality. Progress is mediated by a set of scaffolded “boundary objects”, notably an Idea Proposal Template, a Study Planning Template, and a manuscript scaffold aligned to target conference venues, which provide shared language and checkpoints for participants and mentors during the phases of research idea formulation, study planning, writing the paper, and presenting the paper at the conference. Across successive runs (2023-2025), PRISm has engaged over a hundred and twenty first-time researchers, with steady increases in interim artefacts and final submissions, and with notable role transitions as prior learners return as LBs/RBs, an indicator of community sustainability. Weekly live sessions surface ‘muddy points’, one-to-one clinics address design and analysis barriers, and paced deadlines with flexible adjustments acknowledge working educators’ constraints. Research approach This study adopts a case study design (Merriam, 2002) - two focal cases-to investigate how PRISm participants navigated role transitions and associated identity change across the PRISm ecosystem. A case-study approach is appropriate to get an in-depth understanding of the process (boundary movements over time), context (PRISm’s socio-technical scaffolds), and meaning (participants’ evolving self-understandings). Each case is bounded by one participant’s PRISm trajectory and the programme cycle(s) in which they participated. We purposefully selected two participants who (a) completed at least one cycle as Learners, and (b) subsequently took up support/mentoring responsibilities (LB and/or RB). These participants represent contrasting experiences within PRISm: one demonstrating a successful trajectory as a  RB and another with an unsuccessful 1050 engagement as a LB. The rationale behind this purposive selection was to capture diverse trajectories of participation and transformation within the PRISm ecosystem. This helped in maximising details and richness on boundary movements (novice to mentor roles) while keeping cases comparable on programme engagement. Data for this study were collected via semi-structured interviews: Two interviews (one per case; 50-70 minutes each) guided by a protocol targeting boundary-crossing episodes, identity talk, recognition events, and mentoring practices. The PRISm materials describing roles, phases, and mentoring routines added another layer of detail for the analysis. We conducted the interviews online with prior informed consent, audio-recorded, and transcribed verbatim. The semi-structured interviews included guiding quest ions such as “How did the interactions with others involved in PRISm influence you?”, “What were some of the challenges you faced when transitioning from a practitioner to a researcher?”.

Further we analysed the transcribed data following the guidelines of content analysis (Mayring, 2014) by anchoring on the analytical lenses of Boundary-Crossing Theory (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011), Transfer of Ownership (Warriem et al., 2017), and Legitimate Peripheral Parti cipation (Lave & Wenger, 1991) to interpret and describe role based transitions in LB/RB phases, recognition and agency as identity resources (e.g., being addressed as “researcher,” taking initiative, authorship voice). Such an approach guided the explanation of how participants traversed boundaries between practice and research and how their identities expanded as they moved from Learner to LB to RB within PRISm.

Before joining PRISm 2024, he had experience of approximately two years as a project assistant and coordinator in the department of design at a national institute, where he was working on developing content for research in ‘podcasting’ as a pedagogical platform for education. He has work experience as an instructional designer and in other roles for over 5 years. He teaches courses aligned to the ‘Theatre Acting’ domain alongside pursuing his interest as an independent performing artist. Within PRISm, he transitioned fr om being a learner in 2024 to a RB in 2025, mentoring a new learner in their research journey. His participation led to two publications: a full paper at T4E 2024 and a short paper (authored by his mentee) accepted at T4E 2025. Case 2: The second case is a female participant in the 30 to 40 age group, an experienced academic with around 12 years of service as a teacher in Information Technology at an autonomous institution. She has a considerable experience in EdTech as a practitioner educator. She teaches undergraduate level courses from ‘Computer Science and Information Technology’ domains along with conducting the labs associated with each course. She joined the PRISm 2024 cohort and participated as a learner alongside a colleague from her institution and later registered as a LB in 2025, though she could not continue beyond the orientation phase. Her engagement with PRISm culminated in a short paper accepted in the Experience Report Track at T4E 2024, which she presented. The 1052 PRISm also served as the foundation for her successful PhD proposal submission. The following analysis shows how Case 2 transitioned in and across the batches of PRISm and the role of learning materials, mentoring, and staged participation in these transitions. The following excerpt captures how Case 2 developed ownership over her research while crossing the boundary between teaching and research, using methodological artefacts as scaffolds for this transition.

“For my research related to programming concepts, I have done interviews of selected students and given an activity to students where they have to address two problem statements within a particular time. The laptop screen of students addressing the problem was being captured. Similarly, the interview questions were aligned with the activity they had done. This is to ensure that they are responding to the interview questions genuinely. By doing this, I make sure that I am not simply collecting all the inform ation. Instead, I am collecting the data I need for my research, and I am validating one data set with the other one. At that moment, I felt I was not just their teacher, I am also a researcher now. I am not completely blind to whatever information is coming; I am also verifying it.” This excerpt illustrates Case 2’s emergent researcher agency and ownership within the PRISm. Case 2 describes how she deliberately designed a methodologically rigorous data collection process, combining student activities and interviews, and validated one dataset against the other. The statement, “At that moment, I felt I was not just their teacher, I am also a researcher now” highlights a clear shift in professional identity from practitioner to practitioner-researcher. The interview protocol and screen recording acted as boundary objects that facilitated this transition. These artefacts bridged her dual roles as teacher and researcher: the interview protocol structured the inquiry, and the screen recordings provided verifiable data aligned with her research questions. By using these tools, she navigated the boundary between pedagogical practice and research practice, allowing her to validate student responses and make informed research decisions. In this way, the boundary objects scaffolded her emerging agency while enabling boundary crossing, transforming her teaching activities into a researchable process and strengthening her researcher identity.

The following excerpt illustrates how structured learning materials provided by the PRISm supported Case 2’s transition from a practitioner to a researcher. It demonstrates the role of boundary objects in scaffolding research practices and enabling agency through step-wise guidance. “During the learning and conducting research phase, I have used a number of templates. For example, there is IPT and SPT. And once my paper got accepted into the conference, I used another template for how to design the presentation. What should be the structure of the presentation, how much time I should spend on each slide, what should I reserve for responding to questions from the audience, etc. T emplates were really helpful. They gave step-wise wise detailed instructions. Even though I have learned the research methods from the learning materials, these templates supported me in a practical way.” This reporting highlights how structured learning materials (templates) facilitated Case 2’s transition from practitioner to researcher within the PRISm. Case 2 explicitly references using multiple templates, such as the IPT, SPT, and a presentation design template, to guide different stages of the research process, from planning and conducting research to preparing for conference presentations. These templates functioned as boundary objects, providing a tangible, structured bridge between her existing pedagogical expertise and the unfamiliar domain of research practice. While Case 2 had theoretical knowledge from learning materials of the PRISm, the templates offered practical, step-by-step guidance, reducing uncertainty and enabling her to enact research t asks with confidence. By scaffolding procedural knowledge and workflow, these boundary objects supported the gradual development of agency, allowing her to internalise research processes and translate abstract knowledge into concrete research actions.

Although working in the background, certain everyday elements interfered with the participants' engagement with the PRISm. The following excerpt represents the interplay between structural constraints and individual action in the practitioner-to-researcher transition within the PRISm. “The main challenge was managing the time. We are fully equipped for the teaching and laboratory activities of students. Doing action research is not part of the regular plan of the institute. That is something each teacher has to find out for themselves. The other challenge was the availability of students for participating in the research. For the paper I have written as 1053 part of the PRISm, 13 students from my class participated. But for data collection, I had to contact each of them multiple times. Managing students’ and my schedule was a difficult process. Finally, I took permission from the Director of the Institute and got two days off for regular teaching sessions for students to participate in the data collection. Because of this only I was able to complete the research and the PRISm.” The excerpt demonstrates how Case 2 confronted and actively negotiated multiple challenges while conducting research. Time constraints, student availability, and the absence of institutional policy on action research highlight the practical and procedural challenges she faced. Her strategies, repeatedly contacting students and obtaining special permission from the Director, reflect deliberate boundary-crossing actions, showing how she managed constraints to sustain her engagement with research. This aligns directly with the encountering and navigating boundaries dimension, capturing the tension between existing structures and her agency as a researcher-practitioner. At the same time, Case 2’s experience is shaped by institutional factors. The excerpt explicitly shows that the institution does not formally integrate action research into teachers’ responsibilities, making research a largely self-directed endeavour. The need to secure official permission to adjust teaching schedules further illustrates how institutional policies and authority influence access to resources, legitimacy, and time for research activities. Thus, the institutional influence acts both as a structural constraint and a mediator of research participation.

Moving beyond the boundaries and silos of research sites, with the transfer of research skills are concrete steps towards research scholarship. The following excerpt illustrates how Case 2 carried their emerging research identity and outputs beyond the scope of the PRISm, extending their engagement into formal academic and institutional spaces.

“Rather than others identifying me, I would say I have identified myself as a researcher. The motto of being in the PRISm was that I wanted to register for my PhD. I participated in PRISm and wrote a paper for submission to a conference in 2024, and I enrolled on a PhD in 2025. After that, I need to present  topic of PhD to the committee. At that time, I had the draft conference paper and completed IPT and SPT with me and I shared it in the presentat ion. The PhD research committee to approve my PhD research topic appreciated my presentation, and they were positive about the work I have already done towards the research topic I am proposing for my PhD. This gave me a lot of confidence in the research. I felt people were starting to recognise my interest and ability in the education research.” This excerpt clearly demonstrates how the research identity and competencies developed within PRISm were mobilised beyond the bounds of the programme into another formal academic space of PhD admission process. Case 2 did not treat the PRISm outputs (conference paper, IPT, SPT) as isolated or programme-specific artefacts; rather, they strategically transferred them into a new institutional context, gaining credibility and recognition as a researcher. This indicates a clear extension of boundary crossings beyond the immediate project. Additionally, the excerpt also signals emergent research ownership and agency, as Case 2 states that their identification as a researcher is self-driven rather than externally imposed. The recognition from the PhD approval committee reinforces this agency by providing institutional validation of Case 2’s research capability. As mentioned previously, transitions from participant roles to mentoring roles can act as crucial pointers for the participants in identifying themselves as part of the community and contributing to the overarching goal of expanding the research commitment. The following excerpt illustrates an across-batch transition attempt, where a participant sought to move from being a PRISm learner to taking on a more central role as a Learning Buddy. “I completed the PRISm 2024 and published a paper in the conference. I joined the next ye ar PRISm as a LB, th inking that I could support new learners. My expectation was that I have gained some experience as a learner and based on that, I can support a new learner. I also wanted to get the experience of  LB. However, for some reason, I could not continue in the LB role. The main reason was that I was occupied with some responsibilities in my own institution. The other challenge was that I was not clear about  the roles and responsibilities of the LB in the PRISm. I had attended the orientation session for the LBs and the orchestration team mentioned about the roles and responsibilities of LB, however I could not make it”. This excerpt illustrates an across-batch transition attempt, where Case 2 moved from being a learner in 2024 to taking on the role of a LB in 2025. This reflects the early stage of legitimate peripheral participation, in 1054 which a learner seeks to move from a peripheral (learner) toward a more central (LB), contributing role within PRISm. Case 2’s intention to support new learners demonstrates an emergent research identity and aspiration to extend their experience by mentoring others. However, the transition was not sustained due to competing responsibilities in their home institution and a lack of clarity regarding the roles and expectations of a LB, despite attending the orientation sessions. These challenges highlight the importance of guided participation, including role scaffolding and structured support, in facilitating the movement from peripheral to central participation. This study examined how a national, cohort-based research-practice partnership can organise practitioner learning so that boundary crossing and legitimate peripheral participation become identity expansion. The analysis across the cases revealed key dimensions of transitions within and across PRISm batches. Within a batch, cases encountered and navigated boundaries, used boundary objects to scaffold the transitions, developed owners hip and agency, extended boundary crossings beyond the immediate project, and were influenced by peers and institutions. Across batches, we observed peripheral to central participation that includes sustaining participation and contributing to the community of practice while gradually adopting a research identity. We also found guided participation, where experienced participants model and scaffold learning for newcomers, along with reflective learning and articulation, further facilitating these transitions. One of the key insights from both cases is that participants’ sense of being researchers did not emerge solely from the formal outcome of publishing a paper, which is the tangible output of PRISm.  Instead, the research agency was developed through continu ous engagement with research-oriented thinking and its application in everyday professional contexts. Both participants described moments where they began to see themselves as researchers while participating in PRISm activities, engaging with their RBs, or  applying what they had learned in their institutions. For instance, rather than waiting for the validation of a published paper, participants experienced their researcher self through interpreting data, identifying gaps, giving feedback to peers, or adapting PRISm frameworks tonew settings. This indicates that research identity is constructed during the process, not achieved at the endpoint of publication, but enacted through participation, reflection, and meaningful transfer of learning. This aligns with  literature emphasising that meaningful practitioner engagement in research requires recognition of their practitioner expertise, scaffolded opportunities for authorship and agency, and mechanisms that enable the transfer of learning across contexts (Korhonen et al., 2024; Warriem et al., 2017; Revina et al., 2023).

The findings show strong evidence of boundary crossing beyond the immediate project, where participants used concepts, frameworks, and research habits from PRISm in other institutional or independent work contexts. These applications, such as refining intervention designs, guiding students, or planning personal research projects, served as authentic spaces for practising and reinforcing research thinking. Positive feedback and recognition from these external settings deepened participants’ confidence, confi rming that their research practices had relevance and value beyond PRISm. Hence, the transfer of research reasoning across settings was central to sustaining research identity and agency, even more so than the production of a final publication. Consistent with theories of boundary crossing (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011) and legitimate peripheral participation (Lave & Wenger, 1991), we found that the boundary objects, such as IPT, SPT, weekly online live interactions, etc., which are the pedagogical design parts of the PRISm, gave a shared medium for both the learner and their RB to interact. These interactions help learners and RBs transition to more central roles in the PRISm. However, transitions of participants are hindered under role ambiguity despite motivation. Explicit expectations provide clarity about responsibilities and relational dynamics, while  recognition of these practices reinforces productive engagement. Conversely, even motivated participants struggle when role ambiguity exists; unclear expectations or inconsistent norms hinder boundary transitions, leaving interactions fragmented or superficial. Thus, the effectiveness of participation depends not merely on motivation, but on a combination of clear expectations and staged participation opportunities. Further, this study forefronts the need for mentor onboarding artefacts, micro-clinics and asynchronous mentoring routines, and deliberate recognition practices to stabilise gradual upward identity trajectories. With the insights from this study, we call upon the designers of professional development programmes like the PRISm to reframe these infrastructures as ongoing identity-forming ecosystems that support practitioners’ transition into practitioner-researchers and mentors rather than stagnant training initiatives.
