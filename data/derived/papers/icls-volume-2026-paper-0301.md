# From Design to Usability: Implementing the Mirror Agents Framework through a Multi -Agent Learn-then-Teach Architecture for Dynamic Metacognitive Calibration

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study advances the Mirror Agents Framework from conceptual design to empirical usability validation in authentic K-12 programming classrooms. W e implemented a multi-agent learn-then-teach architecture integrating four coordinated agents and a Teachable Agent that enables students to externalize and calibrate their metacognition through learning-by-teaching cycles. Thirty middle-school students engaged in two iterative learning rounds while the framework implementation captured multimodal beha vioral and metacognitive data. Pre-/post-tests (MAI, JOL) and mixed-methods analyses revealed significant gains in metacognitive monitoring accuracy and self-regulation behaviors, supported by qualitative evidence of reduced agent dependence and enhanced strategy transfer. Findings demonstrate the framework embedded system’s usability as a dynamic metacognitive scaffolding tool and highlight the value of integrating multi-agent feedback loops and teachable-agent calibration for fostering autonomous metacognitive development.

Metacognition-the awareness and regulation of one’s own cognition, is foundational for adaptive expertise in complex domains such as computer programming. Yet traditional interventions rely heavily on static self-reports or post hoc reflection tasks, which capture outcomes rather than the dynamic processes of monitoring and regulation during learning (Azevedo & Gašević, 2019; Winne, 2013). Studies in programming education show that novices often struggle with metacognitive calibration and strategic f lexibility, resulting in inefficient debugging and low persistence (Bergin et al., 2005; Loksa & Ko., 2016). This gap highlights the need for systems capable of dynamically visualizing and supporting metacognitive processes in real time. The Mirror Agents Framework (Liu et al., 2025) conceptualizes metacognitive scaffolding as a distributed, multi-agent mechanism rather than a single intelligent tutor. In this study, we implemented and evaluated the framework within an authentic C++ progra mming environment to examine its usability and effectiveness in supporting students' metacognitive regulation. Accordingly, this study empirically investigates how the Mirror Agents Framework functions when embedded in an authentic programming environment. Specifically, it examines RQ1: To what extent does the framework-embedded system improve students' metacognitive monitoring accuracy and self-regulation during programming learning? This question is addressed through a three-week classroom implementation with 30 middle-school students, using pre -/post-measures of metacognitive awareness and judgment-of-learning calibration combined with qualitative analysis of agent–student interaction logs. Mirror agents framework The Mirror Agents Framework  operationalizes the MAPS model (Frazier et al., 2021) through a multi-agent collaborative architecture  and a two-round learn-then-teach cycle. Working together, the agents transform metacognition from an internal, invisible process into an interactive and data-driven learning experience that enables real-time calibration and self-regulation. The framework was implemented within a multi-agent programming platform, which embeds Mirror Agents into a C++ coding environment for real-time behavioral and metacognitive tracking. This coordination mechanism operates through continuous cycles of monitoring, collaboration, and feedback, enabling the framework to adapt scaffolding intensity and update learner models dynamically. Within the platform, the four Mirror Agents operate in coordinated cycles to provide adaptive scaffolding and continuous metacognitive detection. The Progress Agent serves as the central data hub, collecting behavioral traces (e.g., code submissions, debugging actions, JOL entries) and issuing alerts when learning anomalies or calibration biases are detected. These signals trigger responsive interventions from the Reflection  and Strategy Agents, which adjust prompt frequency or strategy recommendations in real time. The Guide Agent maintains goal coherence and orchestrates information flow among the agents. Together, these mechanisms realize the framework’s dual functions of distributed scaffolding and dynamic metacognitive monitoring within authentic coding tasks. During the subsequent learn-then-teach phase, a separate Teachable Agent engages students in externalizing and calibrating their metacognition based on the Mirror Agents’ collected data.

• Guide Agent: Goal Setting and Planning. Supports learners in decomposing complex programming tasks

into SMART goals, coordinates timing of interventions, and initiates scaffolding sequences across agents.

• Progress Agent: Monitoring and Visualization. Continuously tracks multimodal behavioral traces (e.g.,

code submissions, debugging frequency, time-on-task) to generate cognitive and metacognitive mirrors and detect anomalies such as comprehension gaps or overload.

• Reflection Agent: Calibration and Emotion Regulation. Facilitates metacognitive awareness through

Socratic prompts (e.g., “How do you know this code works?”), provides affective feedback, and guides learners to recalibrate judgments of learning (JOL).

• Strategy Agent: Adaptive Strategy Recommendation. Maintains a dynamic strategy library, suggests or

revises tactics based on task type and prior outcomes, and collaborates with Reflection and Progress Agents for adaptive fading.

• Teachable Agent: Learn-then-Teach Calibration. Engages learners in a “learning-by-teaching” cycle by

mirroring their previous misconceptions. Through iterative diagnosis, explanation, and correction, the learner externalizes and refines metacognitive monitoring and regulation. This integrated architecture enables distributed metacognitive scaffolding: when the Progress Agent detects deviations (e.g., JOL bias > ±15 %), it triggers collaborative responses from the Reflection and Strategy Agents. Scaffolding strength is dynamically adapted as learners progress, ensuring gradual fading and promoting self-directed metacognitive control.

## Participants and procedure

Thirty Grade 7–8 students (M = 13.2, SD = 0.6) participated in a three-week intervention using the multi-agent programming platform, which embeds Mirror Agents Framework in a C++ environment. A one-group pretest– posttest design compared metacognitive changes across three stages: baseline (T0), agent-supported learning (T1), and learn-then-teach calibration (T2). Measures included the Metacognitive Awareness Inventory  (MAI; Schraw & Dennison, 1994), a Judgment of Learning (JOL) calibration task, programming performance, and post-study usability surveys. Internal consistency in the present sample was acceptable (total scale α =.88;  subscale α range:.72–.86).

Qualitative and usability evidence Thematic analysis of 75 000 words of agent–student interaction logs was conducted following Braun and Clarke’s

(2006) six-step procedure. Two coders independently identified and refined codes representing metacognitive

processes, achieving substantial agreement (κ = 0.79). Four themes emerged consistently across the learning (T1) and teaching (T2) phases: (A) Awareness and Monitoring, (B) Regulation and Strategy Use, (C) Autonomy and Transfer, and (D) Affective Regulation. Quantitative coding showed a marked redistribution of emphasis over time—Awareness and Monitoring declined from 38 % to 30 %, and Regulation and Strategy Use from 36 % to 28 %, while Autonomy and Transfer nearly doubled from 15 % to 29 %, indicating a shift from agent-prompted reflection to self-initiated control (overall χ²(3) = 26.11, p <.001, Cramér's V =.18). Adjusted standardized residuals indicated that Theme C (Autonomy and Transfer) showed the largest shift (|z| = 4.89, p <.001), Themes A and B also changed significantly (|z| = 2.32, p <.05), while Affective Regulation remained stable (|z| = 0.52, n.s.), consistent with its roughly 12 % share at both phases. Representative excerpts illustrate this evolution: “Progress Agent’s panel showed I only understood 45 % … Later I realized why my recursion failed.”(T1–S23) “You made the same base-case error I did last week — see why n = 0 should return 1, not 0. ” (T2–S12) These qualitative shifts mirror the quantitative improvements in MAI and JOL results, suggesting that students internalized external scaffolds and developed independent regulatory behaviors. This study provides preliminary evidence for the Mirror Agents Framework as an effective and usable multi-agent system for fostering metacognitive development in authentic K–12 programming contexts. The empirical findings extend prior design work by demonstrating how distributed agents and a learn-then-teach architecture candynamically support planning, monitoring, and self-regulation. The significant improvements in both MAI and JOL measures suggest that learners not only became more aware of their metacognition but also more accurate in calibrating confidence with performance—an essential skill for adaptive expertise (Lee et al., 2025). The results highlight three important design implications. First, the multi-agent coordination may help distribute scaffolding load and distributed scaffolding across metacognitive dimensions, addressing the common issue of “overscripted” support in intelligent tutoring systems. Second, the learn-then-teach cycle proved a powerful mechanism for internalizing regulation: teaching the Teachable Agent required learners to externalize their reasoning, evaluate errors, and re-articulate strategies, thereby reinforcing metacognitive transfer (Biswas et al., 2005; Jin et al., 2024). Third, usability analysis revealed that personalization and feedback tone, particularly the non-judgmental prompts from the Progress and Reflection Agents, are key to maintaining engage ment and trust in AI-driven scaffolding (Wisniewski et al., 2020). From a learning  s ciences perspective, these findings provide empirical support for the idea that metacognition can be socially distributed across human-AI systems. The Mirror Agents framework exemplifies a “dynamic scaffolding ecosystem” in which multiple agents interact w ith learners and with one another to coconstruct regulatory expertise. While this pilot study was limited by its short duration and single-group design, the strong convergence between quantitative and qualitative evidence establishes a so lid foundation for future work. Future work will examine the interaction patterns among agents and learners. This study demonstrates the feasibility of translating a conceptual multi-agent framework into a functioning metacognitive support system validated in authentic K –12 classrooms. Three contributions emerge for the Learning Sciences community. First, it offers an empirical instantiation of distributed metacognitive scaffolding, showing that coordinating specialized agents can support planning, monitoring, and regulation without overscripting. Second, it provides initial evidence that a learn-then-teach mechan ism can promote calibration accuracy and foster learner autonomy within a single intervention cycle.
