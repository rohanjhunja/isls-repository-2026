# Empowering Youth as Researchers: A Case Study of Community-based Participatory Action Research at the Downtown Boxing Gym

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores an approach to mentoring youth researchers for a community-based participatory research project and the impact of that approach on the youth researchers. The paper highlights the importance of providing both training and continued support after training to ensure youth researchers feel empowered as members of the research team and have opportunities to develop transferable skills like communication, critical thinking, and ownership. In examining the impact of the training, findings revealed the youth gained practical research experience, as well as developed a deeper understanding of interview techniques and ethical considerations. The project emphasized the value of youth-led research in amplifying underrepresented voices and offering young people a platform to actively engage in decision-making processes. Ultimately, the study concludes that equipping young people with research skills, alongside ongoing support, profoundly impacts their personal growth and enables meaningful contributions to their communities.

## Introduction

Since the late 1980s, youth research has shifted from viewing young people as "problems" to adopting a positive youth development (PYD) approach, which sees them as valuable resources that can be leveraged for community growth and participation (Perkins et  al., 2003). Unfortunately, even in PYD-based research, youth are rarely represented on research teams, resulting in less ap plicable research findings and missed opportunities  for supporting youth development and agency (Cammarota & Fine, 2008; Ozer & Douglas, 2013). Involving youth in community-based participatory research (CBPR) supports positi ve youth development and ensures their voices are included i n the research process. It helps youth develop esse ntial skills such as technical expertise, leadership, and civic engagement, while promoting equity and inclusion. Youth from marginalized backgrounds bring valuable insights as  co-researchers, using their insider perspective to cre ate a comfortable environment for candid participation (Bautista et al., 2013). However, despite growing support, there are no standardized methods for training and supporting youth researchers. This paper explores the preparation, support, and e xperiences of youth researchers involved in a CBPR project focused on the Downtown Boxing Gym (DBG), an out-of-school initiative serving 200 Black, low-income students in Detroit. DBG provides academic, mentorship, and athletic programs, emphasizing students' academic and physical growth within a supportive community. The active engagement of youth researchers in this project offered a unique perspective on DBG's impact on stu dents’ STEM skills development.  Our goal in the present study is to describe the methodologies used to involve youth in our project with the aim of expanding how youth are prepared and supported to serve as essential members of the research team.

## Literature review

Involving youth, especially marginalized youth, in research serves multiple purposes: it functions as a pedagogical strategy, a mode of resistance, a challenge to trad itional notions of valued knowledge, a decolonizing  tool, and a transformative research methodology (Bautista et al., 2013). Central to this practice is the recognition that the way research about marginalized youth is framed and exe cuted is often problematic (Torre & Fine, 2006), fa iling to acknowledge youths’ lived experiences or integrate their perspectives on effective methodologies. Invo lving youth in research addresses these shortcomings by e nabling youth to define or redefine the problem's n ature and propose solutions to address it (Bautista et al., 2 013). It can also improve access to p articipants and enrich data quality, as youth are often more comfortable sharing experiences with peers. Their familiarity with the community enables them to navigate their surroundings effecti vely, gather data efficiently, and create a comfort able atmosphere that encourages candid responses, offering insights that adult researchers cannot replicate (Anucha et al., 2020; Bautista et al., 2013).

In addition to advancing research, involving youth in the research process directly benefits them as individuals. It offers opportunities to develop val uable academic and professional skills, such as dat a analysis, public speaking, advocacy, communication,  and leadership. It also fosters civic engagement as  youth share findings through platforms such as social media, sc hool events, and theater, strengthening peer connec tions and community involvement. Engaging youth as co-researchers prioritizes their knowl edge, validates their experiences, and centers research on their perspectives (Anucha et al., 2020). While youth researchers are increasingly praised fo r advancing equity in research, their involvement i s not without challenges. Some adults remain resistan t, often due to doubts about the validity of youth-generated knowledge (Bertrand, 2012). Moreover, despite the recognized benefits of involving youth in research, there are no standardized methods for training and supporting them. This lack of best practices not only limits the potential impact of youth participation but also perpetuates skepticism about their contributions, underscoring the need for consistent, robust training methods to fully realize the value of youth-generated knowledge. Therefore, this study aims to describe our methods for actively preparing and supporting youth to serve as co-researchers and the impact of that method on both the youth researchers and th e research project. In sharing this information, we  seek to inform discussions about effective practices in youth researcher training.

## Project context

This study presents the first year of a five-year CBPR project, rooted in a decade-long partnership between DBG and the project’s principal investigator. Youth par ticipants took on the role of co-researchers, helping to design interview questions and then interviewing their pee rs about how DBG supports students’ STEM interests and participation. The research team for the project wa s comprised of three faculty members, four graduate  students, four staff members from DBG, and four youth researchers.

## Participants

For the sake of this paper, which is designed to re flect on the training and support for the youth res earchers, the youth researchers served as the participants. The f our youth researchers were all high school students, with three of them identifying as male and one identifying as female; all four identified as Black or African American. Program design In February 2024, a structured three-day training program was held at DBG to provide youth researchers with both the theoretical foundation and practical exper ience needed to competently assume their roles in t he larger research project. To achieve this, the program had three key objectives. First, it aimed to build strong relationships between the youth researchers and non-DBG members of the research team, fostering a suppo rtive and collaborative environment. Second, the training int roduced them to the project’s scope and goals, help ing them understand the significance of their role within th e research. Third, it covered essential skills such  as understanding the purpose of interviews, ethical research principles, and effective interviewing techniques. The first day introduced participants to the broade r goals of the project and highlighted the importan ce of interviews as a research method in gathering meaningful, nuanced data, especially in the context of qualitative research. Additionally, ethical considerations in research were explored, emphasizing principles such as informed consent, confidentiality, and respect for participa nts, which are vital when conducting interviews and  handling sensitive information.

On the second day, the focus shifted to practical s kills development, beginning with interviewing techniques. Participants learned essential communic ation skills for effective interviews, such as acti ve listening, open-ended questioning, and building rapport with interviewees. Participants also received an introduction to the technology and tools that would be used throughout the project, helping them become comfortable with the digital and technical aspects of data collection and storage.

The final day of the training concentrated on solid ifying these skills through hands-on practice. The participants reviewed the steps involved in conduct ing an interview and participated in mock interview  sessions with the research team, allowing them to apply their newly acquired skills in a controlled setting. These simulated interviews enabled them to practice everything from  setting up an interview to asking questions and re sponding appropriately to answers.

In addition, the team also provided ongoing support after the training to the participants by meeting with them once a week for 30-minute supervision sessions. These meetings served as a platform to address questions and review interview recordings with constructive feedback. Each youth researcher was paired with an individual mentor (three graduate students and the principal i nvestigator), ensuring personalized guidance and ta ilored feedback. The mentors not only discussed strengths and areas for growth in  the researchers' interviews but also helped them navigate challenges and develop their s kills further. The sessions began with a five-minute group discussion where the youth researchers collectively  shared the positives and challenges they experienc ed during the week’s interviews, followed by one-on-one supervision for focused support and an opportunity to process the youth researchers' experiences as first-time interviewers.

## Data analysis

Participants completed a 35-item online self-assessment immediately following the training, cons isting of 30 Likert-type questions and five open-ended responses. The assessment measured their unde rstanding of key concepts, confidence in applying research methods, and perceptions of training effectiveness. Open-ended questions explored key takeaways, the most improved  research skills, areas requiring further developme nt, and recommendations for future training enhancements. A dditionally, participants evaluated various aspects  of training delivery, including content clarity, engag ement, facilitator support, material usefulness, and overall session quality. Descriptive statistics were applie d to analyze the Likert-type items, while thematic analysis was conducted on open-ended responses to identify prevalent trends, key insights, and areas for improvement.

## Findings

Learning and growth experience Thematic analysis of participants’ open-ended responses suggested several key aspects of th e training enhanced their interview skills. First, they described how i mportant various interviewing skills, such as refle ctions, questions, and paraphrases, were to  understanding interviewee responses and promoting d eeper conversations. Another valuable lesson was about interview ethics, which emphasized maintaining professionalism, respect, and confidentiality during interviews. Additionally, understanding what to look for during an interview—such as nonverbal cues and the underlying message in responses—also emerged as a critical takeaway, helping them become more attentive and skilled interviewers.

Three participants also shared which research skills they felt had improved the most from the start to the end of the training. The first participant highligh ted paraphrasing as a key area of growth, explainin g that they could now restate information in a way that ensured  clear communication and accurate understanding of interviewees’ responses. The second participant emphasized their ability to connect with interviewees, noting that they had learned to create a welcoming and comforta ble atmosphere throughout the interview process, wh ich encouraged participants to speak openly. The third participant focused on the improvement in their listening skills, describing how they had become a more attentive and  present listener, which allowed them to capture su btle details and better understand the interviewees' per spectives. Together, these skills reflect the compr ehensive impact of the training on enhancing their research abilities. Results also suggested the impact of the training e xtended beyond gaining experience in the research process, fostering the development of valuable tran sferable skills. Participants reported improving in  areas such as leadership and also noted they felt a strong sense of ownership in the research project. Additionally, participants noted their communication skills were enhanced, wit h participants becoming more adept at conveying ide as clearly and effectively, both in interviews and in team settings. Challenges One challenge that many participants faced was know ing how to act during an interview and how to exten d it effectively. While interviewing, they often struggl ed with striking the right balance between confiden ce and approachability and also felt unsure of how to use body language, tone, and phrasing to make a positiv e impression. Another difficulty was knowing how to e xtend the conversation when needed —many were unsure how to ask follow-up questions or keep the discussion flowing without  losing momentum or taking up too much time. This challenge highlighted the need for more training and support in mastering communication strategies to navigate interviews smoothly and ensure they are both thorough and engaging. To improve future training, participants suggested incorporating more hands-on interview practice, specifically by having individuals play different r oles as the interviewee and interviewer. They belie ve this approach would provide more practice managing different personalities and responses and help participants better understand how to adapt to various interview scenarios, ultimately enhancing their interviewing skills.

## Discussion

Involving youth researchers can be helpful when conducting research projects about youth. Even when youth may have some experience in research, it may be helpful  to have them strengthen their skills and provide c ontinuous support during the research process. In this study, a three-day training was presented to four youth researchers in hopes to enhance their skills and support their dev elopment in research interviews. To ensure youth re searchers had continued support throughout their interviews, the research team continued meeting with them on a weekly basis to provide supervision. Each youth researcher  had an individual mentor that provided feedback on  the interview recording as well as discussed strengths and areas of growth for each interview. This allowed for further skill development and an opportunity for questions to be answered as they arose. A key finding was that youth researchers found the training helpful as it enhanced their interview skills. This finding is supported by literature which highl ights the technical skills, like designing studies,  creating data collection tools, recruiting participants, and implementing methodologies like interviews and surveys, that youth researchers acquire from their involvement (Anucha et al., 2020). Aligned with the literature, as a re sult of the training, youth researchers who participated in our  three-day training felt that they had improved their list ening and paraphrasing skills. In addition, youth researchers felt there were transferable skills gained after the training, for example, a higher sense of autonomy and ownership.

Along with skills that youth researchers can gain from participating in research early on, another benefit of having youth researchers interview other youth is the openness participants may feel with them. Because youth researchers may have a near peer exp erience and may be familiar with those they are int erviewing, the data may be enriched as they have more access to participant s (Anucha et al., 2020; Bautista et al., 2013). Fin dings from the training that included youth researchers' self-perceived improvement in fostering a comfortable and safe environment for participants to be able to share th eir experiences with them aligned with the purpose of having youth researchers conduct the interviews. Even thou gh youth researchers felt they were able to promote  a safe environment, some did note the struggle between bal ancing confidence and approachability. This finding reinforces the need for continuous training, mentoring and supervision as young researchers continue to strengthen their skills.

## Conclusion and recommendation

This three-day long multidimensional training centered youth r esearcher development with the aim to develop their qualitative interview skills and their autonomy in the research process. One main goal of this training was to foster a strong relationship with youth researchers  from the beginning  to ensure a safe space was dev eloped for questions, mistakes, and clarifications. Another go al of the training was to provide continued support  to youth researchers as they put into practice what they had learned in the three-day training. The program included several key components: an int roduction to the five-year project’s scope, goals, and research questions, as well as the rationale for involving youth researchers engaged in the qualitative research and the important role they played in  the larger study. Participants also received traini ng in essential interview skills such as listening, restating, paraphrasing, probing, and summarizing, as well as the opportunit y to engage in mock interview sessions with the research team.

After analyzing youth researchers’ feedback and fin dings, youth researchers noted growth in specific skills, for example, increased understanding in the various question types that could be asked during an interview. Additionally, they reported a notable improvement on their restatement and listening skills after the training as it pertained to gathering more information about a top ic. In addition, they reported a stronger sense of their ownership and greater understanding of their responsibility in their roles as youth researchers. Recommendations for future researchers working with  youth include incorporating more experiential components earlier in the training. Although there was a mock interview on the third day, breaking down the skills and engaging in experiential training may be more e ffective when learning new skills. Additionally, co nducting a practice interview with a participant from the st udy’s target age range could help simulate a real i nterview experience. Including feedback after this mock interview would also provide valuable real-time insights into their skills before they begin actual interviews. While s ome of these recommendations were addressed and app eared effective during weekly supervision meetings, intro ducing these components earlier could establish a s tronger foundation before interviews begin, thereby enrichi ng the data. Despite potential areas for improvemen t, the program design ultimately aims to support youth in their development as researchers.
