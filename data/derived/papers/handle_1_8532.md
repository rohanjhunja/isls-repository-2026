# Empirical Validation of Early Mathematics Learning Progressions

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learning progressions describe the development of sophistication in studentsÕ thinking about discipline-specific topics, and specify the intermediary phases of studentsÕ understanding that lead from foundational knowledge to more advanced thinking. The specificity of this developmental progression can meaningfully inform the teaching and learning process, including the design of classroom assessment resources. The purpose of this presentation is to describe the theoretical and empirical evidence used to specify and empirically validate the learning progressions for two early mathematics learning progressions focused on numeric relational reasoning and spatial reasoning. Empirical evidence was collected from teachersÕ accounts of studentsÕ development of sophistication and task-based interviews with students. The resulting learning progression served as the foundation for classroom assessments designed to provide teachers with actionable data to use in instructional decision making.

Learning progressions to inform mathematics classroom assessments Learning progressions and trajectories articulate t he development of studentsÕ understanding within a domain. They reflect a research-based sequence of how students move from novice to advanced thinking within a domain (Corcoran, Mogat, & Rosher, 2009). In science and mathematics education, significant advances have been made to articulate learning progressions and trajectorie s, respectively. Based on a comprehensive review of  learning progressions and trajectories, Confrey (2018) noted  that there are no substantial differences between the definitions of these theories of learning; as such, we use the term learning progressions herein. Learning progressions describe the development of sophistication in studentsÕ thinking about disciplinespecific topics. This sequential progression of und erstanding moves from studentsÕ foundational knowle dge to more advanced thinking (Bennett, 2015). Progressing  from foundational levels of understanding to advan ced thinking involves a network of intermediary knowledge and skills that characterize the learning progression. By nature, these intermediary knowledge and skills por tray student thinking as a progression toward targe ted understanding. Some have likened these intermediary skills to stepping stones along a path or to hand and foot holds on a climbing wall. Both are useful analogies for describing the knowledge, skills, and cognitive processes that students exhibit as they progress from less to greater understanding in the domain. Three important structural components help define a  learning progression: lower boundary, upper boundary, and intermediary knowledge and skills. Fi rst, the lower boundary of the progression represen ts the foundational knowledge and skills that students need to engage with the intermediary knowledge and skills. These may be conceptualized as pre-requisite knowledge and skills or prior learnings that form the basis for new learning. Second, the upper boundary of the progression refle cts a greater level of sophistication that is expec ted at the culmination of a specific phase of learning, and ma y be determined by curricular expectations (e.g., c ontent standards), canonical disciplinary knowledge, or so cietal expectations of competence (Corcoran et al.,  2009; Duschl et al., 2011). The upper boundary of one pro gression may represent the lower boundary of another. The lower and upper boundaries can be characterized as the entry and exit points of the learning progressi on, respectively, and flank the intermediary knowledge and skills. Third, and finally, the intermediary knowledge and skills can be conceptualized as the incremental pro gression of understanding that accumulates over tim e to represent the nature of knowing in a domain (Duschl  et al., 2011), and include knowledge representatio ns, cognitive processing, and the structure and organization of concepts. The intermediary knowledge and skills are not a rigid set of understandings that must be mastered before progressing; moreover, the nature of the connections between these intermediary knowledge and skills may differ for each person. As part of a larger project, we articulated learnin g progressions for two early mathematics concepts, numeric relational reasoning and spatial reasoning. These learning progressions are intended to form the basis of instruction and assessment in Kindergarten through Grade 2. The purpose of this presentation is to des cribe the theoretical and empirical evidence used to specify and empirically validate these learning progressions. We used an iterative development process that included a thorough analysis of existing literature, input from experts in the field of early mathematics, teachersÕ perceptions of the developmental appropriateness of specific knowledge and skills, and cognitive interviews with children.

ICLS2022 Proceedings 1533 © ISLS Numeric relational reasoning Numeric relational reasoning (NRR) is often defined  as the ability to mentally analyze relationships b etween numbers or expressions using knowledge of properties of operations, decomposition, and known facts (Baroody et al., 2015; Farrington-Flint et al., 2007). Numer ic relational reasoning is closely related to numbe r sense, the ability to work with numbers flexibly (Gersten & Chard, 1999). For example, students use addition facts that they know, such as 5+5=10, to solve unknown facts, such as 6+5 (i.e., “Since 5+5 is 10 and 6 is one more than 5, 6+5 must be one more than 5+5.”). Numeric relational reasoning is not a rote procedure that can be followed step by step. Instead, it requires reasoning through a “str ategic” decision making process based on the relati onships between numbers (Whitacre et al., 2017).

Spatial reasoning Spatial reasoning (SR) – the ability to interact wi th, navigate in, and understand one’s environment –  is a foundational concept for early mathematics (NRC, 20 09). Spatial reasoning is thought to have two prima ry components: spatial orientation and spatial visualization. Spatial orientation refers to the ability to picture objects or settings from different perspectives, such as me ntally re-orienting one-self to see something from above or below. Spatial visualization is used when a child imagines an object and transforms it mentally. Evidence suggests that spatial reasoning skills support students’ overall mathematics knowledge and specific concepts such as place value, relationships between numbers, and operation s (Newcombe, 2010). Students with strong spatial sk ills create mental images of numbers and situations, whi ch strengthens their problem-solving and number sen se abilities. The links between spatial reasoning and mathematics are so strong that “spatial instruction will have a ‘two-for-one’ effect, yielding benefits in mathematics as well” (Verdine et al., 2014). Evidence to empirically recover learning progressions Because learning progressions are intended to infor m teaching and learning, they must be carefully art iculated and sufficient evidence should be collected to eval uate the accuracy of the claims being made about students’ development of understanding. Ketterlin-Geller and colleagues (2013) suggested an  iterative process for articulating a learning progression that included four stages:

1. Specify domain-specific cognitive processes, strate gies, and knowledge using theoretical propositions

about learning, literature reviews, tasks analyses, and empirical models of learning.

2. Organize cognitive processes, strategies, and knowl edge based on plausible interactions identified

through theoretical propositions about learning, student interviews, and analyses by domain experts.

3. Differentiate levels of expertise through evidence from student interviews, experimental research

examining performance differences, and examination of item-difficulty hierarchy.

4. Instantiate cognitive processes, strategies, and kn owledge in test specifications with input from expe rt

review panels, alignment studies, student interviews, and psychometric modeling. Throughout each stage, data should be gathered from a variety of sources in an iterative process. In this presentation, we describe the theoretical a nd empirical process used to empirically validate t he numeric relational reasoning and spatial reasoning learning progressions. For each learning progression, we report results from several sources. First, we report results from an extensive review by experts. Second, we described descriptive and inferential statistical analyses of survey results from teachers in grades K-2. Third, and finally, we report analyses from cognitive interview data with children in K-2 to identify themes in their reasoning skills. Theoretical evidence To articulate hypothesized learning progressions for both NRR and SR, we used an iterative process that included meetings with subject-matter experts, literature re views, internal reviews, and external reviews with subjectmatter experts. As introduced earlier, each learning progression includes lower and upper boundaries as well as intermediary knowledge and skills. Because of the c omplexity of NRR and SR, we divided each construct into subconstructs or targeted learning goals. Each targeted learning goal includes core concepts which are comprised of fine-grained knowledge and skill statements. The se knowledge and skill statements are ordered from least complexity to greatest complexity and specify the g rade level range within Grades K-2 at which they ar e most developmentally appropriate.

Cognitive interviews are grounded in psychological interviewing methods as a way of investigating participants’ cognitive processes and understanding how participants store and structure their knowledge in long-term memory. During the int erviews, participants work on tasks that are often openended and require multiple steps to complete. The i nterviewer asks in-depth questions designed to bett er understand the participant’s thinking.

We included students in Grade 3 to test the upper boundaries of the learning progressions. For NRR, we interviewed 32 students across two timepoints for a  total of 64 interviews. For SR, we interviewed 15 students across two timepoints for a total of 30 interviews. Interview protocols were strategically designed to provide (1) tasks aligned with each of the intermediary knowled ge and skills in the learning progressions, (2) sca ffolding questions, and (3) reasoning questions to better un derstand students’ thinking. To collect confirming and/or disconfirming evidence, all students had an opportu nity to respond to each task, regardless of the hyp othesized developmental appropriateness. We examined correctn ess by skill statement by grade level to examine if  the ordering and grade-level designations of our hypothesized learning progression could be empirically recovered. Qualitative analyses were also conducted to investi gate if additional reasoning concepts were used by students that were not captured in the learning progressions.

Quantitative and qualitative analyses of cognitive interviews provided important information on students’ conceptualization, students’ prior knowledge to work through the content, the connection between concepts, and at what grade level students demonstrate emerging a nd mastery level of understanding. The results from  the analyses of cognitive interviews provided us with e vidence to empirically recover the NRR and SR learn ing progression and make some adjustments.

Study 2: Surveys with teachers Another source of validity evidence comes from surveys administered to teachers in Grades K-2. We administered separate surveys for each learning progression. Educators are uniquely positioned within the teaching and learning process to provide meaningful input; Educators are very familiar with the grade-level curriculum and c an often pinpoint areas where students tend to struggle. Mor eover, teachers may be able to anticipate studentsÕ  errors because of their recurrence over time and across gr oups of students. As such, educators may have insig hts into student learning that can help verify the structure of learning progressions. Approximately 300 teachers responded to the survey on NRR, and 200 teachers responded to the survey on SR. The survey presented each of the intermediary knowledge and skills within each learning progression and ICLS2022 Proceedings 1535 © ISLS asked teachers to consider the grade level taught and respond about the developmental appropriateness of the skill, whether the skill was a focal or prerequisite skill, and how often and when they teach the skill during the school year. We investigated the alignment between our hyp othesized learning progression and teachers’ concep tions about the developmental appropriateness of the skill statements. Survey results yielded meaningful information to support or modify the hypothesized learning progressions for both NRR and SR.

## Conclusions

In this presentation, we describe the theoretical and empirical evidence used to specify and empirically validate the learning progressions for NRR and SR. For each construct, we began by thoroughly specifying hypothesized progressions using input from subject-matter expert s, literature reviews, internal reviews, and extern al reviews with independent experts. To evaluate the accuracy and sufficiency of the hypothesized learning progressions, we collected evidence from various sources, including cognitive interviews with children and surveys conducted with teachers. These data contributed to an iterative process in which revisions were made to the learning progression after each phase of data collection was complete. T he process and resulting learning progressions will  be disseminated during the presentation.
