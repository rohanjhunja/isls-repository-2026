# What’s the Problem Worth Solving Together: How Interdisciplinary Student Teams Construct Shared Problems

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study aims to enhance the design of interdisciplinary collaborative tasks in higher education by examining how university students from diverse disciplinary backgrounds collaborate to define a shared problem. Drawing on mini ethnographic case studies, it compares two student teams. Using a multimodal blending perspective, the analysis explores the resources students utilise in the problem-construction process. Preliminary findings reveal variations in how the teams interpret and approach a task, offering insights to inform the design of interdisciplinary collaborative tasks.

## Introduction

In today’s evolving higher education landscape, cou rses are embracing interdisciplinary project-based learning and teamwork to equip students with skills beyond t raditional boundaries. Despite the emphasis on interdisciplinary collaboration, mastering it p oses challenges  (Lyall et al., 201 9; Spelt et al., 2009). Effective teamwork requires not just devising solutions but a lso establishing mutual comprehension, especially i n interdisciplinary contexts where participants bring  diverse knowledge and perspectives. Moreover, comp lex interdisciplinary issues lack singular solutions, making shared understanding crucial yet difficult to achieve. While collaborative learning has been extensively studied (Bovill, 2020; Valsiner & vander Veer, 2000), interdisciplinary collaboration presents unique cha llenges. Students' interdisciplinary projects often  lack depth, and the process of negotiating and constructing shared problems is poorly understood (Gardiner, 2020). This study aims to contribute empirical knowledge by exploring how students from different disciplines discuss and construct shared problems for collaborative projects and how designed tasks support this process.

## Theoretical framework

This study draws on sociomaterial and distributed c ognition perspectives to explore collaborative learning processes. The sociomaterial perspective views learning as the collective construction of knowledge, emphasising dynamic interactions among students (Valsiner & vander Veer, 2000). The distributed cognition framework sees thinking and learning as collaborative processes di stributed among team members and resources within t heir environment, all aimed at problem-solving (Damşa, 2014; Hutchins, 1995). Additionally, the multimodal blending perspective helps deconstruct the diverse resources  students employ to construct  joint understanding (Markauskaite et al., 2021). Together, t hese frameworks facilitate a comprehensive analysis of students' collaborative problem-solving processes.

Methodological and analytical design This research involved six student teams in three i nterdisciplinary courses throughout a semester. Team 1 and Team 2, from one of the courses, were selected for an initial comparative analysis. These teams were selected due to their diverse disciplinary backgrounds and their involvement in projects that utilised sensors. The course catered to high-achieving postgraduate students and focused on research translation, design, and innovation. Over a period of 13 weeks, students were assigned the task  of developing a prototype along with a n accompanying business case.

In Week 3, their collaborative sessions were primarily focused on identifying a problem to address using sensors. During a seminar, students were introduced to a design thinking method, using sticky notes to write down ideas and related key factors to facilitate the process. Verbal and non-verbal interactions were captured through video, audio, and field notes. These recordings were then transcribed and coded using a multimodal blending approach, aiming to identify the personal and other resources students contributed to the problem construction  space as described by Markauskaite et al. (2021). Thematic coding was employed to construct an initial coding scheme consisting of seven categories: disciplinary or technological knowledge, establishing the problem-solving environment, addressing the assessment task, methodology for problem identification, application of the technology, language—clarifying terms/expressions, and other (e.g., organising meetings).

## Preliminary findings and discussion

The initial analysis of Week 3 data reveals distinct approaches by the teams towards the assigned  task. Despite receiving identical instructions, each team tackled the task differently. Team 1 adopted a slower, more comprehensive strategy, drawing on a wide range of disciplinary and technological knowledge, particularly from students with backgrounds in medicine and applied s ciences (49% of the time). This knowledge enriched their discussions, particularly regarding sensor-related aspects. However, Team 1 did not engage extensively with the proposed design thinking method  (2% of the time). In contrast, Team 2 prioriti sed generating new ideas and maintaining focus  on the task. Although they effectively utilised the design  thinking method and dedicated a significant proportion of time to task discussion (61% of the time), their discussions lacked depth in disciplinary knowledge. Despite their contrasting approaches, both teams successfully completed the task, identifying several potential problems for further exploration.

The observed disparities may stem from several factors. Firstly, Team 1 included more members from science disciplines, potentially finding the design thinking method more challenging to foll ow, whereas Team 2 included members with design backgrounds, making them more familiar with and willing to adhere to the method. Moreover, Team 1’s greater familiarity with sensors, compared to Team 2, in which none of the team members had relevant expertise, might have facilitated deeper discussions on this topic during the initial approach. Contributions and future work The findings of this study provide valuable insights into the dynamics of interdisciplinary teamwork in the initial project stage. They identify the resources students bring to the teamwork and how team composition, in relation to these resources, impacts the problem construction  process. By highlighting the influence of disciplinary backgrounds on students’ approaches to constructing the problem, this research contributes to a deeper understanding of collaborative project-based learning. Additionally, the initial coding scheme offers the analytical framework for future studies. Our future plans include identifying different strategies and exploring the evolution of problem construction over time.
