# Dynamics of Social Interaction in Collaborative Debugging

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how novice programmers maintain social interactions during collaborative debugging. A qualitative case study was conducted with five undergraduate students working in small groups on debugging tasks. Recordings from 12 sessions were analyzed. Findings from our thematic analysis show that social interactions function as both emotional regulation tools and collaborative resources, helping group members manage tension, sustain engagement, and maintain group cohesion. Humor and playfulness particularly supported a positive and productive debugging atmosphere.

Collaborative Debugging (CD)  is a process in which group members jointly identify, interpret, and resolve programming errors. Debugging, originally a computer science concept, refers to the strategy of finding and fixing faulty code in a program (Brennan & Resnick, 2012). Due to the moderately ill-structured nature of debugging (Jonassen & Hung, 2015), effective communication is essential to coordinate cognitive efforts in CD. By adding to the existing literature on the topic (e.g., DeLiema et al., 2023), th is study examines the communication dynamics of novice programmers during with the research questions below. RQ1: What types of social interactions do novice programmers employ during CD? RQ2: What precedes and follows these social interactions during CD? By investigating these dimensions, this study aims to deepen the understanding of how social interactions shape collaboration and group performance in the context of CD.

## Collaborative debugging as a learning context

CD represents a complex learning context in which cognitive, metacognitive, and social processes intersect. CD requires learners to jointly identify, interpret, and correct programming errors through continuous cycles of hypothesis generation, testing, and revision. In educational programming environments, these processes are often mediated by dialogue, negotiation, and shared reflection (Emara et al., 2020; Lui et al., 2024). Debugging serves as both a cognitive and social practice: it demands technical reasoning while simultaneously requiring coordination, communication, and mutual understanding among collaborators (Brennan & Resnick, 2012; Kapur, 2011). In programming and debugging contexts, environments, where tasks are inherently complex and error-prone, being open to communication can help mitigate the challenges (Falkner et al., 2014). Aligning with these perspectives, the present study aims to examine how social interactions within a group occur during CD.

## Method

This qualitative case study (Merriam, 2009) was conducted at a public research university in Turkey. The case was an educational technology course offered to undergraduate students at the university.  There were 5 students enrolled in the course in Spring 2024. Two groups were formed: 3 members (Fiona, Eva, Sam – pseudonyms) in group 1,  and 2 members (Nora, Mike-pseudonyms) in group 2. In each session, participants completed the debugging tasks (programs not functioning properly) in groups on the block-based programming platform, Scratch, for from 10 to 30 minutes.

Audio and screen recordings were made to investigate the social interactions of the groups during CD. In total, 14 recordings of 2 groups formed the dataset. 4 hours and 46 minutes of recordings were analyzed. The data was first coded using ECSAP framework (von Davier et al., 2017). Then, the moments coded with “maintaining (positive) communications” were identified and  coded again using an adapted version of An et al. ’s (2025) framework to determine what type of social interactions occurred.  Seven types were identified  in total of 44 episodes: (1) Affective (Expressing positive emotions through verbal communication or laughter); (2) Cohesive-task (Expressing encouragement and praise to peers through specific discourse); (3) Cohesive-playful (Jokes related or unrelated to the task at hand or banter for purely social functions); (4) Dis-affective (Expressions of 2916 negative feelings such as the desire to disengage from the task); (5) Uncohesive (Negative impact on current group, e.g., lack of respect for peers, blaming rude discourse); (6) Humorous coping (Using humor/laughter to deal with frustration, mistake, self-criticism); (7) Gentle criticism (Criticizing with a soft tone). The episodes were detected and analyzed via thematic analysis. To ensure the trustworthiness, the first author coded the data independently, and then peer review was conducted by the second author for the whole dataset, and the disagreements were resolved.

Types of social interactions Out of 44 episodes of social interactions, cohesive-playful interactions were found in 31 episodes, as the most common one. Humorous coping was the second most common one, which was detected in 10 episodes. The rest were Gentle criticism (5), Cohesive-task (4), Affective (4), Dis-affective (3), and Uncohesive (1).  Additionally, to identify what kind of acts trigger the social interactions, and what follows them, patterns were formed from the data via frequency analysis. resulting in four themes.

Theme 1: Dominance of cohesive-playful and humorous interactions as coping mechanisms Most of the social interactions detected during CD were of a playful and humorous nature. It can be inferred that to release the tension or to cope with the difficulty of the debugging tasks, groups made jokes, or made fun of themselves. In one episode, Fiona uttered “Oh my, I feel like a donkey. ” Sam replied “It is known to be hardworking.” Fiona giggled sarcastically, saying “Thanks a lot for this compliment.” Theme 2: Confusion and frustration as frequent triggers of social interactions During their CD, participants quite frequently got confused when they struggled to pinpoint the solutions of the bugs in the program they were working on, or when they were frustrated with the steps that their partners took. In many of the cases, this tens ion resulted in social interactions, such as gentle criticism or disengaging from the task. For instance, in one episode, Nora said “it was turning [..] why are you doing this? [talking to the program]”, and Mike responded “It doesn’t matter. You aren’t working on it anyway.” Theme 3: Social interactions as a short break when deciding the next move While deciding on the steps to be taken to fix the bugs, participants praised their partners, made funny comments about the task elements, and tried to make sure not to offend their partners. These “social breaks” did not break the flow of task completion and were even beneficial for the debugging process. For instance, when Nora suggested the next step for a debugging task, Mike complimented her: “Hmm. You’re a genius [...] It wants us to do this, right? […] It did not work.” Theme 4: Social interaction as a companion to intense experiences Although neither failure nor conflict are necessarily detrimental to the CD process, and on the contrary, even desirable for problem-solving; this hints at the possibility that social interactions might be a tool to deal with the negativity, or the result of a conflict. Still, a sense of achievement and satisfaction also prompts social interaction. Thus, it can be claimed that when there is an intense positive or negative event during CD, social interaction also occurs. For instance, Eva, Fiona, and Sam cou ld not complete the debugging task properly, trying to change several codes in the program. Fiona said, “If it doesn’t work, we can change  it again, it’s free of charge [joking], isn’t it?” Eva exclaimed “Yes, it worked!” The findings suggest that social interaction is a n inherent element of CD, serving both emotional and functional purposes. It is used as a break during demanding problem-solving, sometimes as a coping mechanism with failure, and sometimes as a companion to rather intense learning experiences such as the joy stemming from achievement, or conflict with the teammates. That is, social communication among novice programmers was not merely peripheral but integrally embedded in a cognitive activity (Mercer & Howe, 2012; Roschelle & Teasley, 1995). The observed interactions reflected how participants regulated emotions, sustained engagement, and managed group coordination during the inherently demanding task of debugging (Isohätälä et al., 2020; Järvenoja et al., 2019). The patterns observed across the four themes shed light on the ways novice programmers use communicative acts as both a coping mechanism and a social lubricant to navigate the complex affective and cognitive nature of  CD (Kaendler et al., 2015).  Further research may focus on how social interactions can be integrated into debugging  processes to examine relevant learning outcomes and how these interactions can be scaffolded to enhance the quality of debugging.
