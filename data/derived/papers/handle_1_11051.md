## Introduction

Games are not only forms of documentation, communic ative expression, and play but critical sites and technologies for learning. Indeed, as recent resear ch illustrates, games not only further the developm ent of domain-specific knowledge (see, for instance, Corredor et al., 2014) but also provide the designed con straints – analog or digital – rendered necessary to learn specific practices (see, Edwards et al., 2019; Gerup et al., 2020). Moving beyond the content/context divide (Squire, 2006), this paper brings together methodological insights from ethnomethodology and conversation analysis with theoretical concepts and tools from game studies and embodied cognition to reimagine what learning looks like in the peripheries of play. More specifically, it examines how the designed landscapes and environments of an immersive escape room  served as a mediational resource that and advanced consequential learning for players to “claim” or “exhibit” understanding (Sacks, 1992). Showing knowing in escape rooms Requiring teamwork, deliberation, critical thinking, and strong communication skills, escape rooms are live-action games designed to create opportunities for players to engage in lateral thinking through gamification.  By game here, I mean a “rule-based system with a variable and quantifiable outcome, where different outcomes are assigned different values, the player exerts effort in order  to influence the outcome, the player feels emotion ally attached to the outcome, and the consequences of th e activity are negotiable ” (Juul, 2006, p. 36).  In escape room play, understanding becomes topicalised. Known for their causal logic loop (challenge-solution-reward), players may anticipate a problem and formulate possible pathway s to solutions th rough questioning  understanding or learn from failure. Drawing on parallel literature that examines how understanding is claim ed in the professions (Goodwin, 1994; Hindmarsh et al., 2009)  and showcases the how the distribution of the mind moves beyond the individual to encompass the situated practices of communities (Hutchins, 1995; Suchman, 2000), in this paper I trace how players on a team take up the role of “demonstrator” to lead and assess understanding.

## Methods

This study draws on data from a larger IRB-approved qualitative project examining how five participants “learned to escape” across a series of multi-linear escape rooms. For the purposes of this study, I focus my analysis on the second of three escape rooms participants experienced, The Artist’s Conundum.

## Participants, data generation, and data analysis

The team under study consisted of five adult participants. Ranging in age from 20-22, three identified as men and two identified as women. To generate data of participant’s experiences of es cape, we video recorded their entire experience in the escape rooms using Go-Pros affixed to each of their heads as well as the surveillance cameras installed throughout the escape room. This combinat ion of video recording techniques allowed us to cap ture a large corpus of data about the participants: speech and no ise making, vocal qualities, movement through space and in relation to one another, gesture, the direct ion of their gaze and  focus of their attention-their framing as camera operators, bodily orientation, and interacti ons/intra-actions with material, the space and other participants. In addition to recording live play, I audio recorded individual interviews with all five participants prior to their experiences in the escape rooms and group interviews immediately following each experience using semi-structured interview protocols. All interviews were between thirty minutes and an hour in duration. Data analysis for this paper was an iterative process of both examining participants embodied interactions during the escape process as well as interrogating speech on its own accord. To understand the relationships among mode, bodies, environments, and materials, I employed an adaptation of Erickson’s (2006) whole-to-part analytic procedure for selecting and analyzing data from the  videotape to make meaning of the participants’ entire experience in the escape room and select an episode  upon which to focus micro-ethnographic analyses. I first watched the video all the way through, using field notes to mark time and note verbal and nonverbal phenomena.

Using insights from previous work examining how demonstra tors claim and exhibit knowing  I focused on significant “demonstrator” episodes or what Jordan & Henderson (1995) refer to as shapes of events, that matched my inquiry and research question. These episodes we re then transcribed following standard conventions utilized in conversation analysis (Jefferson, 2004).

## Findings

Reading across a series of focal episodes / puzzles, findings detail how the delicate shifts in player  orientation, tool use, and proxemics differentiate claiming and showing understanding through escape play. Through each, I trace how the role of demonstrator shifts and is at  times multiple. The body is a critical site in sho wing knowing and claiming understanding. In the first episode I focus on how two players – Walter and Maddie – sought to take up the role of demonstrator and in so doing (re)ori ented their body through a puzzle asking players to colle ct a series of lettered cubes. Rather than simply sitting at the level of a verbal  exchange, Walter demanded that other players continue using the evidence of the puzzle (e.g., lettered cubes) to continue play. His evaluation of Maddie, coupled with his observation of the resources transformed him into a demonstrator of play rather than a passi ve player.

In the second episode, I analyze and discuss how mi sunderstanding may be embodied and recognized by th e demonstrator during play. I highlight how Casey, a somewhat peripheral player in The Artist’ s Conundrum takes up the role of demonstrator to watch her teammate Will misunderstand a directive in a puzzle requiring knowledge of primary and secondary colors. Shifting her gaze between his touch of a button and the light on a painting across the way, I show how – as the lead demonstrator in this puzzle – she used failure as a tool to cultivate learning and problem solving. I then disambiguate between Will’s  incorrect work to showcase how players may continue to play incorrectly so as to outwardly refute the demonstrator’s intervention in an attempt to take up the role.
