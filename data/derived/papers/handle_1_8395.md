# What Time and What Place? Technologies’ Diverging Role in Online Schooling Across Socioeconomical Contexts During Covid-19

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The COVID-19 pandemic destabilized the prevailing grammar of schooling and forced schools to rely on digital technologies to create new routines and practices. This offers a unique opportunity to examine the interplay between digital technologies and the temporospatial features of schooling, and how these relations were mediated via contextual features. Based on semi-structured interviews with principals and teachers in MuslimPalestinian and secular-Jewish schools in Jerusalem, this paper qualitatively analyzes the main technological tools used by educators: instant messaging (WhatsApp), video-conferencing (Zoom) and school-wide platforms (Google Classroom). Our findings stress how technological affordances varied according to socioeconomic status and diverging perceptions of schooling. Specifically, we found that lower-SES Palestinian schools, who faced more acute challenges, utilized digital technologies to restabilize the grammar of schooling in order to monitor and support students’ basic needs, whereas average-SES Jewish schools focused on facilitating informal interactions that catered to students’ emotional wellbeing.

## Introduction

Digital technologies are often presented as nurturing “anytime-anyplace” learning – open, flexible, individualized and student-led learning – that would allow overcoming the staleness of conventional schooling (Selwyn, 2013). In practice, research shows that novel technologies have not had a substantial impact on the overall s tructure of schooling and have been mostly integrated within the prevailing “grammar of schooling”: “the regular structures and rules that organize the work of instruction… standardized organizational practices in dividing time and space, classifying students and allocating them to classro oms, and splintering knowledge into ‘subjects.’” (T yack & Tobin, 1994; p. 454). However, during the COVID-19 pandemic, technology-mediated remote schooling (TMRS) had become compulsory and widespread almost overnig ht. Educators had to quickly negotiate how to rely on online learning tools to create a fully-fledged alt ernative to physical schooling. Therefore, digital technologies were now used to mediate the basic temporospatial facets of the grammar of schooling, thus potentially reshaping it. While it is still early to appreciate the long-term implications of the transition to TMRS, it is clear that it temporarily destabilized the prevailing grammar of schooling, requiring schools to prioritize and choose among competing goals and structures (Reich et al., 2020). The need to pursue such drastic changes therefore  offers a rare opportunity to explore how digital technologies inform reconstructions of the “grammar of schooling”. Inquiries into the use of technologies in education often rely on the implicit assumption that technological devices have fixed affordances and will reproduce t he same modes of conduct within and across educatio nal settings, while ignoring the social and political features of technology-use (Davis, 2020). However, technological affordances are not inherent to tools nor are they static, but rather depend on users' needs, aims and  associated meanings (Dishon, 2021). This is particularly pronounced during TMRS as it relies on having the necessary access to computers and the internet, a home environment c onducive for learning, and caretakers who can suppo rt students (Williamson et al., 2020). Accordingly, th is qualitative study examines the experiences, choi ces and perceptions of principals and teachers in socioecon omically diverse Muslim-Palestinian and secular-Jew ish primary schools. We set out to examine the main tec hnologies educators relied on during TMRS, how thes e different technologies shaped the temporospatial fe atures of TMRS, and how they were mediated through socioeconomic and cultural characteristics. Our aim is not to offer a representative description of schools’ use of digital technologies, but rather to offer theoretic al contributions concerning how educational technol ogies reorganize key aspects of schooling, and how this function is mediated via contextual features.

## Methods

The study was conducted in three Muslim-Palestinian and three Jewish-Secular primary schools in the Jerusalem school district. Studying Muslim-Palestinian and Secular-Jewish schools allows examining how the transition to TMRS was mediated by socioeconomic status and cultural and political characteristics, due to glaring disparities ICLS2022 Proceedings 1013 © ISLS between the two populations in terms of access to physical infrastructure and education (Shtern & Rokem, 2021). All three Palestinian schools had students from very low SES status, whereas Jewish schools were characterized by an average SES population, with pockets of very lower SES students (e.g., refugees.). Schools were chosen based on a purposive sampling, which strived to off er socioeconomic and cultural variation within each  of the populations.

Collecting and analyzing data We conducted semi-structured in-depth interviews with school principals, head of ICT departments (if the school had one), and 2-3 teachers. The interviews were conducted October-December 2020 via the Zoom platform and were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Interviews focused on the transition to TMRS: the challenges a nd dilemmas schools faced, their responses, and participants’ explanations for their choices. Thematic analysis was used to identify the main themes emerging from our data (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Each interview was read and analyzed by at least three team members, and discussed by the whole team, which allowed multiple perspectives to emerge (Cornish et al., 2014). While our analyses explored various aspects of schooling, in this paper we focus solely on those themes that had to with the temporospatial features of TMRS.

## Findings

We identified three digital technologies that were central to TMRS: instant messaging (WhatsApp); vide o conferencing (predominantly Zoom); and schooling-platforms (Google Classroom). These technologies were not only the most prevalent, but they emerged as a comm on aspect of the organization of TMRS: teachers and principals attributed great significance to these technologies, explicating pros and cons for each tool, and further discussing their ramifications on the practices of schooling. While educators also mentioned the use of educational apps tailored for specific content knowledge and ot her generic tools used in classes (e.g., Padlet), t hese were mentioned far less frequently, and were mostly brought up in the more limited context of teaching and learning. Thus, beyond the more detailed uses of different technologies, it is vital to note the dominance of commercial tools. As such, WhatsApp gained a primary role early in TMRS, serving as a means of communicating decisions regarding school operations during lockdown, and sharing educational material and tasks. Two key  temporospatial themes emerged from participants’ interviews: WhatsApp’s porous nature, and its role in the establishment of all-day schooling. The main temporospatial characteristic of WhatsApp was its porous nature. Due to its use to facilitate various formal and informal communicational contexts, it blurred and undermined demarcations within sc hools and between schooling and individuals’ personal lives. Beyond personal use, WhatsApp was used to disseminate information to teachers, for teacher communication with parents, and as a tool for sharing and receivi ng educational materials, often for different classroo ms. This blurring was not only temporal in nature. In more affluent populations, new personal connections were  made between teachers and older students who had t heir own phones. While the class WhatsApp group was used  mainly for sharing information, private chats were conducted in a new, amicable manner. This had rendered WhatsApp a liminal technological space, in which the division between personal and professional life was blurred. This also has to do with the blurring of the temporal structure of schooling. As teachers and students were often chatting during what is normally considered off-hours, the tone and content also geared towards informality.

The second theme centers on the blurring of the temporal distinction between school and home, described by interviewees as all day schooling. Each student could access materials on WhatsApp and react at various times throughout the day, which altered the temporal rhythm of schooling: instead of the uniform routine, students and families interacted with teachers at different pace s and at different times. Moreover, as teachers oft en had to ICLS2022 Proceedings 1014 © ISLS function as “tech-support’ for children and familie s, they felt they had to be “on-call” way past what  was previously expected. Consequently, teachers reported very long work hours during TMRS, many of those s pent on WhatsApp. As WhatsApp blurred the temporal boundaries on communication, teachers noted that its centrality led to a norm of all-day schooling, which was described as leading to a sense of burnout. Zoom and video-conferencing apps Shortly after school closings, video-conferencing applications (predominantly Zoom) were being introduced as core aspects of TMRS. While some schools integrated  Zoom very quickly into their daily routine, others encountered difficulties stemming from scarcity of end-devices, unstable network connections, and poor staff and parents’ technological literacy. In lower SES and Palestinian schools, families had to allocate their technological devices between children at specific times. In some cases, this led schools to organize different Zoom times for different age groups as to prevent overlaps within families. Two key temporospatial themes emerged with respect to the centrality of Zoom: familiar class routines, and breaking spatial barriers. The strongest impact Zoom had on remote schooling w as that it reintroduced familiar class routines: fixed timetables in which Zoom sessions were alloca ted according to disciplines. These familiar routin es were also apparent in terms of the dynamics of each less on: Zoom was perceived as a teacher-centered virtual space. Building on such capacities, it was often depicted as online frontal teaching, with slideshows serving the teacher as whiteboard. According to many teachers, the Zoom  class schedule reinstated structure in students’ l ives and calmed their worried parents. At the same time, Zoom had brought back some familiar challenges of appropriate classroom conduct. In several schools, rules of participation and conduct were restated – cameras had to stay on, no eating was allowed during class, school uniform became mandatory and so on. Though Zoom reintroduced familiar temporal routines, it concurrently broke spatial barriers, changing interaction patterns between teachers and students. First, due to the virtual presence in each other’s home, Zoom was perceived as allowing a better understanding of students’ experiences and emotional states, compared to asynchronous tools. In higher-SES schools, Zoom was explicitly used for creating emotional affinity, ranging from shared breakfasts to more elaborate cases of joint cooking classes. Zoom created a new situation of bl urred boundaries between private familial life, and profe ssional educational life. In lower-SES schools cate ring for families living in dire situations, teachers report ed that Zoom enabled supervision of familial life a nd the wellbeing of students, thus serving schools’ pastor al role in the community. Second, the breaching of personal space was at times a challenge. In more religious Palestinian schools, participants noted issues of privacy due to the exposure of religious Muslim women in their hom e. In higher-SES Jewish schools, Zoom opened the classroom to the scrutinizing gaze of parents. Parents had interrupted teachers during lessons, asking questions, requesting attention to their children, and even correcting the teacher’s grammar. Schooling Platforms School-wide platforms (e.g., Google Classroom) offer a panel of tools incorporated into tailored profiles both at class and student level. These platforms were described as requiring the greatest effort: creating dedicated email accounts, opening in-platform profiles for all students and staff, and teaching teachers and students how to operate them. Consequently, some schools did not attempt to integrate such platforms: one Palestinian school that did not have the required infrastructure, and two higher-SES Jewish schools, where principals explained that this would require a level of effort (from teachers and families) they thought was unrealistic during the pandemic. School-wide platforms offered a hybrid of both task-centered and teacher-centered learning, organizing links to tasks, materials, and live video lessons. In this respect, they provide a digital representat ion of the comprehensive school structure: students can navigate in and out of lessons, teac hers can coordinate Zoom teaching hours between classes, and management can consider which grades get which sessions at what ti me. Thus, the platform had served to create a coherent organizational structure. The personalized aspect o f such platforms – connecting each student to a personal profile – allowed principals, teachers, and parents to keep track of students’ progress. Hence, in terms of the temporospatial organization of schooling, platforms add another layer in that they enhance the summative and quantifiable tracking of students’ and teachers’ actions within the school, in ways more elaborate than were used before.

## Discussion

The exodus to TMRS in light of the COVID-19 pandemic serves as a unique opportunity to examine the potential interplay between digital technologies and the gram mar of schooling. Whereas above we focused on share d themes that emerged across schools, we now focus on  how technology-use was mediated through contextual factors. Specifically, we examine how the temporosp atial organization of schooling facilitated by the various technologies along two main issues: inequalities in access, and diverging perceptions of schooling. First, schools serving minoritized p opulations were limited to relying on digital technologies “at  very specific locations, in very specific times”, i.e., when students have electricity, available end-devices and a stable network connection. Thus, in Palestinian  schools, who faced both challenges in terms of access and or ganizational capacities (e.g., none had a designate d ICT department), educators asserted that TMRS required closely monitoring and supporting students and fami lies. This led lower-SES and Palestinian schools to adopt a more structured approach to schooling. This could explain the somewhat surprising finding that Palestinian sc hools were much faster to integrate school-wide pla tforms, although they were facing much more dire circumstan ces to begin with. Principals explained that withou t a structured organization of TMRS it would be too cha llenging for them to keep track of students’ situat ion and offer the needed support. Second, across all school s, anyplace-anytime learning (mostly via WhatsApp) was perceived not as an opportunity, but rather as a burden. Teachers argued that all-day availability led to a sense of burnout, and highlighted the social nature of schooling, which required gathering teachers and students in a shared synchronous space. Accordingly, better-resourced schools opted for structured synchronous lessons via Zoom. Besides issues of access, diverging definitions of the aims of remote schooling also shaped the functions of technological tools. Notably, the relatively hig her-SES Jewish schools focused less on academics and social supervision, and more on how digital technologies allow establishing informal modes of interaction that cater to students’ social-emotional needs (personal interactions, “extra-curricular” activities), which were deemed more urgent in light of the turbulences of the pandemic. These differences were reflected in diverging use of WhatsApp, but were most apparent in the perception of Zoom’s role. Palestinian interviewees, that stressed the a cademic responsibilities of school to its struggling popula tion, portrayed Zoom as the digital extrapolation o f frontal teaching, intended to maintain academic progress. I n contrast, Jewish interviewees highlighted how Zoo m was used to facilitate emotional bonds. These differenc es might serve an explanation for why lower SES sch ools managed to introduce school-wide platforms in spite of the effort it required, while higher SES schools did not – such platforms were less relevant to their focus on the informal and intimate modes of teacher-student interaction.

## Conclusion

In summary, our analysis revealed that schools main ly relied on free commercial technologies as the pi llars of TMRS (Williamson et al., 2020): WhatsApp (instant m essaging), Zoom (video conferencing), and Google Classroom (school-wide platforms). These played a d ifferent role in reshaping the temporospatial gramm ar of schooling: WhatsApp facilitated a flexible and porous school structure, where schooling was enmeshed with other aspects of teachers’ lives. Zoom, in contrast, was mainly integrated into the familiar grammar of scho oling (Selwyn, 2013). Yet, its blurring of the spatial separation of home and school (a separation often described as a key drawback of conventional schooling), was interp reted very differently according to context. Palest inian interviewees described this as an impediment to their responsibility towards families in dire need, while Jewish interviewees also depicted this as an opportunity to focus more on social-emotional aspects. Finally, schoolwide platforms, the only tools specifically tailored to education, created a virtual version of the grammar of schooling, which facilitated increased centralized control of learning and the collection and analysis of student data.
