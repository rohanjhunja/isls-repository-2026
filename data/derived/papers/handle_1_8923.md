# “The Game Was Designed to Learn to Think” – Player Perceptions of Learning in an Educational Game

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learning within videogames is often examined using pre-test and post-test, and/or by evaluating players’ responses within the design of the game. This study examines young players’ perceptions of learning within an educational videogame by analyzing their reactions and responses captured during gameplay and in an interview after the gameplay session. Our findings indicate that different players perceived different elements to be learning outcomes of playing the game. We argue that educational game designers should consider having different elements such as storyline, content-based puzzles, strategy, social skills, and problem-solving to engage players with diverse interests and prior experiences within the educational game design.

## Introduction & Theoretical Grounding

The videogame industry is one of the fastest growing entertainment sectors. Games are also increasingly recognized as effective environments for learning (Clark et al., 2016; Dindar, 2018). A significant body of research has found the positive effects of playing videogames focusing on four main domains: cognitive, motivational, emotional, and social (Granic et al., 2014).  Game based learning (GBL) literature includes accounts of several studies designed to test games’ affordances for learning and skills development. For example, Shute et al. (2015), showed playing a puzzle game could improve players’ problem solving, spatial skills, and persistence. Barr (2018) studied how undergraduate students perceived commercial game for developing skills and found a broadly positive perception of the games' efficacy for development of skills such as communication skill, resourcefulness, and adaptability. The literature shows that videogames can be a powerful tool for learning; however, there are two related gaps: 1) there is a lack of focus on micro-level interactions that players have with GBL; and 2) there is no work that ties designed elements to the perceived learning experiences of games. Thus, there is a need for empirical work about what players perceive as learning within a game designed for learning which we address in this study by examining the following research question: What game mechanics are recognized as learning elements by players? Our study contributes by expanding prior work on game-based learning in two ways: 1) understanding players’ perceptions of learning within a game, i.e., to identify what players view as learning and 2) providing implications for designing an educational videogame to increase the engagement of players coming from a diverse set of experiences.

## Method

Data was collected from 17 participants between the ages of 11 to 14 (7 females, 10 males), recruited through a list-serve associated with the University of Maryland as well as advertisements sent via the local library. 3 participants identified as African American or Black, 4 as Asian or Pacific Islander, 6 as White or Caucasian, and 4 participants didn’t report their ICLS2022 Proceedings 1081 © ISLS racial identities in the demographic survey.

The data was collected using a Zoom interface where each participant shared their camera and microphone as well as their screen during the gameplay session. Each gameplay session was recorded. Each of the data collection sessions were approximately an hour long including ~40 minutes of gameplay and ~20 minutes of follow-up interview. During the follow-up interview, participants were asked about their impressions about the game and its possible learning outcomes of playing the game. The open-ended questions allowed the participants to discuss what they perceive as learning in videogames, specifically our game, HEX of the Turtle Islands  (HEX). All procedures were approved by University of Maryland Institutional Review Board.

A constructivist grounded theory approach guided the data analysis process (Charmaz, 2014). Our analysis took place over an iterative process using open, focused, axial coding, and memoing throughout the data analysis process (Strauss & Corbin, 1990).

## FINDINGS

Our analysis revealed five main learning themes that emerged from responses about what players thought the game was designed to teach – ecological storyline (one thread of narrative in addition to a larger cybersecurity related plot), content specific puzzle, strategy, social skills, and problem solving. These perceived learning outcomes are not mutually exclusive as some players reported more than one learning outcome of playing HEX. Ecological storyline: This theme refers to players identifying the narrative elements of HEX. For example, Adam recognized the ecological storyline as such, “In the beginning, there was a little bit about [sea turtles–]... the depth of the water with time the sea creatures came out… ” Here Adam is responding to hints that are placed in the game world about the impact that HEX is having on local sea turtle populations. Three players reported that they identify the learning outcome of playing HEX is to learn about the ecology on the islands. Content Specific Puzzles: The content specific puzzle theme emerged from six players’ responses indicating specific content (e.g., code, logic, etc.) related to the domain of the game to be the learning element. For instance, Daniel shared, “I learned about the logic gates and things from the puzzles (Refer to Fig 1.5).” Another player, Maya, mentioned in her interview, “I definitely think the [cipher] code (Refer to Fig 1.3).” Daniel’s response indicates that he recognizes the logical content involved in the wire puzzles whereas Maya’s response emphasized the cryptography content involved in the cipher puzzle. These responses indicate that players pay attention to the content of the puzzles especially if they can identify the objective of having those puzzles in the game. Problem solving: Players’ responses indicated that problem solving was the most prevalent learning outcome of playing HEX, nine players’ responses indicated reference to the different elements of problem solving. For example, Matthew shared that, “ I think it was designed to help you to solve different problems.” Another player, Susan, responded that the learning outcome of playing the game is “how to solve problems – Critically.” The problem solving was a larger theme with implicit references to high level concepts involved in problem solving such as ‘critical thinking’, ‘logical thinking’, ‘decision making’, and ‘remembering’ things to apply those later in the game.

Strategy: The responses where players refer to applying strategy to make efficient use of available resources was labelled as strategy. This category was distinguished from problem solving because responses in problem solving referred to a collection of high order thinking skills whereas strategy was referenced to a specific form of ability. For example, Ben responded that “Probably learning to use your resources and figure out what you could do with your situation with what you had.” His response indicates that while playing, players are required to use strategy to figure out how to make efficient use of the available resources. This theme points to a broader implication of what players do in the game environment can apply in their reallife scenarios. 4.5 Social Skills: The responses where three players refer to social skills such as being friendly, interacting with people, and seeking help from others were labelled as social skills. For example, Ethan perceived the learning outcome of playing HEX is “to be friends with people or ask for help.” ICLS2022 Proceedings 1083 © ISLS This response indicates being social in the game can lead to being social in the real world.

## Discussion & Conclusion

We found that players perceive learning within HEX in reference to different mechanisms involved in the HEX design: ecological storyline, content specific puzzles, strategy, social skills, and problem solving. These findings signify that different players attend to different mechanisms while playing a game. Even though there are similarities in some players' perceptions of learning outcomes associated with playing the game, differences in their responses indicates the ‘emergent experience’ of the gameplay (Squire, 2006). Our findings suggest that as designers of educational games we cannot assume that every player will have a similar learning outcome while playing a particular game. The findings of this study are limited in certain ways. The potential learning outcomes emerged from this study are not an objective measure of all possible learning that happened to the players during gameplay. These learning outcomes are situated in the designed elements of HEX. Different videogame genres provide different kinds of affordances to players in the game. Future research needs be conducted to study how players perceive learning happening in other videogames to address these limitations. Our study is an initial step to understand learning in game-based learning from young players’ perspectives, but we need more empirical studies to see effects of gameplay experiences on a player’s perception of learning outcomes. To conclude, our study findings indicate that different players identify different game mechanics to be associated with learning within the game. Thus, we argue that educational games should have multiple elements such as storyline, content-based puzzles, strategy, social skills, and problem-solving to engage diverse learners in the gameplay.
