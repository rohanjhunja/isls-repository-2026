# The Complexity of Diverse Conceptualizations of Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion in Higher Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines diverse conceptualizations of Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (DEI) in higher education using complex conceptual systems theory. Interviews and analyses reveal four distinct DEI conceptualizations, each centering different aspects and associated practices. Understanding DEI as a complex conceptual system illuminates new directions for inclusive learning environments. The methodology provides a framework potentially applicable to other multifaceted issues in learning sciences.

## Introduction and background

Diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) hold a pivotal and increasing role in the field of learning sciences, by guiding our understanding of power and privilege in the des ign of learning environments and experiences (Esmon de & Booker, 2017). Additionally, there is recent focus on the differing conceptions and definitions of DEI, and how they relate to higher education, as evidenced by several bills regarding DEI. Issues regarding DEI have real world effects. For instance, in medicine, biases affect d iagnoses (Sabin, 2022) and inequitable access to health care  is concerning (LaVeist et al., 2023). This research st udy aims to develop theoretical and practical knowl edge regarding the many different conceptualizations of DEI and its relationship with educational practices. This study uses complex conceptual systems theory which descri bes conceptualizations as complex systems consistin g of many interdependent ideas, and different sets of pr actices as emergent from different conceptualizatio ns (Donaldson & Allen-Handy, 2023). This paper presents preliminary findings  from analysis of the initial 15 interviews out of a projected corpus of 30 interviews once the full study is complete. We are seeking to illuminate the implications of diverse conceptualizations of D EI – and emergent practices – for the learning sciences, particularly in designing more equitable and inclusive learning environments.

## Methods

This IRB-approved study with an NIH Certificate of Confident iality involved semi-structured interviews with faculty members and academic researchers to explore  their beliefs and practices concerning DEI. Transc riptions were coded in MAXQDA Analytics Pro fo r metaphors, descriptive characterizations, values,  DEI elements, and practices endorsed or condemned. For the preliminar y analysis of the first 15 interviews, UCINET was u sed for network analysis to explore correlations among code s (except practices) with  a significance level of P <.01. Girvan-Newman cluster analysis (Girvan & Newman, 2002) ide ntified clusters, and node sizes were adjusted based on betweenness centrality measures. Clusters were interpreted as conceptualizations of DEI. Node s within each cluster were consolidated, and network analysi s was conducted for correlations between practices and conceptualizations utilizing betweenness measures o f centrality and cluster analysis to identify emerg ent practices.

## Findings

The Girvan-Newman analysis produced a network map of conceptua lizations of DEI that correlated at P <.01 with four clusters at Q =.495 (see Figure 1). The Acceptance-Fairness conceptualization is a multifaceted approach that views DEI through the lens of socio-cultural, economic, and mental health elements. It centers on fairness and inclusivity, emphasizing individual needs and promoting equitable access to resources like education and healthcare. The conceptualization essentially p osits DEI as a complex system, interdependent on di verse contributing elements. Multiple nodes related to fa irness play a  big role in this cluster. There seems to be high centrality values in the nodes labeled: ‘education’, ‘pay, income, wealth discrepancies’, and ‘space for everyone’. This suggests that to be able to create a space for  everyone in educational settings, it's  important that socioeconomic status shouldn’t be a determining factor in the quality of education and acceptability of someone. In the Validation-Differences conceptualization, DEI is anchored in t he validation of different perspectives, strengths, and identities. This frame work acknowledges systemic factors but leans heavil y on the interpersonal, particularly the validation of ind ividual lived experiences and expressions of identi ty. Notably, certain elements within this cluster exhibit high b etweenness centrality values including, ‘welcoming’, ‘biases and microaggressions’, ‘implicit unconscious bias (acknowledge)’, and ‘opportunity’. These nodes sugge st that recognizing implicit unconscious biases while foste ring a welcoming environment may reduce biases and microaggressions, ultimately providing  equitable opportunities within educational settings. The Structural-Access conceptualization approaches DEI as a structural issue, concentrating on systemic barriers that affect various social groups. It dema nds equitable access to resources and equal treatme nt, thereby embracing a more systemic, institutional view of DE I. Cluster elements such as ‘disability’, ‘race’, ‘ military status’, and ‘design for disadvantaged,’ all of whi ch have high betweenness values. This suggests that  certain disadvantaged groups face inequitable access due to structural barriers. However, by adopting the ‘design for the disadvantaged’ approach, we can make these groups experience a sense of belonging. The Representation-Underserved conceptualization focuses on representa tion and advocacy for underrepresented and underserved groups in decision-making and resource allocation. Despite its limited connections and smaller size, the  cluster highlights the importance of understanding and designing for disadvantaged and underrepresented groups within th e broader DEI context. The cluster's connections wi th the Acceptance-Fairness and Structural-Access clusters emphasize fostering meaningful interaction s among diverse individuals and designing inclusive and equitable l earning environments to effectively represent under served populations, ultimately promoting greater diversity and inclusion in educational settings. Practices emergent from different conceptualizations Analysis of the relationships between practices end orsed, practices condemned, and conceptualizations of DEI correlated at P <.01 resulted in a network map with five Girvan-Newman clusters at Q =.595. The network map analysis resulted in five clusters, with three alig ned with DEI conceptualizations and two not aligned. The Structural-Access & Representation-Underserved conceptualizations cluster condemns com partmentalizing DEI and lacking accountability, advocating for an integ rative approach with structural changes. The Valida tionDifferences cluster focuses on nurturing trust and valuing diverse perspectives, condemning legacy adm issions and stereotyping. The Acceptance-Fairness cluster underscores intentional inclusivit y in grading and classroom operations, condemning reductionist practices like mandatory training or a colorblind approach.

## Implications and conclusion

The study's findings have implications for designin g inclusive learning environments. Inclusivity goes  beyond traditional race and ethnicity conceptions and incl udes critical and learner-centered constructivist designs. Designing for the disadvantaged is c hallenging as it aims to fit diverse needs. The stu dy reveals that creating inclusive spaces requires addressing systemic varia bles that hinder equitable access to resources and fostering environments where identities and perspectives are validated. The nuanced understandings of DEI invite  a textured analysis of the complexities in diverse learning communities. Future studies could explore the interaction between multiple DEI conceptualizations within lear ning ecosystems. The complex conceptual systems the ory framework can serve as an analytic tool for examini ng multifaceted issues in learning sciences, potent ially uncovering previously obscured connections. The findings also raise questions about how DEI conceptualizations might relate to or inform theoretical perspectives on learning.
