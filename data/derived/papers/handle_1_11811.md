# Finding the Side Doors: Learners’ Uptake and Rejection of Frames in a Museum Exhibit about Climate Change

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Climate change presents major problems to communities globally. While education is seen as a path towards public engagement, educators often encounter challenges, particularly in the United States (US), where climate change remains contentious. Prior research explains this difficulty, showing that climate learning processes are complex, multifaceted, and often influenced by one’s sociopolitical ideologies. Building from such research, this paper positions side doors frames as a means to fostering effective climate education for ideologically diverse audiences. We integrate framing theory with Bakhtinian theory of discourse to conceptualize side door moments and apply this conceptualization to qualitative discourse analysis of empirical data collected at a US natural history museum. Our analysis demonstrates moment-to-moment processes by which learners take up and/or reject framings of climate change. It contributes to learning sciences through a deepened conceptualization of side doors that integrates learning theory towards approaches for effective climate education.

## Introduction

The changing climate poses serious threats to communities globally. In response, educators and researchers have implemented a variety of experiences to support learning around the issue. However, many encounter challenges, particularly in communities where climate change remains a controversial topic, such as those in socio-politically and ideologically diverse regions of the United Sta tes (US). These challenges stem from  the complex, multifaceted, and sometimes counter-intuitive learning processes that prior research ha s shown unfold around climate change (e.g., Zummo et al., 2021). Taking such research into account, this paper conceptualizes side doors as a means to foster  productive climate learning  for socio-politically and ideologically diverse audiences. We draw on t heories of framing (Entman, 1993) and discourse (Bakhtin, 1982) to deepen existing concepts of side doors, and then we integrate theory with empirical data collected at a natural history museum in the  US to show how and when side doors can foster productive engag ement around the contentious issue of climate chang e. In parallel, we contribute theoretical depth to the notion of side doors and demonstrate the potential for this concept to guide climate education for diverse audiences towards a more sustainable world.

## Conceptual framework

Framing A growing body of research from communication (e.g., Moser, 2016) suggests that framing could play a critical role in climate learning. Often used to explain influence of media, framing describes how an issue is portrayed and understood (Supran & Oreskes, 2021). Frames construct meaning by making salient certain a spects of an issue through language and image choice, thereby guiding learners towards particular interpretations  (Entman, 1993; Goffman, 1972). Frames used in text are typically embedded in the broader culture; they reference implicit cultural phenomena; the phenomenon functions as a central organizing idea (Van Gorp, 2007). While extensive work on framing has been done in climate communicat ion (e.g., Moser), burgeoning learning sciences research in this area shows that framing can influence learners’ interpretation of text (Busch, 2021) and use of sensemaking resources (Zummo et al., 2024), although many questions around framing and learning remain. Side doors Scholars in fields beyond the learning sciences hav e suggested that certain frames are effective in cl imate communication because they serve as side doors (e.g., Israel, 2020; Markowitz & Sweetland, 2018), or path s of eliding unproductive patterns common in US discours e (e.g., Eubanks, 2015). For example, reframing cli mate change in ways that do not signal political affilia tion (e.g., “real” versus “hoax”; Jang & Hart, 2015) can offer pathways tonew, productive types of engagement for politically diverse audiences that avoid all too common vitriolic discourse (Dixon et al., 2017). However, there has been limited theory-building work around the concept of side doors, as has there been limited empirical research to better define the concept. For it to be useful to the learning sciences, deeper conceptualizations and stronger ties to learning theory are needed. While research in communication suggests that desir able outcomes (e.g., greater concern, knowledge gain, etc.) can happen in response to  side door frames (Geiger et al., 2017), there is little known about learning processes in response to frames. What mediates or facilitates desirable outcomes is less well understood; we lack explanatory frameworks for why and how side doors can promote learning, therefore limiting their transferability to other situations. Furthermore, there is little known about what side doors look like sit uationally, as most research on framing has been done via surveys or lab-based based studies of individuals (e.g., Nisbet, 2019). Heteroglossia and moment-to-moment uptake/rejection of frames in museums We draw on Bakhtinian (1982) notions of discourse to examine learning, specifically heteroglossia—the capacity of text to contain a multitude of voices—to understand learners’ uptake and rejection of potential side door frames. Bakhtin theorized that text and discourse  is constituted of many voices, representing a multi tude of ways of speaking, writing, and using language that come from previous speakers and writers. Further, the mixing of these voices is ever present —each text represents a mingling of past voices. While novels were central to this theory, the types of text with which people engage have evolved enormously over the past 100 years since Bakhtin lived. Text today includes a myriad of new forms, from social media commenting to television scripts to text messages. We view museum exhibits as a modern genre, with museum visitors as learners reading and interpreting text of the exhibit. Here, we use “text” to refer not only to the text panel s typical of museums but also to the images, multimedia, and physical interactives of contemporary science museums. With a Bakhtinian lens, exhibit text contains a multitude of voices. In the exhibit under study, A Climate of Hope  (ACoH), many of the voices contained within its text reflect and speak to  side door frames, or intentional uses of language  and image to communicate ideas about climate change  in ways that elide unproductive discourse patterns common in the US. Developed on the premise that frames historically used in climate communication are unproductive for engaging wide audiences, ACoH embraces new, strategic frames (see Zummo et al., 2024), some of which have shown promise in other informal learning settings (e.g., Geiger et al., 2017). For example, fear  historically has been a dominant frame (e.g., Zummo et al., 2020), yet its potential to engage is quest ionable (Reser & Bradley, 2017). Similarly, common frames emphasize distance, portraying effects on people, places, and species far off from learners in space and time (McDonald et al., 2015). However, evidence demonstrates greater potential for a local frame (e.g., Jones et al., 2017).  The contrast between the frames of ACoH and those commonly used makes it an important study site, as we aim to understand how l earners take up or reject these less common —yet potentially productive for learning—frames. Specifically, predominant voices within ACoH emphasize the side door frames: hope (rather than fear), local (rather thandistance), community (rather thandebate), and gain (rather than loss). A theoretical lens of heteroglossia  allows us to discern the many voices that learn ers draw on while engaging with the exhibit, as a way to understand how side door f rames are taken up and/or rejected by learners. While Bakhtin theorized about written text, we apply his  ideas to wards understanding learners’ interactional discourse in response to the text of the exhibit. Doing so allows us to understand  which of the many voices of exhibit text learners take up and integrate into their own discourse and which they reject or counter with dominant, unproductive frames of climate communication within their moment-to-moment learning. Study purpose The purpose of this study is to deepen conceptualiz ations of learning via side door frames. While we f ocus on learning about climate change, we do so because it is an issue emblematic of the modern era in which a rapid pace of change has created major problems around which scientific knowledge is  in flux and controversy flourishes (Zummo & Massey, 2022). By advancing knowledge of climate learning, we aim to inform future resear ch on other similarly controversial issues. We ask: In what ways is uptake and/or rejection of side door frames evidenced in discourse? In what ways is uptake and/or rejection related to learners’ views on climate change?

## Methods

This study is a qualitative analysis of video data captured within A Climate of Hope (ACoH), an innovative exhibit about climate change at a natural history museum in Utah, USA (Table 1). We use discourse analysis to identify moments of uptake and/or rejection of side door frames by examining evidence for heteroglossia.

## Context

ACoH was developed through a multi-year, iterative process that involved deep engageme nt with research literature about climate communication and framing (see Zummo et al., 2024). It was installed in late 2023 in the Natural History Museum of Utah (NHMU), a medium-sized US natural history museum, in a region that is sociopolitically and ideologically diverse—that is, a region in which residents hold diverse views on politics, the role of government, and society. Reflecting this diversity, local residents hold a range of views about climate change. The exhibit consists of four main sections: Aspen Grove, Impacts, Solutions, and Connections. In each section, the frames of hope (rather than fear), local (rather thandistance), community (rather thandebate), and gain (rather than loss) are accomplished through intentional word choice, images, and interactivity (see Zummo et al., 2024). ## Participants

Participants were selected from the general population of museum visitors. We recruited participants at the front entrance by offering free admission. We selected for geographic diversity, aiming for a relatively even distribution of home postal codes, both in and beyond the local region.  We recruited only groups (no solo participants) and only participants who had not yet visited ACoH. We collected data from 62 groups, with a total of 162 participants. For this study, we analyzed a subset of data of 30 participant groups, with 69 participants total. Groups were selected to be representative of the larger dataset  in terms of age, gender, home location, ethnic/racial background, and incoming views of climate change. Of this subset, 40 were from the state in which the museum is located, 25 were from elsewhere in the US, and 4 were from Canada. In terms of gender, 34 identified as men, 33 as women, and 2 as non-binary. For racial/ethnic background, 54 identified as white, 8 as other or multiracial, 4 as Asian, 3 as black or African American. To assess climate views, we used the Six Americas Super Short Survey (SASSY; Chryst et al., 2018) —a validated, 4-item measure to categorize into one of six groups, ranging from “alarmed” to “dismissive”. Of the subset analyzed here, 25 participants were “alarmed”, 17 were “concerned”, 17 were “cautious”, 0 were “disengaged”, 1 was “doubtful”, and 4 were “dismissive”. Four did not complete the survey. This diversity of views speaks to the broader sociopolit ical and ideological diversity of the region, as, in the US, climate views are often related to one’s sociopolitical stance (e.g., Dunlap et al., 2016).

## Data collection

Data were collected through a brief pre-exhibit survey and in-gallery video capture. The pre-survey collected demographic information and climate views. Video data were collected by GoPro cameras worn by participants. Participants were asked to spend approximately 20 minutes in the exhibit, moving through  the exhibit along a specific path but interacting with any parts of the exhibit that interested them. At four locations, participants draw 2-3 “conversation cards” intended to provoke reflection and conversation among group members.

## Data analysis

Pre-survey data were summarized. Video data were analyzed through an iterative, inductive process of qualitative discourse analysis (e.g., Gee, 2014; Miles et al., 2018). First, working independently, researchers made content logs for each of the videos of 15 participant groups randomly selected for the first phase of analysis, documenting events approximately every 30 seconds. Through repeated viewings, the same researcher would then identify moments of potential frame uptake/rejection. This initial identification was guided by existing definitions of side doors (Israel, 2020; Markowitz & Sweetland, 2018), as well as the specific definitions of the side door frames intentionally used within ACoH as articulated by exhibit developers: hope, local, community, and gain. Drawing on a theoretical framework of heteroglossia, we viewed these frames as made material through the many voices that constitute the text of the exh ibit. We searched for evidence of the voices of the exhib it text within the discourse of participants, as they inter acted with ACoH. Additionally, we sought evidence of voices that ran contrary to those intended to be promoted within the exhibit, namely those that reflect broader discourses of climate change in the US that are often antagonistic, fatalistic, and/or unproductive towards climate action (see Zummo et al., 2024).  Slowly and iteratively, through engagement with the  data, refined definitions of uptake/rejection took form, with the research team moving inductively from the data to definitions.  To facilitate this process, the research team met weekly to collectively view data and work towards consensus around moments of frame uptake and/or rejection, refining definitions with each meeting. Through this process, we came to term such moments side door moments, door closing moments, or messy middle moments. After having established  definitions grounded both in the data and in our theoretical framing  of heteroglossia, we engaged in teams of two in a process of determining reliability for identification of  moments. One researcher working independently would view at least 50% of the video data analyzed by another researcher, identifying side door, door closing, and messy middle moments. Then, the two researchers would compare sets of identified moments, discussing discrepancies until resolved. Any case that a pair was unable to resolve would be brought to the larger research group. Refinements of definitions were made as needed, and each researcher would then return to all previously analyzed data to alig n moment selection with updated definitions. Once the team reached high alignment, we content logged and identified moments for the remaining participant groups in the dataset. For any moment in which a researcher was uncertain, they would consult with another researcher. If the two researchers could not come to consensus, researchers would bring the moment  to the larger research group where we would collectively view the data and discuss. Finalized definitions of side door, door closing, and messy middle moments can be found in Table 2, which also lays the groundwork for our Findings. ## Findings

In what ways is uptake and/or rejection of side door frames evidenced in discourse? Table 2 shows the many ways in which learners evidenced uptake and rejection of frames. To illustrate, we show examples of uptake of a given frame and rejection of the same frame with two frames: hope and community. The examples that we show here are reflective of patterns seen within the larger dataset. Hope / Fear Uptake of the intended hope frame lived in tension with rejection of it through the use of fear, a prevalent way of communicating about climate change in the US. To show this tension, we draw on the experiences of  Lily and Ethan. Both “alarmed” about climate change and 30 years ol d, Lily and Ethandrew frequently on the frame of fear to communicate, particularly early on in the exhibit experience. When they used this frame, it often coincided with a sense of powerlessness in the face of catast rophe. The Aspen Grove was intended to be a calming experience that fore-fronted hope (see Table 1). However, Lily and Ethan experienced difficulty taking up this frame; instead, anxiety and fear dominated. As they engaged with the exhibit, instead of taking up hope, the impossibility of progress remained front and center. Their feelings of powerlessness interfered with uptake of hope and instead made it easy to reject by falling back on frames of fe ar and anger. Given this lack of uptake and the reliance on a familiar pattern, we view this moment as a door closing moment. However, later in the exhibit, Lily and Ethan  experienced moments in which they  evidenced uptake of hope. For example, Lily described how it was “cool to be able to see what’s actually happening ” rather than just feeling scared.  Similarly, Ethan took up language about solutions already being underway across many sector s (“all the stuff that has happened that is being done”). With both Lily and Ethan, we see heteroglossia, as they incorporate language and ideas from the exhibit into their discourse, particularly around the  significant solutions that are already happening. They were able to do so even amid the tension they experience around their deep-rooted fear and anxiety, evident earlier in the exhibit. Given this uptake, we viewed this moment as a side door moment. In this messy middle moment, evidence for both uptake and rejection of community can be seen. In what ways is uptake and/or rejection of side door frames related to climate views? We observed relationships between learners’ uptake/rejection of frames and their views about climate change. “Alarmed” learners—those most worried about climate change—tended to struggle with uptake of hope and often fell back on framings of fear, as Ethan and Lily did. “Alarmed” learners also struggled with uptake of community, often relying on blame to make sense of the  exhibit and larger climate  situation, as Jean and Frank did. At the other end of the spectrum, “dismissive” learners —those highly skeptical of climate change—also struggled with uptake of community, frequently relying on blame.

Broadly, learners less concerned about the climate—those “cautious”, “doubtful”, and “dismissive” — often struggled with uptake of gain. Much of the gain framing was accomplished through images and languag e that emphasized at-scale solutions, like renewable energy. As less concerned learners engaged with such text, they tended to reject gain through a reliance on solution critique. Instead of taking up the framing that such innovations lead to a better future with cleaner and more affordable energy, they used tradeoffs of renewables (e.g., cost; wind turbines kill birds, etc.) to reject gain. For example, as Pete, a “dismissive” learner in his  70s engaged with an interactive about renewables in Solutions, he claimed “nobody's got that kind of money.” One frame that appeared to offer side door moments  for learners across the spectrum of climate views was local. Most learners, including those skeptical of climate change, evidenced easy uptake of local, evidenced through engagement in productive conversations  around specific climate impacts either local to the  region or to their home community in response to the exhibit. Local appeared to be a potential pathway for all.

## Discussion and conclusion

Across the dataset, we saw much uptake and rejection. Typically, multiple instances of both occurred over  the span of a group’s visit. This phenomenon demonstrates the possibilities for climate education via side door frames, while at the same time showing how tenuous, fleeting, and changeable such moments of connection can be. What could explain uptake and rejection?

We posit that uptake and rejection of frames are related to several factors, including learners’ incoming views of and prior experiences with climate change, as well as aspects of the exhibit. For example, many young adult learners (under age 30) very much struggled with uptake of the hope frame and instead made sense of the exhibit through a prevailing frame of fear, as Lily and Ethandid. Given that this language of fear did not come from the exhibit itself, we suspect that younger people brin g much distress to climate learning, stemming from a lifetime of having experienced the changing climate and the ways in which the future is  rendered uncertain. This proposition is supported by much other research demonstrating the fear and sense of doom that young people bear around climate change and its influences on learning (e.g., Zummo et al., 2020). Similarly, we posit that rejection of the community frame is likely related to learners’ incoming views of climate change and life experiences. We saw rejection of this frame done mainly by learners who represented the strongest climate views—either the  “alarmed” or “dismissive”. We suspect that such rejection i s mediated by lived experience of identity as affiliated with a  distinct “camp” amid climate politics  (e.g., Walsh & Tsursaki, 2018)—a camp to which the other extreme is in direct opposition. In moments of rejection of community, learners tended to use a discourse of blame and divisiveness  common to contemporary US argument culture  that reifies such divisions. As a result, this discourse of blame interfered with uptake of the intended frame, thereby reshaping learning away from paths sought by the exhibit.

Contributions to learning sciences research and practice This study d eepens conceptualizations of side door frames (e.g., Markowitz & Sweetland, 2018)  by integrating Bakhtinian theory to understand moment-to-moment learning. Further, we show how such frames can shape inthe-moment learning and how such learning varies by learners’ climate views, which themselves are likely linked to the diversity of sociopolitical and ideological sta nces that render climate education in the US challenging. Through this work, we see that side door frames can  spark moments of uptake of discourses of climate change that could offer new, productive paths forward for a divided US public. At the same time, we also see how learners’ prior frames and discourses can interfere with uptake and lead to rejection. Broadly, this study shows how learning sciences can be used to explore ideas in communication, while offering conceptual tools from communication to advance empirical knowledge of climate learning. Furthermore, this work can inform the design of climate education experiences. It shows that certain side door frames—hope, community, local, and gain—offer possibilities to reach learners across a spectrum of views, although specific possibilities vary by specific views.
