# Meaningful Movement Towards Campus-Based Outdoor Learning: Characterizing Pre-Scenery With Stakeholder Perspective

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study seeks to characterize what matters to stakeholders as a school moves towards campusbased outdoor learning. Results from a survey (n=87) demonstrate stakeholder alignment in relation to a longstanding outdoor education movement and emerging movements responding to COVID-19. Discussion considers the polylogic of local communities that work to inspire, enable, and sustain commitments to campusbased outdoor learning.

Traditionally, public and private schools alike have separated outdoor education from formal education (Adkins & Simmons, 2002). Against this backdrop, utilizing an urban school campus to organize and sustain outdoor learning opportunities embodies an uncommon and noteworthy institutional commitment. We seek to characterize this commitment as an ontology in motion (Akkerman et al., 2021) by exploring both what outdoor learning currently is and what it is becoming within the institution as well as the community in which it is embedded. Therefore, as part of an ongoing partnership with a US K-8 charter school committed to campus-based outdoor learning, we explore the aspirational goal of organizing a majority of instructional time outdoors. This study specifically explores institutional and community stakeholders’ perceptions of outdoor learning and well-being in order to (re)consider what matters to each group and in wider relation to shifting educational norms associated with COVID-19 mitigation strategies. We employ a descriptive strategy to characterize what at hand or about to happen as these outdoor learning commitments emerge and, in turn, to answer the following questions: (1) what are stakeholders' general perceptions of outdoor learning? (1a) How do these perceptions compare? (1b) how do stakeholders perceive outdoor learning as a response to COVID-19; and (2) how do stakeholders perceive their own current well-being in the midst of COVID-19?

## Methods

This study reports efforts to attune to what is happening and what matters in the present (Akkerman et al., 2021). Campus greening represents a meaningful movement in motion that involves both physical environments and social constructs like well-being.  To understand stakeholder perceptions of taking learning outside, a survey of internal and external stakeholders examined their perceptions of outdoor learning and community well-being. We developed and distributed an online survey via emails to all internal school stakeholders (e.g. parents, teachers, admin) and a sample of external stakeholders who live in the three zip codes nearest the school. The survey generated data about demography, outdoor learning, nature relatedness, and well-being. We developed outdoor learning items based on the relevant literature. We utilized validated scales for nature relatedness (Nisbet & Zelenski, 2013) and well-being (Stiefel et al., 2020), the latter of which was created in response to COVID-19. Fifty-one adult internal stakeholders (6% response rate) and 36 adult external stakeholders (4% response rate) responded. Meanwhile, 201 students also responded (55.8% student response rate). For analysis, we generated descriptive statistics for all RQs and ran independent samples t-tests to compare perceptions (RQ1a).

## Results

In general, a majority of all stakeholders perceived benefits to outdoor learning (RQ1) (see figure 1). We note that participants unanimously responded that outdoor learning is moderately to extremely effective at promoting social and emotional well-being. Similarly, respondents agreed a little (25.3%) or agreed strongly (67.5%) that students who participated in outdoor learning would build positive social and emotional well-being. We also sought to describe the influence of COVID19 on participants' perceptions of outdoor learning (RQ1b), and found that 82% of participants somewhat agreed (14%) or strongly agreed (68%) that they would support campus-based outdoor learning. An independent samples t-test found no significant differences between internal and external stakeholders on this question, demonstrating a strong, shared perception of immediate and direct relevance.

We also compared the positions of different stakeholder groups on outdoor learning in terms of its effectiveness and influence on student outcomes (RQ1b) (see Figure 1). An independent samples t-test found significant differences for one aspect only--the effectiveness of outdoor learning to promote learning in elective subjects (p<.001)--which often reflects differences in relation to barriers (e.g lack of technology). With respect to student outcomes, responses again suggest general stakeholder alignment around three positive student outcomes. However an independent samples t-test found statistically significant differences (p<.05) among stakeholders with regard to increasing students’ current level of academic success and students feeling more connected to their school community.

As a final consideration, we examined individual well-being (RQ2) and found that adults generally labeled their wellbeing good (internal 64% thriving, 0% suffering: external 56% thriving, 0% suffering). Meanwhile, student results (35% thriving, 6% suffering) suggest stronger adverse effects associated with the pandemic. The immediate and direct relevance of outdoor learning cannot be understood apart from a sense of well-being anchored to ongoing health threats and pandemic mitigation.

## Discussion

This study represents an initial, methodic approach to listening and learning from stakeholders as their local school makes meaningful movements towards outdoor learning and the results establish a pre-scene that accompanies the school’s commitment to “take it outside” by greening campus-wide outdoor infrastructure. Results demonstrate strong, positive alignment on campus-based outdoor learning both as a part of formal education and as an influence on physical activity and well-being for students, particularly in relation to COVID-19. These preliminary findings are promising but also remain limited. First, a small sample size, particularly for external stakeholders, precludes generalization about community perceptions but results justify recruiting more participants in subsequent implementations. Second, while the survey results are consistent with a meaningful movement in motion, items written by researchers, risk objectifying this movement and can be qualified locally through a complementary ethnographic approach to understanding the community context, which we consider in closing.

In conclusion, Akkerman et al. (2021) underscore that an ontology in motion must attend to participants' positions, purposes and possible futures at specific points in time (actuality principle). We conjecture that stakeholder perceptions about what matters in relation to (post-)pandemic schooling does not unfold exclusively in a normative space of outdoor learning, but rather in the polylogic space of local communities that are continually in motion, amplified by COVID-19-related challenges (cf. Leander, 2002). As an effort to attune to what is happening and what matters presently for these stakeholders, this study considers multiple logics that work to inspire, enable, and sustain commitments to campus-based outdoor learning.
