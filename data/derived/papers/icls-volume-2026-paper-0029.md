# Epistemic Practices, Emotion, and Belonging in a Right-Wing Education Podcast

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Political contestation about epistemic worlds is consequential for curriculum and the dignity of youth and educators. We analyze one venue where a right-wing epistemic world about US education is constructed, “The Joyful Warriors Podcast” YouTube podcast by Moms for Liberty. We use discourse and interaction analysis of one episode to understand how epistemic practices, emotion, and ideology combine to produce a view of the world in which medical and education experts are corrupt enemies, children are at-risk from “gender ideology,” and “commonsense” mothers are defenders of their children and the nation. The study has implications for learning sciences research on epistemic engagement and LGBTQ+ learners.

Epistemic worlds are fractured, and political contestation around shared reality is consequential for education. In the US, this contestation shapes curriculum  and has significant consequences for the dignity of educators and learners. In this paper, we analyze one venue where a far-right epistemic world about US education is constructed. Between December 2022 and August 2024, Moms for Liberty (M4L), the right-wing movement organization at the forefront of national campaigns against educational freedoms, regularly released new video episodes of “The Joyful Warriors Podcast” on YouTube. In their discussions, truth claims mix  with ideological and emotional dynamics, building a world in which innocent children and the United States itself are under urgent threat. We use discourse and interaction analysis to examine one episode, “Protecting Our Kids and Parental Rights” (Moms for Liberty, 2023). In the beginning of the episode, the host (co-founder of Moms for Liberty), asks the guest about her experience raising children in the COVID-19 pandemic. By the end of the episode, they have built an epistemic world in which youth must be protected from corrupting educators. We ask: 1) What kind of epistemic identity do the host and guest perform in their interactions? 2) How do epistemic practices, emotion, and ideology combine through moment-to-moment interactions to challenge children’s self-knowledge about gender? As they develop ideas about a growing conspiracy in education, medicine, and beyond, they also develop a picture of what it means to know the world as a “common-sense” mother and defender of the nation. Moms for liberty M4L was incorporated in December 2020 as a social welfare nonprofit (Williams, 2022). A few months earlier, as COVID-19 cases exploded, Descovich had been ousted from the Brevard County School Board in Florida, where she had opposed mask mandates and favored quickly reopening schools (Marazzi Sassoon, 2020). While she lost that race, she tapped into a groundswell of anti-mask energy among right-wing parents (Pappano, 2024, p. 5). Since then, M4L has become a vehicle for campaigns against “woke ideology” in schools, focusing on curricula and policies that involve the inclusion and dignity of people of color and LGBTQ+ people. M4L argues that these materials are inappropriate and emotionally harmful for children, teaching them “to hate themselves, one another, their families, and America” (Williams, 2022). M4L argues  that parents must take back control of education from “woke” state agents. In a Washington Post  editorial, the cofounders celebrated that “parents are finally reclaiming our natural right to direct the education, upbringing and care of our children” (Justice & Descovich, 2021, para. 3). By 2024, M4L claimed 310 chapters in 48 states and more than 130,000 members (Perry, 2024). M4L has also helped propel legislative attacks on educational freedoms. Tracking the emergence in the early 2020s of “educational gag orders,” or policymaking that sought to restrict what could be spoken and taught in schools, PEN America observed in 2023 that a wave of state-level proposed legislation shifted from a focus on “divisive topics” including racism to an overwhelming emphasis on LGBTQ+ topics and identities (Sachs & Young, 2023). These gag order laws have a chilling effect on teaching practices, even when they are overly broad and ambiguous about the targeted concepts (for a recent review, see Fr asier, 2024). They are also consequential for youth, who report benefits to their wellbeing when school conditions counter injustice in the broader community (Somerville, 2025).

M4L reflects a longer tradition of white women’s conservative activism in both the post-war period and the contemporary digital landscape. During the Cold War era, white, middle-class suburban women mobilized through local school boards, PTAs, and neighborhood groups to challenge what they saw as moral and ideological 206 threats to their children’s education, such as sex health education, desegregation, and communist influence (Nickerson, 2014). Viewing themselves as intuitive protectors of the home and family, these women used  their identities as mothers and caretakers to lend legitimacy and emotional appeal to conservative causes. Today, rightwing women operate in a similar ideological terrain but with new tools. Leidig (2023) demonstrates that far-right female influencers leverage parasocial intimacy and lifestyle content to circulate similar messages online. Politics and epistemic contestation Right-wing attacks on education also reflect epistemic problems. Anti-DEI arguments are frequently based on faulty claims about what happens in education (Harper, 2025). Scholars have acknowledged the problems posed by apparently growing disagreement about  how to know. Barzilai and Chinn (2020) summarize four lenses researchers have identified on “post-truth” trends: that people are deficient in knowing how to know, that cognitive biases or limitations lead to fallible ways of knowing, that people are not committed enough to truth, and that people have lost a shared epistemology. At the same time, existing research approaches may underemphasize their powered, emotional, and ideological dynamics.  Politicized epistemic practices are further shaped by digital information ecosystems. As Kellner and Kim (2010) observe, platforms such as YouTube have created “new space for laymen to participate in traditionally restricted areas of politics” (p.  13), disrupt ing traditional gatekeeping structures. However, w hile some  actors leverage digital platforms to amplify historically marginalized perspectives (e.g., Lizárraga & Cortez, 2020; Shrodes, 2021), others legitimize dominant cultural narratives, disseminate extremist ideologies, and cultivate tightly knit communities around those beliefs (Marwick et al., 2022). Marwick and Partin (2024), analyzing the construction of evidence among QAnon conspiracy theorists, describe this shift as a form of “populist expertise,” a rejection of institutional knowledge and elite authority in favor of ‘home-grown’ meaning-making practices shaped by personal experience and collective sensemaking. Russo and Blikstein (2023) similarly argue that participatory media environments can be co-opted to guide audiences toward ideologically loaded conclusions under the guise of independent inquiry. LGBTQ+ educational dignity In this work, we are guided by a commitment to LGBTQ+ people’s dignity in education, “the multifaceted sense of a person’s value generated via meaningful participation in substantive intra-and interpersonal learning experiences that recognize and cultivate one’s mind, humanity, and potential” (Espinoza et al., 2020, p. 19). As LGBTQ+ people have gained partial –and still tenuous –social acceptance and certain civil rights, transness remains especially contested, and nowhere more than in the lives of young people. Reminding readers that “transgender” was not widely used language until the 1990s, Gill-Peterson (2018) points to a longer history, from the 1910s on, of encounters between doctors and children who might now be read as “trans.” In this history, she argues, trans children have been infantilized and their self-knowledge dismissed. There are mounting political and legal efforts  targeting the estimated 725,000 transgender youth in the U.S. (Flores et al., 2022). In February 2025, President Trump issued one of the administration’s first Executive Orders, titled “Keeping Men Out of Women’s Sports.” The Order, under the guise of safety, argues for  the “fundamental biological truths between the two sexes” (The White House, 2025).  In June 2025, the Supreme Court ruled 6-3 in Skrmetti v. U.S.  that SB1, Tennessee’s ban on gender-affirming car e for minors, does not constitute illegal discrimination, paving the way for similar bans. The ruling includes  the argument that minors “lack the maturity to fully understand and appreciate” the consequences of their care (U.S. Supreme Court, 2024). As of July 2025, 27 states have passed bans on gender-affirming care (Human Rights Campaign, 2025). At home and at school, too, t he epistemic status of trans children is pervasively challenged  (Pitts-Taylor, 2022; Schey, 2024). Learning sciences research demonstrates, however, that even young children are capable inquirers (Keifert & Stevens, 2019), and we believe youth are capable of important self-knowledge. Conceptual resources We gather literatures from the learning sciences and related fields on epistemic practices, emotion, and ideology to understand the  rejection of trans youths’ self-knowledge that occurs in a conversational podcast as a social achievement that develops through interactional meaning-making. Links between ways of knowing and ways of being Sociocultural theories of learning treat knowledge as becoming meaningful within particular situations of activity (Lave, 1993). People develop understanding through engaging in practice in social worlds, and at the same time, they are produced as certain kinds of people in those worlds (Lave, 1996; Lave & Wenger, 1991). We treat epistemic practices following the same logic —as historically contingent, culturally grounded, and socially produced over time. Epistemic ideals —the standards by which we evaluate e pistemic processes and their 207 products—vary across knowledge domains (Barzilai & Chinn, 2018). The practices and mechanisms for warranting knowledge in a specific context can be thought of as an “epistemic culture” (Knorr Cetina, 1999). Similar to other forms of social practice, engaging in the epistemic practices of a community is one way of becoming recognized as a member of the community. How one knows is inseparable from who one is. Curnow et al. (2018) demonstrated how in the learning of politicization, shifts in identity and epistemology—along with other dimensions of learning that are often held analytically separate—occur together and are mutually reinforcing (Curnow et al., 2018).  Similarly, learning researchers who emphasize “onto-epistemic heterogeneity” argue that ways of knowing and ways of being are tightly bound and that failing to interrogate epistemic assumptions can lead to reifying colonial forms of knowledge and dehumanizing people whose knowledge does not align with the supposed norm (Warren et al., 2020). Mignolo (2009) argues that decolonizing knowledge requires rejecting “the epistemic privileges of the zero point” (p. 161), or presumed neutrality of dominant knowledge systems, that position certain people as less than fully hu man. Curnow (2022) argues that enacting these commitments analytically requires deliberate attention to power and historicity to surface the dominant ontologies and epistemologies operating in a community of practice. Krishnamoorthy et al.  (2024) further illustrate how epistemic norms emerge through affective and relational negotiations, demonstrating how power and positionality shape both what counts as valid knowledge and whose knowledge is recognized. The relationship between ways of knowing and ways of being becomes evident in how M4L participants describe themselves as “normal” mothers —a characterization that draws on broader cultural understandings of “intuitive motherhood” (Nickerson, 2014). This ideal frames “good” mothers as those who possess an instinctive ability to understand and respond to their children’s needs without relying on expert advice or institutional knowledge. In the context of Moms for Liberty, intuitive motherhood operates as an expression of what Young et al. (2024) describe as “ lay epistemology,” a “common-sense” approach to truth that privileges intuition and emotion over data and evidence. For Joyful Warriors, embracing lay epistemology and intuitive motherhood is also a way of signaling and securing their identity within the group. Situated emotion and ideology in the development of knowing and being To understand the role of emotion in relation to epistemic practices and identities, we also draw on the idea of “emotional configurations,” or the situated ways people learn by socially coordinating connections between feeling, sensemaking, and practice (Vea, 2020). In this view, emotion is a meaning-making process in which embodied intensities are constantly becoming mediated within systems of signification (Anderson, 2016; Vea, 2019). Crucially, this perspective sets aside the question of identifying “the supposed interior content or subjective experience of emotion” (Vea, 2020, p. 315), instead attending  to how people engage in meaning-making about feeling and its social import through language, interaction, and embodied engagement. Engaging in particular forms of emotional practice can support one’s social recognition as a particular kind of person in the world (e.g., feeling like a scientist, Jaber & Hammer, 2016). How people construct meaningful relationships between feeling, sensemaking, and practice shapes how their identities, epistemologies, and other practices develop as they become politicized (Curnow & Vea, 2020). For example, embracing fascist conspiracy theories that demonize minoritized groups might come to be seen as appropriate epistemic practice in part because they validate resentment or xenophobic fear. More “positive” emotion may play a role as well, such as joy in telling racist or dehumanizing jokes, which reproduce the social order in mundane, everyday interactions (Pérez, 2022). Similarly, Curnow et al.

(2021) examine humor through the concept of “snark, ” a discursive strategy using emotional expression to

communicate critique. In both cases, humor acts as a powerful social glue, forging group identity, defining the boundary between ‘us’ and ‘them’ (see also Ahmed, 2004) and laying the groundwork for further politicization. Finally, as we attend to how people become identified as certain kinds of people by way of their ways of knowing and uses of emotion, we also attend to ideology. We draw on work by Philip et al. (2017), who traced ideology as sensemaking “that stabilizes, challenges, and/or transforms the distribution of material and symbolic resources in society” (p. 184). Following Stuart Hall, they operationalize ideology as working through representational systems –concepts and images that get connected or “articulated” in specific ways, and which lead to commonsense understandings about how the world is organized. Emotion plays a role in these dynamics, with “affective stances” that individuals take in relation to objects of discussion or each other shaping how ideology develops at the group level (Philip et al., 2017, pp. 200-202; see also Goodwin, 2007, p. 71). Extending this work, Paré (2024) demonstrated how elicited emotion can help shift people’s ideological orientations toward marginalized people. Whereas Philip et  al. focus on how the discussion group in their study interacted in ways that expanded or narrowed ideological possibilities over time, the M4L podcast includes very little evidence of ideological expansion. Rather, we focus on how interactional processes of ideological articulation can lead over time to expressions of support for extreme social hierarchy that are fundamentally irrational. 208 As part of a larger project analyzing epistemic practices in the Moms for Liberty Joyful Warriors Podcast  (see Vea & Shea, 2025; Vea et al., under review), this study employed discourse and interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) to examine a single episode titled “Protecting Our Kids and Parental Rights.”  Our research team initially collected 61 episodes between December 5, 2022 (when M4L began releasing video versions of their podcast publicly on YouTube) and August 12, 2024 (the final episode published before the 2024 US federal elections). We downloaded automated transcripts produced by YouTube for all episodes and cleaned them, focusing on ensuring that speaker name s, overlap between speakers, and the semantic content of speech were accurately represented. Through multiple individual and co-viewing sessions of episodes, we became interested in participants’ constructions of maternal authority and in how COVID-19 was repeatedly invoked as a catalyst for the breakdown of trust between parents and schools. These themes ultimately led us to select a focal episode. We created a detailed content log, which served as a tool in  the selection of three segments containing thematic patterns and discursive structure warranting in-depth analysis. We employed modified conversation analysis transcription conventions (Jefferson, 2004), using up and down arrows to indicate rising and falling intonation, underline to indicate emphasis, repeated vowels and consonants to indicate elongation, and double parentheses to indicate  gestures and other nonverbal interaction. These markings are analytically useful for us as we track the role of emotion in the host  and guest’s language in interaction. Finally, we use regular-weight type to indicate when the podcasters are discussing the “we” or ingroup and bold type to indicate when the speakers are discussing a “they” or out-group. Focusing on epistemic practices, emotion, ideology, and belonging, we  developed analytic memos for each segment, identifying key moments and integrating relevant literature. We characterize interaction’s emotional character through prosodic and embodied markers of affective intensity, including shifts in speech tempo and emphatic delivery (Goodwin & Goodwin, 2000). Drawing on Goodwin (2000), we examine rhythmic pitch contours—dramatic pitch changes and structured repetition —alongside gestural elaboration where “talk and gesture mutually elaborate each other” (p. 1499). Following Philip et al. (2018), we inferred ideological alignment through participants’ explicit agreement, elaborations, and paraverbal cues such as nodding and laughter. As a multiracial, multigendered research team, our relationships to whiteness, patriarchy, and cis-hetero normativity differ in ways that shape our analytic lenses and orientation to this work. Our relationships to gender remain active, unfolding, and unresolved. W e draw on a lineage of gender-expansive thinkers and activists who have demanded space for fluidity, refusal, and becoming, making room for identities that do not settle neatly into fixed categories (e.g., Butler, 2002; Butler, 2004; Rubin, 2012). Our positionalities are integral to how we engage with and interpret these data, in part, because we believe our own self-understandings and have faith in the self-understandings of others. We have chosen to keep the names of the people behind the podcast in our writing because any data presentation using their words would be easily traced to their own published media. At the same time, we urge focus on the practices in which they are engaged, not on the individuals. We strove in our analysis to treat the speakers with respect as full human beings. We hope to show how they make sense to each other while they engage in the complex and collaborative work of knowing because they are building a worldview that is consequential for the lives of young people and educators. We analyze three focal segments from the podcast episode. In Segment 1 (4:26-6:50), the host and guest establish epistemic authority through a shared maternal identity and introduce a conspiracy theory about the supposed ulterior motives of medical professionals. In Segment 2 (11:01-17:10), the conspiracy is elaborated and expanded via a discussion of education. In Segment 3 (36:42-41:00), the logic of conspiracy moves to encompass the epistemic grounding of trans children’s identity. We argue the expansion of conspiracy associations represents a kind of ideological articulation over the duration of interactional time, connecting new epistemic villains through loose association and analogy. Throughout, parents (and especially mothers) are constructed as privileged knowers with the “common sense” to see through the conspiracy. Throughout, the guest  uses vocal emphasis and animated gestures to mark the singularity of the parent’s epistemic role: no one but the mother will guard the child’s best interest. RQ1. What kind of epistemic identity do the host and guest perform in their interactions? Through their discursive and interactional practices, the podcast participants  position themselves as trustworthy insiders with privileged access to “common-sense” truths. Parenthood is an epistemic identity with claims to complete knowledge over the child as an epistemic object —both as a matter of fact (parental vision provides close epistemic access to the child, such that parents’ claims about their children cannot be problematized) and as a matter of moral requirement (schools and educators must not violate the complete epistemic transparency between the parent and child). A good parent, and especially a mother, is someone who has a responsibility to secure this epi stemic relationship with their child and to exclude other adults from it. In contrast, education and medical experts are epistemic enemies, insinuating themselves in this relationship and threatening children’s innocence and safety. RQ2. How do epistemic practices, emotion, and ideology combine through moment-to-moment interactions to challenge children’s self-knowledge about gender ? The establishment of epistemic authority via shared motherly identity warrants the podcast participants’ claims about an expanding conspiracy that slips across sites of social contestation over interactional time. An aura of suspicion comes to hang over medical experts, then school leaders, then teachers involved in “grooming.” Each new threat justifies new emotional reaction and ideological stance-taking, until in the final segment the child’s agency and self-knowledge is collapsed into the mother’s (“my mom says this isn’t what I do”). In the light of widescale political contestation about ways of knowing, we argue that efforts to restrict young people’s self-knowledge do not merely reflect a secure underlying “reality” of gender.
