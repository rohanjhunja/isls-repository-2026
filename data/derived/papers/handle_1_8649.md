# Scaffolding Math Learning and Motivation by Integrating Casual Play in Computer-Based Practice for Fifth Graders

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The difficulties experienced by many students in mathematics are characterized by anxiety and lack of motivation to learn. Intrinsic and even extrinsic integration of play, can influence students' attitudes and increase their motivation to learn. The purpose of this study was to examine the effect of external integration of play as a break between exercises in a computer-based tutorial on the topic of fractions. We hypothesized that compared to non-gamebased learning, students’ perseverance in solving math exercises would increase, which is reflected in performing more questions while investing sufficient time in solving the problems. Our pilot study compares the number practice questions solved between two groups of 5th graders: 30 students in the play condition, and 33 in the control condition. The results show that there is no significant difference in the number of questions answered in each condition, but that integrating play moderates the differences between motivated and less motivated students.

## Background

The main challenge in designing digital games for p romoting mathematics learning is to balance enjoyme nt of play and pedagogical support for understanding. A design with intrinsic integration of the mathematical content blends the mathematical challenge and the game challenge, whereas extrinsic integration combines a sequence of mathematical challenges or tasks with separate, game-based components (Habgood & Ainsworth, 2011). Intrinsic integration has been shown to enhance student learn ing when compared to an equivalent game with extrin sic integration (Habgood & Ainsworth, 2011), but later studies show that intrinsically integrated digital games do not have significant advantages for learning when compared with paper and pencil exercises, except for enj oyment of the learning process (Castellar, De Marez, & Van  Looy, 2015). In addition, educational games that i nclude intrinsic integration with standard mathematics con tent are much more difficult to develop, than inter spersing mathematical tasks with games in extrinsic integrat ion. Specifically, extrinsic integration of mathema tics exercises with action games, using score points as incentives is a growing effort (e.g., Rahimi et al., 2021), but there is still little information of its effects on  students’ motivation to perform mathematical learn ing activities (Barzilai & Blau, 2014). This poster presents a stu dy of the effects of an extrinsically integrated ca sual game wrapped together with a mathematics training platform, on student motivation and mathematics learning. Design We developed an integrated platform that combines a casual game between the stages of a mathematical training task. The mathematical training task (Figure 1, lef t) entails fraction comparison questions, that play ers solve to accumulate points. The casual game (Figure 1, right) is based on a common game - “Flappy Bird” in whic h the bull-shaped player must collect packages of hay to gain points and avoid obstacles. When the players l ose their health points, they are redirected back to the trai ning tasks, and after completing more than six trai ning tasks successfully, the player is redirected to the casual game. The score in the casual game is added to the score of the training task thereby weaving the two activities together. ## Method

The study was conducted with 63 students from two fifth grade classrooms from a state religious school with only boys. There were 35 students in classroom A and 28 students in classroom B. We randomly assigned 30 students to the play condition and 33 students to the contro l condition. The activity spanned 45 minutes and en tailed 30 fraction comparison questions as shown in figure 1. We used persistence in completing exercises and the score of correct responses to fractions questions as depende nt measures of the effect of the game on motivation  and perseverance (Lucas et al., 2015). The time spent on solving questions was also examined and the data was based on log files. Our main hypothesis was that interspe rsing play during the fraction questions will raise  students’ motivation that will promote perseverance. However, we also hypothesize that the lure of gameplay will decrease the time students spend on solving, and may be detrimental to solution accuracy. Our research questions were:

1. Is there a difference in the total number of questions answered by the play group and the control group?

2. Is there a significant difference in the average time students devoted to solving questions and in their solution

accuracy in each condition?

3. How much of the variance in students’ math solution scores, is explained by their game score and time on task?

## Results

Both the control group and the play group students answered an average of 27/30 of the questions, but classroom A students (from both groups) answered significantly less questions (26/30) than their counterparts in classroom B (29/30) (p=0.05). Also, students from classroom A  scored lower on the mathematical exercises (37.5 p oints) than their peers in classroom B (42.7 points) although the difference was not significant (p=0.11). The difference in scores associated with the classroom, was much larger in the control condition (35.8 in A and 44.1 in B) than in the play condition (40.8 in A and 41.8 in B).

To study the 3rd research question, our analysis, revealed that question solution scores in the play condition were strongly correlated with their score on the casual game (r=0.45, p=0.01). The correlation of the math solution scores with the average time for answering each question was much smaller and non-significant (r=0.25, p=0.19). In the control condition, the correlation between average time per question and overall performance score on the math problems was significant (r=0.395, p=0.02).

## Discussion and conclusions

Our study examined the effect integrating a casual game into a mathematical tutor on learners' mathema tical problems solving. We found that motivation and consequent perseverance in problem solving of the students in classroom A was lower than those in classroom B as indicated in the average number of questions they solved in the tutor, and their average overall scores. However, we also show that the play condition mitigated some of the differences between the classrooms in terms of perseverance in answering the questions, and the overall scores-both were closer in the play condition than in the control condition. Moreover, the correlation between the game score and the tutorial score, indicates a possible motivational connection between the two. In the absence of play, less motivated students in the control condition spent less time on the questions, and their variance in performance was probably a result of their predispositions and grit (Lucas et al., 2015). Our use of the game between the stages of the educational task reflects extrinsic  integration since it is detached from the education al content. Previous studies (Habgood & Ainsworth, 2011) have shown that such a combination is less effective than a combin ation of intrinsically integrated learning software. However, our study indicates that also extrinsic integration which is easier to develop, is beneficial for reducing motivational differences between students in mathematics.
