# Reinterpreting Lamarckianism as Generative Mechanistic Reasoning about Natural Selection

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Students often express Lamarckian ideas—that changes acquired during an organism’s lifetime can be inherited—when reasoning about natural selection. Researchers have described this reasoning as arising from incorrect and unproductive misconceptions. Using the theoretical tools of resource theory and data from interviews with college students, we argue that an alternative explanation for students’ apparent Lamarckian reasoning is that they are seeking to provide mechanisms that can account for trait change. Unlike canonical population-level mechanisms, organism-level mechanisms are grounded in plausible changes to organismal forms, physiologies, or behaviors. We found that organism-level mechanistic reasoning arose in interviews when students recognized a need for a mechanistic explanation and shifted into an epistemological framing of in-the-moment knowledge construction. Rather than interpret Lamarckian ideas as misconceptions, we argue that they can be viewed as evidence of students' generative epistemological resources for seeking and providing mechanisms.

## Introduction

Resource models of cognition offer theoretical tool s that can allow researchers to reinterpret misconc eptions in terms of fine-grained, potentially productive resources (Hammer, 1996; Smith et al., 1994). Prior research, primarily in the domain of physics, has argued that sub-conceptual intuitive ideas, such as “closer means stronger”, function as the building blocks of expert understandings of the physical world (Hammer, 1996). In cont rast, resource models have rarely been applied to the dom ain of biology (Gouvea, 2023; Southerland et al., 2 001), leaving gaps in our understanding of the potentiall y productive intuitions that learners bring to reas oning about biological phenomena. In this paper, we argue that a productive approach to reinterpreting misconcepti ons in biology is to examine learners’ intuitive epistemol ogical resources and framings. Using the example of Lamarckianism, we argue that learners can activate generative epistemological resources for understand ing and moving between different types of biological explan ations. What others have interpreted as misconcepti ons can instead be understood as attempts to seek and generate mechanistic knowledge. Complex explanations in biology Biology is frequently described as a science that d eals with complex systems spanning multiple levels,  and expertise requires the ability to propose and integ rate explanations at the appropriate levels (Dauer & Dauer, 2016). Mayr (1961) proposed two distinct levels of biological explanat ion: Ultimate explanations occur at the population level and explain changes in the frequency of traits over generations. Proximate explanations occur at the level of organisms and explain changes in the f orm and/or function of a trait. Biologists seek bot h types of explanations, but when reasoning about evolution, o nly population-level mechanisms are considered correct. Learners have been described as naive, incorrect, or avoidant if they exhibit evidence of reasoning at inappropriate levels (Abrams & Southerland, 2001; Ferrari & Chi, 1998). To illustrate the two levels, consider the question, how did a rockskipper fish evolve the ability to jump? The canonical model of evolution by natural selecti on explains the change as emerging from a population-level mechanism: In a population of fish, some variants were bette r able to jump than others.  Those that were better able to jump survived and reproduced better than th ose who could not and passed the underlying genes t o their offspring. This population-level mechanism explains changes in the frequency of a trait over generations. Students often provide explanations that involve changes within organisms, describing for example how the ability to jump could arise as muscles get stronger through use. Organism-level mechanisms such as changes to the physiology, morphology, or behaviors in the body explain changes in the form and/or function of a trait. While there is evidence that organism-level ideas are common in students’ explanations, interpretations of the function and productivity of these ideas are varied. Some scholars explain organism-level thinking as arising from strongly held Lamarckian misconceptions that appear to mirror the historically incorrect model of evolution through the inheritance of acquired characteristics proposed by Lamarck (e.g., Gregory, 2009). Others have agreed that concepts such as change through use or disuse are incorrect but have presented evidence that students can shift from organism-level to population-level explanations, disputing the  strength and  stability of the misconceptions (e.g., Demastes et al., 1996). In either case, the implication is that organism-level thinking is unproductive and should be dissuaded in evolution instruction. Yet, alone, population level mechanisms provide no details about the nature, size, or sequence of changes that could build new traits. A more complete explanation of evolution accounts both for the change in frequencies of traits and their developmental origins (Endler & McLellan, 1988). For example, explaining the evolution of a complex trait like the mammalian eye involves filli ng in the basic story of gradual change provided by natural selection with a specific series of molecular and physiological changes that could build a comple x eye structure from a simple ancestral state (Oakley & Pankey, 2008). We propose that students, rather than reasoning from a Lamarckian model of evolution, are activating a pro ductive epistemological resource —seeking organism-level mechanisms—and applying it to propose tentative mechanisms for how new traits could be formed. Reinterpreting Lamarckian reasoning as mechanistic Lamarck’s name has become synonymous with his  incorrect model of evolution, which textbooks often  contrast with Darwin’s correct model. As K ampourakis and Zogza (2007) argue, this positioning oversimplifies and distorts evolutionary history. Both Lamarck and Darwin proposed ideas that turned out to be incorrect. Both were scientists who sought and proposed mechanistic models that could e xplain patterns in the natural world. While Darwin emphasized selection as a population-level change mechanism, Lamarck’s  model explained gradual changes in traits  by proposing that hydraulic mechanisms within organ isms could stimulate the growth and development of body parts. A focus on the conceptua l flaws in Lamarck’s model ignores its epistemic contributions such as pushing the field to connect evolution and development. When used to describe student thinking, the term La marckian is used to describe conceptual errors  that fail to include the correct population-level concepts of variation, differential selection, and inheritance  (e.g., Ferrari & Chi, 1998; Gregory, 2009). There is little dispute that organism-level ideas such as change through use or disuse are prevalent in students’ reasoning  (Kampourakis & Zogza, 2007). However, the existence of these ideas is insufficient to explain why and how students are using them. We explore the possibility that, like Lamarck, students are using organism-level ideas productively, to propose mechanisms for  trait change. To better understand the function of organism-level ideas in students’ reasoning, we turn to the tools of resource theory.

## Theoretical background: Resources and framing

We use a resources-based model of cognition to characterize students’ reasoning about natural s election and reasoning shifts  over the course of an interview.  Resource-based models of cognition propose that minds are systems made up of fine-grained interacting components commonly referred to  as “resources” (Hammer et al., 2005). Reasoning emerges from such systems when subsets of resources are activated in interaction with the local situation. Compared to “conceptions”, resources are proposed to be finer-grained and quasi-independent (Brown, 2013). An affordance of resource-models is that unlike misconceptions models, they c an explain shifts and context-dependencies in reasoning.

Researchers have proposed resources of various type s. In this study we examined interactions between conceptual and epistemological resources. Conceptual resources are the ideas used to explain phenomena whereas epistemological resources are fine-grained ideas about the structure, nature, and func tion of knowledge (Elby & Hammer, 2001). For example, knowledge can be unders tood as something that exists and is transmitted or  as something that can be constructed and reshaped. The  type of knowledge needed to answer a question is a lso an issue of epistemology – should I provide a fact, a description, or a mechanism? In any moment of reasoning many different types of resources may be activated together. For example, the act of proposing a novel mechanism could be understood to involve epistemological resources about the type of knowledge to be constructed as well as th e specific conceptual ideas that comprise the mecha nism. One way to understand the coordinated activation of sets of  resources is in terms of framing. In simple terms,  framing is a process of interpreting “what is going on” in a spe cific situation (Tannen & Wallat, 1987). In the lan guage of resources, framing is a process whereby sets of coo rdinated resources are activated in response to a f amiliar context (Hammer et al., 2005).

Epistemological framing is a broad description of v arious ways in which a person might interpret the knowledge structures and practices that are appropriate or valued in a situation (Scherr & Hammer, 2009). These framings can influence the conceptual knowledge that students activate. For example, as Louca et al. (2004) report, when students interpreted a classroom discussion to be about why leaves change color in the fall, they offered teleological ideas such as, “the tree no longer needs leaves”. Later in the conversation, when the teacher signaled that she wanted to know how the color change happens in terms of “steps,” students began invoking ideas about cells and the movement of pigments  to propose mechanisms. As this example illustrates, students can activate different resources as their understandings of situ ations change. Rather than characterizing students as able to reason mechanistically or not, resource-models seek to understand when different framings a re activated, maintained, or disrupted.

## Study context

We analyzed a set of interviews conducted with comm unity college students who were taking a tropical b iology course in Costa Rica. The course included field exc ursions during which instructors pointed out and id entified organisms for students to observe. Later, students were asked to individually complete an assessment t ask in which they were asked to choose an organismal  trait they had observed and write an explanation fo r “how that trait could have evolved from an ancestor that did not have that trait.” The instructor of the course (and author 4) then interviewed each student individually about th eir answers. His intention was  to use the interviews as a teaching tool to first elicit students’ ideas and then provide individualized instruction. For this study, we analyzed only the first part of the interview during which students were explaining their thinking.

From reporting knowledge to constructing organism-level mechanisms Across the five interviews with students who offere d organism-level conceptual resources (Table 1) we saw a similar pattern. In the first part of the interview s, students began by sharing explanations that they  appeared to have already constructed. They did not read what th ey had written on their worksheets, but they looked  down at them as if referencing what they wrote. Otherwise, their gaze was mainly directed at the interviewer. While there was some variation in markers of certainty and form ality, for the most part, students spoke in a steady tone with minimal pauses, aligning with a reporting knowledge framing. The framing of the interview shifted when the inter viewer asked a question or prompted for an explanation that students appeared to interpret as asking for a mechanism.  In some cases, he was intending to press for a population-level mechanism. ## Discussion

The organism-level ideas raised by the five students we studied would typically be considered evidence of Lamarckian misconceptions —incorrect commitments to the inheritance of within-generation changes to organisms (Ferrari & Chi, 1998; Gregory, 2009). Our purpose i n this paper is not to definitively endorse or rule out this interpretation. We do not have sufficient evidence from Anna, Dina, or any of the students interviewed to make strong claims about the nature of their concep tual understandings. Though we do argue that this ambiguity itself challenges the strength of assertions about the certainty with which student data can be used t o support interpretations of Lamarckian misconceptions.

Instead, our claims concern epistemologies—how students understand and enact biological explanations. We suggest that perhaps students’ evolutionary reas oning does align with Lamarck, not because of conce ptual incorrectness, but because of a shared commitment to seeking and producing mechanis ms. Rather than attribute organism-level ideas to faulty conceptual frameworks, we cla im that these resources arose at epistemologically appropriate moments, as students shifted from reporting on knowledge that they had previously constructed t o actively constructing novel explanations in the moment. Tang and Hammer (2024) make a similar argument about the function of anthropomorphic reasoning in the explanations of young children. Anthropomorphis ms function to “fill in” for mechanisms that studen ts are seeking, suggesting not only that students can use their kno wledge of human-like behavior as resources to build causal stories, but also that they are doing so because they recognize a gap that needs to be filled with a mechanism. What makes anthropomorphic reasoning productive, Ta ng and Hammer argue, is that it supports an epistemological framing of inquiry and allows student s to sustain knowledge-construction activities (ibid.). Similarly, we reinterpret Lamarckian reasoning as evidence of students’ productive resources for both recognizing the need for and constructing novel mechanisms.

One type of evidence for this claim comes from our analysis of epistemological framings of interview roles. The behavioral markers we identified suggest that  students were aware of and attempting to communicate the tentativeness of their ideas. Second, the types of explanations students provide d suggest the activation of epistemological resources related to seeking and proposing mechanisms. When pressed to explain “how”, some students interpreted the situation as requiring a mechanism at the organism level. This suggests that at a minimum, students can tacitly differentiate between mechanistic and other types of explanations. Prior work has questioned the capability of student s to construct mechanistic explanations  (Abrams & Southerland, 2001; Kampourakis & Zogza, 2007). Abrams and Southerland (2001) found that when students from second through twelfth grade were asked “how” vario us biological phenomena occurred, they often provided answers that explained “why” the trait or behavior might benefit the organism. For example, when asked  how a plant grows towards light, students replied that it  needs to grow towards light. One possible explanat ion for why we saw stud ents providing mechanistic explanation s is that the students in our study were older  – enrolled in community college. However, aside from Anna none of  the other students were biology majors or had take n college-level biology. Some, like Kim, revealed that they h ad not taken biology since middle school. This background combined with the use of “everyday” reso urces about organism bodies suggests that the desir e and ability to seek organism-level mechanisms may be common. An alternative explanation is that the students in our study had already provided “why” explanations earlier in the interview and were now being pressed for additional “how” explanations by the interviewer. This press m ay have activated mechanistic resources in two ways. First, by contrasting with earlier why explanations, this pressing made a clear distinction between why and how, helping students activate knowledge about this distinction  (Louca et al., 2004). Additionally, the pressing may have signaled to students that they needed to go beyond their pre pared responses and speculate about something they were not sure about. Students may avoid “how” quest ions not because they don’t understand them, but be cause they don’t see interviews as a space in which to speculate. Further, students' explicit responses and affective  reactions showed that they were puzzled or perhaps even intrigued by the mechanistic gap between ancestral and evolved traits. Population-level explanations assume the existence of variation in trait forms but don’t  directly explain the origin or development of this  variation (Endler & McLellan, 1988). It is notable that the t raits chosen by the students who activated organism-level mechanisms are all relatively complex  (Table 1). Attributing the evolution of jumping to a series of abstract mutations does not provide a complete mechanism for its evolution. In their attempts to bridge gaps, students activate d a productive set of conceptual resources for maki ng sense of how traits can emerge and change through b odily processes (Table 1). These ideas may have ori gins in students’ everyday understanding of their  own bodies as well as knowledge of how organisms ca n get stronger, move, change shape, or learn new behaviors (Hatano & Inagaki, 1994). We saw some evidence to support this in the way Anna acts out the movements of the fish wit h her own body. Later, reflecting on her response, she describes taking the perspective of a fish, imagini ng “how it would feel.” Similarly, Dina appears to activate her prior knowledge that plants move towards light in t he moment as she looks out the window, referencing the everyday world.

## Conclusion and future work

Students' reasoning about natural selection has been studied widely, from the elementary to college-level. In most of these studies, the difficulties of learning natural  selection are emphasized. One contribution of appl ying a resource-based model to student data is that it can reveal p roductive intuitive knowledge from which deeper understandings can be built. To date, only one conceptual resource for reasoning in the domain of biology has been identified (Southerland et al., 2001). We propose that it can be generative to explore how students may be activating and using intuitive fine-grained epistemological resources. In addition to i ntuitions about what bodies and organisms cando, students may have intuitive ideas about what needs to be explained when reasoning about biological systems.

One gap left by this study is an understanding of h ow students reconcile organism and population-level mechanisms. Anna moved between discussing population-level ideas about natural selection and organism-level ideas about trait change, but we don’t know if she recognized the two explanations as distinct or how she might integrate them. Further, this work opens questions about the extent to which students recognize mechanistic gaps in their own reasoning or in the curriculum. If the y do, these gaps could be  used to create opportunities to intentionally support opportunities for knowledge construction in biology education.
