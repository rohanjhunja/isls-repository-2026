# How Simulation Design Shapes Academically Productive Talk: An Epistemic Network Analysis of Teacher Discourse in Middle-School Physics

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Dialogic teaching, through Academically Productive Talk (APT), enhances student learning. While digital simulations are common in science education, their specific impact on teacher discourse patterns remains underexplored. This study examines how simulation design, Multiple Representations (MR) versus Single Representation (SR), shapes whole-class discourse structure and teachers’ use of APT moves. One teacher and 99 eighth-grade students from two classes in Western China participated. Using Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) and discourse analysis of 147 teacher talk turns, we compared APT patterns between two conditions. ENA revealed distinct strategic repertoires: the MR condition showed an integrated, collaborative network centered on reasoning and building on ideas, involving more students in shorter, focused turns. The SR condition exhibited a linear, teacher-guided pattern dominated by elicitation and challenge, with longer explanations from fewer students. Overall, simulation design relates to teacher facilitation and student participation, suggesting that multiple representational tools may afford more collaborative-dialogic classrooms.

Dialogic teaching enhances student engagement and learning outcomes through structured teacher-led discourse (Michaels & O’Connor, 2015; Tao & Chen, 2024). With professional development, teachers can employ strategies such as A cademically Productive Talk (APT) to foster more active and cognitively demanding classroom environments, encouraging deeper thinking and collaborative knowledge building (Bouton et al., 2025; Chen, Chan, et al., 2020). In parallel, digital learning environments, particularly computer simulations, have gained prominence for their interactive and visual affordances, which support conceptual understanding in science education (Li et al., 2024; Testa & Catena, 2022). The design of simulations, including their representational complexity, significantly influences learning processes and outcomes (Jaakkola & Veermans, 2020; Trypke et al., 2024). However, extant research on APT analyzes dialogue at an aggregate classroom level or focuses narrowly on teacher behaviors, potentially overlooking nuanced interaction patterns shaped by specific instructional tools. This study investigate s how different simulation types, namely multiple versus single representations, shape whole-class discourse structure and teachers’ APT patterns in junior high physics classrooms. Academically productive talk Dialogic pedagogy encompasses several frameworks aimed at fostering productive classroom discourse, including Academically Productive Discussion (Michaels & O’Connor, 2015), Accountable Talk (Michaels et al., 2008), and Productive Classroom Dialogue (Pehmer et al., 2015). This study adopts the framework of Academically Productive Talk (Resnick et al., 2010), which is grounded in Vygotsky’s (1978) sociocultural theory and situated learning perspectives (Brown et al., 1989). APT emphasizes knowledge co-construction through social interaction, as students verbalize, share, and refine their thinking collectively (Michaels & O’Connor, 2015). Table 1 outlines eight APT talk moves aligned with four instructional goals, contextualized for physics learning (Chen, Chan, et al., 2020).

Research indicates that productive discourse positively influences student engagement (e.g., emotionally, cognitively, and behaviorally), leading to improved learning outcomes (Sedova & Sedlacek, 2023; Tao & Chen, 2024). Teachers’ adept use of APT moves is crucial for fostering such discursive engagement, a characteristic of high-quality instruction (Bouton et al., 2025; Chen, Zhang, et al., 2020). Consequently, recent efforts have emphasized teacher professional development (TPD) aimed at integrating productive talk to promote more active and equitable student participation (Bouton et al., 2025; Wang, Yang, et al., 2025). Nonetheless, few studies have investigated how digital learning environments, such as simulations, shape teachers’ APT moves and associated discourse structures in physics education.

In contrast, single representation (SR) approaches use one modality, offering limited support for comprehension (Jaakkola & Veermans, 2020). Integrating MR into dynamic simulations has shown strong efficacy in science learning (Li et al., 2024; Testa & Catena, 2022). Empirical studies indicate that MR simulations support conceptual transfer and long-term retention by facilitating transitions between representations (Ainswor th, 2006; Rexigel et al., 20 24). Cognitively, they reduce working memory load and manage cognitive demands more effectively (Schwamborn et al., 2011; Trypke et al., 2024). Motivationally, vivid visual elements enhance engagement (Magner et al., 2016), while the multi-perspective nature of MR fosters deeper reasoning and supports personalized learning (Almasri, 2022). Despite these benefits, few studies have examined how different representational designs influence the quality of classroom discourse, particularly the use of APT in physics instruction. The present study This study examines how simulation representational design (MR vs. SR) is associated with the enactment of productive classroom discourse. Grounded in Bandura’s (1986) social cognitive theory, which posits reciprocal interactions among environment, behavior, and personal factors, we treat representational design (MR vs. SR) as a key environmental variable. We expect  that MR simulations, by providing diverse semiotic resources, may be associated with  more interconnected APT strategies (e.g., integrating reasoning prompts with collaborative additions). This strategy pattern may co-occur with more distributed student participation, consistent with the socio-constructivist orientation of APT.  To examine these dynamics, we employ Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) to model the co-occurrence structure of teacher APT moves, moving beyond frequency counts to capture strategic patterns. Our research addresses the following research questions (RQ):

• RQ1. How do MR and SR simulation environments differ in the overall structure of classroom discourse?

• RQ2. What are the differences in the network structure of teacher APT moves between the MR and SR

conditions?

• RQ3. How are these quantitative differences manifested in the qualitative flow of classroom dialogue?

## Method

This study utilized data from a larger intervention program that integrated simulation-based instruction to foster productive talk in junior high school physics classrooms in Western China. The program was implemented during the fall semester of 2024 after obtaining formal ethical approval.

## Participants and procedure

33 The participants included one male physics teacher (who held a master’s degree and had over one year of teaching experience) and 99 eighth-grade students from his two parallel classes. The teacher voluntarily took part in the intervention and received three 40-minute professional development workshops on employing APT and simulations in instruction.

The two classes, comparable in size and overall academic performance, were randomly assigned to Class A (n = 47; 22 males, 25 females) and Class B (n = 52; 28 males, 24 females). The instructional topic, Melting and Solidification, was selected based on consultations with five experienced educators. The teacher utilized two types of pre-made simulations (e.g., depicting Glauber’s salt and ice melting; Figure 1) tailored to the local curriculum to ensure ecological validity. In Class A (the Multiple Representations, or MR, group), students engaged with simulations featuring toggleable representations (animation, table, graph, formula). In Class B (the Single Representation, or SR, group), simulations displayed only one repre sentation at a time. In this study, a representation refers to a domain-specific external depiction of the target phenomenon that uses a distinct semiotic system (e.g., dynamic animation of states/particles, a  numerical data table, a Cartesian graph). On-screen labels or brief instructions were treated as interface text rather than additional disciplinary  representations. Classroom sessions were video-recorded and subsequently transcribed verbatim, with speakers identified in consultation with the teacher.

Figure 1 Different Types of Simulations on the Melting of Glauber’s Salt Coding classroom dialogue transcripts Transcripts were segmented into talk turns, defined as uninterrupted stretches of talk by a single speaker and bounded by a changing speaker. A total of 292 talk turns (MR: 150; SR: 142) were transcribed. Teacher talk turns (147 total; MR: 75; SR: 72) were coded using an established APT framework (Chen, Chan, et al., 2020; Resnick et al., 2010), which includes the categories: Say More, Revoice, Press for Reasoning, Challenge, Restate, Agree & Disagree, Add On, and Explain Other (Table 1). Each teacher talk turn could receive multiple APT codes when multiple moves occurred. Teacher turns that did not enact any APT move (e.g., routine information-seeking questions or conventional topic initiations) were coded as “None”. Inter-rater reliability was established by two researchers performing independent cross-coding. Cohen’s kappa was calculated on a code-by-code basis, revealing substantial to almost perfect agreement with values ranging from.705 to.926 (M =.81; Landis & Koch, 1977).

## Method of analysis

Classroom Discourse Analyzer To examine the overall structure of classroom discourse (RQ 1), we utilized the Classroom Discourse Analyzer (CDA; Chen, 2020; Wang, Chen, et al., 2025) to automatically analyze transcripts for low-inference features, including the n umber of student speakers, word counts  per turn, and teacher-student turn-taking patterns. Furthermore, high-inference discourse features, such as the specific characteristics of utterances, were identified and coded manually.

Epistemic Network Analysis To address RQ2, we used Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) to model the co-occurrence structure of APT codes in teacher talk (Bowman et al., 2021). ENA is useful for examining not only how often discourse moves occur but also how they are connected within segments of interaction. Analyses were conducted using the ENA Web Tool (v. 1.7.0; Marquart et al., 2018). We applied a moving stanza window of 9 lines to model links between current and prior codes, balancing variance explained on SVD1 and model stability. Group differences in network 34 structure were tested using Mann-Whitney U tests on the resulting projection coordinates. ENA visualizations consist of points in a low-dimensional space and corresponding network graphs, where node positions and connection strengths illustrate the structure of code associations.  We compared the resulting network structures across the MR and SR conditions and interpreted them together with qualitative transcript analysis for RQ3. This section reports the results of the comparative analysis of dialogic teaching in the Multiple Representations (MR) and Single Representation (SR) conditions. We first outline the overall structure of classroom discourse, then detail the patterns and network structure of the teacher ’s APT moves, and finally contextualize these quantitative findings with illustrative dialogue excerpts. Comparison of overall structure of classroom discourse While the proportion of teacher talk was comparable across groups (MR: 50.0%; SR: 50.7%; Table 2), the structure of student participation differed markedly. The MR condition showed a higher proportion of individual student turns (35.3% vs. 23.9%) and engaged nearly twice as many unique speakers (22 vs. 12). Conversely, the SR condition was characterized by a higher proportion of choral responses (25.4% vs. 14.7%).  Differences also emerged in contribution length: on average, student turns were shorter in MR than in SR (8 vs. 16 words per student turn), whereas the average total contribution per student speaker was higher in SR than in MR (45 vs. 19 words per student speaker).

## Key findings and contribution

ENA uncovered distinct teacher strategic repertoires across conditions. In the MR setting, APT moves formed a dense, “integrated collaborative” network centered on Press for Reasoning, Say More, and Add On, reflecting a pedagogy that connects reasoning with collaborative knowledge building and aligns with the socio-constructivist principles of APT (Michaels & O’Connor, 2015).  This pattern is consistent with prior work arguing that MR environments can offer diverse semiotic resources that may support reasoning and help manage cognitive demands, potentially enabling teachers to focus on collaborative discourse (Almasri, 2022; Schwamborn et al., 2011; Trypke et al., 2024). In contrast, the SR condition elicited a sparse, linear network dominated by Say More and Challenge as a “guided challenge” pattern aimed at correcting and refining student contributions. The more limited representational affordances in the SR simulations may be linked to a narrower range of discursive moves and a more directive facilitation style in this setting. These findings extend prior work on teacher facilitation and student engagement (Bouton et al., 2025; Chen, Zhang, et al., 2020; Wang, Yang, et al., 2025) by highlighting the role of instructional tools, not just teacher or student factors, in shaping dialogic patterns. Methodologically, ENA captured structural nuances in teacher talk that frequency-based analyses would overlook, offering a clearer view of how tool affordances may relate to teachers’ strategic adaptations. At the student level, MR related to more distributed participation, with more students contributing shorter, focused responses. SR, by contrast, featured more choral responses and longer explanations from fewer students, reflecting a less student-centric dynamic. One possible interpretation is that MR may lower perceived participation barriers.

For practice, these findings imply that simulation design shapes conversational possibilities. Teachers and curriculum developers may consider selecting multi-representational tools to foster collaborative reasoning. Professional development could  likewise highlight how digital environments influence discursive strategy, empowering teachers to leverage MR tools for more equitable dialogue.

## Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, the involvement of a single, motivated teacher ensured internal consistency for the comparative case design but limits the generalizability of the findings. Accordingly, the present environments in an information-rich case, rather than as an estimate of the prevalence of APT moves in everyday classrooms. Future research should involve multiple teachers to account for variations in pedagogical style and to explore how teacher beliefs interact with tool affordances. Additionally, while ENA powerfully models the structure of teacher talk, current tools do not  fully support the statistical modeling of nested data structures (e.g., turns nested within students, within classes). Future methodological developments in this area would strengthen such analyses. Finally, the study focused on teacher strategies and overall discourse structure; future work should directly measure the corresponding cognitive and affective outcomes for students to more definitively link these dialogic patterns to learning gains.
