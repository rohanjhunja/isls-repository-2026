# Beyond Knowing: An Empirical Study of a KBCP-Based Intervention to Promote Metacognitive Awareness and Learning Strategy Use

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This pilot study addresses the persistent "knowing-doing" gap, where students fail to apply effective learning strategies. We investigated the efficacy of a five-day summer institute curriculum grounded in the Knowledge-Belief-Commitment-Plan (KBCP) framew ork for 34 primary and lower-secondary students. Our findings reveal that this brief intervention yielded statistically significant gains in metacognitive self-report scores and markedly increased the articulation of strategy use in students' learning plans. We attribute this success to the KBCP framework itself, which functions as a metacognitive scaffold to systematically guide learners through self-regulatory processes. These results provide a strong rationale for educators to integrate the full KBCP fra mework, prioritizing metacognitive and resource management strategy instruction.

A consensus in the learning sciences confirms that effective learning strategies are crucial for improving academic achievement, self-regulation, knowledge retention, and fostering learners’ metacognitive abilities (Pintrich & De Groot, 1990; McKeachie  et al., 1986). Research consistently shows that many students gravitate towards inefficient strategies (Karpicke et al., 2009), guided by flawed metacognitive judgments and a focus on immediate performance over durable learning (Kornell & Bjork, 2007; Morehead et al., 2015). This challenge is particularly acute in China's primary and lower secondary schools, where most students show low strategic competence, and explicit strategy instruction is rare (Tian  & Zhang, 2010). Compounding this issue, even when students can correctly identify superior strategies, they often fail to adopt them, citing barriers such as low self-efficacy, perceived effort, and ingrained habits (Rea et al., 2022). These findings underscore that interventions focused solely on disseminating knowledge are insufficient to enact meaningful change. The Knowledge-BeliefCommitment-Planning (KBCP) framework (McDaniel & Einstein, 2020) offers a  more comprehensive model, aiming to bridge the gap between knowing about effective strategies and actually using them.  Despite its theoretical promise, empirical validation of KBCP with younger learners remains scarce, particularly in contexts where explicit strategy instruction is limited.  To address this gap, the present study is guided by the following research questions: Does a KBCP-structured five-day curriculum produce statistically significant improvements in metacognitive awareness among primary and lower-secondary learners? (RQ1) Does the curriculum increase the frequency of evidence-based learning strategy use in students' self-designed academic plans, and do gains differ across cognitive, metacognitive, and resource management strategy categories? (RQ2) Design This five-day summer institute provided a structured, classroom-based curriculum that integrated domain-general learning strategies with authentic disciplinary tasks, explicitly mapped onto the KBCP framework. The course began with expert-led lectures and inquiry-based activities about memory systems and mechanisms of memory formation. Fundamental knowledge from the science of learning helps students understand how the brain learns and provides the theoretical foundation for the effectiveness of learning strategies. Guided by McKeachie (198 6), the summer institute  adopted a hierarchical  taxonomy of learning strategies. In the cognitive strategies module, we operationalized three strategy types—rehearsal, elaboration, and organization—based on prior classifications  (Weinstein & Mayer, 1986). The instructional design drew on established reviews in cognitive science applied to classroom practice (Perry et al., 2021) and on foundational work on generative learning (Fiorella & Mayer, 2015), which together informed the learning activities. The metacognitive strategies module adopted a general theoretical framework guiding students through processes of planning, monitoring, and evaluation (Quigley et al., 2018). To address the low learning motivation reported by over half of the participants, the resource  management strategies module on effort management targeted cultivating a growth mindset and regulating motivation; Implementation The Summer Institute was implemented in July 2025 in classroom settings by four teachers trained in the KBCP framework and learning strategy instruction. It enrolled 34 students from 10 cities across two cohorts (Grades 5 – 7 and 7–9). Before each module, students completed entry tasks to generate learner profiles and receive tailored suggestions to guide their plans.

At the beginning and end of the Summer Institute, 34 students completed pre-and post-tests to evaluate the impact of the KBCP-based curriculum on their metacognitive development and strategy use.  Metacognition was assessed using the  30-item Children’s Metacognitive Questionnaire  (Zhang, 2012), which comprises six dimensions—self-awareness, motivational beliefs, strategy use, planning, monitoring, and regulation—rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Prev ious validation with a sample of 313 participants demonstrat ed satisfactory reliability and validity. To assess strategic transfer, students first designed a learning plan for one subject at the beginning of the program and later created another plan for a different subject, enabling us to examine whether the strategies could be transferred to a new learning context.

This study set out to evaluate whether a KBCP-based curriculum could close the well-documented “knowing– doing” gap by improving students’ metacognitive awareness and the autonomous use of effective learning strategies among primary and lower-secondary lear ners. In direct response to those aims, the present pilot demonstrates that a brief, curriculum-level intervention produced statistically significant gains in metacognitive self-report scores and a substantive rise in the frequency of strategy references i n students’ learning plans. These outcomes indicate that the intervention met its proximal objectives of increasing metacognitive orientation and the explicit articulation of strategy use. One potential explanation for why metacognitive strategies saw the largest gains is that the KBCP framework itself functions as a metacognitive scaffold. Its structure guides learners through a sequence of inherently metacognitive acts: from appraising information to form a Belief, to setting a personal goal via Commitmen t, and finally, to operationalizing that intention through strategic Planning. As prominent Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) frameworks suggest (Zimmerman, 2000), such planning, monitoring, and evaluation processes are the critical hub that translates foretho ught into effective performance. Our curriculum 2187 was engineered to make this process explicit by guiding students through the full KBCP sequence within every module and featuring a dedicated metacognition section. It is therefore plausible that the significant gains in metacognitive skills were not an in cidental outcome, but rather a possible result of an instructional design providing guided, systematic practice in self-regulation. From a practical standpoint, primary and secondary educators could transcend conventional models that solely transmit strategic knowledge, instead integrating all four elements of the KBCP framework within their instructional practices. The notable increas e in metacognitive and resource management strategies in our results provides a strong rationale for prioritizing these two types of strategies in primary and lower-secondary learning strategy instruction, particularly within time-constrained settings. Several limitations merit consideration. The absence of demographic profiling, including prior achievement, socioeconomic background, and prior strategy instruction, constrains generalizability and precludes meaningful individual-difference analyses. The u se of different subjects for pre-and post-intervention plans, though designed to assess transfer, introduces cross-disciplinary variability as an uncontrolled factor. The five-day, time-constrained setting leaves open whether observed gains are durable or  extend to naturalistic classroom contexts. Finally, collecting both outcome measures within the program raises demand characteristic concerns, as students may have responded in accordance with perceived expectations rather than genuine practice. Future work should employ longitudinal designs with richer demographic controls, integrate behavioral measures, and embed the curriculum within regular school schedules to assess scalability.
