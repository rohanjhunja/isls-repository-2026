# Using Markov Chain Analysis to Study the Relations Between Emotions, Cognitive Actions, and Performance in Collaborative Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Collaborative problem-solving (CPS) in STEM+C education involves cognitive coordination and emotional regulation during joint tasks. Prior research has examined discrete affective states in learning environments but less is known about how these emotions evolve over time and affect CPS behavior. This study investigates the temporal dynamics of five emotions—engagement, confusion, boredom, delight, and frustration—using Markov Chain analysis of data from high school pairs building computational models in the C2STEM environment. Emotional transitions aligned with cognitive processes, seen in interaction patterns like PLAY, ADJUST, and BUILD, to analyze affect during modeling. Results show that emotional trajectories closely relate to cognitive actions, including construction, simulation testing, and debugging. Transitions that maintained engagement linked to productive collaboration and stronger performance, while ongoing frustration and boredom indicated disengagement progress.

## Introduction

Self-regulated learning (SRL) and socially shared regulated learning (SSRL) are dynamic processes that integrate cognitive, affective, and metacognitive strategies to support learning goals (Azevedo et al., 2017; Broadbent & Poon, 2015). While SRL occurs in individual learnin g, SSRL emerges in collaborative settings where gro up members collectively regulate their learning processes. In collaborative environments, such as those in STEM+C (Science, Technology, Engineering, Mathematics, and Computing), both individual learning processes (SRL) and collaborative learning processes (SSRL) often coexi st and interact. Cognitive strategies like summariz ing and problem-solving, metacognitive strategies such as planning,  monitoring, and evaluating, and the regulation o f emotions are critical SRL and SSRL processes (Schunk & Zimmerman, 2023; Winne & Hadwin, 2013; Panadero & Järvelä, 2015). The transition of emotions in ind ividual learning is well-explored, showing that when students do not understand a concept, they ofte n experience confusion, which, if not addressed, pr ogresses to frustration and eventually boredom (D’Mello & Graesser, 2012). In contrast, how these emotional trajectories unfol d in a collaborative learning environment is not well understood. Despite their importance, the interaction between SRL and SSRL processes in collaborative STEM+C learning environments remains underexplored. Collaborative learning in STEM+C environments offer s unique challenges and opportunities for understanding learning processes. These environments, such as Collaborative, Computational STEM (C2STEM), integrate the learning of STEM concepts and practic es wit h computational thinking, enabling students to collaborate to solve complex, real-world problems (Hutchins et al., 2020; Cohn et al., 2024). In such settings, students engage in actions such as identifying prob lems, testing solutions, and debugging models,  while also coordinating shared responsibilities. However, stud ents do not always apply these strategies effective ly, often relying on trial-and-error and working individually rather than collaboratively (Viberg et al., 2020). While affect and cognition have been studied extensively in individual learning contexts, less is known about how they interact during collaborative modeling activities (Taub et al. 2021). Emotional transitions offer a valuable lens for understanding how students respond to challenges during collaborative learning. While prior work has modele d affective dynamics in individual contexts (D’Mell o & Graesser, 2012; Akpanoko, Ashwin & Biswas, 2024; As hwin and Guddeti, 2019), collaborative settings add complexity through peer interaction and shared prob lem-solving (Emara et al., 2021). Furthermore, standard emotion detection techniques such as facial express ion analysis or voice-based classificatio n tend to focus on static or isolated affective states and are often limited to individual learners (Ahmed et al., 2023; TS and Biswas, 2024; Fonteles et al., 2024). These approaches overlook the continuous, multim odal, and socially influenced nature of emotional trajectories in CPS tasks. Addr essing this gap requires methods to capture how lea rningcentered emotions evolve over time in response to students’ actions and collaborative interactions. This study investigates the temporal dynamics of emotional transitions and their alignment with students’ system interaction patterns during collaborative modeling in STEM+C. Using multimodal data and Markov Chain analysis, we examine how transitions between five l earning-centered emotions —engagement, confusion, boredom, delight, and frustration—unfold in relation to students’ actions. RQ1: How do emotional transitions during collaborative p roblem-solving relate to system interaction patterns and indicators of task performance?

RQ2: Are specific system interactions associated with transitions between different emotional states? Answering these questions provides insight into how  learning-centered emotions evolve during collaborative tasks and how they reflect students' progress or struggle. This work informs the design of adaptive learning systems that respond to students’ real-time emotional and cognitive needs. Learning environment and data The study took place in high school classrooms in t he Southern United States, where students worked in  pairs to develop computational models of kinematics (the mot ion of objects) using the C2STEM learning environme nt. C2STEM provides a block-based platform for building computational models by combining knowledge of physics and computational thinking (CT). Students build thei r models using domain-specific blocks that focus on initializing and updating variables, such as positi on, velocity, and acceleration. Students use the ex ecutable simulation environment, animations, graphs, and var iable inspection functions to simulate, analyze, and check their models as they construct them by dragging and dropping blocks (shown in Figure 1). Figure 1 C2STEM Learning Environment The study involved 24 10th-grade high school students (12 identifying as femal e, 11 as male, and 1 as non-binary/non-conforming) participating in a six-week kinematics curriculum. The curriculum included instructional and challenge tasks. The 1D challenge  task required students to simulate the motion of a  truck accelerating to a speed limit, cruising, and then slowing down and stopping at a stop sign. The 2D challenge task required students to build a simulation model of a drone delivering packages to two targets. Students were assigned to dyads to work collaborati vely on these tasks. While they were encouraged to share responsibility for completing the task, their  collaboration was not scripted. Data was collected  through multiple modalities. Log data recorded system inter actions, consisting of time-stamped student action records in five categories: BUILD, ADJUST, DRAFT, PLAY, and VI SUALIZE. BUILD actions involved adding new blocks to create an executable model, while ADJUST actions reflected modifications to existing blocks. DRAFT actions involved manipulating blocks not yet connec ted to the executable model, similar to "commenting " in traditional coding. PLAY actions involved simulating the model to observe its behavior, and VISUALIZE actions involved using data tools like graphs or tables to examine simulation results (Ashwin et al., 2024). Video and audio data captured student interactions using Open Broadcaster Software (OBS). Videos were recorded at 30 fps, and audio recordings were transcribed and segmented into approximately 39,000 utterances. Videos were manually annotated for five learning-centered emotions: engagement, delight, confusion, frustration, and boredom. Each annotation also list ed whether the emotion occurred during on-task or off-task behavior. Since students worked in dyads, each student's emotions were annotated separately for each video. This was essential because one student might experience confusion while their partner had already understood the task, leading to distinct emotional states. Averaging the  emotions across students in a dyad would obscure s uch individual differences, making separate annotations critical for accuracy in analysis.

## Methodology

We aligned emotional transitions with students' sys tem interactions using time-synchronized log data. Emotions were observed manually using video, audio, and scre en recordings of students interacting with the C2ST EM environment. The video data, captured through webcams, provided insights into students' facial expressions, tone of voice, and body language. Screen recordings comp lemented this by revealing students' actions within  the learning environment, offering additional context f or their emotional states. All modalities, includin g webcam videos, screen recordings, and log data in CSV file s, were aligned using system timestamps to ensure a ccurate temporal synchronization. Two trained annotators re viewed the combined data to label five learning-centered emotions: engagement, confusion, boredom, delight, and  frustration. These emotions, representing emotiona l engagement, were further categorized as on-task or off-task based on the context, where on-task behavior reflected behavioral engagement (Sinatra et al., 2015; Ashwin and Guddeti, 2020). For instance, a student feeling delighted due to a peer’s humorous remark unrelated to the ta sk was categorized as off-task delight. Although off-task emotions were rare, they were included to better differentiate the nature of emotional engagement during learning. Across the dataset, the annotators achieved a high inter-rater reliability score (Cohen's kappa = 0.93), ind icating substantial agreement.

Cognitive actions and performance through emotional transitions To better understand how emotional transitions reflect cognitive and strategic processes, we examined how action sequences aligned with shifts in emotional states. For instance,  sequences like PLAY → ADD often preceded a transition from Engagement to Confusion, reflecting exploration of new ideas or problem space. Transitions from Confusion to Frustration, frequently associated with sequences like A DD → STAGE, revealed moments of breakdown in model logic or unsuccessful debugging. In contrast, STAG E → CONNECT transit ions, which commonly led to Frustration → Engagement, indicated successful problem r esolution and re-engagement. While detailed performance outcomes are beyond the scope of this short paper, preliminary analysis suggests that transitions that sustain or return to  Engagement —particularly after moments of Confusion or Frustration—tend to align with stronger task performance. In co ntrast, persistent negative transitions, such as repeated Frustration without recovery or sustained Boredom, often correspond with reduced success. The se findings support the idea that emotional transitions not only mirror students’ Problem-solving trajectories but also serve as early signals of emerging success or struggle in collaborative problem-solving tasks.

## Limitations

This study has several limitations. The analysis wa s conducted with a small sample of high school stud ents from the United States, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other cultural or educational contexts. While the study focused on indivi dual-level emotional annotations to preserve the nuance of personal emotional transitions during collaboration, it did not consid er dyadic or group-level emotional dynamics. Additionally, this paper focuses on the role of emotional transitions.  It does not  examine broader contextual factors such as prior knowledge, peer relationships, or task difficulty, which may also shape affective trajectories in coll aborative learning.

## Conclusion and future directions

This study examined the temporal dynamics of emotio nal transitions during collaborative problem-solving in STEM+C contexts using Markov Chain analysis and mul timodal data. Findings showed that transitions such  as Confusion → Engagement and Frustration → En gagement reflected moments of productive recovery, while persistent Boredom or unresolved Frustration signal ed disengagement. These emotional dynamics were clo sely tied to students’ cognitive actions, such as iterat ive model building and simulation testing. Transitions that sustained or returned to Engagement often aligned w ith stronger task performance. These results sugges t that emotional transitions —not just static affect —can be meaningful indicators of problem-solving progress and cognitive engagement. Future work should explore ho w emotional transitions can be used in real-time to guide adaptive scaffolding, particularly in collaborative settings where affective states are influenced by both individual and peer dynamics. Expanding this research to inclu de group-level affect, diverse learner populations, and longitudinal data can offer deeper insights into how emotional and cognitive processes co-evolve during learning and how they can be effectively supported by intelligent systems.
