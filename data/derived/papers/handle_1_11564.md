# Where Do Pre-Service Teachers Look at During the Observation of Classroom Videos? A Matter of Salience

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Understanding where pre-service teachers direct their gaze during classroom video observations offers valuable insights into their visual priorities. Using a mixed-methods approach, we analyzed n=45 pre-service teachers’ gaze distribution through eye-tracking technology and retrospective interviews. They prioritized surface-level cues such as teachers’ tone or students’ sitting posture over deeper content-related elements. This highlights the need for trainings to enhance professional vision, directing their attention beyond salient features for a more comprehensive understanding of classroom interactions.

Pre-service teachers’ professional vision and the role of salience Professional vision–teachers’ ability to notice and reason about releva nt events in the classroom (Seidel & Stürmer, 2014)–is a crucial competence for effective teaching (Blö meke et al., 2015). In-service teachers with extensive classroom experience have well-developed knowledge schemata —mental models that influence what they notice (Gegenfurtner et al.,  2023). These schemata guide their gaze to events  that are crucial for effective teaching. In contrast, less experienced student teachers (pre-service) focus on more overt, visually salient features, showing a more stimuli-based (bottom-up) noticing (Wolff et al., 2021). Here, salience refers to the degree to which certain elements or events catch and hold the observer's attention. In classroom observation, these elements could range from sudden movements, loud noises, gestures, or specific interactions between students and teacher. A salient event naturally draws a teacher’s attention, yet it may overshadow non-salient but more relevant events. Characterizing salient situations in the context of behavioral classroom management, several studies found that pre-service teachers are able to notice students who apparently misbehave (Yamamoto & Imai-Matsumura, 2013). However, while in-service teachers monitor both the whole class dynamics and individual student behavior and learning progress, pre-service teachers primarily focus on student discipl ine (Wolff et al., 2015; 2017). In addition, p re-service teachers primarily notice teachers’ reactiv e intervention strategies rather than preventive strategies, likely due to its salience  (Gabel et al., 2025b; Reupert & Woodcock, 2010; Wolff et al., 2015; 201 7). Characterizing salient situations in the context of instructional classroom management, prior research found that pre-service teachers mainly focus on teachers’ actions such as their omnipresence in the classroom (Gabel et al., 2023). Stahnke and Blömeke (2021) also found  that pre-service teachers mainly focused on the teacher in b oth whole-group and partner-work settings, while in-service teachers directed their gaze in the partner-work setting more towards student learning and group dynamics. We can therefore imply that  it is often challenging for pre-service teachers to notice important, non-salient events when at the same time, salient event s occur (vanden Bogert, 2016; Wolff et al., 2015). For this reason, it is important to understand which events appear salient to preservice teachers and which ones are less salient, i n order to direct their attention to these non-salient events. Therefore, defining which events attract pre-service teachers’ attention is a n important step into understanding how to promote pre-service teachers’ professional vision into a more top-down driven process. Aim and research questions We assume that pre-service teachers' gaze is not random but is influen ced by the inherent salience of several classroom elements  (Wolff et al., 2021). Expanding previous findings, we take a closer look at pre-service teachers’ gaze and their saliency perception in two instructional classroom management situations. RQ1: How do pre-service teachers distribute their gaze in different classroom management situations? RQ2: What characterizes saliency in classroom management situations when no disruptions occur?

## Methods

We conducted a mixed-methods study with n = 45 pre-service teachers in the beginning of their studies (MSemester = 2.2; SD = 1.9), on average 20.7 years old (SD = 2.7). While they watched a classroom video of a language lession in middle school, w e measured their gaze with a stationary Tobii Pro E ye-Tracker Nano (60Hz). After a 9-point calibration, participants were instructed to mark all situations that are relevant to classroom management with a mouse click during the video observation. Directly after video viewing, they explained why they perceived these situations as relevant. This served for data triangulation. For analysis, we chose two sequences of instructional classroom management  (see fig.1). In the first situation  (teacher instruction; 17sec), the teacher is instructing the subsequent partner work standing in front of the class while the students listen attentively. As the material is on the back wall of the classroom, some of the students turn around to the back, but without disturbing the instruction. In the second situation (partner work; 14sec), the students are working in groups. The tea cher is walking around and making sure that the students understand their tasks. ## Findings and discussion

Adressing RQ1, pre-service teachers fixated more often  on the teacher  during her instruction  rather than the students (t (43) = -8.45; p <.00). In the partner work situation, the pre-service teachers fixated on the teacher and the students  more evenly (t (43) = -.90; p =.37) –contrary to previous findings (Stahnke & Blömeke, 2 021). However, we identified different profiles of gaze distributions in each situation: those who focus primarily on the teacher (50% ≥ of all fixations on the teacher), those who focus primarily on the students (50% ≥ of all fixations on the students), and those who showed mixed gaze d istribution. Addressing RQ2, we analyzed their verbal data qualitatively. Teacher-focused participants emphasized the clarity and tone of the teacher's instructions, reflecting their attention to verbal aspects. For example, a participant described that the teach er “has already divided the groups [for the next task]... and [her instruction] is very clear to understand ” (P1). Another participant noticed “the teachers’ polite tone of communication” (P2). In contrast, those with a mixed or student-focused gaze noticed students’ behavioral engagement  and how it related  to the teachers’ instrucion, e.g. “they [the students]  are supposed to look behind to see where it [the material] is, but in the end, it also distract s the students from it [the instruction]” (P3) or ”it seemed to me that when she  [the teacher] was gone, they [the student group on the left] weren't really back on topic and weren't really working on their task, because they were sitting at  the table relatively relaxed.” (P6).

Bringing together previous research, the following picture of pre-service teachers perceived saliency in classroom management situations emerges: In behavioral classroom management situations, the most salient events for pre-service teachers are those that involve student mis behavior and reactive teacher interventions (Gabel et al., 2025b; Reupert & Woodcock, 2010; Wolff et al., 2015; 2017; Yamamoto & Imai-Matsumura, 2013). In instructional classroom management situations, t he most salient events for pre-service teachers are those that involve teachers (Gabel et al., 2023; Stahnke & Blömeke, 2021) and their verbal instructions (e.g. tone and clarity) as well as students’ behavior and rather superficial engagement (e.g. body movements, sitting posture). These findings have significant implications for te acher training programs. By understanding  which events are rather salient for pre-service teachers, training can be tailored to help them develop a more deeper view of classroom dynamics  and to prioritize events that are relevant over those t hat are merely visually prominent. According to the findings, it is further important to differentiate between their different  gaze profiles when fostering their professional vision. Moreover, by understanding how saliency shape their professional vision, they can be made aware of any potential biases and guide them to a more balanced and holistic view of the classroom, especially in diverse and evolving educational envi ronments (Gabel et al., 2025 a). This study also indicates that context plays a crucial role in determining salienc e and that different classroom settings evoke more or less salience-based observations. Future research should explore how specific teach ing styles, teaching s ubject (Stahnke & Friesen, 2023) as well as pre-service teachers goals (Daumiller et al., in press) might influence salience and gaze patterns. Consequently, by delving into the nature of salience, this research aimed to map out where preservice teachers look at during classroom video observation and provided insights into a deepened perspective on the complexities of pre-service teachers’ professional vision and their cognitive processes.
