# Examining Asian American Student Experiences with Racism in STEM

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Asian American students studying STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics) in higher education in the United States are not as well studied in comparison to other minoritized groups. Our research examines how these students navigate through their STEM and university environments and how racism may impact that navigation. Preliminary results show differences in experiences when comparing first-generation Asian American students and adopted Asian American students to other Asian American students.

Systems of oppression, such as white supremacy, have caused inequities in current systems and institutions in the United States. In higher education, many studies have attempted to unpack the impacts of these inequities on racially minoritized students, particularly Latine and African American undergraduates. However,  there is a research gap on how Asian American students experience and navigate systems of oppression. In some STEM (science, technology, engineering, and mathematics) studies, Asian Americans are not positioned as members of underrepresented racial minorities, and are instead grouped with white students (McGee, 2016). However, there are many ethnic groups that fall under the Asian American label which remain underrepresented in STEM. Beyond culture, there are clear differences among each of the ethnicities in terms of socioeconomic status, educational attainment, etc. The monolithic term also upholds the model minority myth, a belief that all Asian Americans are able to be successful. W hile this may seem positive, there are numerous negative effects as a result of this stereotype (Museus & Kiang, 2009). The portrayal of success of Asian Americans as a whole leads to a disregard for students from ethnic groups that have a greater education debt  than other groups; this perceived success also contributes to others’ belief that Asian American students do not require resources and supports (Museus & Kiang, 2009). Another such result of the model minority myth is that the diversity of Asian American student experiences is understudied. Our study addresses two main research questions: (1) How do Asian American students in STEM navigate their campus environments both inside and outside their discipline? (2) To what extent do Asian American students see racism as impacting their navigation? Conceptual frameworks Deficit-based thinking and models in education often work against students despite the intention to “fix” (Weiner, 2006). This type of thinking contributed to inequities for students of color (Valencia, 2010). Yosso (2005)’s Community Cultural Wealth (CCW) framework highlights the knowledge and skills students can offer and bring to the classroom, and combats deficit models, identifying a different type of cultural capital that communities of color possess. The CCW framework is composed of six capitals:  aspirational, familial, linguistic, navigational, resistant, and social capitals.

Additionally, to inform the STEM aspect of our research study, w e also applied  two STEM Identity models to inform our coding process. Collins (2018) breaks down STEM identity development as being impacted by psychological/individual factors, STEM contextual/environmental factors, and cultural factors. Dou & Cian

(2021) defines STEM identity as a combination of interest in STEM, performance-competence, and recognition

in STEM.

We designed interview questions using Yosso (2005)’s C CW framework and situated them within STEM. To recruit students, we sent emails to Asian American student groups at  three institutions in the United States: two doctoral universities (R1 and R2), and a primarily undergraduate institution.  The survey asked for demographic information such as ethnicity, gender, and first-generation status as well as ask ed for the student’s major, their 2391 year, and whether they self-identified as Asian American. We selected students who self-identified as Asian American, currently/formerly studying in a STEM discipline, and have been studying at their campus for more than a semester. Afterwards, we reached out to students from a variety of majors, genders, and ethnicities from the group that fit into the previous criteria. The students were interviewed in-person and via Zoom for a maximum of one hour and were compensated for their participation in the study. In total, we interviewed forty-three students: eleven from the R1 doctoral university, and sixteen each from the R2 doctoral  university and the primarily undergraduate institution.

The coding process was iterative, with meetings occurring every few weeks both to ensure that researchers were on the same page and to update the codebook with new codes and subcodes. The codebook was updated one framework or model at a time. The initial coding process was informed by the CCW framework (Yosso, 2005). We also developed code s for Asian American identity, STEM identity, and sense of belonging. STEM identity codes were informed by Dou & Cian (2021) and Collins (2018)’s frameworks for STEM identity development. Sense of belonging codes used Calabrese Barton & Tan (2019)’s rightful presence framework. The coding of the transcripts was completed through Dedoose; no data analysis has been conducted yet.

## Preliminary results

Thus far in our data analysis, we have begun to unpack areas such as how students view their Asian American identity, how being Asian American impacts students’ STEM identity, and how students use their family, friends and culture, to navigate through their STEM degrees.

As for community cultural wealth, we have thus far found some differences in familial capital between first-generation Asian American students and other Asian American students. For first-generation students, they described how their parents were unable to help them with their studies but still offered support in various ways, such as through food and gifts. Some other students discussed how their parents utilized their connections to help them build a foundation for their future; one student explained that  one of their parents worked in a hospital and used those connections to acquire a volunteer position for them. In the area of Asian American identity, we see differences between how adopted Asian American students and non-adopted Asian American students perceive pressures to study STEM. Non-adopted students are likely to experience pressure to study STEM, through family, society, or both. However, for adopted students, we see that they do not experience that pressure. One student acknowledges that the pressure exists for other Asian students who are not adopted, but they themselves did not experience it as a result  of who they have grown up with: … In terms of like, my Asian American identity, I am adopted by a white mom, a  single white mom. So, I honestly like never really had the parental pressure of like, choosing like the one of like, the trinity of like a doctor, engineer, lawyer, typed out, I know, a lot of like, Asians with my age with Asian parents go through. At the end of our analysis, we hope to find not only themes within CCW unique to Asian American students, but also connections to between themes that could then be explored further in future studies.
