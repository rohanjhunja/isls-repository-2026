# Evaluating the Impact of MathByExample on Student Mathematics Achievement

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study evaluates the impact of MathByExample, an elementary mathematics intervention incorporating worked examples and self-explanation prompts, on fifth-grade student math achievement. Grounded in the worked example effect and self-explanation strategies, MathByExample aims to reduce common misconceptions and improve conceptual understanding in mathematics. We assessed the intervention’s impact using a three-level cluster-randomized control trial involving 1,143 students across 50 schools in Maine. Though the average treatment effect of MathByExample on student math outcomes were not statistically significant, a dosage analysis revealed that the number of attempted MathByExample items significantly predicted mathematics achievement. These findings suggest that consistent engagement with worked examples and self-explanation prompts improved learning outcomes. The results support the value of scalable, low-cost instructional tools rooted in cognitive science and highlight the importance of dosage when implementing such interventions in real-world educational settings.

Mathematics achievement remains a significant challenge in elementary education, with only 41% of 4th-grade students in the United States demonstrating proficiency according to the National Assessment of Educational Progress (U.S. Department of Education, 2019). Traditional elementary mathematics practice activities may perpetuate misconceptions that persist beyond elementary school (Booth, Barbieri, Eyer, & Paré-Blagoev, 2014). As failure-related declines in motivation can further hinder success (Anderman, 2003; Wang & Pomerantz, 2009), mathematical misconceptions should ideally be addressed before middle school, when students intensi fy their learning of algebraic concepts. The MathByExample  intervention, developed through a research-practice partnership between the Strategic Education Research Partnership (SERP) and Temple University, offers a promising approach that incorporates research-based techniques into classroom assignments to improve student learning outcomes while being easily integrated into existing teaching routines.

## Theoretical framework

Learning via worked examples The worked-example principle suggests that students learn more effectively when they spend some practice time studying worked examples rather than solving all problems independently (Sweller, 2006; Sweller & Cooper, 1985). This principle derives from cognitive load theory (Sweller, 1988), which posits that humans' cognitive capacity, or working memory, is limited in what it can process. Traditional problem solving may overload this cognitive capacity because students must generate the processes to solve the  problem while potentially attending to irrelevant features, leaving little room for schema development or learning (Sweller, 2006). Worked examples reduce this extraneous load by allowing students to attend to relevant aspects of the problem to develop schemata for the problem type.

Engaging with worked examples is more efficient than merely solving problems. Students who study worked examples as part of practice need less learning time (Schwonke et al., 2009). Additionally, worked example practice reduces errors and improves both conceptual and procedural mathematics knowledge (Booth et al., 2015; Sweller & Cooper, 1985). The effectiveness of worked examples is enhanced when both correct and incorrect examples are used. Incorrect examples, clearly marked as such, help students confront common mistakes and improve their encoding of important problem features (Booth et al., 2013). Students benefit more from explaining both incorrect and correct solutions compared to explaining correct answers exclusively (Barbieri & Booth, 2016, 2020; Durkin & Rittle-Johnson, 2012; Grosse & Renkl, 2007; Siegler & Chen, 2008). 1163 Learning via self-explanations Self-explanation is a metacognitive strategy that involves explaining information to oneself while reading, solving problems, or attempting to learn (Chi, 2000). High-performing students naturally engage in more self-explanation compared to low-performing students (Chi, Bassock, Lewis, Reimann, & Glaser, 1989); however, students at any performance level who are prompted to self-explain while reading learn more than those who do not self-explain (Chi, De Leeuw, Chiu, & Lavancher, 1994). A meta-analysis of 64 research reports found a mean effect size of g =.55 for self-explanation interventions (Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi, & Winne, 2018). Self-explanation likely improves learning by facilitating the assimilation of new information with previously held knowledge (Chi et al., 1994). When individuals self-explain, they integrate various pieces of knowledge from both instructional material and their own prior knowledge, generate inferences to fill gaps in their knowledge, and make new knowledge and connections explicit (Chi, 2000; Roy & Chi, 2005). Pairing selfexplanation prompts with worked examples has been found to provide additional benefits compared to studying worked examples alone (Rittle-Johnson & Loehr, 2017). When self-explanation prompts are combined with correct examples, students make their prior knowledge explicit and fill existing gaps (Roy & Chi, 2005). When students explain incorrect examples, they receive negative feedback and must confront and reject similar misunderstandings, ultimately improving their conceptual understanding (Booth et al., 2013). MathByExample MathByExample was developed through an iterative process involving feedback from teachers, coaches, and mathematics experts, then tested in a series of studies to determine feasibility and promise for improving student outcomes. The intervention consists of 68 math worksheets for 4th grade and 69 worksheets for 5th grade, all aligned with the Common Core State Standards for Mathematics (CCSSO, 2010). The assignments are organized into blocks covering key mathematics concepts and common misconceptions, including topics such as multiplying multi-digit numbers, place value, decimals, basic operations with fractions, multiplying and dividing fractions, geometry and volume, and ordered pairs.

Each MathByExample assignment includes both correct and incorrect worked examples that alternate between worked examples requiring self-explanation and practice problems to solve. Examples are clearly marked as correct or incorrect and include prompts targeting common misconceptions and errors in 4th and 5th grades. The materials are designed to be flexible and easily integrated into regular classroom routines, usable as "do-now" activities, pretest review, exit tickets, or post-lesson practice. The intervention's design reflects several key principles that make it accessible to educators. First, it requires no costly in-person professional development, instead providing online resources including answer keys, implementation suggestions, sample scope and sequences, explanation of targeted concepts, and videos of mathematics experts describing assignment design and best practices. Second, the materials are available in both print and online formats, with printable PDFs available free of charge and an online version offering learning management functionality. Third, teachers can use whichever assignments cover material currently being addressed in their classroom and in any order, regardless of student population or curriculum scope and sequence. This flexibility allows the intervention to complement rather than replace existing curricula while providing research-based enhancements to traditional practice activities. Present study The present study explored the effect of worked examples and self-explanation through the MathByExample materials on student mathematics performance. We  aim to determine whether the well-established student outcomes found in smaller studies scale-up in a large randomized controlled trial across the state of Maine. Our two primary research questions are as follows:

1. To what extent does MathByExample improve students’ mathematics outcomes?

2. To what extent does the effectiveness of MathByExample depend on intervention dosage?

Design The study design is a three-level cluster-randomized control trial with random assignment occurring at the schoollevel. We used school size, urban-rural classification, demographics, and prior mathematics achievement data to initially match schools, combi ned with a constrained randomization procedure to minimize baseline differences between groups. Matched schools were randomly assigned to either treatment or control. We measured the impact on student mathematics achievement on a proximal assessment after the end of one school year. 1164 All participants in the study consisted of 5th grade mathematics students in Maine. We selected Maine as the site for our study as many students struggle to achieve proficiency in mathematics. Results from the Maine state assessment reveal a large drop-off in performance between 3rd grade and 6th grade, thus there is a great need for interventions to improve math learning in 4th and 5th grade  in Maine. (Maine Department of Education, 2020). The sample contains data from 5th grade students across three cohorts. The analytic sample contains data from 65 teachers (37 treatment, 28 control) representing 50 (28 treatment, 22 control) schools. All 1,143 students (506 treatment, 637 control) with co mplete data from non-attriting classes are included in the analytic sample. Our study team is currently in the process of collecting demographic data from the participating districts. Materials Pre-and post-assessments Students’ prior mathematics knowledge was measured with a pre-assessment containing 14 multiple-choice released 4th-grade PARCC standardized test items; two of the items contained multiple parts yielding a total score of 23. The assessment was delivered via an online platform prior to administration of the MathByExample assignments, Cronbach’s alpha =.67. There was no significant difference between pre-assessment scores for students in the MathByExample condition (M = 13.3, SD = 4.4) and those in the control condition (M = 13.0, SD = 4.7), p =.46, Hedge’s g =.09. The post-assessment was also developed to assess 5th-grade mathematics knowledge at the end of the school year using 14 released PARCC items. This measure was similar to the prior knowledge measure, however, it covered 5th-grade content  (e.g., equivalent fractions, fraction multiplication, analyzing patterns, ordered pairs, and 3D geometry, etc). Two of the items contained multiple parts yielding a total score of 23. The assessment was delivered v ia an online platform at the end of the participating school year and had a Cronbach's alpha of.66. MathByExample dosage MathByExample dosage refers to the amount of the intervention that the students received. Dosage was calculated using student workbook data for each individual student. Coders examined the workbook data and determined the number of total items that a student attempted to solve. An attempt w as defined as any time a student tried to complete the item. This means that students did not necessarily need to correctly solve the item. We investigated dosage as a possible moderator because attempting a higher dosage of MathByExample  items was previou sly shown to positively impact student mathematics performance (McGinn et al., 2023; McGinn et al., 2024). Given the laborious nature of coding student attempts, coding for study years 2 and 3 are ongoing. As such, analysis for research question 2 only includes student dosage data for study year 1. The analytic sample contains data from 11 teachers representing 9 schools. All 143 treatment students with complete data from non-attriting classes are included in the analytic sample.

## Procedure

Each teacher participated in a half-hour, online training which varied by condition. The training reviewed the study purpose and implementation guidelines. At the start of the school year, teachers in both conditions administered the student pre-assessment. Treatment teachers were asked to administer the pre-assessment prior to using any MathByExample materials. Treatment teachers were also asked to assign at least 30 MathByExample assignments for math practice over the course of the school year, while cont rol teachers assigned their business-as-usual activities for math practice over the school year. These assignments were designed to align to how materials and assignments are otherwise used in math classes. On average, treatment teachers assigned 31.5 MathByExample assignments (SD = 8.31). Teachers were asked to administer the MathByExample assignments at times they deemed appropriate during the year. Teachers did not have to assign every assignment, especially if they did not cover that material in their curriculum, but they were asked to use at least 30 of the assignments during the school year. Teachers were given the freedom to assign the assignments in any order and were told to treat the assignments as they would any other assignment in their class. Students were allowed to work alone, in partners, or in groups, at each teacher’s discretion. Each worksheet was designed to take about 10–20 minutes to complete. Each week all teachers completed brief implementation logs to provide information about their m ath classes.

We used a three-level hierarchical linear model to account for the nested structure of the design: students within teachers, and teachers within schools. This analysis allowed us to determine MathByExample’s average impact on the student achievement (as measured by our proximal post-assessment). Fixed effects covariates include: (at 1165 the first level) a measure of prior mathematics achievement (as measured by the proximal pre-assessment), and (at the third level) dummy coded variables for the school blocks. We used this model to address our first research question: To what extent does MathByExample improve students’ mathematics outcomes? We found, on average, that students who used MathByExample  assignments (M = 12.4, SD = 4.76) scored higher on the post-assessment than those in control (M = 11.7, SD = 4.46) condition, but this difference was not statistically significant, F(1, 43.16) = 1.15, p =.28, Hedges’ g =.14. Next, we addressed our second research question: To what extent does the effectiveness of MathByExample depend on intervention dosage. To do this we examined workbook data from students who received MathByExample materials in study year 1. We found that the number of items students attempted to solve in their MathByExample workbooks positively predicted their post-assessment scores, F(1, 48.22) = 26.19, p <.001. See Figure 1.

The present study sought to evaluate the effectiveness of the MathByExample  in improving fifth-grade students’ mathematics performance and to examine whether the dosage of the intervention moderated its impact. Grounded in cognitive learning theories, particularly from worked examples and self-explanations, MathByExample  was designed to align evidence-based instructional techniques with everyday classroom practices. Although the effect of MathByExample on mathematics achievement was not significant, the findings of workbook data in study year 1 show that MathByExample dosage was a significant predictor of mathematics achievement. The positive predictive relationship between MathByExample  dosage and math achievement reinforces the theoretical underpinnings of the worked example and self-explanation effect. Students who interacted with a greater number of worked examples may have had increased exposure to correct problem-solving strategies and opportunities to self-explain errors and correct reasoning. Furthermore, increased engagement with self-explanation prompts may have heightened students’ metacognitive awareness, enabling them to identify, explain, and correct misconceptions. Importantly, this dosage effect aligns with prior findings from McGinn et al. (2023, 2024), which also indicated that engagement with MathByExample materials predicted student mathematical outcomes. However, since the dosage analysis was limited to one study year due to ongoing coding efforts, it will be important for our future work to expand on the dosage analysis to include all participating cohorts.

Our findings offer practical implications for educators. First, the positive association between MathByExample dosage and math achievement  reveals the importance of c onsistent and repeated use of MathByExample materials. Teachers using MathByExample  should be encouraged to integrate the assignments regularly. Second, our study illustrates how an intervention based in cognitive science can be translated into accessible, low-cost, and flexible classroom practices. In sum, the findings from this study contribute to a growing body of literature supporting the use of worked examples and self-explanations as effective instructional strategies in elementary mathematics. While the overall effect of MathByExample was not s ignificant, the positive dosage effect provides evidence that engagement with the intervention materials is linked to improved learning outcomes in math. Future research should further explore dosage thresholds, long-term impacts on student achievement, and the differential effects across student subgroups.
