# Key Elements of a Critical Approach to Financial Literacy

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Financial Literacy is a topic of recent emergence, still subject of debate about its epistemology and disciplinary background. While Latin American governments are implementing National Strategies for Financial Education under the guidance of multilateral institutions, arguments against these initiatives consider that they perpetuate oppressive practices while displacing the focus of the problem from the financial system to individuals. This poster presents a theoretical proposal for a critical approach to financial literacy to address these concerns.

## Introduction

Scholars from disciplines like education, sociology, anthropology, and geography have raised concerns about the dominant paradigm of financial literacy promoted by  multilateral organizations (e.g., OECD, the World Bank, AFI, among others) (Blue & Grootenboer, 2019; Hutte n et al., 2018; Lucey, 2022; Pinto, 2017; Willis, 2 017; Zokaitye, 2017). Financial literacy initiatives hav e been focusing on increasing individuals’ involvem ent with financial system (Prabhakar, 2019), aiming to foster economic growth and reduce economic inequality. However, these expectations remain unmet and, in most countries, the gap between the richest and the poorest is increasing. In this context, several Latin American governmen ts, supported by multilateral organizations are developing and implementing Estrategias Nacionales de Educación Financiera – ENEF. ‘National strategies for financial education’ provide the guidelines for deploying financial education, defining common goals, identifying relevant challenges and obstacles, and establishing  priorities and a coordinated plan at the national level (OCDE/CAF, 2020). These strategies are highly relev ant considering that they configure power and polit ics in financial education, thereby influencing the definition of the why, how, with whom, and for whom of fi nancial literacy initiatives.

The purpose of my poster is to discuss the theoreti cal foundations of a critical approach to financial literacy useful to assess the impact of these national strategies and to direct the attention of these policies to the interests of the most vulnerable populations.

Challenging the dominant paradigm Different voices within the learning sciences field have been calling on scholars to embody the political context in their research questions in recent years. For ex ample, The Politics of Learning Writing Collective (2017) advocates incorporating issues like culture, identi ty, gender, race, and power in the discussion about  learning. Philip and Sengupta (2021) urged scholars to adopt a contrapuntal approach in their designs and theori es for recognizing and challenging the sociopolitical contexts in which learning happens. Finally, Penuel's (2019) idea of infrastructuring acknowledges the need to redesi gn infrastructural components of policymaking to su pport learning sciences innovations that otherwise would not be sustainable or scalable. Research on financial literacy would benefit from the adoption of these approaches in their analyses. Sociocultural perspectives present useful frameworks to study how people learn to manage their finances while paying attention to context and relationships  between individuals. For instance, Esmonde's (2016) six distinctive elements from sociocultural theories to  analyze power provide a solid base to describe the  power asymmetries between people and the financial system. Gutierrez's (2008) sociocritical literacy framework offers an alternative perspective to inspire individuals t o find alternatives to the oppressive practices of the financial system, respond to the poverty caused by capitalism, and design their own futures. Elements of a critical financial literacy framework This proposal of a critical financial literacy framework elaborates on ideas previously discussed by authors from different disciplines. For this discussion, I will introduce three elements from these discussions tha t follow the ideas presented in the previous section.

The first element is the importance of offering imm ediately relevant, age-appropriate, and inclusive content (Pinto, 2017). The design of financial educ ation interventions requires careful consideration of the audience to assess their characteristics, needs, and expectations. One-size-fits-all designs have prov en to be ineffective and are considered perpetrators of systemic inequity (Lucey, 2022). The second element problematizes the excessive focus on individual wealth accumulati on as the principal purpose of financial education (Blue & Grootenboer, 2019). If the goal is to reduce inequa lity, a holistic approach that privileges collectiv e well-being ICLS2022 Proceedings 2076 © ISLS would be more appropriate (Pinto & Coulson, 2011). Finally, the third element relates to financial lit eracy as a response to the influence of financialization (i.e., the presence of finance in all aspects of everyday lives) (Lucey, 2022). Instead of playing a normative role followin g the premises of financialization, it is essential  to facilitate the critical engagement of individuals with the growing sphere of finance (Hutten et al., 2018) enabling them to think about the economic and financial implications of their decisions (Willis, 2017). These elements provide important perspectives to be included in the study and design of new strategies and interventions about financial literacy from a critical approach.

## Discussion and further steps

Considering financial education as a mere tool for increasing financial inclusion is simplistic and doesn’t respond to individuals’ needs or welfare. Financial educati on’s potential to help people make better decisions  and think critically should be acknowledged and privileged instead of considering that people will do better onl y because they hold a financial product. Moreover, implementi ng ENEFs in Latin American countries could provide new opportunities for their citizens if they adopt a critical financial literacy framework. Understanding what people need and expect from financial education and the financial system in general, is indispensable to designing better programs. At the meso-and micro-levels, design-based research (e.g., social design experiments, DBIR) could be helpful to imple ment the ideas presented and assess the effectivene ss of alternative designs of financial education. Anticolonial frameworks could provide practical elements to consider in alternative designs. Future research projects should focus on groups highly affected by inequality and poverty like women, youth, and rural populations in Latin America.
