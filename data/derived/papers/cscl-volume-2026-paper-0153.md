# Exploring Tangible Interfaces for Learning 3D Geometry and Basic Structural Engineering Concepts

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores ways tangible interfaces can be used as a scaffolding tool for children to better understand 3D geometrical concepts during familiar cube structural activities, and bridge building activities. The study found that through embedded visual and audio feedback, six-to twelve-year-old children were able to identify and address gaps in their own learning, self-correct, and increase their understanding of 3D geometric concepts. Children were also able to successfully apply their understanding to basic engineering concepts in structural building activities. Overall, this study helped to identify principles for designing a framework for tangible interfaces able enhance children’s learning experiences of 3D geometry.

Understanding geometric concepts in two and three dimensions can be challenging for children, as traditional representations often fail to effectively convey spatial relationships (Battista, 2007; Sarama & Clements, 2009). While hands-on manipulatives like  geoboards make abstract ideas more tangible, they usually lack built-in scaffolding for independent learning (Resnick, 1998; Manches & Price, 2011). Tangible User Interfaces (TUIs) enhance these tools by incorporating computational feedback, enabling learners to explore concepts through embodied interaction with real-time guidance (Schneider & Blikstein, 2015). However, designing TUIs that connect learning concepts with familiar everyday interactions and real-world applications (e.g., structural engineering) remains a challenge. This gap results in missed opportunities for children to develop independent 2D and 3D geometry skills, emphasizing the need for innovative approaches that integrate hands-on exploration with support for self-directed learning. Draw ing on constructivism, constructionism, sociocultural theory, and embodied cognition, this study examines how tangible interfaces can enhance children’s spatial reasoning and self-monitoring while building stable 3D structures. It addresses two questions: (RQ1) How can tangible interfaces with real-time feedback support children’s self-monitoring and understanding of structural stability? and (RQ2) What design principles foster self-monitoring and self-correction in tangible geometry learning environments? TangiBuild (3D Geometry) Extending our earlier work on TIEboard (Zaidi et al., 2025), a TUI leveraging familiar geoboard-based interactions for 2D geometry, TangiBuild uses Strawbees (Strawbees, 2025) construction materials to support reasoning about structural stability in hands-on 3D construction. A clip-on module built with the M5StickC Plus2 uses an IMU sensor to detect tilt and provides real-time multimodal feedback through a traffic-light LED system, haptic vibration, and short onscreen hints. Rather than giving exact answers, the system offers partial guidance that encourages children to identify weak points and test possible reinforcements themselves. Children used TangiBuild across three activities of increasing complexity: cube construction, bridge building, and a free-play tower challenge.

Across six sessions, children engaged in cube, bridge, and free-play tower  building activities. Each activity started (pre-test) and concluded (post-test) with a brief 4 item question for the children. For the cube and bridge activities, the surveys required children to rank structures from weakest to strongest. Children needed to examine how the bars were placed in the cube or bridge (e.g., horizontally or creating the shape of a triangle) to determine if it is reinforcing the structure to be strong or weak. During the free play tower activity, children were given a preconstructed tower and asked to reinforce the structures by placing the bars. We tracked the number of diagonal and horizontal bars they added before (pre-test) and after the activity(post-test). Bars that were placed diagonally were counted as effective bar placement, and bars that were placed horizontally were counted as ineffective placement. Video recordings and field observations captured how children interpreted feedback, negotiated design choices, and applied reinforcement strategies. Children demonstrated improved understanding of structural stability across activities of increasing complexity. The average percent accuracy score involving children’s cube activity(n=49) from pretest (M=68%, SD=.395) to the posttest (M=98%, SD=.099) significantly increased at t(48) =-5.392., p < 0.001. The average percent accuracy score for the bridge activity from pretest (M =78%, SD=0.36) to the posttest (M= 99%, SD= 0.069) significantly increased at t(52) = -4.285, p < 0.001. In the free play tower activity (n = 58), we assessed children's ability to reinforce a pre-constructed tower with effective bar placements. Each child's effective and ineffective placements were individually calculated. A paired-sample t-test revealed significant learning gains: effective placements increased from an average of 58% (M=58%, SD=0.340) in the pre-test to 85% (M=85%, SD=0.175) in the posttest (t(57) = -7.779, p < 0.001), while ineffective placements decreased from 40% (M=40%, SD=0.335) to 15% (M=15%, SD=0.175) (t(57) = 7.741, p < 0.001). This indicated that children learned to implement effective strategies (See Figure 2a). Among the participants, 29 children increased their total placements from an average of 7 bars pre-test to 10 post-test, improving their effective placements while reducing ineffective ones. In contrast, 23 children who reduced their placements from 13 to 9 also increased their effective bars, suggesting selfcorrection. Even those maintaining the same number (n=6, average of 7) might have self-corrected (See Figure 2a). Observations further showed that children interpreted feedback collaboratively and used it to test alternative solutions. For example, when a child questioned why the lights weren't turning green, peers suggested adding diagonal support and testing a new placement. After this revision, the child realized the structure had stabilized. This illustrates that hands-on manipulation, along with visual and sound feedback, helps students address learning gaps, self-correct, and deepen their understanding of 3D geometric concepts.
