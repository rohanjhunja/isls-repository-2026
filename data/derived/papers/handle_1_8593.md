# The Gateway for Optimizing Design of Micro-Credentials:Higher Education Faculty

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite the growing prevalence of micro-credentials in higher education, there is still no consensus on best design practices. To address this, we identified existing design knowledge held by faculty that designed micro-credentials in a pilot program at a large public research university. We found that faculty may know how to design micro-credentials to address credentialing affordances, but may need support in recognizing and designing for assessment affordances.

## Introduction

A micro-credential is a small learning experience in terms of length of time to complete and the amount of knowledge and skills that are assessed (Abramovich, 2016). Relatedly, a digital badge often accompanies a microcredential as a “visual representation of learning and skills” (Besser & Newby, 2020, p. 484). Because microcredentials are significantly different from traditional credentials (Ghasia et al., 2019), the same processes and principles that educators apply when designing the corresponding learning experience may not be optimal. Simply put, designing a micro-credential for a learning opportunity is different thandesigning the learning experience that accompanies a diploma, certificate, or course grade. This is problematic because, although micro-credentials are increasingly used (Besser & Newby, 2020), there is little research about what are the optimal design processes for different types of micro-credentials. This problem is compounded since we know that a design process influences factors such as student persistence, sense of progress, and perceived learning (Gray & Diloreto 2016; Groening & Binnewies, 2019). A misapplied design process may be the reason for unsuccessful micro-credentials (e.g., low interest, misrepresentation of the credentialed knowledge) and may even hamper learning outcomes. To identify optimal design process for micro-credentials in higher education, we investigated how faculty leveraged the affordances of micro-credentials in their design process. Guiding our investigation were the following exploratory questions: What are faculty perceptions of micro-credentials? How do faculty address these perceptions when they design a micro-credential? We intentionally chose broad, guiding questions to uncover “subjective insights, intuitions, hunches, and ideals” that could serve as a “crucial basis” (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995, pp. 8-10) for building design knowledge for micro-credentials.

Theoretical approach This study was informed by literature that theorizes design as a form of knowledge that is enacted (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995), and by research of design processes where the designer is a mediator of knowledge (McKenney & Schunn, 2018). Therefore, we aimed to reveal design decisions (i.e., knowledge) held by faculty (i.e., mediators of knowledge) that designed micro-credentials.

## Methods

We interviewed five faculty members on their experiences (e.g., practices, processes, decisions) designing a microcredential as part of a pilot program at a large, public research university. Interviews were transcribed, to which we applied a hybrid process of inductive and deductive thematic analysis. We first conducted open coding, and the concept of ‘affordances’ emerged as a core theme. Next, we reviewed our initial codes and identified two main subcategories of affordances: assessment and credentialing. To determine how the identified affordances were considered in the faculty’s design processes, we examined literature to identify design attributes of quality assessments and quality credentials. In our final round of coding, we used the design attributes that were repeatedly referenced (i.e., in at least three peer reviewed articles) as an a priori template (Crabtree & Miller, 1999) to analyze how faculty designed micro-credentials to address assessment and credentialing affordances.

## Findings and conclusion

ICLS2022 Proceedings 1826 © ISLS We found that all of faculty perceived micro-credentials in terms of their assessment and credentialing affordances. When describing their experiences designing a micro-credential, faculty recognized the following qualities of assessment design, as applicable to the design of a micro-credential:  Formative and summative components; Alignment with delivery method; Sustainable assessment; Clarity of outcomes and criteria; Optimized for the unique aspects of the learning opportunity; Equity-oriented. Faculty recognized the following qualities of credential design, as applicable to the design of a micro-credential: Meets needs of employers; Prepares learners to succeed in the workforce; Understood by stakeholders.

However, in designing a micro-credential, faculty addressed the assessment and credential affordances to varying degrees. While the design attributes of quality credentials were ubiquitously applied by the faculty, few attributes of quality assessments were consistently applied. This suggests that for micro-credentials to be optimized, faculty may need additional support to be as consistent with the affordances of assessment in the design process as they are with credentials. This support could come in the form of professional development, where faculty can explore how credentials and assessment are symbiotic.

Our findings also indicate that some critical attributes of assessment design were largely not discussed, or not applied (e.g., aligning with the delivery method, ensuring clarity of outcomes and criteria, establishing equityoriented assessment). Therefore, we suggest that faculty may need support in recognizing and integrating assessment qualities when designing micro-credentials. To address this need, support may include planning sessions that highlight strategies for integrating qualities of good assessment design, facilitating meetings with experienced micro-credential designers for knowledge-sharing, and structuring design tasks through templates and models. We also conclude that further research is needed to refine and build upon the design considerations revealed in this study, and to determine their applicability to micro-credential design practice across other institutions of higher education. More specifically, educational design practice is situated and highly shaped by institutional environment and socio-cultural system (Butler & Schnellert, 2012). We recommend research that extends to other types of higher education institutions, including two-year institutions. Additional research may also identify potential disciplinespecific factors relating to micro-credential design. Faculty design decisions are closely related to “disciplineembedded assumptions and beliefs” (Stark, 1988 p. 221). A micro-credential for one type of knowledge may have different assessment and credential affordances than a micro-credential for another field.
