# The Impact of Conversational Agents on Children’s and Parents’ Engagement During Shared Reading

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Engagement is an important aspect of parent-child shared reading interactions. However, little is known about the nature of engagement patterns while parent-child dyads engage with AI-powered CAs in at-home reading contexts. In this study, we analyze diver se observable patterns of engagement during shared reading. We examine the overall impact of an artificially intelligent conversational agent (CA) that augments parent-child reading interactions by observing and analyzing engagement patterns betw een parents and children as they read together.

Interactive shared book reading promotes preschool-aged children’s early literacy and future reading comprehension (Grøver et al., 2019; Mol & Bus, 2011). Conversational agents (CAs), a type of AI-powered digital technology that supports shared reading through real-time prompts, have the potential to enrich parent-child interactions around print books (Leech et al., 2025; Smeets & Bus, 2012). For effective shared book reading, both the parent and child need to be engaged (Lingwood et al., 2023; Oertel et al., 2021). Key indicators of children’s engagement during reading are reflected in their emotional tone, nonverbal behaviors (e.g., pointing to the book or visually attending to it), and verbal attention (e.g., questions, answers, and comments), and their involvement and active participation (Fredricks et al., 2004; Strouse & Ganea, 2017). Engagement functions as a central mechanism in shared reading, shaping the quality of parent-child interaction and supporting learning and language development (Hirsh-Pasek et al., 2015; Strouse & Ganea, 2017). While prior studies have explored engagement during shared reading with CAs  in lab settings, showing increased child participation and interaction (Xu & Warschauer, 2020; Xu et al., 2021), there is limited understanding of how AI-powered CAs impact at-home reading interventions and engagement patterns over time. This study examines how AI-powered conversational agents shape behavioral engagement patterns in parent–child dyads during shared reading in at-home contexts over a four-to five-week period. The intervention group engaged in shared reading with a CA, whereas the control group engaged in traditional shared reading without the CA. This study investigates (RQ1) whether children and parents in the intervention group exhibit greater increases in engagement from Session 1 to Session 2 compared to those in the control group, and (RQ2) whether children's and parents' engagement differs between traditional shared reading and CA-assisted shared reading within the intervention group during Session 1.

The study included forty children (20 female, 20 male) between the ages of four and five years old who participated with a parent (38 female, 2 male). Children were predominantly White (n=24), with smaller representations of African American (n=7), Asian (n=4), Hispanic (n=4), and mixed race (n=1). Eligibility criteria included having a child in the age range (4;0 to 5;11), an annual household income of less than $75,000 in the previous year, speaking English at least 75% of the time at home, and having access to an Android smartphone and a reliable home internet. The average annual household income for participating families was $51,167 USD (SD = $17,698), with a median of $45,000 USD.

Income was included as a proxy for socioeconomic status, which has been shown to influence variability in parent–child interaction and conversation.

## Procedure

Participants from both conditions completed two 45-minute remote sessions over a four-to five-week period, which included at-home readings between sessions. Participants were randomly assigned to two conditions: the intervention group (n = 20) and the control group (n = 20). In Session 1, dyads in both groups first engaged in a traditional shared reading of The Little Red Fort (Brenda Maier) without the CA. The intervention group then completed a second book reading, How to Find an Elephant  (Kate Banks), with the CA. The control group did 749 not engage in CA-assisted reading in Session 1. In Session 2, conducted approximately five weeks after Session 1, dyads in both groups completed both a traditional reading and a CA-assisted reading interaction. The app consisted of an AI voice-activated reading companion that listens to parent-child reading interactions and provides prompts to support shared reading. The system used voice-recognition technology to detect predefined marker words embedded in the text, defined as specific target words or phrases from the book that, when spoken aloud during reading, triggered the app’s virtual character to produce a chime and provide a prompt tailored to the book’s content and context (e.g., ‘What would you need to find an elephant?’). Both CAassisted and traditional reading interactions were video-recorded for later transcription and coding. Measure To analyze children’s and parents’ behaviors, the shared book reading videos were trimmed into 30-second intervals and coded using ELAN (Version 7.0; Max Planck Institute for Psycholinguistics, 2025). Two research assistants coded each 30-second interval for four dimensions of child engagement (active participation, availability for reading, affective state, child involvement), as well as parent involvement, which captured the parent’s engagement with and scaffolding of the child during shared reading. Scores ranged from 0 to 4, where 0 indicates the lowest level of engagement and 4 the highest. Each recording captures the parent, the child, and the book. If the child or parent went off camera, the research assistants coded the interval as 0. The coding sche me used in this study was adapted from Strouse and Ganea (2017), incorporating elements from the Leuven Involvement Scale for Young Children (Laevers, 1994). Inter-rater reliability was established on 50% of the sample between the two raters with substantial agreement, κ =.74 (Active Participation), κ =.71 (Affective State), κ =.72 (Child Involvement), κ =.73 (Parent Involvement), κ =.69 (Availability for Reading), all p <.01. The remaining data were then coded independently by the two raters.

## Preliminary results

Session 1 coding is complete, and Session 2 coding is currently in progress. Preliminary descriptive analyses for Session 1 indicate that children’s active participation was higher in the CA-assisted reading (M = 1.52) than in traditional reading (M = 0.95). Affective state showed a similar pattern (M = 2.35 vs. 1.96), and child involvement (M = 1.94 vs. 1.29) and parent involvement (M = 1.83 vs. 1.07) were also higher in the CA condition. In contrast, visual attention was higher during traditional reading (M = 2.90) thanduring CA-assisted reading (M = 2.17). Paired-samples t-tests indicated that affective state, active participation, child involvement, and parent involvement were significantly higher in the CA condition, whereas visual attention was higher in the traditional condition (ps <.05). Upon completion of Session 2 coding, we will conduct within-subject and between-condition analyses to examine engagement patterns across conditions and changes from Session 1 to Session 2. Preliminary findings indicate that CA-assisted shared reading was associated with higher children’s active participation, affective engagement, and involvement, as well as greater parent involvement, compared to traditional shared reading. In contrast, children demonstrated higher visual attention during traditional reading. Prior work suggests that  CAs can scaffold interactive reading exchanges, and earlier work with this CA found higher parent-child conversation during CA-supported reading (Xu et al., 2021; Xu & Warschauer, 2020; Leech et al., 2025). A strength of this design is the within-subjects comparison, which provides stronger evidence for the findings, as the same children showed different patterns of engagement depending on the presence of the CA.

## Implications

We identified multiple areas for future research.  While our preliminary findings suggest a positive  relation between CA-assisted reading and engagement, longitudinal studies are needed to assess  long-term effects. As shared reading is tied to later academic success (Grøver et al., 2019; Mol & Bus, 2011), this study suggests opportunities for CAs to be extended to formal learning settings, such as pre-school settings, to further assess engagement patterns, particularly in shared reading between teachers and students. Careful, ethical, and equitable considerations need to be taken when designing these tools to avoid bias, discrimination, or privacy violations. This study provides evidence that affordable, accessible, and effective tools that align with evidence-based literacy practices can be implemented across different learning settings, and these tools can benefit and reach children from diverse backgrounds. These data can be used to inform educational interventions and focus on maintaining social interaction between adults and children, all while supporting children’s language development, emergent literacy skills, and later academic success.
