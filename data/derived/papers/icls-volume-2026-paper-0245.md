# Learning Lessons and Their Impact on Student Success in First-Year Biology Classes

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The first-year introductory biology sequence provides a crucial foundation for students' careers. Some groups, such as first-generation college students (FGCS), lag behind their non-first-generation peers in these introductory courses. One explanation is that FGCS are not given the tools or background to succeed in college-level coursework. In addition, students regardless of first-generation status tend to underutilize evidence-based study strategies. Here, we presented students with a series of asynchronously delivered online lessons for the purpose of teaching students to use evidence-based strategies in their introductory biology classes. Our initial results suggested that learning lessons helped students learn necessary study skills to succeed in their biology courses. Overall, our research aims to support FGCS and other underrepresented students, potentially helping to level the educational playing field in introductory biology.

Keywords: STEM education, equity, course-based intervention, first-generation college student Introductory science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) courses are pivotal entry points to STEM degrees and STEM occupations (Hatfield et al., 2022).  Experts project that by 2031 the number of STEM jobs will increase by 10.8% to 10.94 million jobs (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2022). With a growing demand for STEM jobs, there will be a concurrent need for STEM majors completing their degrees. However, 48% of students who enter a STEM program will leave that program within 6 years, commonly referred to as the “leaky pipeline” (Chen et al, 2013). This is perpetuated by significant disparities in achievement between disadvantaged groups in science (Fry et al. 2021). Fo r example, first-generation college students (FGCS; where neither parent has completed a 4-year college degree) (U.S Department of Education, 2011), tend to underachieve in university classes, scoring lower grade point averages (GPA) compared to non-FGCS, where either parent had completed a 4-year college degree (Marco-Bujosa, et. al, 2024). This is a well-documented phenomenon across multiple studies and institutions (Chen & Carroll, 2005; Lopèz et al, 2023; Strayhorn, 2006). Disadvantaged students like FGCS are also more likely to face  social, language, cultural, and academic preparedness barriers to succeed  in college level coursework (Stebleton & Soria, 2012). Researchers  found that underperformance by FGCS is not due to a lack of “talent” (Salehi et al., 2019) but rather differing levels of college preparedness. This gap in college readiness reflects the sociocultural inequities that exist in the U.S. (Florence, 2017). The lack of preparedness in these student populations may be attributed to a few barriers: a lack of access to quality education pre-college, differing amounts of financial stability, and mental health stigmas leading to feelings of imposter syndrome (Weisen, 2024). Many FGCS have reported feeling behind in their first semester STEM classes. In addition, some students feel ostracized when hearing their professors state that content should be review —when  it might be for students who went to better high schools and/or had  better opportunities prior to college (Marco-Bujosa, et al., 2024).

In 2020, Carl Weiman proposed that first semester university science classes could contribute to these inequities—stating that physics instruction and the curriculum itself could be supporting gaps that began prior to college. To help close the gap between FGCS, and their peers, learning modules that teach evidence-based strategies to improve class performance were developed  and implemented in introductory biology classes. These strategies include techniques like spaced and retrieval practice and metacognition and/or self-directed learning, which are well supported by the literature (Carpenter et al., 2022). Instruction on metacognitive skills can impact student learning, especially in students with low academic performance (Dang et al., 2018). This is particularly notable because first semester university science  classes come at a critical time for STEM students. First semester  university GPA is the preeminent predictor for retention and graduation within STEM majors (Dika et al., 2016). We hypothesize that direct instruction on how to study effectively can not only improve student performance but may also help close achievement gaps between FGCS and non-FGCS, allowing 1707 for more equitable outcomes and a more diverse STEM workforce. The objective of our study is to discover the effects of learning modules on course performance across students and specifically between FGCS and non-firstgeneration students. Participants (n=4 63) were undergraduate students enrolled at a large public research university in the rocky mountain region of the United States. All students were enrolled in a first semester general biology course at the university which covered topics such as m acromolecules, cell structure, the central dogma of biology, and gene expression. The course contained a mix of b iology majors and non-majors. Participation in the learning modules was optional, and students received extra credit for participating. Data was collected in the Fall 2024 semester.  Students first completed a pre-class survey. The survey included a demographics section which asked students their gender, Pell  grant status  (federal grant given to undergraduate students with demonstrated financial need), first-generation status, major, extracurricular commitments, and if they have  taken Advanced Placement /International Baccalaureate Biology in high school. Final grades were also collected for students who consented along with which and how many learning modules were completed.

Over the course of the semester there were six learning modules for students to complete (Table 1). The learning modules were spread across the semester and aligned with milestones in the course, such as completing the metacognition module after their first exam.

Each learning module was accompanied by  a short worksheet to walk the student through what they learned and how to apply it into their study habits. At the end of the semester, average mid-term scores and final course scores were obtained for students who consented to participate in research.  Protections were in place to avoid perceived coercion and Institutional Review Board oversight was in place. 1708 Statistical analysis was performed using R Studio. First, we calculated descriptive statistics such as mean and standard deviation. Then to determine the significance of the trends that appeared between module completion, first generation status and final grade, we used Welch’s t-tests (West, 2021). To test the impact of the learning modules on student performance we first examined the differences in final grade between students who were low completers (completed 0-2 learning lessons) and high completers (completed 3+). A total of 362 students were low completers, and 101 students were high completers. Using  a Welch’s t-test, we compared the means between low completers (M = 87.9, SD = 8.7), and high completers (M = 90.4, SD = 7.5). High completers were found to have higher grades at the end of the semester than low completers t(181) = -2.9, p <.01.

To determine if module completion affected FGCS and non-first-generation students differently, we conducted Welch’s t-tests. First, we compared the final grades of 88 non-first-generation high completers (M = 90.9, SD = 7.6) to 310 non-first-generation low completers (M = 88.1, SD = 8.7). We also compared 49 FGCS low completers (M = 86.4, SD = 8.5) and 13 FGCS high completers (M = 87.2 SD = 6.4). We found that nonfirst-generation high completers obtained significantly better final grades than both the non-first-generation low completers, t(158) = -2.9, p <.01 and the FGCS low completers, t(90) = -3.1, p <.01 (Figure 1). We observed no statistically significant difference between FGCS high and low completers t (25) = -0.4, p >.05 (Figure 1). The average final grade for non-first-generation high completers (M = 90.9, SD = 7.6) was higher than the average final grade for the 13 first-generation high completers (M = 87.2, SD = 6.4), and Welch’s t-test found the difference to be marginally significant t(17) = -1.9, p =.0717 (Figure 1). To work towards equity and close achievement gaps between disadvantaged groups early in their undergraduate STEM education requires interventions that meet the needs of the students. We feel that it is critical that this intervention occurs early in their undergraduate education to ensure  a strong foundation for success throughout their biology studies. The goal of our work was to improve students’ ability to learn through direct instruction on evidence-based study strategies within the discipline of biology. Here we tested to see if async hronously delivered online learning modules couched within the specific disciplinary context would help students, and in particular, close achievement gaps between FGCS and non-FGCS students. We tested the relationship between learning module completion, first-generation status, and academic performance. Previous research into the lower performance of FGCS is supported by the marginal significance that we found between first-generation status and final grade. We also observed that higher module completion is correlated with a higher final grade when analyzing the whole study population. When separated into first-generation and non-first-generation groups, we found that learning module completion was correlated with better 1709 class performance in the non-FGCS (Figure 1). High completer FGCS tended to have higher final course grades, however, this increase in performance was not shown to be statistically significant (Figure 1). These preliminary results highlight the potential of the learning modules as pedagogical tools for incoming university biology students. Utilization of our learning modules could be an important step toward leveling the playing field in the introductory classes by improving the ability for students to learn effectively. The asynchronous design of the modules allows for easy integration into other introductory biology classes beyond our university. Overall, this work could inform the optimal method of instruction to teach undergraduate students how to learn prepare for class.

## Limitations

In our study, we had an unbalanced sample size with few FGCS participants. Consequently, our results may not have been representative of the general FGCS student population. Our data is reflective of the university’s student population where there is a FGCS percentage of 14.8%, which is lower than the national average (Bahrami, 2024). Another limitation of the study is the use of the final grade as an indicator of improvement between learning module groups. As the modules were completed across the semester, strategies learned at the end of the semester would not have been applied to the first midterm. There is also the possible influence of self-selection bias where high performing students would be more likely to complete learning modules. In future semesters, learning module completion will be a required task for all students. We also noted tha t the overall grades in the class were universally high with an average final grade of 88.4% and standard deviation of 8.5%. This lack of variance creates a ceiling e ffect which limits  our ability to understand the full effects of the learning modules. It may also be important to look at performance in other introductory biology classes, such as the introduction to molecular biology class, where there is more variability in final grade and based on our unpublished observations, a much wider achievement gap between FGCS and non-first-generation students.

In conclusion, we sought to determine how our course-based intervention helped students, and particularly FGCS, to succeed in an introductory biology course. While our initial results are not shown to extend  specifically to FGCS populations, we found that there was a statistically significant improvement in grades amongst the overall student population after receiving direct instruction in the form of a short video and activity, asynchronously delivered via the learning management system. Therefore, our future work will seek to understand how these modules are benefiting the students, and what, if any, impact they have on FGCS. Since FGCS do not always have the same privilege of access to education on study skills and learning tactics, our learning modules could be a way to level the playing field. It may also be that they are beneficial to other disadvantaged or minoritized groups in the sciences, which we will explore in future work. We plan to collect more data to combine across multiple semesters and expand our pool to include students at institutions that have a higher percentage of FGCS. Learning module completion will also be made mandatory to avoid self-selection bias. We also plan to utilize surveys like the CLASS-BIO (Semsar et al., 2011) and MSLQ (Pintrich et al., 1993) to assess exactly how these modules influence students’ attitudes and motivations towards biology as well as their study habits. This will help us understand how the learning modules are impacting the students.
