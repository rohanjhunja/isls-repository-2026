# Navigating AI Ethics: Pre-service Teachers’ Reflexivity of Ethical Knowledge about Artificial Intelligence

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This field study within an authentic teacher education lecture explores how pre-service teachers (N = 274) process diverse knowledge, such as technological and ethical knowledge about artificial intelligence. We found that instructional support targeting cognitive processing significantly supports acquiring and applying AI-related knowledge. To deepen our understanding of processing different knowledge domains, we will assess teachers’ navigation behaviors and knowledge acquisition patterns within epistemic network analysis, offering insights into effective instructional design.

## Introduction

Integrating artificial intelligence (AI) technologies in education introduces a spectrum of ethical and pedagogical challenges. As emerging professionals, pre-service teachers must develop an intricate understanding of AI encompassing technological mechanisms and the broader ethical-societal implications. This dual-layered understanding is vital for making informed decision s that prepare students for a world deeply influenc ed by AI technologies according to societal values (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; OECD, 2021). Knowledge integration, however, presents significant difficulties, especially for novices to complex and often abstract knowledge concepts about AI (Lehmann et al., 2019). Research has consistently demonstrated that scaffolding interventions — such as utility-value interventions and worked examples — can effectively enhance knowledge acquisition (Backfisch et al., 2024; Barb ieri et al., 2023). Whether and how the integration  of these technological and ethical knowledge components into teachers’ professional knowledge can be fostered is an open question. To support knowledge integration, utility-value interventions and examples have proven to be effective (Backfisch et al., 2024). To this end, we investigated whether  a  utility-value intervention (motivational intervention) and/ or a worked  example (cognitive intervention) differently contri bute to pre-service teachers’ knowledge integration and the acquisition of integrated knowledge. Therefore, we implemented an online learning environment comprising multiple hyper-texts that dealt with technological and  ethical-societal aspects of AI in education. Based on previous research (e.g., Barbieri et al., 2023; Canning et al., 2018), we hypothesized that preservice teachers who received a utility-value intervention or a worked example should demon strate higher levels of knowledge integration and achieve higher test scores than those without any support. Furthermore, we explored if there is a synergistic interaction effect if students received a utility-value intervention and a worked example. Our study, therefore, investigates the impact of th ese interventions in a naturalistic educational set ting, with a special focus on how they foster reflexivity  and ethical deliberation among pre-service teachers.  By combining quantitative outcome measures with qualit ative insights, we aim to provide a comprehensive a ccount of knowledge integration in authentic learning environments.

## Participants and design

To examine how future educators engage with AI knowledge in a realistic educational context, our field study was embedded in an authentic university lecture we taught in the Master of Education, dealing with digital media in education. The study comprised N = 274 students (69% female, Mage = 24.58, SDage = 2.2 0, Msemester = 9.13, SDsemester = 2.84).

Our study was based on a 2×2 between-subjects design, crossing the factors utility-value interventions (yes, no) and worked examples (yes, no).

Design and interventions Our 2×2 between-subjects design explored the effects of utility-value interventions (motivational support) and worked examples (cognitive support) on knowledge integration. Each intervention was embedded within an online hypertext learning environment  (duration of about 45 minutes), linking curated resources on the technological with the  ethical dimensions of AI. The utility-value intervention was implemented as an expert video, accompanied by a writing task designed to help stud ents elaborate on the video’s content before engagi ng with the learning environment (see Backfisch et al., 2024). The worked example was presented after the learning phase and provided a step-by-step demonstration of how to apply the acquired kno wledge. P re-service teachers navigated through these resources while their navigation behavior within the learning environment was captured by log data for subsequent analysis.

## Data collection

To complement our quantitative measures  within our pre-and post-knowledge tests, we collected log data and written essays from pre-service teachers. The essays are answers to authentic real-life ethical dilemmas outlined in open-ended questions in the posttest  addressing integrated knowledge with technological and ethical-societal components. Two independent raters coded the responses using a predefined coding scheme (Brisson et al., 2020).

## Results

To test our hypotheses, we realized analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) with the learning outcome as t he dependent variable.  The analyses showed a significant main effect of worked  examples on both basic knowledge (p =.014) and integrated knowledge  (p  =.042), indicating that pre-service teachers who received a worked example had higher test scores of basic and integrated knowledge than pre-service teachers who did not receive the worked example. The utility-value intervention showed no significant effect on basic and integrated knowledge (p >.203). There were also no interaction effects. Further analys es indicated that deeper processing operationalized by higher test scores in the writing tasks of the utility-value intervention (ICC >.88) led to higher performance in far transfer (p  <.001). As this is an ongoing study, our qualitative data will be analyzed until the conference.

## Discussion

We demonstrated that worked examples, rather than u tility-value interventions, effectively supported  the integration of ethical and technological knowledge about AI. Often treated separately, these two domains pose unique challenges for pre-service teachers to reconcile within educational settings. Our results highlight that cognitive support through worked examples offers es sential structured guidance, enabling novices to br idge and integrate these complex knowledge components. Howev er, additional support mechanisms may be required to fully harness the potential of utility-value interventions in promoting comprehensive AI l iteracy–both in future research and teacher education. The insights into effective instructional support are to be systematically integrated into teacher education curricula and translated int o practical implementation within the design and development of course formats for pre-service teachers.  A limitation of the present study is its relatively  short time frame, which may not fully capture the long-term development of integrated AI literacy. Future research should address this limitation by collecting and analyzing longitudinal data over an extended period, allowing for deeper insights into the sustained impact of our instructional interventions.  Moving forward, we will conduct further process analyses of the log data within an epistemic network analysis (Backfisch et al., 2024), which will be presented in a detailed discussion at the upcoming conference po ster session. This ongoing analysis  will advance our understanding of effective strategies for fostering reflexive AI literacy, equipping future teachers for responsible engagement in today’s and tomorrow’s digital classrooms.
