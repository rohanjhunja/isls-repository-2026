# University Students’ Conceptions of Reflective Learning in Education Courses

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Reflective learning experience has been considered crucial in higher education. Accordingly, most programs have embedded reflective learning experiences into the curriculum. However, there is a dearth of research on students’ conceptions of reflective learning and how they perceive the value of their experience. This study aimed to explore how university students taking education courses understand reflective learning. A qualitative analysis of data collected from 12 student interviews and 161 open-ended survey responses revealed four different ways of understanding reflective learning: engaging with content, evaluating learning, relating learning to other experiences, and reframing understandings and perspectives. The findings have implications for design of learning environments for reflective learning.

## Introduction

Reflective learning experience has been considered crucial because of its importance both in formal and informal learning environments. In formal learning environme nts, reflective learning experiences provide studen ts with opportunities to engage in high quality dialogue (B rockbank et al., 2002; Dukewich & Vossen, 2015), de velop learner agency (Lin & Schwartz, 2003), and examine the effectiveness of their learning strategies (Ert mer & Newby, 1996). Reflection has also the potential to cultivate lifelong and transformative learning that go beyond the context of classrooms (Brockbank et al., 2002; Tan, 2021). In informal learning environments such as social media and the Internet, for instance, students are often presented with a great deal of misinformation  and disinformation. Thus, developing reflective skills and mindset helps them question and challenge the source and validity of the information rather than readily con sume it. In the context of workplace learning, refl ection can serve as a tool for dealing with uncertain and complex issues (Chan & Lee, 2021) and coping with the demands of multiple expectations (Ryan & Barton, 2020). It is also argued that reflection is central in the co ncept of graduateness as it underpins the skills and attributes that are valued by employers (Wharton, 2017). Because of the above-mentioned potential benefits, there has been widespread calls for integrating reflective learning into courses and experiences in  higher education. As a result, reflection has beco me a requirement of many university courses (Ertmer & Newby, 1996; Quinton & Smallbone, 2010; Rivers et al., 2014). However, little is understood about how to develop reflective skills (Clara, 2015; Ertmer & Newby, 1996). Besides, reports indicate that students’ engagement in refle ctive learning is minimal (Mair, 2012; Power, 2016). Mair

(2012) argued that the value of reflective learning in higher education is seldom realized as students’ engagement

is very unsatisfactory.

Students’ minimal engagement in reflective learning can be attributed to the various elements of learning environments. Among these elements is students’ own conceptions of what reflective learning entails. Research on students’ conceptions of learning has generally gained considerable attention because of its association with learning strategies, approaches to learning, and ef fective learning (Loyens et al., 2009). Conceptions  are often adopted based on perceived learning contexts (Trigw ell & Ashwin, 2006) and develop and change through educational experience (Entwistle & Peterson, 2004; Loyens et al., 2009). Thus, purposes and underlying values of reflective learning that are conveyed either implicitly or explicitly by instructors or their educational programs determine the forms and focuses of reflection (Ecclestone, 1996). Understanding students’ conceptions provides insights about the nature of learning environments designed to foster reflective learning and student engagement. Research on reflective learning employed an array of approaches to examine students’ reflective learning experiences and/or develop frameworks for the purpose. Some researchers have relied on learning artifacts such as journals, portfolios, and reflective assignments to gauge levels of students’ reflection (e.g., Fund et al., 2002; Kember et al., 2008). Others employed structured su rveys and conducted quantitative analysis to determ ine students’ reflective skills and to compare the leve ls across certain demographic variables (e.g., Kemb er et al., 2000). Both approaches provide insights into the na ture and level of students’ reflective skills and e xperiences. However, more evidence of and insights into the why and how of students’ approaches to reflective tasks can be obtained by looking into their conceptions of what reflective learning entails. This paper therefore a imed to explore students’ conceptions of reflective learning. The study was guided by the research question, “ What are students’ conceptions of reflective learning in the context of education courses?” ICLS2022 Proceedings 115 © ISLS Conceptions of Reflective learning Conceptions refer to the different ways people understand a particular phenomenon (Marton & Pong, 2005) that mediate their reactions to situations involving the phenomenon (Pratt, 1992).  In the literature, there exist a variety of conceptualizations of reflective learning ranging from a simple awareness of events and thoughts to  critique and transformation of assumptions and hegemonic pra ctices. Dewey’s (1933) conceptualization of reflect ive learning relates to a rational and systematic process of problem solving. Building on Dewey’s work, Clara (2015) emphasized the notion of transformation of an incoherent situation to a coherent one as central to the concept of reflection. In his transformative view of learning, Merizow (1991) understood reflection as critique of assumptions and taken-for-granted beliefs. Others considered self-examination and inquiry as central in the reflective learning process (Boyd & Fales,1983; Mortari, 2012).

These diverse interpretations of reflective learning allow educators to adopt or develop perspectives that align with the purposes they pursue in their courses. These perspectives have the potential to influence students’ conceptions of reflective learning. This is because conceptions are situational and exposure to different learning contexts impacts how students adopt and develop the m (Smith & Blake, 2009). For instance, research sho wed that constructivist conceptions of learning were reflected by students who experienced a learner-centered learning environment compared to students who were enrolled in a conventional curriculum (Loyens et al., 2009). Students’ conceptions, which are adopted based on p erceived learning contexts, would influence their approaches to and engagement in the task (Trigwell & Ashwin, 2006). Cognizant of such relationships, we believe that efforts to foster reflective learning in highe r education would benefit from an understanding of students’ conceptions. This paper therefore aimed to explore the different ways students understand reflective learning in the context of education courses.

## Method

This is part of a larger study on perspectives and practices of reflective learning in higher education. The purpose of this paper is to understand and describe students’ conceptions of reflective learning using data from interviews and open-ended survey at a western Canadian univers ity. To achieve our purpose, we adopted a phenomenographic research approach. At the heart of phenomenographic research is participants’ conceptions of the phenomena of interest. The underlying theoretical assumption is that there are only a limited number of ways of understanding a given phenomenon (Entwistle, 199 7; Richardson, 1999; Tight, 2016). In this study, t he phenomenon of interest is students’ conceptions of reflective learning. Data were collected from students in the Faculty of  Education in 2020. Although reflective learning is expected to be incorporated in different programs and disciplines, we focused on students taking education courses because they are expected to have a more nuanced view of reflective learning. Thus, education courses that were scheduled to be offered in the academic year were f irst identified. Then, the brief course description s that were posted online to assist student enrollment were examined pertaining to inclusion of reflective learning experiences. Accordingly, 22 courses that included reflection in the course descriptions, learning outcomes, and/or assessment strategies were selected as sources of data. Such preliminary assessment was important to ensure that participants who had the experiences of the phenomena of interes t are included which is considered important in phenomenographic studies (González, 2011). Then, students were accessed via their professors and invit ed to complete a survey and to volunteer for interview.

A total of 274 students participated in the survey. Students were comprised from undergraduate, graduate and professional development programs. Graduate stu dents are MA/MSc/MEd students who were enrolled in different programs such as mathematics education, curriculum studies, educational leadership, and equity studies in education. Undergraduate students are comprised from different faculties of the university, but the y were all taking education courses. Students of the professional development program (PDP) are teacher education students who were admitted to the program after completing a bachelor’s degree in different disciplinary areas and were preparing to become elementary or secondary school teachers. Data were collected through interview and open-ende d survey. Interviewing is considered in phenomenography a primary method to understand part icipants’ conceptions of phenomena (Åkerlind, 2005; Marton, 1986) because of its capability for eliciting detailed and rich account of participants’ lifeworld (Ashworth & Lucas, 2000). We employed a semi-structured approach to interviewing that involved improvising quest ions and probes when appropriate. The general purpose of  the interview was to explore students’ understandi ng of reflective learning, the kind of opportunities they  were provided to engage in reflective learning and  their perceptions of barriers to engage in the processes.  However, the data used for this study focuses on t heir conceptions of reflective learning. Examples of int erview questions that helped to elicit participants ’ understanding include: How do you describe reflecti ve learning? In what ways do you think you have bee n engaged in reflective learning in this course? How did you go about reflection in the course? How is b eing ICLS2022 Proceedings 116 © ISLS reflective important in higher education? Interviews were conducted both in-person and virtually. Since most of the data were collected during the COVID-19 pandemic, phone and zoom interviews were used depending on the participants’ choice. Among the interviews we conducted for the larger study, the analysis for this paper is based on data from twelve participants. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. The survey included both close-ended and open-ended items that were intended to answer the research questions we formulated in the larger project. However, in this paper we used responses to one open-ended item, i.e., How do you define reflective learning? Among the 274 students who completed the general survey, 161 students responded to this question. Of these, 51.6 % were taking professional development courses, 29. 8% undergraduate courses, and 18.6% graduate courses.

In phenomenography, data analysis is a recursive pr ocess in which transcripts are read several times, pertinent data are identified and segmented, and th en the underlying meanings of the sorted data are c ompared (Åkerlind, 2005; Forster, 2016; Tight, 2016). Accordingly, we sorted, segmented, organized, and examined data from the responses to the open-ended item in the survey and the interview transcripts. Then, we read the transcripts multiple times to explore similarities and differen ces in participants’ conceptions. The process resul ted in the development of categories of descriptions, which are the main outcomes of phenomenographic analysis (Åkerlind, 2005; Entwistle, 1997; Svensson, 1997).

These descriptions imply that reflective learning entails going beyond rote memorization and learning and rather focus on engaging in higher order thinking processes pertaining to the content being learned. When asked about how they understand reflective learning, one interviewee replied, “I think just generally it's not blindly following and consuming the material that we are given but taking time to almost like digest this, and have it make sense in for a personalized understanding of whatever it may be” (GDs22). So, the main facet of this category of conception is that reflection targets content with the aim of developing an in-depth understanding of what they are learning. Reflective learning as evaluating learning process In this category, reflective learning is understood  as looking back to learning goals, strategies, act ivities, and outcomes. This category is different from the previous as reflection mainly targets the “how” than the “what” of learning. The following excerpts from four participants’ responses to the question on what reflective learning is clearly demonstrates this conception.

UGs01: From a student perspective, looking deeply at study habits, strategies, and skills; and motivations for learning. Analyzing these areas and deciding whether they are effective and serving goals, and how improvements can be made in the future.

PDs134: Reflective learning is understanding both how you l earn and the influences that allow you or disallow you to learn.

GRs56: I would think about how engaged I was, what my circumstances were at the time, what factors contributed to or detracted from my learning or non-learning, and the result of those factors on my learning. UGs13: You complete the task and then you reflect on it-how you approached the task, what strategies, what went well etc.

Here, students reflect on learning activities and processes that they were engaged in. An important facet of this conception is that reflection targets not o nly the goals they set, the learning strategies the y chose and employed, or the activities they performed, but also the factors that are associated with the way they approach the tasks. Such a reflection often aims to identify wha t worked well and what did not in the learning proc ess and thereby inform future learning. UGs20’s definition of reflective learning succinctly describes this ai m. The participant defined it as “looking back on your own  learning/studying to think about how it went, how it could have been better, and what you cando to make it better.” Reflective learning as relating learning to past and future experiences In this category, reflective learning is seen as making connections between learning and personal life experiences and anticipated future practices. The connections can be of different kinds wit h different purposes; however, participants viewed it as a key characteristic of reflective learning that makes it distinct from ICLS2022 Proceedings 118 © ISLS non-reflective activities. One of the common types of connection evident from participants’ responses is between course contents and personal experiences. That is, reflective learning is viewed as relating concepts and ideas encountered in the courses to personal life experiences. Reflective learning as relating entails connecting course concepts not only to personal lives but also  to previously learned concepts and anticipated profess ional practice situations. The following excerpt fr om a participant illustrates the importance of making connections between contents over time: Reflective learning (or at least the process of ref lective assignments in this course) combines summation of topics and encourage extension of pres ented ideas and philosophies into previously covered topics/information as well as wh ether the topics/information agree or disagree with personal, currently existing values. (PDs153) Another facet of connection is expressed in terms o f applying learning and/or personal experiences to other situations. Participants illustrated various forms and directions of transfer of learning and experience which include applying concepts or theories learned in courses to practice situations, or future practice; applying personal experiences to the understanding of the course expe rience; or use learning strategies that are found e ffective in future learning. Among these variety of application s, the most common one entails the use of course ba sed knowledge and skill in personal and professional li ves. UGs59’s response to the question of what refle ctive learning is typifies this conception. The participant stated, “reflective learning is applying what you learn in class to your daily life. Applying it can take the form of thinking about the material, discussing it with someone else or creating beneficial activities for your family's development.” The notion of application is not only from contents to personal and professional life but also from per sonal and professional experience to course experie nces. UGs40’s response indicates the bidirectional nature of application between content and personal experiences. The participant stated, “applying own experiences on what is being taught and what things I learn apply to  my own life.” Another participant also described reflective learning as “using past experience to contemplate current issues and guide future actions.” In general, this category is different from the pre vious categories in its focus on examining current learning experiences in relation to past and antici pated future experiences. The purpose could be simp ly to facilitate understanding of what they are learning or consider the relevance of what they are learning by examining its applicability.

Reflective learning as reframing understandings and experiences In this category, reflective learning is considered  as changes in ideas, understandings, practices, or  personal perspectives. Although experiences based on the previous three categories would ultimately lead to such kinds of changes, what differentiates this category of conce ption is its prime focus on the outcomes of the ref lective learning process. As such, it entails acquiring new  ideas pertaining to subject matter content; refram ing prior assumptions and perspectives; and adopting new ways of doing things like learning or teaching. One of the outcomes is questioning personal perspectives and biases. That is, participants understood the outcomes of reflective learning in terms of reframing preconceived ideas and beliefs. One participant, for example, described reflective learning as: “critically think ing of your internalized attitudes, assumptions, be liefs, and experiences and how we can shift our views to better consider the experiences and contexts of others” (PDs188). A related aspect of reframing focuses on modifying, adding to, or correcting previously acquired knowledge or just acquiring new knowledge. In general, this conception emphasizes the changes achieved as a result of reflective learning experiences. Participants’ responses may include both the proces ses and objects of reflection; however, seeing thin gs anew and/or reframing existing understandings, beliefs and assumptions are seen as central to the concept of reflective learning.

## Discussion

Our findings indicate that students understood refl ective learning in four qualitatively different way s: engaging with content, evaluating learning, relating learning to other experiences, and reframing one’s own understandings and beliefs. Scholars contend that students’ conceptions are situational and are influenced by the nature of learning environments they experience (Loyens et al., 2009; Smith & Blake, 2009). Thus, the four categories we identified in this study provide insights into the kind of ref lective learning experiences and tasks that student s have been engaged with. These experiences vary in their focus  ranging from disciplinary concepts to personal bel iefs and life experiences.

The goals of engaging with content at greater depth; evaluating one’s own learning strategies; and exploring concepts and theories in relation to one’s prior experiences and assumptions are all relevant. However, considering the uncertainties and complexities of the contemporary world as well as the abundance and pervasive influence of digital information on students’ formal and informal learning, the focus of reflective tasks on content, learning strategies and personal experiences may not suffice. Gorski and Dalton (2020) question the adequacy of achieving personal transformation through reflection on one’s experiences and biases, albeit an important goal by itself. We concur with their call for the need to p rovide students with opportunities of examining heg emonic practices and taken-for-granted assumptions and thereby transforming structures and systems to pursue justice in the wider society. In addition, explicit instructio n of conceptualizations of reflective learning that  go beyond content and personal reflection would provide students opportunities to explore alternative possibilities and aim towards the larger goal of social justice.
