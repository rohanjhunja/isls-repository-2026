# Mental Effort and Task Performance in Collaborative Learning: The Impact of Group Awareness

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigated the role of group awareness in supporting collaborative learning by examining its impact on mental effort (as measured by brain activation levels) and task performance during collaboration activities. 70 individuals from a university were recruited, with mental effort measured from the right temporoparietal junction (rTPJ) region of the brain using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) and task performance was assessed under two collaborative learning conditions: with and without group awareness. The results showed that learners performed significantly better on the task with group awareness, but exhibited significantly greater mental effort without group awareness, suggesting that group awareness facilitated improvements in learners’ collaborative learning performance while reducing the learners’ mental effort for task completion. Researchers and educators could consider incorporating group awareness tools to allow learners to view and track each other’s contributions, progress, and engagement levels, to manage students’ mental demands and foster their collaborative learning performance.

## Introduction

The development of collaboration skills has become a critical part of 21st-century learning. With the proliferation of Information and Communication Technology (ICT) u se, students have more opportunities to interact and collaborate with one another in Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) environment s, co-constructing knowledge, improving problem-solving, and engaging in higher-order thinking (Dillenbourg, 1999). In collaborative learning, students grapple with th e challenge of achieving their learning goals while  managing their mental effort effectively and efficiently (Za mbrano et al., 2019). Previous research has highlig hted several effective strategies for fostering collaborative le arning, such as promoting dialogue, negotiation, and consensusbuilding, as well as utilizing methods like scaffolding, problem-solving, peer review, and role-play (Persico et al., 2009). Additionally, the use of technology helps to  foster joint tasks, communication, resource sharin g, and coconstruction (Jeong & Hme lo-Silver, 2016). Past research remains equivocal as t o which support for is more conducive for producing quality work in collaborative activities and posits that additional support is necessary for optimal collaboration (Wang, 2009). How students in terpret and use these supports may also influence t he collaborative learning processes and consequent per formance outcomes (Pifarré, 2014). An integration o f pedagogical strategies and technologies have been shown to foster successful collaborative learning experiences. The concept of mental effort pertains to the cognit ive resources required to meet task demands, as outlined in Cognitive Load Theory (CLT; Kester & Ki rschner, 2012; Paas et al., 2003). As CLT expands t o the area of collaborative learning, it highlights the  effects of human cognitive structure on information  processing within groups. Collaborative learning increases men tal effort demands due to the need for coordination  and understanding peers' knowledge (Dillenbourg & Bétrancourt, 2006). Effective group awareness can alleviate these demands by clarifying partner expertise and streamlining group activity coordination, reducing mental effort and promoting more effective discussions among group me mbers (Chen et al., 2024; Janssen & Bodemer, 2013; Kirschner et al., 2008).  Identified gaps in the literature include a lack of  exploration into how individual mental effort impacts group dynamics and collaborative performance, the nascent development of physiological measures for assessing group awareness (Jeong & Hmel o-Silver, 2016), and mixed findings regarding the role of group awareness in collaboration across various dimensions (Chen et al., 2022, 2024). This study aims to address these gaps by investigating the relationship between grou p awareness, mental effort, and task performance us ing physiological measurements.

## Literature review

Group awareness in collaborative learning Collaborative learning promotes active interactions  among students, encouraging the generation of idea s and integration of diverse perspectives in a supportive environment (Wray, 2009). However, simply grouping students does not guarantee effective collaboration or impro ved learning outcomes (Johnson & Johnson, 1999). Re search shows that students often face challenges in achieving high-quality knowledge exchange and co-creation (Barron, 2003). Issues such as unequal participation (e.g., free-riding; Saqr et  al., 2022), superficial engagement (e.g., shallow discussions; Ollesch et al., 2021), and negative emotions (e.g., disengagement; Chen & Chang, 2019) can hinder effective group interactions, emphasizing the need for pedagogical support to enhance CSCL (Chen et al., 2022; Janssen & Kirschner, 2020).

Group awareness addresses these challenges by improving mutual understanding among group members, helping learners recognize each other’s roles, expe rtise, and contributions, and fostering a shared un derstanding (Jeong & Hmelo-Silver, 2016), reducing cognitive effort (Schnauber t & Bodemer, 2022), and enhancing task efficiency. Group awareness is broadly defined as " the state of being informed about relevant aspects of group members or the group as a whole" (Schnaubert & Bode mer, 2019). This includes awareness of group member s’ participation, knowledge, skills, and sentiments, a s well as the actions they are undertaking and the objects they interact with (Bodemer & Dehler, 2011). Such awaren ess enables learners to reflect on their interactio ns, adapt behaviors, and coordinate effectively, leading to better collaborative learning processes and outcomes. To introduce group awareness in CSCL, tools and mechanisms have been developed to provide students with relevant information about their peers. These group awareness tools facilitate the coordination and regulation of collaborative learning by offering insights into  group members' actions, contributions, and knowled ge (Buder & Bodemer, 2008). For example, the Participation To ol developed by Janssen et al. (2011) provides real-time feedback on engagement by analyzing chat messages a nd presenting comparative data. This allows group members to assess their participation relative to o thers, motivating greater involvement and improving  group performance. Similarly, other group awareness tools  enhance learning outcomes by promoting collaborati ve elaboration, effective task distribution, and resolution of knowledge gaps (Bodemer, 2011). Some empirical studies demonstrate the benefits of group awareness tools in CSCL. Students using these tools showed higher levels of participation, cognit ive and metacognitive engagement, and improved task performance compared to those without such support (Pifarré et al., 2014). A recent meta-analysis of 46 studies confirmed a moderately significant positive impact of group awareness on learning outcomes, attributin g these gains to increased awareness of group members’ acti vities, knowledge, and emotions (Chen et al., 2024). By enhancing information sharing, co-regulation, and alignment with prior knowledge, gro up awareness fosters meaningful learning and critical discussions (Erkens & Bodemer, 2019). Beyond cognitive benefits, group awareness also sup ports socio-emotional dynamics within groups. It cultivates positive emotions, motivation, trust, and effective conflict management, thereby improving social cohesion and group dynamics (Hernández-Sellés et al., 2019). This dual role of group awareness—enhancing both task efficiency and socio-emotional well-being—makes it an indispensable element in promoting effe ctive collaborative learning processes and outcomes in CSCL.

Conceptualizing and measuring mental effort within group awareness in collaborative learning Mental effort refers to the actual cognitive resources used by the individual to accom modate and meet task demands (Kester & Kirschner, 2012; Paas et al., 200 3). As the Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) in collabora tive learning underscores the influence of human cogniti ve architecture on the distribution and processing of information within groups (Kester & Kirschner, 2012). Mental effort and cognitive load are not the same. Mental effort is influenced by task characteristics, individual traits, and interpersonal interactions, whereas cognitive load is primarily associated with the demands on a perso n's working memory and cognitive resources (Kester & Kirschner, 2012; Paas et al., 2003). In short, the mental effort aspect of CLT focuses on the human-centered dimension of cognitive load. Group awareness foster s successful collaborative learning by enhancing ta sk coordination, facilitating knowledge sharing, and m inimizing the chance of errors (Gutwin & Greenberg,  2004). These improvements, in turn, diminish unproductive transactive activities and lower the mental effort demanded by cognitive load (Janssen & Bodemer, 2013; Kirschner et al., 2008). While collaborative learning imposes additional men tal effort demands (Dillenbourg & Bétrancourt, 2006), such as the need to coordinate (Clark & Brennan, 1991) and model others' knowledge (Sangin et al., 2011), group awareness may reduce this burden by making partner’s expertise clear, thereby decreasing the mental effort costs of collaborative learning (Chen et al., 2024; Janssen & Bodemer, 2013; Kirschner et al., 2008). Specifically, mental effort is decreased as group members do not have to place more ef fort into coordinating group activities in the collaborative content space (Janssen & Bodem er, 2013). In addition to reducing mental effort, g roup awareness tools that inform students about other me mbers’ knowledge or opinions can improve collaborat ive learning by encouraging more structured and goal-oriented discussions (Janssen & Bodemer, 2013). Measuring cognitive effort through self-reported surveys and physiological assessments, suc h as gaze patterns and heart rate, has provided valuable insi ghts into mental effort during collaborative learni ng (Keskin, 2020). More recently, advances in neuroimaging tech niques have enhanced the portability of brain-based measurements, enabling neurophysiological assessmen ts, such as fNIRS, EEG, and fMRI, to be conducted i n naturalistic settings (Jeong & Hmelo-Silver, 2016). These methods offer objective, real-time insights into cognitive load and mental effort by identifying bra in regions associated with memory, attention, and p roblemsolving (Zakeri et al., 2023). While it is well-established that the brain functions as an intercon nected network, specific region s have also been identified to play pivotal roles i n certain activities. For instance, the right temporoparietal junction (rTPJ) has been implicated  in attentional and social cognition networks (Kral l et al., 2014), which are integral to collaboration. Previou s research has noted that increased visual short-term memory load suppresses rTPJ activity, resulting in inattentional blindness, impaired detection of unexpected stimuli (Todd et al., 2005), prolonged reaction times, and greate r deactivation of the rTPJ region (Wu et al., 2015). These findings underscore the importance of exploring bra in activation patterns to generate reliable, quanti fiable data, thereby improving research accuracy, informing the design of more effective learning environments and task structures, and highlighting the rTPJ's critical role in managing mental effort. Some gaps have been identified from the literature review. Firstly, while research often examines individual mental effort, there has been little exploration of how it manifests within group dynamics or influences collaborative learning and task performance. Secondly, using physiological measures to measure learners’ mental effort with group awareness support in various coll aborative learning contexts is still in its early s tages. Thirdly, although it is widely believed that group awareness  plays an imperative role in successful collaborati ve learning (Schnaubert & Bodemer, 2022), studies have also rep orted mixed findings on the impact of group awarene ss across behavioral, cognitive, and socio-emotional dimensions (Chen et al., 2022, 2024), wit h group awaren ess having a positive effect or no effect on learning performance (Chen et al., 2022, 2024). The mixed findings could be attributed to experimental conditions (e.g., difficulty levels of the learning materials and time duration), types of group awareness t ools used, and learner characteristics (e.g., perce ived need for self-regulation of positive emotions and behavior modelling in the context of p eer interactions). For example, in instances where group norms hinder collaborative learning, group members may withhold information from others, impeding effe ctive collaboration process (Chen et al., 2022). Moreover, if the intervention duration is short, there may not be sufficient time to observe significant changes due to implementing group awareness (Chen et al., 2024). This study aims to address these gaps by using a neurophysiological lens to explore how group awareness influences learners’ mental effort and task performance in CSCL, providing new perspectives on the role of group awareness in these processes. Using neurophysiological indicators of mental effort, for example, affords not only an objective measure, but also, one that is sensiti ve to fluctuations in task complexity and mental ef fort required to meet it as indicated by the brain activity (Scheiter et al., 2020). The research questions are: RQ1: What are the differences in learners’ CSCL per formance between collaborative learning with and without group awareness?

RQ2: What are the differences in learners’ mental effort  in CSCL between collaborative learning with and without group awareness?

In line with the research questions, this study hypothesizes the following: Hypothesis 1: Learners have higher collaborative le arning performance with group awareness than without group awareness.

Hypothesis 2: Learners exert higher mental effort during collaborative learning without group awareness than with group awareness.

## Participants and experimental design

70 participants from a university were recruited and paired with unfamiliar partners to minimize inter personal familiarity effects. Each dyad completed two rounds  of collaborative tasks in a single experimental se ssion: one with group awareness support (CoLGA condition) and one without (CoL condition). The exp erimental conditions were presented in random order. In both tasks, participants were instructed to work together with their partners to conceptualize ideas to deisgn an everyday product in response to a given scenario. The tasks were structured such that participants would first be provided with 2 mi nutes of individual preparation time before moving into 5 minutes of collaborative discussions to integrate their ideas. During individual preparation, participants recorded their ideas in separate ideation columns, whereas i n the collaborative phase, they shared the same wor kspace (Figure 1). The experimental design of this study integrated group awareness support into a CSCL environment using an online interface (Google Slides) that prov ided live updates with participant-specific contributions. Aligned with the definition of group awareness, this setup allowed participants to access both their own and their partner's knowledge, work, and progress. In the group awareness condition, participants could view their own and partner’s ideas during the individual preparation p hase and reference these ideas during the collabora tive co-construction and ideation phase.

## Data collection and analysis

This study uses brain activation patterns, derived from neural responses collected during the experimental session, as a physiological representation of mental effort.  Individual brain activity from both participants i n each dyad were simultaneously record ed during the experimental sessions with an fNIRS s ystem (Oxymon III, Artinis Medical Systems, The Netherlands). Each participant  wore an fNIRS cap with 7 optodes, forming 6 channe ls, over the right temporoparietal junction (rTPJ). The  rTPJ was selected as  a key brain region to represent mental effort regulation given its established role in social cog nition. Raw data was stored and analysed on a compu ter with the Oxysoft software version 3.2.72 (Artinis M edical Systems, The Netherlands). Changes in indivi dual hemodynamic activity, specifically in oxyge nated hemoglobin at the rTPJ, were assessed as a me asure of brain region activation throughout the tasks. The activat ion values were then preprocessed and averaged acro ss the individual preparation and collaborative discussion  durations respectively. The averaged values served  as a measure of mental effort exerted by the participants during each task. The design solutions generated by the dyads were evaluated by two raters using an adapted scoring rubric to measure task performance. The performance outcom es were measured by task accuracy (quality) and tas k completion time (efficiency; Chen et al., 2022). Le arning efficiency is calculated by the ratio of mental effort to task performance, with successful task performance in collaboration requiring reflection, monitoring, regulation, and support from group roles (Janssen & Kirschner, 2020; Jeong & Hmelo-Silver, 2016). Scores were assigned on a 1-to-5 scale across four dimensions—Completeness, Elaboration, Functionality, and Innovation. Both raters co-coded the first 10 artifacts to achieve an inter-coder reliability (Cronbach’s Alpha) of.96,.93,.82, and.85 for the four dimensions respectively. After which, the remaining 25 pairs were divided between the raters for independent scoring.

## Findings

To test the research hypotheses, paired samples t-tests were run to compare the collaborative learning performance and physiological assessment of mental effort betwe en the two conditions (with and without group awareness). For the first hypothesis, there were significant differences in task performance scores between the CoLGA and CoL conditions (t = 2.633, p <.01), signifying that the participants performed better in collaboration learning when there was group awareness as opposed to without group awareness (Table 1). Therefore, the first hypothesis was supported.

For the second research hypothesis, this study found that the rTPJ scores measuring brain activation i.e., mental effort was the highest at CoL IP in comparison to the CoL GA condition. Further, there were significant differences in mental effort scores between the CoLGA and CoL conditions (t = -2.104, p <.05), signifying that the participants exerted greater mental effort on collaboration learning when there was no group awareness as opposed to with group awareness present. Therefore, the second hypothesis was supported. ## Discussion

This study examined the influence of group awareness support on learners’ collaborative learning perform ance and mental effort, particularly, the differences in task performance and mental effort levels between collaboration activities with and without group awareness. This study found that the learners performed signif icantly better on collaborative learning when there was group awareness (CoLGA) as opposed to when there was no group awareness (CoL). This infers that the learners were able to collectively generate their own ideas while being aware of the group dynamics, roles, and contributions (with group awareness). This finding can be justified based on several key insights from literature. Using technology and tools, group awareness facilitates more efficient knowledge sharing, group cohesion, cooperative behaviors, tas k coordination, and social dynamics regulation (Che n et al., 2022; Erkens & Bodemer, 2019; Janssen & Bodemer, 20 13), allowing for better integration of diverse perspectives, and more effective collaboration (Janssen et al., 2011; Schnaubert & Bodemer, 2022). Additionally, when learners are aware of each other's contributions, they can monitor and adjust their actions accordingly, which optimizes their performance in collaboration. The a bility to recognize and respond to group members' n eeds results in more focused and productive interactions  (Chen et al., 2024). This highlights the critical role group awareness plays in enhancing both cognitive and social aspect s of collaborative work, ultim ately leading to improved task performance outcomes.

Further, the current study also found that learners exserted significantly greater mental effort when there was no group awareness as opposed to when group awareness was present, which has been explained by literature on cognition and group dynamics. The groups' socio-emotional experiences contribute and motivate effec tive collaborative learning (Janssen et al., 2011; Schna ubert & Bodemer, 2022). In CoL condition, lea rners only communicated verbally with access to their own init ial ideas. This reliance on r eal-time verbal discussion likely increased cognitive demands, as participants had to articulate and justify their ideas on the spot. In contrast, CoLGA ** * provided group awareness support, allowing learners to continuously reference both their own and their partner's written ideas throughout collaboration (Yücel & Usl uel, 2016). This access likely simplified communica tion by reducing the need for lengthy verbal explanations, clarifications, and negotiations. As a result, the learners’ mental effort decreased, making it easier to understand and integrate their partner's ideas without the extra effort of realtime verbal articulation. The group awareness  of oth ers' group work contributed to smoother communicati on likely explains the lower rTPJ activation observed in CoLGA. In the absence of group awareness, learners may have struggled more with task coordination, knowledge sharing, and understanding each other’s contributio ns, leading to increased mental effort. Furthermore, the learners may become more focused on their own ideas, leading to inefficiencies, redundancy in task performance, and increased mental effort as they manage the task with less shared understanding (Chen et al., 2022; Janssen & Bodemer, 2013). When group awareness  is present, individuals have a clearer u nderstanding of their group members' knowledge, skills, and roles, reducing the  efforts required for managing interactions and tas k coordination (Janssen et al., 2011; Schnaubert & Bo demer, 2022). This awareness fosters open communica tion and allows for more efficient collaboration, where individuals can focus on problem-solving and integrating diverse ideas instead of using up mental resources to monitor and adjust to others’ actions (Janssen & Kirschner, 2020). Therefore, the presence of group awareness likely reduces the overall mental effort required for the task, as the learners can rely on shared understanding and support, making the collaborative process smoother and less cognitively taxing.

## Limitations and future recommendations

A limitation of this study is that it investigated group awareness by comparing collaborative learning with or without group awareness  using technology (Chen et al., 2022, 2024). However, researchers agree that group awareness can be a powerful group interaction mechanism even without using technology  (Chen et al., 2022, 2024). Therefore, future research can  explore which specific features of group awareness tools play a role in collaborative learning.

Furthermore, the design of group awareness support in this study adhered only to its broad def inition, allowing participants to be informed about each other’s participation and knowledge. However, prior research has further classified group awareness into several dimensions such as social, behavioral,  and cognitive (Bodemer & Dehler, 2011), which were not specifically targeted in this study. Instead, group awareness benefits were offered through a single platform without dedicated tools f or each dimension. Hence, the approach of this study may be limited, as evidence suggests that focusing solely on behavioral group awareness has minimal impact on learning outcomes compared to cognitive or social dimensions  of group awareness (Li et al., 2021). This suggests that future research should avoid examining individual group awareness dimensions in isolation and instead explore how different aspects of group awareness contribute to task performance collectively. Specifically, studies could identify which group aware ness elements, in unique collaborative contexts, lead to  improved CSCL learning processes and outcomes (Chen et al., 2022, 2024).

Thirdly, mental effort and task performance can be measured using multimodal methods, with the inclusion of other variables such as pupil dilation  for real-time cognitive effort measurement and verbal data f or revealing learning behaviors, collaboration dynamic s, and socio-emotional regulation, offering a comprehensive view of collaborative mechanisms and performance. A nalyzing interview data may also help to further un cover challenges in coordination, knowledge sharing, and understanding during collaboration, highlighting group awareness's role in alleviating these difficulties.

Finally, this study only used a simple 7-minute collaborative learning task to examine the group awareness’s impact on mental effort and collaborative outcom es. To improve generalizability of these findings, future research should explore longer, more complex  scenarios involving larger groups or hybrid (in-person and virtual) designs to further validate group awareness’s benefits.

## Conclusion

To conclude, the findings of this study provide imp lications for educators to incorporate group awaren ess in collaborative learning to improve students’ learning performance while managing their cognitive demands. Design of collaborative learning environments Instructional designers could prioritize the integr ation of tools and strategies that enhance transpar ency in group awareness in collaborative learning settings (Chen et al., 20 22, 2024). Group awareness  designs and strategies should be clear without inducing further mental demands. This could include features such as shared visualizations of group member contributions, real-time feedback on task progress, and clear delineati on of roles and expertise within the group.

Assessment practices Assessments of collaborative learning should consider not only individual contributions but also the dynamics of group awareness. Evaluation methods could incorporate peer assessm ents, self-reflections on group interactions, and assessments of group processes to better unders tand how group awareness   influences overall performance, Since we did not target any specific types of group  awareness in this study (Bodemer & Dehler, 2011; Li et al., 2021), designing group awareness  practices should involve looking at the effectiveness of group awareness dimensions such as behavioral, cognitive or social – on improving performance and thereby, cognitive demands. Mental load management Educators might develop strategies to teach learner s how to enhance their own awareness of group dynam ics, thereby reducing unnecessary cognitive strain durin g collaborative tasks. Schnaubert and Bodemer (2019) explored group awareness tools which improved regulation of effort to reduce cognitive strain and educators may adopt ways to optimize such tools for enhancing learner performance.
