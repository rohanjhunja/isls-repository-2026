# Scaffolding Students’

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Students experience uncertainties while solving open-ended collaborative problems. They can feel overwhelmed and question their abilities to manage these uncertainties and, thus, need to be supported in this process. Scaffolds such as concept-maps and reflection prompts have shown to be useful in supporting students’ collaborative problem-solving. Hence, we investigate the influence of concept-maps and reflection prompts on students’ self-efficacy while managing uncertainty in an open-ended collaborative problem-solving task. Twenty-three master’s students participated in mixed-method classroom research where they collaboratively designed an online learning environment. Findings suggest that the scaffolds can foster productive uncertainty management by initiating productive discussions to manage uncertainties. Maintaining and reducing uncertainties emerged as popular strategies indicating that students became comfortable with uncertainties and were willing to leave them unresolved before reducing them for making final decisions. Furthermore, their self-efficacy increased significantly over time while managing uncertainty.

## Introduction

There has been an increased emphasis on engaging university students in authentic and complex problems as part of their learning experiences (Glazewski & Ertmer, 2020). Such problems are open-ended, and students often face a lot of uncertainties during the problem-solving process (Boros, 2024). Uncertainty refers to experiences of wonderment, confusion, or being unsure about someth ing (Jordan & McDaniel, 2014). These can arise during collaborative problem-solving tasks due to the problem’s complexity, lack  of knowledge, conflicting and ambiguous information, and prior experiences (Hartner-Tiefenthaler et al., 2018; Kaur & Dasgupta, 2024). When facing uncertainties, students can feel overwhelmed and question their abilities to manage them whereas engaging meaningfully with uncertainties during collaboratio n fosters learning (Kaur & Dasgupta, 2024). However, the ability to tackle uncertainties often depends on students’ belief in their ability (self-efficacy; Zimmerman, 2000). Students with high self-efficacy are likely to regard challenging moments a nd uncertainties as opportunities to learn (Bandura, 1982; Zimmerman, 2000)  whereas s tudents with low self-efficacy tend to avoid challenging situations (Ahmad & Safaria, 2013), demonstrating the necessity to scaffold productive uncertainty management. The use of scaffolds, various forms, and their purposes have been researched extensively in the past (e.g., Quintana et al., 2004). However, not much research exists on scaffolding uncertainty manageme nt (Kaur & Dasgupta, 2024). Based on prior research, we gather that scaffolds like concept maps can present a visual way to decompose complex issues and uncertainties into smaller and manageable piece s (Mayer & Fiorella, 2021; Quintana et al., 2004). Together with reflection prompts, these scaffolds can enable process management (Chen, 2020). Thus, we aimed to investigate  the influence of concept-maps and reflection prompts on students’ self-efficacy while managing uncertainty. We hypothesize that these scaffolds can help student s break uncertainties into manageable chunks, fostering their self-efficacy and enabling productive uncertainty manage ment. The specific research questions are – (RQ1) What types of uncertainties and management st rategies emerged during collaborative problem-solving in a master’s level course?, (RQ2) How do c oncept-maps and reflection prompts influence master students’ self-efficacy in managing uncertainty durin g collaborative problem-solving?, and (RQ3) How does the influence of concept-maps and reflection prompts on master students’ sel f-efficacy in managing uncertainty vary across different student teams?

## Theoretical framework

Uncertainty Management (UM) Uncertainty is generally perceived as a negative oc currence, leading to efforts to prevent or reduce i t (Griffin & Grote, 2020). However, many studies argue that it s hould be cultivated rather than escaped (e.g., Jordan & McDaniel, 2014). Uncertainty is described as a personal experience, where one lacks knowledge and feels unsure about a specific situation (Jordan & McDaniel, 2014). The authors highlight its relevance in educational contexts where students regularly encounter uncertainty while learning new things (Jordan & McDaniel, 2014). Here, students often work collaboratively in teams, which demands both social and cognitive a bilities. Roschelle and Teasley (1995) argue that for collaboration to be e ffective, individuals need to engage actively and c ontinuously align their actions to achieve a common understandi ng, which makes it hard to escape situations that trigger uncertainties. Kirch and Siry  (2012) note that “uncertainty originates and exists  in dialog and is a product of interaction with others and the world” (p. 263), highlighting that uncertainty is rooted in collaborative activities. Individuals make use of specific strategies and beh aviors to deal with uncertain situations, including reducing (e.g. ask questions to peers and teachers,  analyze problems, trial-and-error), maintaining (e.g. doubt an idea, procrastinate), ignoring (e.g. avoid issues, pass off tasks), or even increasing uncertainty (e. g. expand the problem scope, brainstorm alternative ideas) (Jordan & McDaniel, 2014). These strategies can vary depending on the stage of the problem-solving task. People usually try to reduce  or avoid uncertainty (Griffin & Grote, 2020), as it can be a source of discomfort and anxiety (Hi rsh et al., 2012). Therefore, it seems logical to t hink that uncertainty should be minimized as much as possible. Nevertheless, in some situations, consciousl y cultivating uncertainty can have benefits. For instance, introd ucing uncertainty can promote creative problem-solving by encouraging people to think outside the box and consider various possibilities (Jordan & McDaniel, 2014). During this exploration phase, uncertainty can be increase d at first, but this process ultimately allows stud ents to reduce it by refining their options to identify the most effective solution (Jordan & McDaniel, 2014). Ignoring uncertainty can however hinder the learning process as  the issues stay unaddressed when uncertainty is avo ided (Jordan & McDaniel, 2014). Students’ belief in their ability to tackle uncerta inties also plays a crucial role in determining which strategy is used and how uncertainty is managed (Zimmerman, 2000). Self-efficacy Self-efficacy describes the confidence individuals have in their ability to manage tasks and accomplish objectives in different situations (Bandura, 1982).  Individuals with high self-efficacy see challenging moments and uncertainties as opportunities to learn (Bandura, 1 982; Zimmerman, 2000). Thus, they do not perceive m istakes as negative, but rather as opportunities to grow fr om them. Further, students with high confidence tend to participate and keep going even in difficult situations (Zimmerman, 1995). This sug gests that they have a strong belief that such situations can be overcome. Dunbar et al. (2018) state that th ese students strongly believe they cando the task successfully, while being aware that they could fail in doing so. However, students with low selfefficacy are prone to steer clear of complex challe nges (Schunk, 1989). This propensity often extends to social environments, where they shy away from peer interac tions (Ahmad & Safaria, 2013).  Thus, we believe that the level of self-efficacy will influence the way university students  manage uncertainties. Further, we argue that specific scaffolds can help improve students’ self-efficacy thereby fostering productive uncertainty management. Concept maps and reflection prompts as scaffolds Concept-maps serve as learning tools to visually organize i nformation (Novak, 2008).  By representing the knowledge in a visual manner, students can make con nections and form a coherent mental model (Mayer & Fiorella, 2021). This can be beneficial for managing uncertainty, because concept-maps can break down complex issues and uncertainties into smaller, more  manageable pieces, meaning the task becomes less ov erwhelming (Quintana et al., 2018). Concept-maps are particularly useful in group settings, because they get students involved in open-ended activities that require discourse and negotia tion, therefore fostering interdependency (van Boxt el et al., 2002). We draw on these affordances of concept-mapping and use it as a scaffold by encouraging students to break down their uncertainties into smaller manageable pieces and identify possible interconnections between the uncertainties. The shared representation of the concept-map also enables students to identify and acknowledge the presence of uncertainty; a challenging process for students (Kaur & Dasgupta, 2024). Reflection prompts are structured questions, which encourage s tudents to think deeply about their learning experiences and outcomes (Menekse et al., 2022). This allows students to evaluate themselves  and the collaborative process. The reasoning behind the decision to implement this tool in this study, is that it gets students to actively identify and externalize their uncertainties, which might help to manage them more effectively.

## Method

In this mixed-methods research using convenience sampling (Etikan  et al., 2016), 23 students (groups of four) participated in the context of a Dutch educational psychology master’s course focused on how people learn and designing effective learning environments. Students collaboratively designed an online learn ing environment as part of a nine-week course after gathering requirements from teachers. Once a week (except week 2), guest lectures covered relevant learning theories for 45 minutes, whereafter another 45 minutes were dedicated to group project-work. During in-class groupwork from weeks 4-7 (i.e., periods of intensive in-class group discussion), the first author collected data via observation, audio recordings, and open-ended surveys.  From week 4 onwards, students were introduced to concept-mapping and reflection prompts  help them articulate and manage their uncertainties. Each group received a large blank sh eet, several colored markers and an additional shee t listing possible categories for their concept-map. These categories included prompts to help them  initiate their discussions (e.g., “target audience” category included the prompts “Who is our target audience?”). We used discourse and thematic analysis (Gill, 2000) for identifying types and strategies of uncertainty management using codes by Jordan and McDaniel (2014). We used ‘individual in a group’ as our unit of analysis (Kaur & Dasgupta, 2024). Thus, our analysis focused on the interplay of individual and group uncertainty management. Data on selfefficacy were collected three times (weeks 1, 5, and 9) using a modified General Self-Efficacy (GSE) survey (Schwarzer & Jerusalem, 1995). One-way repeated measures ANOVA was used to analyze the GSE data. Openended reflection surveys were distributed immediately after the lesson in weeks 4-7 to capture students’ personal experiences of uncertainty during the design process.

## Findings and discussion

For RQ1, across the class, students (individually) primarily experienced four types of uncertainties: 1) Choosing appropriate theories and formulating the problem statement, 2) Finding clients, 3) Applying theoretical knowledge to practical design, and 4) Technical challenges and limitations. These were evidenced in the student conversations and varied by project’s phase. For instance, in the early phases of the group project (week 4), students were uncertain about their problem statement and relevant educational theories (e.g., “We are not sure about choosing the appropriate theories based on the needs of the teacher”). They were also uncertain about finding clients (schoolteachers) or choosing between multiple clien ts (e.g., “We had an uncertainty about which client we were going to use since we had two options and they were  both interesting”). Across weeks 4 and 5, students faced uncertainties related to technical aspects and cons traints of using the Go-Lab environment (e.g., “Our biggest concern is the implementation of our game into go-Lab, as Go-lab is restricting our design choices.”). In week 5, the teams faced uncertainty related to applying theory to practice (e.g., “We felt uncertain about how to properly translate our problem into learning objectives”). ## Conclusion

We extend previous work on identifying uncertainty man agement strategies with young school students (e.g., Kaur & Dasgupta, 2024) to university students. Find ings indicate that university students also adopt s imilar strategies as school students when navigating uncertainties, but they are more capable of collaboratively selecting productive strategies. The use of concept-maps and reflection prompts likely influenced these strategies. Findings suggest that these scaffolds may foster productive uncertainty management by initiating productive dis cussions and visually breaking down uncertainties. Moreover, these scaffolds likely fostered self-efficacy while managing uncertainties. This can be important for scaffolding students who avoid challenging problems (Ahmad & Safaria, 2013). Th is research provides a practical way for teachers t o effectively foster students’ self-efficacy and uncertainty management, enabling them to effectively  use open-ended challenging problems in project-based curricula in universities (Hartner-Tiefenthaler et al., 2018). Future research can build on these significant findings to develop further scaffolds to support students’ uncertainty management and their self-efficacy.
