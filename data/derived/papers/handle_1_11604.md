# Co-Constructing Research Questions with Informal Practitioners within a Networked Improvement Community

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Through a collaboration among six informal learning organizations and a research team across the United States, our goal was to consider how to engage parents, caregivers, and classroom teachers as facilitators of learning with their children when experiencing failure within making activities. In this paper, we describe how we worked together to develop research questions that recognize the complexity of collaborating with organizations that have different ideas, theories of change, and organizational capacity. We further highlight this through sharing the journey of one organization, Creative Reuse Center. We intend for this paper to serve as an example for building partnerships focused on solving problems of practice according to practitioners’ needs rather than researchers’ interests.

## Introduction

How do research questions get formed and decided when working in collaborative groups? Developing a research question is the basis of scientific inquiry, and in education and the learning sciences, many models exist to support practitioners in informing th e research question, as well as the production of r esearch. Many methods for researchers and practitioners to collaborate exist,  from participatory action research, research practice partnerships, design-based implementation research, and networked improv ement communities (Penuel et al., 2020). While all of these partnerships aim to share power between practitioners and researchers, we focus here on work done in a networked improvement community (NIC). A NIC involves a learning community using improvement science techniques to study a problem, design interventions, and review outcomes together (Bryk et al., 2011; Penuel et al., 2020). In collaborative partnerships, co-producing a research topic, aim, and question is a hallmark of equitable partnerships (Smith et al., 2023). However, there is little scholarship regarding how research questions are co-created in partnerships. Therefore, the purpose of this paper is to offer an analysis of researchers’ own inquiry into how research questions were develo ped across six organizations through a shift in pow er and recognizing how our partnering organizations had different ideas and theories of change from one another.

## Methods

An intrinsic case study was utilized to understand the particular nature of a situation of interest, t he manner in which research questions were developed within our collaborative partnership (Stake, 1995). Our networ ked improvement community consisted of six organization s and three researchers from three different instit utions. The data for this study included video recordings o f our virtual meetings and shared google slides tha t included written documentation and links to outside visuals (e.g., driver diagrams). The recordings of the first four virtual meetings and the written documentation were reviewe d, paying specific attention to conversations aroun d establishing a direction for the research. The written documentation detailing the problems and questions as they were shared and refined were collected and compared.

Insights Throughout this partnership our community’s main fo cus remained on adult/child groups experiencing fai lure in making, however there was variation across our comm unity in who was the focus and what the interventio n entailed. By the end of our first meeting, initial wonderings and interest were distilled into four key ideas: (1) We all have different perspectives of failure and of s uccess, (2) Adults possess a product-oriented mindset, often fixing issues for kids even before issues occur, (3) Adults may not know a lot about what helps kids l earn or persist when they experience a failure moment, and (4) Educators lack the confidence and the tools tonegotiate adult-child interactions when an adult takes control of o pportunities for a child to struggle and grow  through failure. Three of these are framed with a deficit p erspective, focusing on what a particular group lac ks or does wrong. The ideas center around mindsets, adult perception of failure, and educator facilitation. To translate these ideas into a problem of practice  the research team used the framing of “How Might We” (HMW) statements (Bryk et al, 2015). Six organi zations developed 36 HMW questions in the second meeting. Examples include (a) HMW model failure-positive facilitation techniques that parents can copy, in  the makerspace and later at home?; (b) HMW use adults’ passion to “finish” a project to support a child’s learning in moments of failure?; and (c) HMW help educators feel excited rather than nervous about dealing with these kinds of scenarios? In the third meeting, as we began to narrow down to a joint research question, several different foci emerged. Four organizations were interested in how to help caregivers support a child experiencing fai lure. One of these, City Science Museum, was interested in suppo rting adults to see and celebrate failure as a part  of the learning process for their child. The Museum of Eng aging Science was interested in helping educators f acilitate adult-youth interactions during moments of failure. The Creative Reuse Center was interested in supporting adults to actively engage in a drop-in creative makerspace activity. It was at this point that the research team decided to shift from attempting to narrow down to one researc h question to embracing many approaches that highli ght the uniqueness, complexity, and needs of each organizat ion. This decision shifted who held the power in ou r community, from the research team funneling the six  organizations toward a shared research question to the research team supporting each organization as they developed their own approach to adult interactions with children when experiencing failure in making.

By the fourth meeting, five organizations developed  their aim and driver diagram (i.e., visual tool to represent a working theory of change; Hinnant-Crawford, 2020) centered around engaging with adult-child groups experiencing failure in making. Two of these focuse d on caregivers supporting their child/ren. City Sc ience Museum, for example, stated their aim as: During a two-hour design challenge, observe five adult caregiver s celebrate their child going through the engineering  design process. Two focused on educators supportin g adultchild groups. Museum of Engaging Science, stated: Educators use a variety of facilitation methods and strategies to normalize and celebrate failure in maker activities with intergenerational groups. Creative Reuse Center focused on supporting adult making: Support adult attendees  to directly engage with creative/playful making in collaboration with their children or in parallel play. These are all different approaches tackling the same question of how to facilitate adult-child groups.

## Discussion

In this paper, we shared how our partnership focused on solving problems of practice and tailoring investigations according to practitioners’ needs rather than researchers’ interests, particularly through the creation of individual research questions with six organizations that had different ideas, theories of change, and organizational capacity. Originally this collaboration was framed as a learn ing community investigating a shared complex proble m. We developed this collaboration into a networked impro vement community by using improvement science tools and embracing different approaches amongst partners foc used on addressing adult-child interactions with failure in making. The networked community part of our NIC was key. Though we initially struggled to come to one central research question to focus on, we believe this work  was stronger because this was a network of people with different ideas and approaches rather than just a single organization in partnership with researchers. Organizations offered valuable feedback and refined each other’s ideas throughout the process. We all learned from the different approaches utilized to inform the research question s and interventions. The experience was knowledge-building for partner organizations as they used new-to-them improvement science tools to do research on th eir own while also getting support and resources from researchers and peer practitioners doing similar work. The research team tinkered with the partnership structure to share po wer across the collaboration because we wanted to p ush our understanding of problems and solutions grounded in real-world contexts within informal learning environments. A networked improvement community offers a structure for embracing the messiness of complex problem solving with multiple partners, allowing for different approache s to solve the same problem while sharing results a nd learning from each other.

While there is some consensus about effective pedag ogical and environmental design choices for learning within museums, rigorous research approach es to test varying approaches have not been as robu st as PreK-12 formal school environments. Variation betwee n informal institutions in the U.S. is significant,  as there is a broad breadth of guidance for museum accredita tion, funding staffing, missions, resources. Theref ore, focusing on the variation of the environment (or th e systems in which the problem occurs), was essential to understanding what change could be meaningful. Part ners needed to identify spaces ripe for innovation and necessary change that fit their space. For example,  the Creative Reuse Center began intentionally play ing music to make adult visitors feel welcomed. In other spaces, a change in mu sic was not an option (a constraint in their system), even if practitioners thought that change would be helpful. The organizations in this study s hared an interest in the topic of learning through failure in makerspaces, but there is much room for continued work in coconstructing research projects in multiple and more varied conditions.
