# Students’ Understanding of the Nature of Engineers and Engineering During an Integrated Life Science and Engineering Curriculum

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines elementary, middle, and high school students’ understanding of engineers and engineering practices. I probed students’ views through drawings and interviews. Through analysis of these data, I intend to gain insight on how students conceptualize who engineers are, what they do, and how they do it. I aim to identify deficits in engineering knowledge as opportunities for tailored instruction in future curricula.

Engineering is an approach to problem solving that necessitates satisfying constraints  such as time, cost, safety, and ease of production (National Research Council, 2012). Through a set of iterative practices known as the engineering design process (Koen, 2003), engineers develop solutions to everything from mundane corporate requirements to major societal issues. Many states have placed an increased emphasis on engineering within K12 education (NGSS Lead States, 2013). Despite this, students often lose interest in STEM fields during middle school (Archer et al., 2010). One reason this might be happening is that many students have vague or incorrect ideas about what engineers do (Blotnicky et al., 2018). The current study examines students’ understanding of these concepts in three different settings at three different grade levels as they complete an integrated life science and engineering curriculum. In a culmination of their learning, students follow the  engineering design process as presented in the curriculum to design and build a solution to a local life science problem. By gathering data about what students know about engineers and engineering while they are actively engaged in engineering practices, I hope to elucidate areas where students' understanding is strong along with areas where students need additional support. ## Participants

Data from three distinct student populations were collected. The first group consisted of rising third-and fourthgrade students enrolled in an informal, one-week engineering summer camp program (n = 27). The second group was made up of seventh graders (n = 82) enrolled in an independent, private middle school. The third group comprised ninth graders (n = 37) attending a suburban, public high school. The seventh and ninth graders completed the curriculum as a part of their formal science instruction during the fall semester. All three student groups were located within the western United States.

Drawing and writing activity Like previous studies (e.g., Fralick et al., 2008; Karatas et al., 2010), all students completed a drawing and writing activity. First, students were instructed to “Draw an engineer or engineers.” After completing the drawing, students were asked to write a brief description of what is depicted in their drawing. Student interviews A sample set from each student group was interviewed. In the summer camp group, a convenience sample of willing students (n = 1 6) was selected. For the seventh-and ninth-grade groups, students’ drawings and descriptions of engineers were roughly sorted into high-, mid -, and low-understanding groups within their respective grade levels. High-understanding responses depicted or described engineering as a multi-step process. Mid-understanding responses depicted or described exactly one engineering practice. L ow-understanding responses did not depict or describe any engineering practices. Four-to-five students were randomly selected from these groups to be interviewed. In total, n = 14 seventh-grade students were interviewed, and n = 13 ninth-grade students were interviewed.

Interviews were semistructured and lasted approximately ten minutes. Researchers asked students to describe what skills engineers need and what decisions engineers make. They also asked students to identify a problem that could be solved with engineering and what the students would do to solve it. Additionally, the students who 2349 completed the formal curricular program (seventh-and ninth-grade students) were asked a follow-up question about how, if at all, the curriculum had influenced their perceptions of  engineers or engineering. I plan to analyze the data collected from the drawing activity as well as the interviews to look for evidence of how these different student groups understand who engineers are, what engineers do, and what engineering practices allow engineers to do their work. The framework I plan to use for this is based on Valladares’s (2021) work that describes three “Visions” for scientific literacy  that move from the knowledge of scientific concepts to social transformation. I believe three similar “Visions” can be applied to engineering knowledge. I anticipate identifying themes related to each of the three visions within the student drawing/writing activity responses and the student interviews. I also anticipate identifying clusters of the activities (e.g., designing) and identities (e.g., women) students mention. I’ll also examine what kinds of problems students believe can be solved with engineering.

## Preliminary results

Preliminary examination of the drawing activity data and interview transcripts, revealed that  many students describe engineers and engineering in terms of engineering practices (Vision I). Some students could not correctly identify a practice that engineers specifically engage in (Level 0). These students often attributed the work of other professionals such as mechanics or physicians  to engineers. Those students who did correctly identify an engineering practice or skill were further separated into Levels 1 (depicting one engineering practice) and 2 (depicting engineering as a process)  (See Table 1). There were some  student responses and interviews that mentioned engineering as a force for societal change that will be further coded for Vision II or III-level engineering literacy.
