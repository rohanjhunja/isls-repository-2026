# Towards a Robust Theory Toolbox for CSCL: Mechanisms, Models, and AI

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This symposium aims to foster new scientific discussions about how the CSCL (Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning) field can advance towards more robust theories. Such advancements depend on further development and formalization of models, causal mechanisms, and the creative application of different types of generative AI. The problem description is rooted in the increasing gap between theoretical positions and actual data, also emphasizing that data alone cannot explain any phenomenon. The overarching argument is that computational modeling approaches can be employed to formalize models of collaborative scripts, temporal collaborative processes, multi-level collaboration, and multimodal activities and that varied uses of AI can amplify such efforts. These models can be based on causal mechanisms and can be generalized to collaborative learning phenomena rather than just specific populations. We provide a rich view of the CSCL field that includes pathways to theoretical robustness, practical tools and solutions for collborative learning in the age of AI.

We observe a widening gap in CSCL — as in other behavioural research — between theories and data. Progress in instrumentation has enabled the capture of collaboration of unprecedented width (duration, number of participants), breadth (multi-level and-modal), and depth (granularity) (Reimann, 2021). The availability of multimodal data with high temporal granularity leads to the question of how this can inform theories of collaboration and learning. The problem is that most of the (psychological, for instance) theories we use in CSCL have not been conceived with this kind of data in mind. They do not predict or explain unaggregated behavioural data such as event sequences, only changes in aggregated variables. They do not account for the situatedness of cognition and interaction, but abstract away from context. They are (meant to be) tested with statistical models for linear relations that cannot account for feedback relations in dynamic and complex systems.  Hence, there is a need to develop theory and along with that, to reflect on what kinds of theories the field needs. This involves, looking intoneighboring disciplines for considering epistemic partnering. The conference motto, "Partnering with Purpose," is interpreted epistemically here: We ask  which concepts, theories, and methods from related fields should inform multidisciplinary CSCL research. This question is significant because there is a consensus against a single, unified theory of CSCL and a standardized diversity; the items inside are varied but useful.  While others have proposed what should be in a social sciences theory toolbox more generally (e.g., Bell et al., 2012), this symposium focuses on CSCL. We suggest including a specific type of theory tool— namely, mechanisms—and provide examples of such.  (There will be other types of tools in the box.) The CSCL researcher also picks tools from a method toolbox, and we recommend addition s to the method box: computational modeling  and application of generative AI. We suggest these t ypes of tools because they can be instrumental in developing robust theories and methods. Identification of collaborative learning mechanisms, c omputational modelling, and causal explanations help ensure that CSCL enhances its collective and cumulative capacities to develop as an interdisciplinary scientific field. Over the last 25 years, philosophers of science have studied how  descriptions of mechanisms and mechanical explanations address causality in fields such as biology, neuroscience, cognitive sciences and cognitive psychology (Bechtel, 2009; Hedström & Ylikoski, 2010; Ludvigsen and Frøytlog, 2026). The argument here is that mechanistic explanations, as a meta-perspective, may improve the quality of learning research because it offers a pathway to more precise explanatory models that can be used across research communities in learning 589 sciences and CSCL. Mechanical explanation can give us deep insight in a central concern in CSCL;  the multiple levels and timescales problem in the study of collaborative learning (Ludvigsen, et al 2025). A mechanism-based explanation "...provides an answer to a how-question underlying a causal why-question. In other words, a mechanism-based explanation tells us how the cause produces its effects by describing the process by which this happens." (Ylikoski, 2018, p. 16). In the social and behavioral sciences, causal mechanisms are used to explain specific outcomes (observed in a case study or experiment, for example) and to develop theories about (social and psychological) mechanisms (Elster, 2015; Frith & Frith, 2012). Following Ylikoski (201 8), we call the former a causal scenario and the latter a mechanism scheme. An example of a mechanism scheme from sociology is Schelling's (1971) segregation mechanism, and a corresponding causal scenario is a specific application of the mechanism to explain an empirical case (e.g., Caetano & Maheshri, 2017). While a single mechanism scheme can be fairly compact, applied to hypothetical or empirical scenarios, it often needs a combination of mechanisms, all of which interact with situational context over time. Hence, simulating how the mechanisms unfol d in a specific scenario requires the use of computers to achieve inferential robustness. Computer modelling of processes takes place in many sciences, but we see very few attempts to do this in CSCL, for one exception see Abrahamson et al.  (2007).  Modelling needs a strong conceptual base both with regards to theory and methodological stances (Guest & Martin, 2021). Computer modelling is a kind o formalization strategy has been a success in science and engineering, where mathematics is the unifying language. Other forms of formalization are logics (Bringsjord, 2008) and specifications for progr amming a computer (Cooper & Guest, 2014). Whilst very rare in CSCL and the Learning Sciences in general,  formalization of theory (for example, in form of a computer model) is frequently practiced in other collaboration sciences (for instance: Cushman & Gershman, 2019; van Eijndhoven et al., 2023). We treat computational modelling as a form of theory building, different from theory testing through statistical or computational data modelling (see also Wise & Schwarz, 2017). Epistemically, we see the key function of computational models in helping to answer 'what-if' questions: what if this were the case rather than that? In other words, models have an inferential function; they support counterfactual reasoning (Woodward, 2003; Ylikoski  & Aydinonat, 2014). Based on mechanistic models, one can conceptualize a set of tools to explain collaborative learning at multiple levels. These tools could include mapping processes from behavioral data (clicks) to constructs used in developing analytic models and formalized models. Additionally, we need to create new tools for explaining learning as temporal processes. While this has been addressed in numerous qualitative studies for some time, new techniques allow for analyzing richer datasets over time based on formal models. This approach is connected to the multi-level nature of collaboration, as it moves from neuro-level to cognitive and social processes or examines learning in institutional settings. Gen-AI tools are already being used for multi-model coding (Cohn et al., 2025), conceptual refinement, and theoretical exploration (Wise, 2024). Core concepts in CSCL, such as scripts, orchestration, and many practical complexities, can now be shifted from design issues toward mechanism schemas involving modeling and formalized approaches. This transition enables us to move from mere descriptions to robust explanations. Agent-based models (ABMs), Bayesian network models, and cognitive architecture –based models (such as those based on ACT-R) each offer complementary ways of understanding group learning phenomena. ABMs simulate learning at the collective level by representi ng individuals as autonomous agents who interact according to simple behavioural rules (Wilensky & Rand, 2015). Through these interactions, emergent group-level patterns can be studied, such as how peer influence, communication structures, or heterogeneous strategies affect overall learning outcomes. New work leveraging stochastic generative AI models with memory state tracking as agents offers particualrly exciting promise here. Bayesian network models emphasise the probabilistic dependencies among variables. They can capture how beliefs and knowledge states develop within a group, making them well-suited for modelling shared reasoning processes and the propagation of misconceptions or consensus formation. Models based on cognitive architecture, like ACT-R, add a psychologically grounded layer by simulating the cognitive mechanisms of individuals, such as memory retrieval, attention, and problem-solving strategies. When extended to group contexts, these models can show how individual cognition interacts with social processes—e.g., how shared tasks are coordinated or how cognitive load influences collaboration. Taken together, these modeling approaches provide a multi-scale perspective: from micro-level cognition to meso-level interactions to macro-level emergent learning dynamics. The issues highlighted here, and more generally the challenges of knowledge integration across studies with different epistemologies within learning science and CSCL research, should encourage the community to engage in discussions about future studies and conceptual work in CSCL. Additionally, the wave related to AI/GenAI affects the CSCL field and should inspire us to reconceptualize the conditions for human learning (Baker and Reimann, 2024) as well as how to define an appropriate unit of analysis. To achieve this, both modeling and mechanical explanations could contribute steps toward strengthening CSCL theory. ## Computational modelling for data analysis

CMs are particularly needed for learning analytics in the context of collaborative learning, sometimes called collaboration analytics (Martinez-Maldonado et al., 2021). Not only can interactions between students collaborating via computer be traced in form of log files, but using ambient  and sensor technologies we can now trace interactions that take place in physical space as well, ranging from movement and gesture to eye gaze and bio signals. The wide availability of multimodal data of high temporal granularity in combination with rapidly advancing data analysis methods leads to the question of how these can inform theories of collaboration and learning.

Phrasing the question in this way might seem at putting the cart before the horse: Shouldn't it be the theories and research questions that drive the development of methods? This is a chicken-egg question of little practical relevance: Methods/instruments have always influenced what questions are raised and which explanations are suggested (Latour & Woolgar, 1986). Somewhat ironically perhaps, we need more theory because we have more and different data now, data of different kind at different levels, and more streaming data. This creates challenges for statistics and the ways we use statistics (Franke et al., 2016). For instance, measures of significance that depend on the number of observations quickly become useless as criteria for selecting important variables. Machine learning offers many other methods for identifying potentially relevant relations in large sets of data. However, a purely inductive, theory-agnostic approach is ultimately not satisfying, certainly not for advancing scientific theories. Those need to be tested more directly.  A problem we face with most collaboration theories is, however, that they have not been conceived with these new kinds of data in mind. Since the term “model” is used in many ways (for examples see Magnani and Bertolotti, 2017), Figure 1 has the purpose to make clearer the distinction between the two interpretations of relevance here: statistical models—i.e., models of data —and theoretical  models ((a) and (b) in the figure). In modern science, models are predominantly computational in nature. These computer simulations are generative: they produce model data that can be directly compared and/or via statistical tests with empirical data. Typical examples are climate models, models of disease spread, and models of school segregation. Theories are usually too general and too abstract to be compared directly with (statistical models of) empirical data. 591 Figure 1 The difference Between Testing Statistical and Theoretical Models Since statements in theories are designed to address general cases, they tend to abstract away from individual and contextual differences. In CSCL, however, these differences are essential for explaining relevant phenomena; for instance, collaboration and learning are considered mediated, if not constituted, by technologies (a type of 'context'). And both learners and context change over time; otherwise, we'd not be studying learning. Thus, the kinds of models useful for CSCL research need to provide parame ters to account for differences if not for individual learners  (or groups), then for archetypical learners  (or groups), and they need to  generate event sequences (minimally: summary statistics of those). This means models need to be executable implemented  as computer programs.

Up to now, computer models that simulate collaborative learning are much less prevalent than statistical and inductive models. This is, for example, evidenced by the absence of a single instance in the comprehensive overview of collaboration analytics methods by Martinez-Maldonado et al. (2021). Simultaneously, there is a significant amount of model-based research in other areas of collaboration research, such as on teams in organizational science (Blanco-Fernández et al., 2024). Differences in methods education between the disciplines may explain this. Most psychological researchers, for instance, are not trained in modeling beyond constructing statistical models of their data (and use off-the-shelf methods for that). Economists, on the other hand, construct models of something other thandata: semi-formalized or formalized versions of theories. However, it could also be that the 'feeder' disciplines for CSCL —psychology and computer science, most notably,—do not produce the kind of (learning) theories that candirectly inform computational models. Aside from research on tutorial dialogue, there appears to be little current theoretical foundation for developing generative models for collaborative learning that could be compared with detailed empirical data (see, e.g., Ber gner, 2017). A recent collection on theory building, published in Educational Psychology Review (Greene & Robinson, 2024), does not include any examples of formalization or computational modeling. This points to an interesting direction: CSCL itself might need to focus on theory building at a more foundational level. Computational modelling for theory development As the arrow back from Model to Theory in Figure 1 is supposed to indicate, model construction influences theory building. Epistemological questions concern not only the relationship between empirical data and the model but also between theory and model. Formalization in general and computational modeling in particular force scientists to "...explicitely document an instance of what their theory assumes, if not what their theory is." (Guest & Martin, 2021). Rather than bureaucratic accounting, these are creative yet rigorous ways to explore the specific implications of theories (Ylikoski & Aydinonat, 2014). Modeling should encourage us to think deeply about the phenomenon or capacity, both before and in addition to analyzing any data. This includes considering which assumptions in a particular model derive from the theory and which are ad hoc assumptions created solely to make the model ' work'. While some might argue that this additional epistemological burden hampers theory development, others contend that there is value in exploring models beyond mere application, such as through conceptual exploration (Ylikoski & Aydinonat, 2014).

A related point about the relationship between models and theories is that models can support counterfactual reasoning in a precise way: by adjusting model parameters and observing how these changes affect a model’s behavior, researchers can ask and answer what-if questions. This is relevant not only when interventions in the system studied are possible to create counterfactual situations through experiments, but also in fields like education, where laboratory experiments are often not feasible. This logic of counterfactual reasoning — highlighted as essential to all causal explanations by Woodward (2003)—applies equally to changing the structure of models, not just their parameters (Ylikoski & Aydinonat, 2014). 592 It is clear that theories describing mechanisms are useful for building computational models. This is because, as mentioned earlier, mechanistic explanations describe how a cause produces an effect. Suppose we have two theories that address, among other things, how subject matter knowledge influences problem-solving. Theory A simply states a causal relationship: The more domain knowledge, the better the problem-solving results (less time needed; fewer mistakes made). Theory B suggests that problem-solving takes the form of a search process through a problem space and specifies two search strategies, formalized as a backward-chaining meansends algorithm and a forward-chaining schema-based algorithm. Theory B is mechanistic: each of these search strategies is a mechanism. (Note that having a mechanism does not imply determinism: mechanisms may not always activate in the right circumstances, and they may not execute perfectly without errors, but when they are activated and run correctly, the algorithms explain how the problem will be solved). It is obvious that theory B is much closer to programming a computer model capable of executing the algorithms (see the classic work of Newell and Simon, 1972) than theory A, which provides neither a mechanism nor a formalization. Collaboration minimally involves two levels: the individual actor and a social structure such as a pair, a group, or a team. The preferred method to model these layered, laminated systems is Agent-Based Modeling (ABM), with NetLogo as an example of a software toolkit (Wilensky & Rand, 2015). Individuals are modeled as actors capable of sensing, decision-making, and acting within an environment that includes other actors among its entities. The emergence of properties at the group level, such as leadership roles (Cao et al., 2020), is modeled by explicitly defining in computational terms how actors’ actions and interactions contribute to social structures and how these, in turn, influence and constrain actors’ choices. The existence of a practice of modeling collaboration in fields like social and organizational psychology and human factors research (e.g. Lapp et al.,

2019) supports the validity of this approach. The fact that ABM —and modeling in general —is used much less

often in studies of collaborative learning highlights the need to further promote a model-based approach to theory building in collaborative learning.

To summarize, my argument is that CSCL should evolve into more of a modeling science. Notably, it should employ computational modeling not only as a technique to analyze data —as part of the methodological toolbox—but also as a way to articulate and investi gate its theories and explanations: serving as a tool for theory development. This approach would greatly enhance the robustness of theories and findings, especially if the theoretical assumptions are articulated as mechanisms.

Paper 2: Learning analytics and generative AI tools Alyssa Wise, Fanjie Li Early recognition that the growing scale, granularity, and multimodality of learning data requires and enables theories that explain its situated, dynamic and interdependent nature positioned learning analytics to attend not only how to generate insight from data but also how to connect data, analysis, and theory (Wise & Shaffer, 2015). This potential is especially strong for developing mechanistic theories of collaborative learning, as the field of learning analytics is inherently concerned with learning processes, has advanced methods for analyzing and modeling the temporal and multi-level nature of collaboration (Chen et al., 2018), and has long been concerned with integrating diverse data streams. Recent generative AI capabilities expand these possibilities, enabling more flexible, scalable, and context-sensitive analyses to surface complex interactional patterns. Learning analytics and generative AI can inform understanding of collaborative learning mechanisms in two related ways (Wise, 2024). First, they can be used to test, refine and challenge existing theories by designing analytics that operationalize conceptual models of collaborative processes and compare them to observed activity. For instance, Hou et al. (2025) applied lag-sequential analysis and sequential pattern mining to coded notes in Knowledge Forum extend understanding of  the differences between productive and improvable discussion threads. They identified distinct patterns of emotional evolution in each that may help explain previously found differences in cognitive effort and sustainment of discussion. Second, they can support new theory building by revealing previously undetectable interactional patterns. For example, Schneider et al. (2018) used eye-tracking to analyze temporal dynamics of joint visual attention, finding that shared initiation, rather than single-learner dominance, was associated with greater learning gains, thus extending joint attention theory to include the notion of visual leadership and highlighting the value of its distributed enactment. To better understand how learning analytics can advance theorizing in CSCL, it is useful to distinguish the different kinds of methodological tools that enable this work.

Learning analytics tools Tools for mapping clicks to constructs: Establishing theoretically grounded links between raw data (e.g., clicks, comments) and elements of cognitive and social activity is critical to ensuring analytic validity. This involves a 593 systematic process of defining and validating constructs across levels and timescales. For example, Lee and Tan

(2017) demonstrated how the construct of a promising idea  in knowledge-building dialogue could be identified

in Knowledge Forum by combining text mining (to reveal relationships among keywords) with network analysis (to identify highly connected ideas based on betweenness and degree centrality). Tools for modeling learning as a temporal process: Temporal learning analytics examine how collaborative activity unfolds over time, revealing sequences and transitions of interaction. Techniques such as sequence analysis and process mining make it possible to examine evolving patterns of coordination and meaningmaking. For example, Zhou and Kang (2023) modeled gaze, speech, and artifact manipulation using multilevel vector autoregression to advance theorization of joint attention as a dynamic coordination process where shifts in visual and verbal alignment support the co-construction of shared understanding in collaborative problem solving. Tools for modeling the multi-level nature of collaboration: To understand how individual and collective processes co-evolve, multi-level models link actions across individual, group, and contextual layers. Techniques such as hierarchical linear modeling and epistemic network analysis can reveal how individual activity contributes to collective meaning-making and how group dynamics, in turn, shape individual learning over time. For example, coding learners’ online interactions for cognitive, metacognitive, and regulation processes, Saqr and López-Pernas

(2023) used techniques such as stochastic process mining to trace transitions between these constructs at multiple

levels and timescales, revealing how regulation in problem-based learning emerges through an i nterplay of individual self-regulation and collective co-and shared regulation activities. Tools for working with multi-channel and physical-space data: Integrating multimodal traces, such as log data, artifacts, speech, gesture, and gaze, enables richer representations of how learners engage in in collaborative activity. Advances in computer vision, speech recognition, and data synchronization have expanded possibilities to develop richer understandings of how the embodied, material, and spatial dynamics of collaboration mediate learning processes. For example, by aligning discourse and log data  from students’ collaborative computational modeling work, Emara et al. (2021) showed how tool interactions, such as running and modifying simulations, acted as tangible anchors for co-regulation. Generative AI tools AI-augmented (multimodal) coding: Many approaches to analyzing collaborative learning build on qualitative coding of raw data according to a conceptual scheme representing cognitive, social, interactional and/or regulatory aspects of learning processes. While manual methods limited scalabi lity and attention to rich multimodal traces, early efforts towards automation using natural language processing and supervised machine methods were able to extend existing human annotation patterns to much larger quantities of data. The emergence of stochastic generative AI models opens possibilities to not only scale up human coding (Lee et al., 2025) but also improve reliability by repeating model runs until results stabilize (Ramanathan et al., 2025) and reduce the amount of human coded data needed for training using chain-of-thought and active learning methods (Cohn et al., 2024). It also facilitates integration of multimodal evidence such as gaze, gesture, and expression. The resulting volumes of rich coded data can then be subjected to temporal and mu lti-level modeling to granularly examine how multi-dimensional patterns of individual activity contribute to collective meaning-making and how group dynamics shape individual learning. Moreover, the ability to adapt AI coding models across contexts  supports cross-situational comparison of how key constructs manifest and interact under varying conditions, moving from abstract mechanisms towards contextually grounded patterns that extend CSCL theory. AI-informed conceptual  refinement: Generative AI coding can also act as a catalyst for theoretical advancement, clarifying the definitions, boundaries, and relations among collaborative learning constructs. Traditional reliability frameworks treat coder disagreement as noise, but such moments of divergence often mark theoretically generative sites where construct boundaries blur (Hammer & Berland, 2014). Zambrano et al. (2023) further observe that divergences between human and AI coders can reveal not only machine error but weaknesses in construct definitions themselves. The ability to iteratively conduct multiple coding runs, refine coding schemes, and convene ensembles of AI coders enable s researchers to explore what disagreement, uncertainty, and borderline cases reveal about conceptualizations of the cognitive, social, interactional, and regulatory elements of collaboration. Rather than flattening variability, AI affordances can be used to detect, document, and make productive use of ambiguity, transforming coding into a reflexive process that strengthens construct validity and theoretical precision. Such processes directs AI coders to identify borderline cases, shifting from binary to gradient code applications and using an AI coding committee to analyze distributions of judgments and expose recurring ambiguities (Wise, 2024). Examining AI reasoning traces then reveals underspecified definitions, overlapping categories and weak code –evidence alignments, enabling recursive refinement of coding schemes as theoretical artifacts (Ramanathan et al., 2025) and supporting the generation of informed ground truth as a dynamic synthesis of human judgment and model-derived confidence.

594 AI-partnered theoretical conjecturing: A further opportunity lies in using generative AI as a partner for inductive exploration of large volumes of collaborative learning data to surface potential categories, relationships, and salient processes. Researchers and AI engage in cycles of complementary noticing, questioning, and idea refinement to surface intriguing patterns of collaboration across transcripts, videos, or multimodal traces (Wise et al., in review). Drawing on generative AI’s representational ar chitecture, its pre-trained semantic embedding space, attention mechanisms, auto-regressive nature, and long-context capabilities, researchers can invite models to identify provisional regularities across cognitive, social, interactional, and regulatory dimensions. For example, AI might be prompted to examine antecedents to shifts in coordination or contrast episodes of disagreement a nd resolution within a student group. The AI-identified constellations serve not as findings but as provocations for interpretation, prompting researchers to interrogate why particular moments and sequences appear connected, what underlying processes might account for their coherence, and to search for confirming and disconfirming cases. Researchers can then engage with these ou tputs reflexively, recontextualizing excerpts within the broader corpus, comparing alternative framings, and documenting interpretive shifts. Such an approach extends interpretive commitments to trustworthiness as part of the process of theory building and refinement. Paper 3: CSCL Scripts and Classroom Orchestration: From Design to Mechanisms and Models Davinia Hernández-Leo In CSCL research, scripts and orchestration represent two interwoven strands of work on how to structure and manage collaborative learning in technologically mediated settings. In this section, I sketch how these learning-design-oriented approaches can be interpreted through the lens of mechanism thinking and computational modeling, and suggest links to a “theory toolbox” agenda. CSCL scripts: Scaffolding interaction via structure The notion of a collaborative learning script (or CSCL script) has been one of the key approaches to applying learning theory in the design of collaborative learning  (Vogel et al. 2017). In its broadest sense, a CSCL script defines how collaborative learning should unfold, who interacts with whom, through which tasks, and in what sequence. Scripts can be understood as a kind of mechanism scheme: they encode a conjectured process by which a designer expects the script structure to trigger certain interaction s (e.g. elaboration, negotiation, mutual regulation) to produce desired learning outcomes.  Scripts can operate at different levels of granularity. Macro-scripts define global activity structures (e.g., individual → pair → group → plenary), whereas micro-scripts scaffold discourse moves (e.g., prompting justification, critique, or elaboration). Yet, they share and combine similar mechanics. From a mechanistic perspective, a script defines components (roles, tasks), activities (argumentation, regulation), and organization (phases, temporal sequence, distributions of roles and resources, transitions between social planes). The “script mechanism” operates when these components interact to produce emergent group learning outcomes. For instance, a script may posit that “in the discussion phase, role X elaborates arguments, role Y critiques them; this debate triggers deeper elaboration and thus boosts conceptual integration.” That is a causal hypothesis in the mechanism sense. However, scripts have remained at the l evel of design rather than formal explanatory models.

In terms of modeling CSCL scripts, one line of work has focused on the development of Collaborative Learning Flow Patterns (CLFPs, e.g. Jigsaw, Pyramid) and CSCL script pattern languages, which capture recurring pedagogical designs as reusable models that link theoretical rationales to practical design structures (Hernández-Leo et al., 2010). These patterns articulate configurations of script mechanisms that are thought (based on somewhat limited but suggestive evidence) to produce productive collaboration, thus making the implicit mechanism hypotheses of successful designs more explicit and transferable. Advances in learning analytics and in multimodal, high-granularity data now make it increasingly feasible to test and refine pattern-based models empirically—linking them to fine-grained behavioral sequences observed in authentic collaborative learning scenarios.

Another strand of research has focused on formalizing CSCL scripts within educational modeling languages (e.g. such as IMS Learning Design), to enable their computational representation. These formal representations allow roles, activities, resources, and sequencing rules to be encoded in a machine-interpretable format, supporting both the authoring of script components and the specification of mechanisms that define their interrelations, as well as automated enactment. Beyond facilitating implementation and scalability, such computational representations open pathways for model-based analysis and refinement of learning designs, enabling systematic examination, simulation, and iterative improvement of the pedagogical structures that 595 underpin CSCL scripts.  The representation of CSCL scripts could be further developed as parameterized mechanism models. This involves articulating scripts as structured generative processes that specify role-dependent interaction primitives, conditional transition rules, and feedback dynamics. Such formalization would further enable the mapping of script mechanisms onto computational representations suitable for agent-based simulation and probabilistic inference. For example, under this framing, a “critique –respond–elaborate loop” constitutes a recurrent generative mechanism encoding the causal dependencies that drive argumentative learning dynamics. Estimating or simulating the parameters of such a mechanism across varying group compositions, temporal regim es, task structures, and fading actions provides a means to test mechanistic hypotheses about coordination and knowledge construction processes.

Orchestration: Managing complexity in practice While CSCL scripts primarily address the design-time structuring of collaborative learning, orchestration concerns the runtime management of this complexity as learning unfolds in real classrooms (Amarasinghe et al., 2022). The notion of orchestration emerged to capture how teachers, tools, and learners coordinate multiple parallel activities, temporal rhythms, and social configurations in technology-enhanced environments. From a mechanistic perspective, orchestration can be seen as the dynamic regulation of interacting components—scripts, tools, and participants—to sustain productive learning mechanisms under real-world constraints. A challenge in the orchestration of CSCL scenarios is the burden it  places on teachers (studied as “orchestration load”, Amarasinghe et al., 2021), prompting the field to explore technologies that can alleviate this demand—such as dashboards for monitoring and controlling scripting mechanics, or intelligent agents that provide adaptive support. Recent research has emphasized the potential of human–machine synergies in orchestration, envisioning systems where teachers and AI-based tools collaborate dynamically. Efforts in this line of work have shown how (real-time) learning analytics can guide adjustments to the scripts based on their pedagogical intentions (e.g. pattern structure) and the observed learning interactions (Amarasignhe et al., 2022). These pedagogical intentions have so far been grounded mainly in basic structural conditions for collaboration —such as ensuring sufficient group size —but they would benefit from models that capture deeper interactional and epistemic dimensions of collaboration. Such models could enable orchestration technologies to respond to the  quality of learner engagement, the emergence of shared understanding, or the alignment between collaborative processes and learning goals.

In this line, an important development has been the introduction of the “orchestration graph” metaphor, as a modeling approach that bridges the gap between script design and real-time orchestration. In this formalism, vertices represent learning activities (e.g., individual reading, small-group discussion, plenary sharing), while edges denote pedagogical transitions or dependencies between activities, often annotated with weights that capture the likelihood or strength of a transition (e.g., the probability that successful completion of activity A leads to activity B). The orchestration graph further incorporates operators—such as merge, fork, loop, and condition—to express branching, parallel processes, and feedback loops. Collectively, these elements define a stochastic process model that can be used to reason about, and even compute, probabilities of learner states and trajectories through the activity space. Conceptually, the approach enables the computation of expected activity flows, branching probabilities, and the simulation of alternative pedagogical designs. The same framework also lends itself to integration with learning analytics: learners’ paths through the orchestration graph—and their deviations from expected trajectories—can inform real-time adaptation and diagnostic feedback. Other analytics layers, such as teacher analytics, bring further opportunities for data-driven modeling in the field. For instance, Prieto et al. (2018) showed the possibility of automatically extracting orchestration graphs from multimodal classroom data —including wearable sensors, eye-tracking, audio, vi deo, and accelerometry. This work illustrates the feasibility of connecting fine-grained multimodal traces to higher-level orchestration representations, thereby highlighting their potential to inform the development of computational models that bridge the observable dynamics of classroom practice with the theoretical constructs of CSCL. Moving forward Moving toward mechanistic and model-based approaches to scripting and orchestration in CSCL involves aligning research on collaborative learning design with computational representations that capture how learning processes unfold and adapt. This perspective envisions models as systems of interlocking mechanisms —formalized detection–decision–action loops —that can be simulated, parameterized, and refined through empirical data. Agent-based and stochastic models enable “what-if” analyses of design strategies u nder uncertainty, revealing how their configurations and human variability influence collaborative dynamics. Parameter estimation and calibration further ground these models in observed learning behaviors, supporting theory refinement and data596 informed learning design. These models should be multi-level, integrating macro-level orchestration decisions with micro-level interaction mechanisms. AI offers both opportunities for the development of these models, and for their applicability to support co-design and co-orchestration frameworks —in which human and machine agents share pedagogical decision-making and regulation through sensing, prediction, and feedback. Realizing this vision, however, requires balancing formal rigor with pedagogical interpretability and fostering collaboration across disciplines and perspectives in CSCL research to ensure that computational mechanisms and models remain meaningful and actionable.

Frøytlog The learning sciences, as an interdisciplinary field of knowledge, has been maturing through the contributions of the learning societies journals (The Journal of the Learning Sciences (JLS) and The International Journal of Computer Supported Collaborative Learning (IJCSCL)), conferences, handbooks, and policy reports such as “How People Learn”. Over the last 25 years, the learning sciences has established itself as the most important field dedicated to explain human learning (Cress, Rosè, Wise & Oshima, 2021). The concerns raised in this contribution focus on whether the community of learning science scholars should develop a meta-stance to improve our  field of science/knowledge. This involves assumptions, conceptual stances, research designs, one can claim that neither statistical studies nor  qualitative studies conducted in this field are explicit about the kinds of explanations they propose or hypothesize. Similarly, although different types of effect studies provide interesting results, the explanation for both how and why it works scratch the cognitive surface and in addition it is often absent. Furthermore, qualitative studies deliver interesting and nuanced findings about students’ actions, moves and positioning, as well as the use of resources and cognitive processes, but seldom explain the mechanism involved. If mentioned in quantitative or qualitative studies, explanation is mentioned more at an intuitive and informal level than through explicit and formalized descriptions and argumentation. However, a few learning scientists have initiated such work.  James B. Greeno has been the leading scholar involved in such efforts in the learning sciences (e.g. Greeno & Engeström, 2014). Our claim is that we need studies that can address mechanisms, explanation and causality in new ways.

Considering the problem description, this contribution offers suggestions on the types of explanations that can be developed to increase the cumulative knowledge about human learning based on what is defined as mechanistic explanation (Bechtel, 2009; Glennan & Illari, 2017). A meta-stance in the learning sciences/CSCL can be considered as a response to questions about the lack of unification and formalization (Baker & Reimann, 2024). It can contribute to improving the sciences, as it transcends the use of various analytic stances and methods, bridging both epistemological and ontological differences across fields of knowledge. Mechanisms and mechanistic explanations in CSCL The new mechanistic philosophy became increasingly influential during the 1990s. In 2000, a “manifesto” was published by Machamer, Darden, and Craver (MDC) with the title Thinking about mechanism. A central concern regarding this new way of explaining scien ces, such as causality and generalization, was raised. This position in philosophy of science argues that causality  should be seen as constitutive when consider ing how generality in sciences can be explained. The turn toward mechanistic explanation emphas izing that we need to first identify a phenomenon in order to describe it. The consequence of this for the learning sciences is that we need to consider not only epistemological questions about how knowledge has developed but also about what knowledge is. One important premise that should be emphasized here is that it is the study of phenomena that constitutes mechanistic explanations. A significant definitions of the term mechanism have been proposed: “A mechanism for a phenomenon consists of entities (or parts) whose activities are organized so as to be responsible for the phenomenon” (Glennan & Illari, 2017). This definition consists of the following elements—the entities and their parts, the components, and the activities and interactions that the mechanism performs and is responsible for. This definition of mechanism is rather generative, can include a broad range of mechanisms, and can be used to describe different mechanisms in the fields of knowledge dependent on the phenomenon that one seeks to expla in. Towards formalism – learning mechanisms Learning processes is constituted through moment-by-moment actions. To describe and explain this phenomenon we propose a formalism based on communication theory, and discuss it based on concepts from the philosophy 597 of science (Ludvigsen et al 2025). To develop a common ground as part of learning processes, several interactional moves are necessary. In today's digitized environments with AI tools and infrastructures, it obviously mediate human moves both directly and indirectly through what we can call algorithmic and digitized contexts in which we perform activities.

This representation is an extension of Baker et. al, (2021). In this exchange, A and B are two participants in dialogue which unfolds in a context of tools and infrastructure. First, in move A1, A shares a proposition, P. The context of tools and infrastructure is a flexible concept which allows us to take the evolving computational resources of interest to CSCL into account. The explanation for the effects of the moves and patterns could be anchored and specified by the concept of learning mechanism. By developing mechanical explanations, we can increase preciseness, accuracy, interestingness and specify level of generality (e.g. Carton, 2024). Synthesis The four contributions offer the CSCL field a set of tools to develop more robust CSCL theories. They share the assumption that we need formalized models and a more unified language – a meta-stance. This approach would rely on analytical models and formal modeling of collaborative learning, with computational artifacts as the core phenomenon. Mechanism and mechanistic explanation can be tested using statistical methods adapted to the complexities of collaborative learning. We should build on the progress made in CSCL so far; however, in fundamental shift is needed: the types of theories used to explain social and psychological phenomena must move from variable-based to mechanistic models. Although this idea is not new in cognitive and behavioral sciences, it remains largely unrealized. This change in meta-theoretical orientation requires adopting computational modeling because theories alone cannot explain  all relevant aspect of CSCL phenomena. Theories should provide key concepts and mechanisms, but it’s through theoretical models that we can explore how these assumptions influence behavior and ultimately connect with behavioral data (Ylikoski & Aydinonat, 2014). We see no alternative way to develop theories that address the situated nature of human behavior and the emergent properties of social interactions. This will become even more crucial as genAI introduces extremely powerful tools and infrastructure that can enhance this work.
