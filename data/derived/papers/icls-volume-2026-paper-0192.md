# Distributed Cognition Meets Culturally Relevant Pedagogy: A Cognitive Journey Through Board Game Redesign

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents a qualitative thematic analysis of a focal group engaged in redesigning a board game in junior high school. Grounded in distributed cognition and culturally relevant pedagogy, we identify four interconnected themes that explain how collaborative sense-making accelerates: (1) Digital and cultural tools as semiotic resources; (2) Peer dynamics and unequal influence; (3) Distributed leadership and participation; (4) Dialogic facilitation and k nowledge construction. The findings enhance our understanding of how a group’s cognitive engagement becomes most productive when material, symbolic, and cultural resources converge, highlighting the fragile yet transformative role of cultural relevance in equitable, design-based learning.

Redesigning board games offers a valuable opportunity for collaborative learning, enabling students tonegotiate meaning, express their ideas, and collectively build knowledge through engaging activities. In the learning sciences, game-based activities are gaining popularity as design-based learning environments that foster creativity, identity development, and equity (Arnab et al., 2019; Rahimi & Kim, 2021). This paper focuses on a group of junior high students in a Canadian classroom  who redesigned a cooperative board game,  Forbidden Island. Drawing on sports culture, this group utilized shared fandom, multilingual skills, and digital tools to guide their redesign process. Their journey showed periods of strong teamwork, interspersed with challenges such as uneven roles, feelings of isolation, and fragile inclusion. To understand how students’ cultural resources and material tools influence cognition and participation, we utilized two theoretical frameworks, namely, distributed cognition and culturally relevant pedagogy. Distributed cognition considers learning as an emerging property that involves people, tools, and culturally organized practices (Hutchins, 2000). Culturally relevant pedagogy recognizes students’ cultural identities and lived experiences as resources for learning (Ladson-Billings, 2014). While they both highlight collaborative and situated aspects of learning, they are rarely examined together in empirical classroom contexts. This study addresses that gap by analyzing how distributed cognition unfolds within a culturally anchored design-based project. The following research questions guided the study: How do students’ social interactions, cultural resources, and material tools converge to mediate collaborative design thinking during a board game redesign task?

## Theoretical background

Distributed Cognition situates learning within a framework that encompasses people, artifacts, and symbolic systems, rather than limiting it solely to an individual's  mind. Cognition emerges through coordinated activity, where knowledge is shared, externalized, and mediated by tools and cultural representations. It  highlights three interdependent dimensions of knowledge distribution:  Social distribution,  where cognitive activity is shared among peers through dialogue, negotiation, and role-taking; Symbolic distribution, where language, diagrams, metaphors, and other semiotic resources function as scaffolds for reasoning; and  Physical distribution,  where tools and artifacts offload cognitive effort, extending learners’ capacities (Boyle et al., 2023; Hollan et al., 2000; Hutchins, 2000). From this perspective, learning involves not only individual sense-making but also the systemic organization of people, tools, and representations within a specific context. Analyses of distributed cognition thus foreground how artifacts shift roles, how collaboration evolves, and how meaning is constructed through collective rather than solitary processes (Xu & Clarke, 2012). Culturally relevant pedagogy advances an asset-based approach that validates students’ cultural identities while promoting academic achievement and critical consciousness (Caingcoy, 2023). Rather than treating culture as peripheral, it embeds learners’ lived experiences into the curriculum, positioning them as resources for engagement and understanding. Key tenets of this approach include: cultural affirmation, i.e., instructional practices that validate and celebrate students’ identities; equitable participation, i.e., structures that ensure all voices contribute meaningfully; and critical engagement, i.e., awareness of how social and cultural dynamics shape learning (Gay, 2018; Ladson-Billings, 2014).  In collaborative design contexts, it highlights how students’ funds of knowledge (Gonzalez et al., 2006) can serve as 1442 legitimate resources for design, meaning-making, and identity expression. Both frameworks share a commitment to situated, relational views of learning. Distributed cognition  recognizes the spread of knowledge across people and artifacts, where culture plays a vital role (Tomasello et al., 2005), while culturally relevant pedagogy highlights how cultural identities shape participation and engagement (Caingcoy, 2023). Theoretically, bringing these two approaches emphasizes how both culture and tools play important roles in learning. Cultural anchors (e.g., metaphors, multilingual practices) help students connect new concepts to lived experience, while material tools (e.g., digital devices, artifacts) gain cognitive value only when embedded in familiar cultural references. Despite this alignment, empirical research at this intersection remains sparse, particularly in design-based learning contexts where students’ cultural resources are explicitly mobilized. This study employed a Design-Based Research (DBR) approach, enabling us to investigate learning processes in situ, while attending to the evolving interplay between tools, tasks, and cultural resources (The Design-Based Research Collective, 2003). The research was conducted in a suburban Canadian junior high school with a culturally and linguistically diverse student body. Data were collected across 10 redesign sessions, including video recordings, transcripts, field notes, and artifacts. We conducted a thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006), combining deductive coding (guided by distributed cognition and culturally relevant pedagogy constructs) and inductive coding (emerging from the data). The unit of analysis was interactional episodes where collaborative design decisions occurred. Themes were iteratively refined through collaborative discussions among the research team, with ongoing comparison across data sources to enhance analytic rigor. Using the theoretical framing as our analytic tool, we examined how students make sense of what they are learning in ways that feel real and relevant to them. The distributed cognition was examined across social (roles and influence), symbolic (language, metaphors, representations), and material (tools and artifacts) dimensions, while cultural relevance was examined through cultural affirmation, equitable participation, and critical engagement. Integrating these lenses allowed us to analyze how cultural resources shape what becomes visible, valued, and shared within the distributed learning episodes. This integrated perspective shifts the analysis from coordination alone to under which conditions coordination becomes equitable. In this paper, we discuss a group that comprises three students: Milo, Amir, and Mo (all pseudonyms), who redesigned the game, Forbidden Island  (Figure 1a; Leacock, 2010), and created a soccer-themed game called Football Legends. Students were from different cultural backgrounds and had some fluency in Arabic Language. Football, also known as soccer, is one of the most popular sports worldwide. The group anchored their redesign in soccer culture, drawing on their shared enthusiasm for the sport. Football Legends featured a total of 22 Trophy cards and 24 Stadium/Player cards (Figure 1b). Instead of keeping the cooperative game structure in its original game, the group decided to design a competitive game that 2 or 4 players can play. Game mechanisms were modeled after a real soccer game, such as having 11 players per team. VAR (video assistant referee) check, Tie Breakers, etc. The main goal of the game is to collect all the trophy cards, including the World Cup, the Europa League, and the UEFA Champions League (UCL). Below, we highlight three themes that emerged from our analysis. Digital and cultural tools as semiotic resources Students transformed mobile phones and laptops from classroom distractions into powerful cognitive tools. When Milo used his computer to share stadium images (Figure 2b), it sparked animated discussions as Omar and teammates visualized design possibilities together. "Let’ s copy this stadium," and "How do you draw these curves?" they wondered, as abstract ideas took concrete form.  Milo’s laptop became a bridge connecting talk, gestures, and sketches, anchoring shared attention to their design challenge. Students also incorporated cultural 1443 elements into their learning through soccer metaphors, team loyalties, and multilingual exchanges. Soccer provided familiar language for understanding rules, allowing students to map structures onto designs (Figure 2a). "Five golden cards win the Champions  League; three get Europa,"  Amir explained excitedly. Multilingual counting "Waahid, Ithnaan, Thalatha" (Arabic counting) created rhythms for turn-taking. These cultural touchstones became symbolic tools that energized participation and sparked creativity, embodying culturally relevant pedagogy (Ladson-Billings, 2014).

However, success was not automatic. Without teacher guidance, rich cultural moments stayed confined within small groups and were never shared as resources. When these lively discussions were turned into formal worksheets, students’ writing often grew vague, with procedures that did not match the clarity of their spoken language. Cultural anchors that united the group sometimes caused divisions, as playful teasing about team preferences occasionally marginalized one of them  -- Mo had a different team preference from others. This revealed a crucial insight: while cultural and digital resources significantly expanded students ’ meaning-making repertoires, they needed deliberate scaffolding to become public, testable tools for collaboration (Tomasello et al., 2005). The most successful moments happened when teachers provided clear frameworks, such as  live rule-card templates, structured role rotations, and call-in practices that helped students transform culturally rich conversations into formal artifacts without losing their authentic voice or reinforcing social hierarchies. These moments illustrate symbolic distribution and cultural affirmation, where language, metaphors, and lived experiences function as shared cognitive resources.

Peer dynamics and unequal influence Peer interactions fostered both cognitive development and group cohesion by providing a safe environment for tentative ideas. When  Mo asked, “Do we need the fire [a symbol in the original game]  or not?”, classmates responded with encouragement (Amir: The fire we don’t need, we already have enough of the cards for fire.), keeping the design challenge lively and flexible. This collaborative process transformed their discussions into lasting artifacts, i.e., rules, cards, and sketches. Endorsements from poorly engaged peers, however, significantly impacted the final designs, while feedback from highly engaged individuals was sometimes overlooked. For example, Milo, who initially disengaged, made major decisions: “But I think we’ll go with the first one” (increasing the number of trophy cards) while the ideas of Mo, who was a consistently engaged member, were sometimes disregarded by other members (Amir: “Mo, we don’t need game pieces, that’s it.”). Groups in designbased learning share not only information and tools but also care, confidence, and status  that influence which ideas are adopted into the shared workspace (Bastani & Kim, 2022). To balance influence in the team, members used call-ins, rotated engagement roles, and employed low-stakes sentence starters like “Can we test our game?", and used live templates/worksheets to quickly capture ideas. They also publicly acknowledged contributions, and personal disclosures helped foster group rapport. Boundary-setting phrases such as "You can’t say that word” during conversations served as a protective accountability measure, especially when framed as an invitation, but could become exclusionary if used as public shaming. Effective facilitation focused on using invitational language to sustain community norms without alienating members. Therefore, participation in distributed systems depends not only on access to tasks but also on how influence circulates unevenly, raising critical questions about whose ideas become part of the collective design.

Distributed leadership and participation Participation was further shaped by coordination structures that organized the distribution of work, responsibility, and authorship within the group. Leadership did not reside in a single individual but emerged as a shifting, activitydependent process across phases such as ideation, testing, and documentation. Students adopted roles such as tester, recorder, and rules-writer, which helped distribute cognitive labor and enabled multiple contributors to 1444 shape the evolving design. For example, Milo and Amir jointly navigated the task of stadium drawing, demonstrating reciprocal guidance and fluid role exchange rather than fixed hierarchies. However, the presence of roles alone did not guarantee meaningful participation. In some instances, role enactment remained procedural. Brief check-ins (e.g., “State your roles”) reflected surface-level compliance, where responsibilities were named but not actively taken up in practice. In contrast, when roles were tied to visible contributions such as documenting decisions or leading testing cycles, students engaged more substantively, and authorship became more distributed. Supporting structures, including prompts, templates, and moments of reflection, sometimes helped clarify responsibilities and sustain engagement, but their effectiveness depended on how they were integrated into ongoing activity. When embedded within the workflow, these structures supported coordination; when treated as standalone requirements, they tended to produce compliance rather than collaboration. Overall, distributed leadership functioned as both a mechanism for organizing group activity and a site where participation could either expand or narrow. These findings suggest that equitable collaboration requires not only the assignment of roles but also the continuous alignment of those roles with meaningful contribution, ensuring that responsibility and influence remain mobile rather than fixed.

Dialogic facilitation and knowledge construction Ms. Clara (Science) and Mr. Ken (Humanities) were the teachers who played a crucial role in influencing the interaction between students, tools, and representations. When teachers adopted dialogic stances, posing open-ended questions, modeling possibilities, and responding contingently, students maintained ownership of their ideas and progressed from exploratory talk to developing testable designs. For example, rather than seeking approval, Clara articulated game mechanics and design criteria aloud, externalizing her thinking into shareable knowledge. In contrast, directive interventions often constrained student authorship. Commands such as “Fix the game” reduced open-ended design to compliance, without redistributing cognitive work. Dialogic scaffolds extended students’ symbolic reach by incorporating cultural and material resources, such as digital tools, sketches, and soccer metaphors, into shared problem spaces, supporting the transformation of ideas into durable artifacts. Responsive facilitation was most effective when teachers used exploratory probes (e.g., “How would we know it’s fair?”), which amplified student framings while providing necessary structure through tools like rule cards and test logs. While time-bound directives were occasionally useful for pacing, overuse limited participation and idea development. Overall, dialogic facilitation expanded collaborative meaning-making and shared authorship, whereas directive approaches constrained the circulation of ideas, highlighting how facilitation shapes both material and symbolic distribution in the learning system (Ladson-Billings, 2014). The findings show that collaborative design flourishes at the intersection of cultural meaning, material tools, and facilitative practices, but only when these elements are intentionally aligned. Cultural resources are not merely contextual but function as  active semiotic and relational mediators that shape how ideas emerge, circulate, and stabilize within group activity. At the same time, culturally relevant pedagogy becomes actionable in design-based learning only when students’ cultural anchors are trans lated into shared, materialized artifacts that can be collectively tested and refined. Across the three themes, we identify recurring tensions that structure equitable collaboration in practice: between cultural expression and formalization, where rich ideas risk remaining private unless externalized; between participation and status, where influence is unevenly distributed despite shared tasks; and between dialogic and directive facilitation, where the teacher moves either to expand or constrain the system’s cognitive possibilities. These tensions highlight that equitable collaboration is not an inherent outcome of group work, but an achievement that depends on how social, symbolic, and material resources are coordinated. By situating our analysis at the intersection of these two interconnected concepts of learning, this study contributes to the learning sciences in three ways. First, it extends distributed cognition by showing how cultural resources function as active semiotic and relational mediators of collaborative thinking. Second, it operationalizes culturally relevant pedagogy within design-based learning by demonstrating how cultural anchors must be transformed into shared, materialized artifacts to support collective work. Third, it identifies key tensions between cultural expression and formalization, participation and status, and dialogic and directive facilitation that shape equitable collaboration.
