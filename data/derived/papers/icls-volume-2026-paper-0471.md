# Beyond Symbols: Highlighting Multilingual Learners’ Mathematical Practices in Integrated STEM Space

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how multilingual middle school students engaged in mathematical practice during a collaborative engineering design challenge in an integrated STEAM summer program. While mathematics in school contexts is often reduced to symbolic procedures, our analysis reveals that students engaged in rich mathematical practices through spatial reasoning, inference-in-action, generalization, and collaborative problem-solving. Grounded in sociocultural and embodied theories of learning, the study demonstrates how mathematics emerged as a dynamic, bodily, and socially mediated practice embedded in material interaction. Recognizing these practices expands conventional definitions of mathematics, foregrounds the contributions of students from nondominant communities, and underscores the importance of centering mathematics as a vibrant component of STEM education.

Research over several decades has shown that mathematics is frequently conceptualized through formal language, symbolic notation, and conventional disciplinary discourse (Martin, 2009; Nemirovsky, 1994; Radford, 2009). This framing emphasizes procedural fluency while overlooking the embodied, material, and social dimensions of mathematical reasoning—producing what Peterson (2005) called “number numbness” (p. 10), a detachment from meaning rooted in the systematic separation of mathematics from students’ lived experiences (Gutiérrez, 2013; Barwell et al., 2007). When mathematics is recognized only in canonical, symbolic-discursive forms, we risk obscuring the generative practices that emerge among multilingual students from historically nondominant communities (Gutiérrez, 2002; Marshall, 2023).

This narrow conceptualization is particularly consequential in integrated STEM environments — makerspaces, robotics labs, design camps —where students routinely engage in problem-solving, spatial reasoning, and logical coordination (Kayumova et al., 2025; V ossoughi et al., 2016). Scholars have documented how mathematics in many interdisciplinary STEM models becomes subordinated, functioning as a tool for measurement rather than as a domain of conceptual inquiry (English, 2016; Kelley & Knowles, 2016). When mathematical practices emerge through collaborative, embodied activities such as engineering design, they are often overlooked precisely because they exceed dominant, school-sanctioned representations of what counts as legitimate mathematics (Baldinger et al., 2020).

## Theoretical framework

Building on sociocultural theory (Vygotsky, 1978) and embodied approaches to mathematics education, this study conceptualizes mathematical activity as a socially and materially mediated practice distributed across people, artifacts, and bodily actions. Nemirovsky’s (1994) notion of body-syntonic abstraction and Radford’s (2009) theory of semiotic mediation demonstrate that bodily and gestural engagement can serve as the foundation for abstraction and generalization. Arzarello et al. (2002) further documente d how gestures and spatial orientations act as semiotic resources through which learners construct meaning; Ma (2017) extends this lineage by emphasizing multi-party, whole-body interaction.

Following Moschkovich (2024), we treat mathematical practice as a culturally organized form of communication and reasoning that includes modeling, generalizing, and sense-making, rather than mere procedural execution. This perspective allows us to recogniz e multilingual learners’ full communicative repertoires—speech, gesture, movement, and material interaction —as legitimate mathematical resources, foregrounding embodiment and multilingualism as epistemic assets (Gutiérrez, 2017; Kayumova et al., 2025; Nasir et al., 2008).

Proceeding from a sociocultural–embodied stance, we designed an instrumental case study (Stake, 1995) situated in a two-week summer STEAM program in the Northeastern U.S. serving multilingual middle school students. 2842 We focus on one focal pair —Inez and Robert —whose design and programming of Arduino-based autonomous vehicles provided sustained opportunities for embodied, multilingual reasoning. We collected ten hours of multiangle video to capture gesture, gaze, spatial arrangement, tool manipulation, and talk, supplemented by fieldnotes and analytic memos. Transcription was multimodal: alongside speech, we documented pointing, aligning, rotating, and reorienting—bodily moves that often carry the work of generalization and inference. Analytically, we employed Interaction Analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995), informed by Ma’s (2017) account of multi-party, whole-body interaction and Radford’s (2009) semiotic mediation. Through iterative viewings, we identified “hot spots” (Ferguson et al., 2018) —junctures of uncertainty or transition when mathematical sense-making was most audible. Following Schoenfeld (2020), we treat students’ multimodal contributions as mathematical practice itself, not as approximations of some canonical standard. Our data reveal four recurring dimensions of mathematical practice: spatial reasoning, mathematical inference-in-action, generalization, and collaborative problem-solving. These practices emerged through the students’ physical and collaborative engagement with materials, tools, and each other throughout the engineering design and troubleshooting process.

Spatial Reasoning. Students frequently oriented, aligned, and repositioned materials to ensure proper fit and functionality. Robert rotated the chassis across multiple orientations to simulate how weight distribution would affect performance; Inez repeatedly rotated the chas sis from right to left while securing a motor, demonstrating deepening familiarity with geometric alignment and physical constraints —not through abstract formulas, but through materially grounded sense-making. Mathematical Inference-in-Action. When the robot moved in circles rather than straight, Inez and Robert collaboratively traced the problem to a mismatch in wheel direction. Their decision to reverse the wiring —rather than replace components —was not a rote fix; it was a principled adjustmen t grounded in iterative observation, directional feedback, rotational symmetry, and the relational behavior of parts within a system. Generalization. Inez’s choice to skip redundant steps—noting “This one already has screws” and moving to the next component —reflected an emerging generalization strategy. Her actions appear informed by accumulated perceptual knowledge and material familiarity, allowing her to abstract patterns from prior experience and apply them efficiently in new contexts.

Collaborative Problem-Solving. Throughout the task, Inez and Robert co-constructed solutions through dialogue and distributed tool use. When Robert encountered difficulty inserting a screw in a confined space, he handed the screwdriver to Inez, who adapted her grip and eventually shifted to using her fingers. These negotiated, complementary roles illustrate how mathematical sense-making was socially distributed across participants. This study demonstrates how collaborative engineering design reveals rich mathematical practices that school contexts routinely overlook. Beyond formal symbols and formulas, mathematics emerged as a lived, embodied, and social practice (Gutiérrez, 2017). The four practices identified —spatial reasoning, inference-in-action, generalization, and collaborative problem-solving—mirror the abstraction and coordination central to professional engineering, and together they highlight the breadth of mathematical knowledge students bring to design ecologies. Recognizing such forms expands narrow definitions of mathematics, valuing embodied reasoning, teamwork, and multilingual sense-making (Barwell et al., 2007; Gutiérrez, 2002; Kahveci et al., 2025; Kayumova & Kahveci, 2023; Nasir et al., 2008). Reframing mathematics as a multifaceted practice embedded in design and collaboration affirms diverse students’ capabilities, promotes equity, and connects classroom learning to authentic interdisciplinary STEM work.
