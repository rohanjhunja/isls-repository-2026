# Students’ Journeys with Boundary Tools: Comparing Home and Classroom Encounters with a Lab Experiment

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Science learning is often confined to culturally scripted spaces such as classrooms, laboratories, and museums, each carrying expectations for how inquiry should unfold. This paper examines what happens when a science tool is intentionally designed to move across those spaces as a boundary tool. “Lab-on-a-Book” is a low-cost, portable kit embedded in a journal format for conducting chemical experiments. We analyze two enactments of the same cyanotype activity (one by an individual student at home and another in a secondary classroom) to explore how learners orient toward experimentation (how they question, hesitate, proceed, or seek validation). Findings show how the tool’s material and cultural design enable distinct forms of inquiry and reflection across contexts, positioning portability as a design strategy for expanding where and how science learning can occur.

Studies of science learning have traditionally focused on fixed spaces such as classrooms, laboratories, and museums (Borch & Svabo, 2025). Each of these settings carries cultural expectations about what inquiry should look like: laboratories often emphasize rigor and instrumentation, classrooms foreground curriculum and task completion, and museums can invite curiosity and display (Latour & Woolgar, 2013; Allen, 2004; Lemke, 2001). While these spaces enable particular forms of participation, they can also limit how learners experiment, improvise, or draw connections across contexts (Harris & Ballard, 2021; Gutiérrez, 2016). Research on learning ecologies has shown that knowledge develops across homes, schools, and outdoor environments (Barron, 2006), yet sc ience education is still often treated as segmented across these sites, with practices studied in isolation (Marandino, 2017). Rather than examining settings separately, we ask what becomes visible when a science design is made deliberately portable and intended to travel with students into their own environments. By enabling activities to move across contexts, we can observe how cultural expectations can shape inquiry and open possibilities for new blends of practice (Vadeboncoeur et al., 2014; Tinker & Krajcik, 2011). Lab-on-a-Book was created with this intention: it integrates reactive papers, embedded materials, and open-ended experiments into a low-cost, notebook-sized kit designed to accompany learners beyond institutional settings – into homes, classrooms, or other everyday spaces (Sotério & Blikstein, 2025). In this paper, we examine two early enactments of the same activity: one by an individual student working at home and another within a secondary classroom. In doing so, we consider portability not merely as a technical feature but as a cultural reframing that unsettles assumptions about where and how science learning occu rs (Harris & Ballard, 2021; Stromholt & Bell, 2018). Here, portability is the design feature, while contextual reshaping of inquiry is the phenomenon under study. We ask: How does inquiry change when a portable science inquiry tool moves across contexts, and what differences in engagement does this movement reveal?

## Theoretical framework

Cultural expectations of science learning spaces Science learning has often been examined within the boundaries of formal institutions, yet research in learning ecologies has demonstrated that knowledge develops across multiple settings (Barron, 2006; National Research Council, 2009). These studies empha size that learning is not confined to a single site, but is accumulated and reconfigured as learners move through different experiences and relationships. However, science education has typically treated these sites as distinct domains, each governed by cultural expectations about what counts as legitimate inquiry: in classrooms, inquiry is frequently shaped by curricular goals and task completion (Bang et al., 2013; Lemke, 2001); laboratories emphasize precision, control, and specialized equipment (Latour & Woolger, 2013); museums and science centers invite curiosity, observational engagement, choice, and control (Falk & Storksdieck, 2005; Harris & Ballard, 2021). These expectations not only structure how learners participate but also constrain the forms of  experimentation, uncertainty, or improvisation that are permitted (Bang et al., 2013). Examining how the same science activity unfolds across different settings can help make these contextual 2051 constraints visible. This perspective views contexts as active forces that shape learners’ epistemic stances: the ways they approach, question, and make sense of scientific phenomena (Harris & Ballard, 2021).

## Context, portability, and boundary tools

Boundary objects  are artifacts that move across environments and are interpreted differently by different communities, supporting learning through mechanisms such as identification (recognizing differences between practices), coordination (enabling activity across environ ments), reflection (reinterpreting practice through comparison), and transformation (developing new practices through interaction) (Gutiérrez, 2016; Akkerman & Bakker, 2011). Differences in how inquiry is enacted across environments are reinforced by a situative perspective that frames learning as participation in distinct activity systems shaped by interactions between learners, tools, and local norms (Greeno & Engeström, 2006). When a tool moves through kitchens, classrooms, or public spaces, it does not simply adapt tonew settings; it encounters environments with their own cultural scripts for inquiry (Latour & Woolgar, 2013; National Research Council, 2009). While the literature on boundary objects has offered important insights into how  artifacts operate across settings, less attention has been paid to how tools might be intentionally designed to support movement between learning settings. In this paper, we build on this tradition by examining how learners interact with Lab-on-a-Book, a tool intentionally designed to travel across learning environments (Sotério & Blikstein, 2015). We use the term “boundary tools” to emphasize their role as intentionally designed boundary objects that support movement across learning environments through design features such as portability. In this view, portability is treated not as a technical feature but as a cultural stance rooted in a commitment to sustaining inquiry wherever learners engage with science (Harris & Ballard, 2021; Lemke, 2001). This perspective allows us to examine how mobility may enable forms of engagement that structured environments might not reveal and how inquiry practices are reconfigured as they move across contexts. Portability operates here as an analytic probe to examine early boundary-crossing processes.

## Research design

This study adopts design-based research principles to report on early enactments from an iterative design cycle informing design refinements (Cobb et al., 2003). Our analysis examines how a portable science toolkit is enacted in different contexts and what  these enactments reveal about the cultural conditions of inquiry. Guided by the perspective that learners’ engagement is shaped by context, we approach portability as a conceptual lens, using the movement of a single tool across settings to explore how environments invite different forms of experimentation. This orientation aligns with our broader aim to understand how designed mobility navigates the affordances and constraints of distinct learning environments.

## Context and participants

Data were drawn from two early enactments of the same cyanotype experiment using Lab-on-a-Book, a notebooksized science kit that embeds chemical materials and open-ended prompts, paired with a custom AI chatbot. The first enactment involved a 12-year-old learner who conducted the experiment at home in the United States as part of an exploratory pilot; the second took place in a secondary school classroom with a group of 14 students aged 14 during a scheduled science class facilitated by the research team i n Hong Kong, China. These two cases were selected for being contrastive, illuminating how context can shape how they question, proceed, collaborate, or seek confirmation. While the experiment remains the same (cyanotype), each case represents a distinct encounter between learner, tool, and environment. All participants provided guardian consent in accordance with institutional research protocols.

Both enactments were documented through video recordings, photographs, and researcher field notes, focusing on how participants interacted with materials, followed or deviated from prompts, and verbalized reasoning. manipulation, hesitation, collaboration, or validation). Second, interpretive memos traced how these forms related to contextual features such as available space, materials, and social in teraction. Analytic observations were discussed among authors to refine interpretations and consider multiple possibilities, prioritizing contextual insight. The goal was not to compare performance, but to characterize the affordances of each context for inquiry and sense-making. Patterns emerging from these analyses informed our discussion of portability as a cultural and design principle.

Classroom encounter with the boundary tool In the classroom, students worked in small groups, speaking quietly within their clusters but rarely across them. Time was externally defined, and they moved collectively through each step following the instructor’s cues. They gathered around others’ work only when invited, waiting for their turn to present. Even anticipation was synchronized, as students counted down minutes while watching the clock. The experiment became a coordinated progression rather than an individual exploration. Procedures were treated as fixed, even when unsaid. Students quickly adopted recognizable roles: some acted as guides, explaining steps or directing peers, while others deferred and asked, “Is this right?”  Interaction centered on collective troubleshooting, as groups adjusted exposure times or repositioned objects in response to one another’s suggestions. Within this structured setting, students still found small pockets of informality: quiet jokes and brief laughter that ran alongside the official task, forming a subtle layer of peer connection. The chatbot, though available, remained peripheral. It was us ed for confirmation rather than conversation, often to verify procedural details. Some students shared responses with nearby tables, resulting in repeated prompts across logs. Most questions mirrored the book’s language, seeking factual validation rather than personal explanation. At home, the toolkit supported personal exploration, inviting hesitation, reflection, and wonder. The student wrote in her own terms, blended science with everyday talk, and worked at her own pace. In the classroom, the same design became procedural and collective, shaped by peer comparison and time constraints. These enactments illustrate inquiry as a contex t-based practice (Morris, 2025; Bang et al., 2012). The home setting afforded interpretive flexibility, while the classroom foregrounded accountability and correctness (Stroupe, 2014; Lemke, 2001). Even the chatbot reflected these contrasts: at home, it functioned as a conversational partner; in school, as a search tool for quick validation (Solyst et al., 2024; Luckin & Holmes, 2016). Such differences show how contexts shape how learners question, interpret, and deviate (Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003). Portability makes these epistemic boundaries visible by crossing them (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011; National Research Council, 2009).

From a design perspective, portability functions less as a technical feature than as a cultural reframing (Harris & Ballard, 2021). Boundary tools should anticipate multiple enactments rather than a single stable use. Their potential lies in material and cultural traits that support this elasticity: affordability, which lowers barriers; hackability, which supports modification without fear of loss; adaptability  across settings; and alignment with familiar cultural forms (Horn, 2018), such as books that travel with learners. Ownership of the book supports sustained engagement, often absent in time-bound lab activities (Bevins & Price, 2016). Portable tools allow inquiry to unfold across time and space, enabling learners to revisit and extend their work. We view these boundary tools as emancipatory (Freire, 1996) in that they enable mobility, repetition, and appropriation, thereby widening access to hands-on science. When science moves, it adapts to the hidden curriculum of its surroundings (Ingold, 2011). Portable designs make these shifts visible by revealing the many ways scientific inquiry can live. Taken together, these cases illustrate early boundary-crossing mechanisms revealed by the tool’s mobility. The contrast between enactments makes visible processes such as identification (differences in expectations for experimentation), coordination (adapt ation of the same toolkit to different participation structures), and reflection (how experimentation is taken up differently across settings). Moments of negotiation also appeared in how participants interpreted instructions, sought validation, and aligne d their actions with local norms. While transformation cannot yet be observed, these early encounters suggest how portable designs may create conditions for boundary-crossing learning.
