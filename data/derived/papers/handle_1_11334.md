# Orienting Attention to Natural Co-Participants: How and When Educators Prioritize Material Sensing in a Nature-Based Preschool

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In nature-based learning (NBL), the natural material environment is understood to actively shape student learning and foster positive human-nature relationships. However, the process through which the natural environment shapes student learning in NBL environments is under-researched. I contribute to this area by examining the relationship between situational variables and how educators orient learners’ attention to natural material co-participants. By analyzing video collected in a 4-week nature-based preschool day camp, we find that educators vary their approach to orienting the preschoolers’ attention on the basis of whether the children are already engaged with an activity, if there is a need to redirect children’s actions, and how ephemeral the experience of the natural co-participant is. These varying approaches ultimately reinforce particular messages about nature-culture relations, including a hierarchy of importance among natural co-participants and human supremacy.

## Background

Developing positive, respectful relationships betwe en humans and the natural world is necessary for  a ddressing ongoing environmental collapse. Nature-based learning (NBL), which refers to a wide variet y of educational designs that involve engagement with natural materials and spaces, posits that simply en gaging with natural materials or being in nature is enough to trigger r elationships of environmental stewardship or human responsibility for preserving nature (Kuo et al., 2 019). However, as work focusing on both Indigenous natureculture relations (e.g., McDaid Barry et al., 2023) and work utilizing Indigenous onto-epistemology to understand non-Indigenous contexts (e.g., Hecht & Nelson, 2022; Kr ishnamoorthy, 2023) make clear, fostering particula r relations does not come from co-presence alone but is strongly shaped by the intera ctions introduced and supported by human social actors (Bang & Marin, 2015). This study addresses the need for detailed examination of how human-human-natural co-participant interactions shape what is learned in NBL spaces by focusi ng on how educators at one NBL preschool actively oriente d children’s attention toward natural co-participants, identifying key contextual factors that shaped inte ractional patterns. I then propose that th is collective of interactions result in communication of particular nature-culture relations, even when those messages where n ot the primary aim of the educators.

Sociocultural processes in NBL Social factors such as the immediate influence of other humans and the macroscale impact of cultural norms shape all learning spaces and processes (Rogoff, 2003). S ociocultural theories of learning focus analytic at tention on how culture shapes individual development (Vygotsky  & Cole, 1981), both through how education is organ ized and through social interactions within learning environments. Any intentional educational space involves learners and more-knowledgeable-others who facilitate learning by provide direct instruction, demonstrate activities, ask guiding questions, or simply set up the educational  space. Ultimately, more-knowledgeable-others steer learners toward particular ways of knowing and being in the world, which are inherently cultural. These s ociocultural processes are also present in NBL environments, whe re educators serve as the more-knowledgeable-others directing student attention (Kloos et al., 2018), offering explanations (Skalstad & Munkegye, 2022), and framing “appropriate” activities in particular spaces (Mackinder, 2017). Further, natur e itself-including both living and non-living entities and larger systems-can serve as a more-knowledgeable-others, if participants orient to them as having something to offer (Simpson, 2014). Within preschool NBL environments, prior research identified that educators often see their role as facilitating adve nturous or risky play (LeMasters & Vandermaas-Peeler, 2023), deferring to student interests (Cerino, 2023), and supporting skill building (Bergan  et al., 2024). As Prins and colleagues (2022, p. 10) stated in their summary of  research on teacher roles in nature play situation s, “the character of the teachers’ mediation between children and between children and the environment influences how the aff ordances of the nature-based environment are actualized.” In other words, social and cultural forces, particularly as enacted through NBL educators, fund amentally shape what child-material interactions are made possible and, ultimately, what relationships are fostered. For the purposes of understanding how  everyday interactions shape NBL outcomes, I highlight two contributing processes: meso-level nature-culture relations, and micro-level joint attention.

Socializing nature-culture relations NBL empirical research consistently finds student g ains in terms of nature connectedness, feelings of environmental stewardship, and ecological knowledge (Kou et al., 2019). However, each of these gains reflects a particular form of nature-culture relation or cultural narratives about moral, ontological, and epistemic relationships between humans, non-humans, and more-than-humans (Bang,  2015; Pitt, 2018). For example, various onto-epistemic theories-including new materialism (Barad, 2007; Haraway, 20 16) and Indigenous knowledges (Cajete, 2000) - acknowledge the multidirectional impact of all enti ties’ agency and emphasize the interconnectedness of all beings. In Indigenous education, t his form of nature-culture relation might be realized in how people treat more-than-human others as responsive partners (McDaid Barry e t al., 2023) and in kinship relationships (Engman & Hermes, 2021). This perspec tive stands in stark contrast to dominant Western n atureculture relations, which tend to emphasize the non-agency of more-than-humans compared to humandominance (Bang & Marin, 2015) and the need for stewardship relationships between humans and Land (Stapleton & Lynch, 2021; see Table 1 for a summary of differences). Wh ile there are NBL studies that use material agency as a lens to interpret empirical data (e.g., Änggård, 2016; H arwood & Collier, 2017), these studies stop short o f claiming that participants themselves held this perspective. In stu dies that have focused on educator talk in NBL spac es, the focus has largely fallen on how educators suppo rt connections between child-nature experiences and factual or curricular content (e.g., Kloos et al., 2018) or  reinforcement of particular cultural narratives su ch as gender (e.g., Nugent et al., 2019). As work related to nature-culture relations and larger sociocultural theory make clear, what educators themselves perceive and thus teach a bout the nat ural world is critical to the perspectives that children will develop. As such, I pull inspiration from studies within the Learning Sciences regarding how natureculture relations are socialized (Bang et al., 2015), including attention to time (McDaid Barry et al., 2023), morethan-human co-presence (Engman & Hermes, 2021), and use of agency  markers in language (Konopasky & Sheridan, 2016). Using these frameworks as sensitiz ing constructs keeps attention on which nature-culture relations are made locally relevant or not by NBL educators. When an educator actively orients attention toward a natural co-participant, this is an intentional, concerted act, signaling there is something particularly impo rtant about that moment. While the concept of joint  attention-or coordination of sensory activities between two o r more people toward a single object or event-has largely been studied in terms of infant and very early childhood development (Moore & Dunahm, 1995), there is growing literature on its interactional components throughout life. For example, children will often direct th eir parents to “look” as a request for visual attention and assess ment of the child’s actions (Tam, 2021).  Joint attention is not limited to visual orientation. Studies of touch, ta ste, sight, and smell (e.g. Mondada, 2016; Goodwin, 1997) have identified ways in which each potentially private, internal sensation can be made public and part of intersubjective engagement. The critical transition from internal se nsation to public display of sensation is the momen t of expressing the relevance of that sensation to the u nfolding moment (Mondada, 2019), either through vis ible displays of sensation or verbal commentary. In the context of this study, educator moves to establish joint attention toward sensory experiences with natural materials m ay be a precursor to more explicit educational move s, such as scaffolding or explicit socialization of nature-culture relations.

## Study context

This project took place within a research-practice partnership between myself and a local nature based preschool, Northern Sprouts Nature Preschool (NSNP; pseudonym). NSNP operates on a half-day schedule during both the 9-month school year and a single month “day camp” at a large regional park, held in June. As communicated via their website, NSNP adopts a primarily child-centered pedagogical approach emphasizing educators  responding to children’s interests as opportunities to support gross and fine motor, social-emotional, executive function, and literacy skill development. Natu re is explicitly framed as a “third teacher”, and s tudents and educators spend nearly all of their time outdoors. In terms of natu re-culture relations, NSNP states they promote social responsibility in terms of “ecological sustainability”, but the specific meaning is left undefined. Data and analysis Data for this project was collected during the June  day camp, meaning the teachers and students had al ready known each other for many months, but the environme ntal setting of the park was relatively new. As suc h, this study does not investigate intentional initiation of nature-culture relations within this school, such as an educator explaining rules for tree climbing early in the sch ool year, but instead focuses on routine, everyday practices related to nature-culture relations as expressed between children and  educators who have an extended prior history. Video (total length of 344 minutes) was recorded by myself alongside a single focus class of 12 students aged 3 to 5 years old and two educators, Lisa and E mily, who each had over 10 years of early childh ood educational experience. In this study, I focus on m oments where educators directed children’s attention toward a particular natural co-participant. While this means my analysis emphasizes relatively uncommon situations, these moments are potentially significant deviations as they represent interventions beyond regular practice (Schegloff, 1993). Of course, many moments of child initiated joint attention were captured in the larger data set, but as I was interested in educator socialization in this study, those moments are not examined here. The video was segmented for moments where educators  themselves oriented a child or multiple children’s attention to something in the natural material world, resulting in 33 clips. Prior to deep analysis, I shared video clips back with the NSNP educators for joint sensemaking about both their pedagogical values and  the specifics of the recorded moment (DeLiema et al., 2023). These insights, including commentary about unrecorded contrasting cases, ideas about their “normal” practice, and unspoken considerations, influenced subsequent focal clips selection and analysis. Ultimately, 6 clips w ere identified for focal analysis based on their re flection of educator-defined contextual settings and contrasts between e ach other, allowing for analysis to focus on how different yet common approaches to orienting attenti on arose in this context. The 6 focal clips were tr anscribed with embodied actions bolded. To capture physical o rientations between teacher, student, and natural c oparticipant in the space, each transcription includes still images, with timing indicated via asterisks. Each clip was then analyzed using Interaction Analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995), including multiple rounds of iter ative transcription and data sessions with other research ers. After analyzi ng each clip in its own right, I then looked across the clip corpus and the NSNP educator’s corresponding commentary to consider the nature-culture relations being communicated collectively across the 4 week s ummer camp. I specifically emphasize linguistic  moves identified in prior nature-culture relations empirical work,  such as treating  more-than-humans as responsive (McDaid Barry et al., 2023), using name words (Bang  & Marin, 2015; Stapleton & Lynch, 2021), and prono un usage (Konopasky & Sheridan, 2021), and educator explanations provided during joint sensema king. While this interpretive layer goes beyond what is solely happe ning in any one video recorded clip, the analysis a pproach is reflective of the need for longitudinal perspectives when examining nature-culture relations development.

## Processes for orienting attention as shaped by context

The educators in this study oriented children’s att ention through a variety of processes based on key contextual factors including a) the ephemerality-or possibility of changing quickly-of the phenomenon for noticing, b) if the children were already engaged in activity with the natural co-participant, and c) if the educator identified the need to verbalize respectful engagement with others.

When looking at this c ollection of 6 cases, we can see both pedagogical values reflected and moments w hen they come into tension. Particularly in moments  of tension, the educators communicated nature-culture relations more in line with Western norms. Prior literature has emphasized that Western nature-culture relations are often settled expectations in learning environments when other forms of relating are not explicitly emphasized (Bang, 2015). Further, environmental stewardship as a valued form of relating does not inherently question Weste rn onto-epistemological frames, including hierarchies of importance, human supremacy, and the helplessness o r non-agency of natural entities (Taylor, 2020). These 6 cases show how Western nature-culture relations are socialized by educators via moment-to-moment interactions, even when that is not the main pedagogical goal in the moment. NSNP educators identified modeling how to express i nterest and deepen inquiry as their “default” form for orienting attention and as a primary reflection of their child-led value. In the inquiry opportunity interactions, while natural co-participants have a sensory impact on the educators and children (tem perature, sound, feeling), the educators were not expressing markers of relati ng to them as agentic beings. Specifically, natural  co-participants were referenced with “it” pronouns and passive verb tense (“is”). In cases where children engaged in their own activities of interest, there were moment s of disruption or destruction to natural co-participants. Educators had to make in-the-moment decisions about the relative value of continuing the child-led activity versus the impact that activity might have on others. In the cases where children were already engaged with a natural coparticipant, we see the educators communicating bot h stewardship responsibility and hierarchy of impor tance in terms of impact. In the case of Rock Face, the act of climbing on the s andstone had the potential to impact both children below-a safety concern if one slips-and to increase weathering on the natural co-participant. The potential for injuring another child in this case i s treated as the priority for stewardship. In Rocks a nd Grass, the activity of tossing rocks versus plants into the river was called out in terms of the different impact the two materials would have on the river co-participant in the long term. In both case s, there was another natural co-participant-the sandstone and the plants-being impacted by the ongoing activity, but the potential for harm and thus need for stewardship was not treated as a priority. This may be because attending to the impact on them as well might not be possible while still pursuing the children’s act ivity of interest. Thus, by focusing their pedagogi cal approach on being child-led first, NSNP educators may have inadvertently co mmunicated Western norms of human supremacy, treating na tural co-participants as non-agentic and prioritizing continuation of human acti vity and impact on humans over impact on natural co-participants. It was in cases of particularly ephemeral experienc es with natural co-participants that educators de-emphasized child-led approaches and reflected nature-culture relations that emphasized natural co-participant agency. In both ephemeral cases, the educator s performed moves while orienting attention that we re considered uncommon by educators in this NBL preschool, includ ing shushing the children, lifting them up, and ask ing children to hear something instead of using an “I” framed observation. In both of the se cases, the children were particularly quick to respond, something the educat ors when joint sensemaking around these clips ident ified as a reflection of these moves only being used when some thing extra special was happening. These moments al so reflect th e greatest level of agentic relationship to natural –+ co-participants; the educators used verbal and embodied directives to support the children to trea t natural co-participants as responsive (McDaid Barry et al., 2023). The sound of rushing was the creek communicating that it was changing and thus the children should step back. The heron may fly away with loud noises, so the children should be quiet while looking and moving nearby. However, upon reflecting on these video clips, the NPNS educators commented on the various analogous situations where these additional orienting attenti on moves would not be warranted (Schegloff, 1993). For example, the co-presence of a squirrel would not be treated in this same way as the heron because squirrels were seen as “common” and likely to be encountered many times. This  differentiating between natural co-participants-based on their perceived ephemerality-may indirectly communicate nature-culture relations where natural co-participants are placed on a spectrum of impo rtance. The heron is treated as worthy of attention, responsiveness, and consideration of its agency, while a squirrel i s not. As such, even when in the moment educator mo ves emphasized ideas more reflective of non-Western nature-culture relations, such as  natural co-participants as responsive communicators, the decision of when to actively orient attention or not to over many instances reflected Western norms of hierarchy.

## Conclusion

This study explores the everyday moments of socializing natural-culture relations within a single NBL preschool. By focusing on the relatively uncommon moments of e ducators orienting children’s attention to natural coparticipants, I identified three key s ituational factors  that contributed to the choice and process of educators actively shaping immediate human-natural co-participant interactions (Prins et al., 2022) - ephemerality, children’s existing engagement with the natural co-participant, and educat or determined need for redirection in terms of respect for impact on others. Throughout t hese examples, educator actions reflected prioritiz ation of child-led pedagogy first and communicating environmental stewardship nature-culture relations second, echoing prior findings related to NBL educators’ understand ing of their role (Bergan et al., 2024; Cerino, 202 3). When those two values came into tension, educator moves communicated Western nature-culture relations such as human supremacy based hierarchies, intentionally or  unintentionally. This study contributes to the lar ger conversation regarding how understandings of nature-culture relations become normalized in everyday lea rning contexts (Bang et al., 2015; Engman & Hermes, 2021; Pitt, 2018). Of course, this study is a limited exploration of one NBL preschool. The educational philosophy and practices seen among these educators cannot be generalized to all NBL spaces. Further, by focusing on moments of educator-initiated orienting of attention,  I do not capture the totality of messages regarding  nature-culture relations that arose in this NBL preschool, such as  following child-initiated attention to a natural co-participant. For example, the clip selection procedure resulted in excluding a moment when an educator discussed wh y the children should not kill bugs, a critical message t hat might include communication of both stewardship responsibility and natural co-participant agency. However, this study does contribute to the literature by revealing patterns in educators’ practices for orienting atte ntion and bridging to larger nature-culture relations messages. While these findings might transfer to other NBL spaces with further research, they also offer leverage points for considering how to proactively change NBL education, particularly by identifying how Western nature-culture relations are unintentionally reinforced.
