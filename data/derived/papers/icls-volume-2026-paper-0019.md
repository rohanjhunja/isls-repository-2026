# MusicLOGO 2.0: A New Computational Representation for Music Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper introduces MusicLOGO 2.0 (ML2), a free online platform designed to engage learners in foundational musical concepts, such as melody, harmony, and form, through composing music with computer code. The platform introduces a novel interval-based representation that encodes music through relationships between notes. We report on a study examining how nine participants with diverse musical and coding backgrounds composed music in ML2 during an open-ended, think-aloud task. Participants’ approaches were shaped by prior musical knowledge, with the platform scaffolding the translation of musical ideas into code. Representing music via intervals encouraged reasoning in relative pitch, revealing underlying musical patterns. Throughout the process, code took on layered meanings —embodied, spatial, musical, and numerical—as participants coordinated them in composition.

From the moment of birth, individuals are exposed to music in many forms: in television shows, on the radio, and at the grocery store. Entirely by enculturation, young children gain deep insights into how  the music they are exposed to is structured (McMullen & Saffran, 2004). This inductive process cultivates an intuitive grasp of the grammar of music, regardless of whether individuals receive formal training or consider themselves musically inclined. Rese arch has shown that individuals are sensitive to musical qualities from an early age. For example, infants exhibit preferences for consonant over dissonant intervals (Trainor et al., 2002). As they grow, individuals develop a tacit understanding of core musical principles, including how notes and phrases are organized into patterns and how certain notes can create and resolve a sense of tension (e.g., Lerdahl & Jackendoff, 1996; Trehub et al., 1986). These intuitions even allow individuals to predict what will happen next in music (Hannon & Trainor, 2007). For some children, musical intuition is further refined through formal training. Formal training introduces learners tonew forms of musical representation, such as notational systems and instruments, and provides theoretical abstractions for organizing pe rceptions. Such training allows individuals to think qualitatively differently about sound and music, leading to improved auditory memory and processing (Hannon & Trainor, 2007). However, access to such training is often uneven. Traditional instruction tends to favor a small subset of students—those with early demonstrated ability or with the financial means to afford private lessons. Consequently, the opportunity to deepen one’s musical understanding through formal training is frequently shaped more by access than interest (Alegrado & Winsler, 2020). This paper introduces MusicLOGO 2.0 (ML2), a free, online platform designed to build on learners’ existing musical intuitions towards developing a richer understanding of music while also learning about computing. The tool is intended to promote a deeper understanding and appreciation of music as a ubiquitous form of human activity by enabling learners to engage with foundational concepts such as melody, harmony, and musical form. ML2 leverages the affordances of computational representations (Bamberger & d iSessa, 2003; Roberts & Horn, 2024) and introduces a novel interval-based representation that encodes musical ideas through the relationships between notes. We report on a study exploring the cognitive affordances of this new representation of music, examining how nine participants with diverse musical and coding backgrounds constructed musical compositions with code during an open-ended, think-aloud task. Participants’ approaches to composition were shaped by their prior musical knowledge, with the platform providing scaffolding to support translating musical ideas into code. Translating musical ideas into code required them to think in terms of relative pitch rather than absolute notes, which in turn prompted insights into underlying musical patterns. Through this process, the code itself acquired multiple layers of meaning —embodied, spatial, musical, and numerical —and participants actively worked to balance these aspects and establish coherence between them. Music theory 111 Western music is organized around a twelve-note pitch collection that divides the octave into equal parts. From this chromatic set, smaller seven-note subsets are used to create diatonic scales, which serve as the foundation for Western tonal music. These scales, such as major and minor, are defined by specific sequences of intervals — measured as the difference in pitch between two notes. Across cultures, music is generally tonal, with scale s anchored by a central pitch, the tonic, which serves as a “home base” (Castellano, Bharucha, & Krumhansl, 1984). In Western music, m ovement toward and away from the tonic generates a sense of stability, tension, and resolution, which shapes the affective trajectory of a musical piece. This pattern of stability and instability is fundamental to how listeners perceive musical structure. Harmony arises from the simultaneous sounding of pitches, typically in the form of chords derived from the underlying scale. Like scales, chords are built from intervals, usually by stacking three notes in thirds within a scale. Chords are also arranged hierarchically, with some functioning to reinforce the tonic and others to create tension. For example, the dominant chord (built on the fifth note of the scale) typically resolves to the tonic, producing a sense of closure. These tonal and harmonic relationships unfold over time within a broader structural framework known as musical form. Form is the arrangement of musical themes or  sections, providing structure that guides the listener through the piece.

Put simply, musical scales and keys are a subset of notes with some pitches being more important or stable than others, and the movement towards and away from stability gives structure to musical compositions. However, tonal hierarchy is not merely an analytical construct but is deeply rooted in how individuals cognitively perceive pitch across cultures. Numerous experimental studies have shown that listeners perceive a hierarchy of stability among pitches within a key (see Krumhansl, 1990, 2000). Rather than processing pitch continuously (in terms of raw frequency) or absolutely (as fixed chromatic notes), listeners “chunk” pitch information into categorical intervals within scales. Dowling (1978) outlined a theory of melodic memory based on two schemata: melodic contour and musical scale. Dowling’s experiments showed that listeners could recognize melodies even when transposed or slightly distorted, as long as the contour and scale  context were preserved.  Cross-cultural studies show that while the general tendency to organize pitch relationally is widespread, the specific tonal hierarchies listeners perceive depend on the structure of the musical scales they are familiar with  (Castellano, Bharucha, & Krumhansl, 1984; Krumhansl et al., 2000).

Thus, work in music cognition has suggested our perception of pitch relies on relational structures within the scales we are exposed to, rather than exact pitches, and the underlying construction of these scales has properties that profoundly impact how we experience music. Affordances of computational representations As a musical representation, computer code has unique affordances for representing musical structure. Bamberger and diSessa (2003) found that learners working with mathematical and computational formulations of music gained a better understanding of the underlying structure of music, which they argue can be obscured by other forms of notation. Similarly, in a study investigating how undergraduate participants with varying amounts of musical and programming experience composed music on a music+coding platform, Roberts and Horn (2024) found that participants employed coding abstractions such as loops and functions to represent musical patterns. Many music+coding environments prioritize programming skills without explicitly supporting the development of music al knowledge (Roberts & Horn, 2025). Thus, learners sometimes struggle to realize their musical ideas due to limited understanding of pitch relationships, harmony, or form. For example, McKlin et al. (2021) found that learners frequently encountered barriers to expression stemming from a lack of foundational music knowledge. Similarly, Shapiro et al. (2017) and Hamer (2004) argued that such platforms should incorporate more explicit instruction in music theory to better support both computing learning and creative expression. Finally, Roberts et al. (2025) found that while learners in coding environments drew on music theory concepts such as scales and beat values, the underlying representation of pitch and rhythms of many music+coding environments conflicted with how learners conceptualized music. The authors recommended that future platforms more directly account for musical structure in their design. Different approaches to representation We next examine different approaches to music+coding environments, which differ primarily in their choice of musical primitives—that is, how users encode notes and rhythms. The first class of representation we will examine are note-based representations, where compositions are expressed as sets of notes with pitch and duration. In this paradigm, the user encodes pitch through entering raw frequency values (e.g., 440 Hz), note names (e.g., A4 or 112 C♯5), or numerical codes such as MIDI numbers. Although learners have access tonew computational abstractions, this usage of code is otherwise isomorphic to many conventional musical representations that encode compositions as a series of note events, like standard music notation or sequencers. One example of this was Jeanne Bamberger’s original MusicLOGO, which was a minimal extension to the LOGO programming language. The most fundamental procedure was the NOTE procedure, which triggered a synthesized sound with a specified pitch and duration (Bamberger, 1979). Learners could represent pitch using numbers from-24 to 35, spanning a five-octave range from C3 (-24) to B7 (35). Note-based encoding has also been utilized in more recent work. The TunePad (Horn et al., 2022) and Sonic Pi (Aaron & Blackwell, 2013) platforms both encode notes as MIDI values, with duration expressed as a decimal value representing beats. MIDI is a protocol that expresses pitch as a numerical value between 0 and 127.

Another type of computational representation of music allows learners to arrange and trigger musical samples with code. This approach represents a different level of musical abstraction compared to note-based composition. Rather than specifying individual pitches and rhythms, learners work with larger musical units which encapsulate multiple musical elements, such as melody, harmony, and rhythm. By composing with samples, learners engage with music composition at a higher structural level, shaping entire sections of sound rather thandefining musical events at their most granular level. For example, the EarSketch platform provides learners with a vast library of samples, allowing them to create music through coding in Python or JavaScript by composing with pre-recorded sounds (Magerko et al., 2013). Similarly, code.org’s Music Lab (Music Lab: Jam Session, n.d.) allows learners to use block-based coding to combine samples from popular artists. While the note-based and sample-based paradigms use sequential, procedural control flows, flow-based environments use a dataflow model in which programs are constructed as directed graphs with nodes representing operations and edges representing the flow of data. This model enables users to design complex multimedia and signal processing systems through a graphical interface rather than using conventional text-based coding environments. In music, two examples of flow-based environments are Max (Puckette, 1991) and Pure Data (Puckette, 1996). Both tools were developed by Miller Puckette at IRCAM and have become a key tool in music technology and interactive media, supporting real-time audio synthesis, algorithmic composition, and installation art. Aimed explicitly at younger learners, Minces et al. (2023) developed M-Flow, a browser-based environment for flow-based music programming. In an early evaluation of the platform, the authors found that it was able to effectively engage fourth-grade students in music+coding activities. Work comparing these different representations of music has been limited. A study comparing how middle school students used TunePad and EarSketch reported greater rhythmic and computational complexity in TunePad projects, and more varied form and instrumentation in EarSketch, though these differences may reflect curriculum design rather than platform features (McCall et al., 2022). Petrie (2023) conducted a mixed-method case study of classes using either EarSketch or TunePad and found no significant differe nces in overall performance between platforms, though each offered distinct approaches to learning and creation. Drawing on this prior work on music and coding, we see that computational representations offer distinct affordances for thinking about music. Yet many existing music+coding environments do not explicitly prioritize music learning, and the absence of sufficient scaffolding or instruction on music leaves many learners struggling to realize their ideas if they lack prior music training. Our platform responds to these challenges by explicitly addressing music theory concepts to better support rich and expansive forms of computational and musical learning. The next section details how our platform’s design embodies these principles. Design of MusicLOGO 2.0 MusicLOGO 2.0 (ML2) is a free web application for creating and sharing musical compositions made with Python code. ML2 is named in honor of Jeanne Bamberger and her work with the original MusicLOGO, which investigated how computing might serve as an epistemological tool for thinking about music (Bamberger, 1979). The foundations of our work are deeply rooted in constructionist  (Papert, 1993) and sociocultural approaches to learning, and ML2 is intended to be an open-ended learning environment in which learners can gain insights about music and computing through creating personally meaningful musical artifacts. The platform targets learners from grades 4–12 through early undergraduate levels, building on the demonstrated success of similar music-coding environments across formal and informal settings. It is designed with a low floor and high ceiling, offering novices an easy entry point while supporting deeper engagement for advanced users through a full-featured implementation of the Python language. The platform accommodates diverse creative workflows, allowing learners to translate ideas from tools like the virtual keyboard into code, tinker with musical parameters (Resnick & Rosenbaum, 2013; Roberts et al., 2025), live-code in real time (Aaron, 2018), remix others’ work (Magerko et al., 2013), and incorporate randomness or procedural generation to explore algorithmic composition. 113 Figure 1 The MusicLOGO 2.0 interface.

Interval-based composition In MusicLOGO 2.0, learners create compositions by specifying intervals between pitches.  To more closely align with how learners think about pitch, the system situates intervals within the context of a specific scale. By embedding tonal relationships directly into the design of the representation, the platform reflects the way many learners mentally organize pitch—relative to a key or scale rather than as isolated notes. This design choice aims to minimize extra translational steps that would otherwise be required when moving between a learner’s intuitive understanding of pitch relationships and the formal representation used in code. In practice, it allows users to compose and manipulate melodies within a familiar tonal framework while still retaining the flexibility to easily shift keys or to work chromatically when needed. While we used Western scales familiar to the learners we work with, the principles underlying the design could be adapted to other tonal systems. Our interval-based composition model draws inspiration from the vector-based drawing of Turtle geometry (Abelson & DiSessa, 1980; Papert, 1993), where movement and direction are more important than individual positions or points in space. Just as Turtle geometry enabled learners to explore geometric and mathematical relationships through movement, ML2 allows learners to explore musical relationships through intervals. Platforms like Sonic Pi and TunePad operate at the note level, analogous to drawing a picture through specifying cartesian points in space. In contrast, EarSketch uses larger, phrase-based musical units in which learners manipulate predefined musical phrases, analogous to creating a picture using only premade shapes. While this approach can offer a quick introduction to concepts from music and computing, it may not engage learners with the underlying mechanics of musical structure to the same extent as more expressive representations. Interval-based composition is intended to maintain the flexibility to manipulate individual notes while also prompting learners to think more abstractly about musical structure. One of the key affordances of this representation is the ability to treat musical ideas as manipulable, computable entities. Rather than seeing a melody or motif as a fixed sequence of notes, learners can conceptualize it as a musical object that can be tr ansformed through code. For example, a learner might define a short melodic phrase and then apply transformations to it, such as shifting it to a different register or key while preserving its internal structure. Therefore, our interval-based approach is intended to allow learners to engage directly with the tonal relationships that structure music and gain insight into how musical ideas relate to one another. API definition Songs in ML2 are short Python scripts that encode musical actions using a small set of domain-specific commands. The core abstraction is the Turtle class, which encodes a melodic line that can be layered with others to produce more complex musical textures. The learner composes music by specifying how Turtles move across a piano keyboard through a sequence of musical intervals, with this movement show n as a turtle icon traveling along the keys as the music plays. Once instantiated, Turtles can perform music al actions: ● sing(beats, volume=100): Sustains the turtle’s current pitch for a specified number of beats at a given volume (0–100).

● wait(beats): Rests for a given duration, enabling rhythmic articulation. ● up(steps) / down(steps): Moves the turtle’s pitch by a number of scale degrees. Additionally, ML2 provides functions to monitor a turtle’s internal state to guide control flow. Its follower mechanism enables synchronous multi-agent coordination, allowing learners to build chords or parallel melodic lines by specifying relationships between turtles rather than coding each part individually. 114 Nine p articipants were recruited from a group of undergraduate coaches who were facilitating summer youth programming. In selecting participants, we aimed to balance the different ranges and combinations of prior music and coding experience. Participants’ coding backgrounds ranged from complete beginners to those who had completed introductory courses or had advanced experience. M usical experience varied similarly, from having never played an instrument, to participation in high school band, to extensive formal training. Prior to participation, coaches all participated in training sessions with ML2. Each participant provided signed informed consent as well as verbal assent to be video recorded. Participants were tasked with composing a short, original melody using code in MusicLOGO 2.0. They were asked to aim for at least 30 seconds of music in any style. To gain insight into their decision-making, participants were instructed to think aloud and describe their actions as they worked (Ericsson & Simon, 2002) and to vocalize their musical ideas by singing or humming. Participants were allowed to continue working until they felt their composition was complete. This composition component lasted 30–45 minutes and was followed by a brief discussion about the participant’s process and background with music and computing. The entire session took about an hour, though some finished earlier. We analyzed participants’ work with the platform by segmenting and coding video recordings of each task. The first author (R1) began by inductively coding participants’ creative behaviors at a high level (e.g., generating a melody, adjusting beat values, translating a melody into code). Using a spreadsheet, R1 recorded timestamps, codes, and notes, defining the boundaries of each episode primarily at moments when participants listened to their output and “moved on.” For each episode, R1 wrote analytical notes describing how participants reasoned about music and its connection to computing, reviewed transcripts, and selected illustrative interview quotations to triangulate participants’ stated thinking with their observed actions. This initial pass allowed us  to segment videos into discrete semantic units capturing both actions and key moments of reasoning. From these open codes and memos, R1 developed a hierarchical coding scheme that captured how participants reasoned about musical concepts such as pitch, harmony, and form, and how they related these concepts to code and other representations. R1 and R2 then applied this coding scheme to the entire corpus, again noting start and stop times, top-level and granular codes, and detailed transcriptions or summarie s of actions. The coding scheme was iteratively refined as new phenomena emerged. Throughout the process, the team synthesized these episodes into narrative-driven analytical memos to trace participants’ evolving patterns of reasoning across the study. In this section, we begin by examining how participants engaged with the platform and its interval-based representation to reason about music, focusing on how they translated musical ideas into code and, conversely, interpreted code as an expressive form of musical representation. We then show how these interactions, supported by the platform’s scaffolds, not only facilitated the coding process but also prompted spontaneous insights into tonal and numerical relationships of music.

Reasoning about pitch In line with previous work, we observed that participants engaged in a form of “translation” while composing in ML2 by first developing musical ideas and then decomposing these ideas into code. They began by developing short musical phrases either on the keyboard or by singing. They then broke these ideas down into pitch and rhythmic values for each note and wrote commands to play them. For pitch, this meant determining the interval size—that is, how many steps separated the last encoded pitch from the next  note. Participants used distinct Participants with more musical experience calculated intervals mentally, drawing on internalized representations of pitch relationships. To do so, participants compared the current position in the melody with the previous note and either determined the interval size aurally through their understanding of the scale or applied their knowledge of music theory. Thus, this process required them to hold multiple pitches in their head and then apply their intuition or active problem solving techniques to figure out the interval. For example, Celeste largely developed and translated her musical idea into code by leveraging her knowledge of music theory. In selecting notes, she often chose notes that she knew were consonant with the accompaniment and other notes in her composition. Her knowledge of music theory also aided her in recognizing intervals. Early in her process, Celeste wanted to outline the notes of a D major chord with a Turtle. Because she knew both the pitches that made up the chord as well as the relationship between the pitches, she was easily able to write code to play these notes. In discussing her process, Celeste indicated that she not only kept track of individual notes and measured the intervals between them but also situated those pitches relative to the current musical scale: “When I knew I wanted to go from the first to the fifth note [of the scale], I kind of  just did that in my head.” Another participant, Sophia, had substantial musical training and translated intervals largely automatically. She mentally tracked her position in 115 the scale, calculated intervals using her understanding of music theory, and translated groups of notes while singing through them to check her work. She referred to note names while coding and added them as comments to annotate the code and improve its readability.

Such participants often drew on intuitive notions of “small” and “large” intervals and could often recognize and translate small intervals of one or two steps automatically without engaging in active problem solving. However, for larger intervals, this process required more deliberate problem solving. For example, Liana began by developing part of her melody by singing. She then sang through the melody while writing code for each note. For scalar notes next to each other on the keyboard, she coded several consecutive notes without pausing. When she reached a trickier large interval (such as a fifth or a seventh), she sang the two pitches several times and then “sang” through the scale in her head, counting the steps between the two notes to determine the interval. When asked about her process, Liana specified that she kept track of the previous note’s position, saying “I know what note I just played. Where am I going? How many [steps] is it moving up or down?” In this way, participants drew on three elements to identify intervals: the note’s direction relative to the previous one, the magnitude of the interval, and its relationship to the scale in use. By contrast, participants with less musical experience leveraged the affordances of the platform as tools to support their interval calculations. Rather than mentally counting the notes of the scale, they treated the keyboard as an intermediary, or object-to-think-with, by physically locating their current position and counting steps to the next note. The spatial layout of the keyboard thus functioned as a way to make abstract pitch relationships physical. Kendra explained, saying “I counted physically. I would play the keyboard, and I’ll just count the number of keys.” Sienna described her strategy in similar terms: “I was just like looking at the key separation on the keyboard and like trying to use that in my head to go up and down a certain amount.” In t his way, participants created a link between the musical interval, the physical “key separation” on the keyboard, and coded representation of the Turtle’s movement.

Even participants without any prior musical experience made use of the keyboard, although they sometimes had more difficulty. For example, Damon had no prior formal experience making music but expressed a strong interest in it. Like other participants, he developed his initial ideas primarily by singing. He began by thinking of a short opening motif and then moved to decomposing it into code by singing through the idea and writing code for each note. He sang the interval of a fourth but could not find the notes on the piano. Instead, he tried values in code somewhat arbitrarily, guided by intuition about high versus low and small versus large steps— for example, choosing a random value to move the turtle to a low note and then another to go up, adjusting when the value proved too large. Thinking aloud, he said, “so when I want to change something, bring it up or down, I change numbers. I’m like, ‘how far is it from the previous [note] I was playing. Maybe this will work a little bit higher.’” When he became frustrated after not being able to find the interval value through trial and error in code, he shifted to the keyboard to translate his ideas into code despite lacking prior keyboard experience. He spent considerable time searching for the correct notes, again using trial and error until he matched the pitch he had in his head. Once he located a note, like other participants, he determined the interval by counting from the position of the previous note, represented by the Turtle icon.

Scaffolding translation Across experience levels, the keyboard and Turtle icons functioned as intermediary tools for participants to develop, translate, and verify musical ideas. Novice musicians in particular used the keyboard’s spatial layout to break melodies down interval by interval, counting the number of steps between notes. Regardless of prior experience, nearly every participant sang during their interviews while working through their idea, and the keyboard helped them connect what they heard or sang to the numeric steps they needed to program. The keyboard thus acted as a bridge between embodied musical knowledge and the symbolic notation of code by providing a concrete intermediary. Ravi described how using the platform shifted his thinking about pitch relationships from abstract numbers to embodied scale relationships, saying: “It makes you think more in terms of the scale and in terms of the actual physical keyboard […] and that kind of forces you to think more about the actual action of playing the keyboard. Even when you’re programming it, you’re actively thinking about the keyboard at all times because you need to know the distance between the notes.” Along with the keyboard, the Turtle icon in ML2 acted as a second “object to think with,” paralleling participants’ mental navigation of the keyboard. This externalized the algorithm many were already applying mentally to decompose melodic ideas into intervals and also facilitated debugging.  Ravi, for example, suspected he had made an error and said, “Let me just watch the turtles once.” By listening to and observing the turtle’s movement, he confirmed that his idea was correct. Although he could not fully verify it through aural skills alone, combining this with the spatial movement of the Turtle allowed him to see that his code did in fact match his musical idea. Similarly, Sophia reported that she used the Turtle icons to check the correctness of her translations 116 and to annotate her code with note names: “I looked at the Turtles … I think the turtles were helpful whenever I was commenting what note I was on.” Thus, for Sophia, the Turtles reduced the cognitive load of keeping track of where she was in the melody by serving as an icon that marked the current melodic position. Together, these features of the platform scaffolded participants’ processes by linking auditory, embodied, spatial, and symbolic representations of pitch. The keyboard and Turtle icons helped externalize thinking about intervals and scales, provided immediate feedback for verification, and supported participants in bridging between their embodied musical knowledge and the numeric commands required for programming. Reading code as music Participants did not simply translate their musical ideas into code; they also translated the code back into music, treating the code itself as an expressive form of musical representation. Several participants “read” the code by tracing through it with th eir mouse, tapping out rhythms, or singing through sections line by line. Rather than working only from abstract numbers, they used this process to “hear” the section and anticipate how a change would sound before making edits. In doing so, they connected the numerical representation of code not only to sound, but also to their own embodied sense of pitch and rhythm. For example, Sophia traced through the code to diagnose an error with pitch after realizing her comments no longer matched the notes she had programmed. After she located the source of the error by reading through the code, she cross-referenced the comments, the piano roll, and her own singing to identify the mismatch, corrected the code, and updated the annotations accordingly. Sophia, Celeste, Eliza, Liana, and Kendra all followed a similar process of tracing through code with the mouse while audiating or “playing” it in their head to identify where they wanted to change a rhythm. Other participants went beyond simply checking individual notes or durations; they combined pitch and rhythm to ‘perform’ the code, singing through sections as if reading from a musical score. As an experienced singer, Liana consciously attuned to the sensation of how intervals ‘feel’ to sing, using this embodied knowledge to guide her compositional decisions. When she began her melody, she said “it feels like something you could sing to” which demonstrates how she applied this kind of embodied heuristic in selecting musical material. Liana used singing and pointing as a way to navigate her code. At several points she sang through sections to find her place, copied or repeated phrases while singing along to decide how much to copy, and traced with her mouse to identify rhythmic errors or missing commands. In one instance she needed to add a rest so two voices would align; she looked at the timeline to find the duration, sang the passage, and then fixed the code. Kendra likewise sang through copied sections of code while moving her mouse along the lines to check the intervals and rhythms, then adjusted the timing of a phrase based on what she had sung. Across these examples, reading code as music helped participants externalize and refine their understanding of pitch and rhythm. We see this as evidence of how our representation conveyed musical meaning, and thus served as an expressive form of musical representation. The code became not just an abstract set of instructions but a musical text they could “play” with their body. Discovering musical patterns Even within the brief interviews, we found that participants voiced spontaneous insights into musical patterns. In translating their musical ideas, participants relied on a relative understanding of pitch—identifying notes by their relationship to a refere nce pitch rather than as isolated entities. Thus, this process required them to think about how pitches relate to one another rather than treating them as discrete, disconnected values. As they moved between sound and code, many began to notice recurring t onal structures, such as scales and chords, emerging from common interval patterns, and at times they linked these to computational abstractions. We observed this increased awareness of musical structure in every participant, across varying levels of musical experience, though it manifested in different ways. At times, both Sienna and Liana copied and pasted lines of code after they noticed repeatin g patterns. For example, they began writing code for a downward scale but noticed the lines repeated (all down(1) and sing(1)), so they copied and pasted the lines to play the scale. This behavior suggested they were thinking in terms of larger conceptual or gestural units of music, while recognizing patterns in the code that represented the structure of the scale. Even though they were not explicitly linking this to coding abstractions, they were still thinking about music as recurring and transformable patterns. Several participants began to connect the musical patterns they were writing to computational abstractions such as loops. For example, at one point Kendra started to code a downward scale. Like Liana and Sienna, she had translated two notes and was working on the third when she noticed the lines of code were identical. She then rewrote her code to use a for-loop, condensing the scale into three lines of code instead of six. Later, when working on the next phrase, she encountered the same scalar pattern agai n but moving up the scale. This time, she recognized the pattern immediately and coded it directly as a for-loop. Reflecting on this later she explained: “I wrote the first 2–3 notes of a scale and I thought, wow, I think I'm writing the same thing. So I made 117 it into a for loop.” Kendra also reported seeing other musical patterns — such as arpeggios, in which each note is two steps above the previous one—and attributed this noticing to the platform’s representation of intervals, saying: “I was super aware of the fact I was using arpeggios. I think if [...] I was just making the notes individually without seeing the relationship between them, I wouldn’t even register that I was doing that. Because I started seeing a pattern, [that] my numbers were almost always the same, it was less and less trial-anderror because now I know that if I want to make this kind of interval, or just kind of like arpeggio, it’s this number of notes up from the original. I definitely think if I keep using it, it’ll be super instinctive or like intuitive, at least how to make a chord.” Similarly, Ravi began by writing individual commands to play each note of a scale. Noticing that each note moved by the same interval, he rewrote his code as a for-loop. Finally, some participants began to treat musical phrases as computable entities, transforming entire sections rather than composing note-by-note. One such way was through transposition, which allowed participants to create new musical ideas by shifting existing motifs up or down in pitch. Damon, for example, had coded a section of music and decided to wrap the section in a for-loop to “see what will happen.” He initially coded it in a w ay that directly repeated the idea several times, but quickly began experimenting with adding intervals offsetting repetitions. In describing his intention, he said “If you hear it playing now, it’s like [the same] 4 times. So after maybe two times, I want it to go a little bit higher. So on one [repeat], I want to keep it as it is, but [...] if it's equal to two, then I should go down by 7.” He then added a conditional statement to control how the motif was transposed on each repetition. Like Damon, Celeste also experimented with sequencing ideas later in her process. She also inverted musical motifs by reversing the pattern of intervals. To do so, she copy-and-pasted a previous sequence and changed the commands from “down” to “up” while preserving the size of the intervals. In our analysis, we focused on how participants used the interval-based representation to reason about pitch and how they made sense of its multiple meanings through bidirectional translation between sound and code. Participants did not simply translate their musical ideas into code; they also “read” the code back as an expressive form of musical representation. This process allowed code to carry multiple layers of meaning —embodied, spatial, musical, numerical, and computational—while also spurring participants to notice patterns and relationships within the music.

In line with prior work (Petrie, 2023; Roberts et al., 2025), we found that participants approached coding differently depending on their prior musical experience. While more experienced participants leveraged their music theory and aural skills, those without prior musical experience relied on the platform to help scaffold this translation. We contend that the platform’s design provided effective scaffolding, allowing participants across experience levels to engage with fundamental music concepts while creating authentic musical artifacts. Designers of STEAM and creative coding platforms should consider the diverse strategies that learners bring and should account for the way prior artistic experience shapes their cognitive and representational approaches. As Papert (1993) wrote, computing can “recast powerful ideas in computational form” (p. 183). In this way, we view computation as a means of engaging with the “powerful idea” of music by making relationships between musical ideas more salient to learners. Music and computing learning can be mutually reinforcing; as we observed, the use of computational abstractions was often driven by musical problems: for example, employing loops to represent scales or functions to organize song sections. As illustrated by Damon and Celeste’s examples in the prior section, the affordances of the representation can also open new compositional pathways that leverage connections between music and computing. However, we do not mean to suggest that computational representations should supplant other musical forms; rather, we see computing as working alongside a range of representations, including physical instruments, sheet music, and invented notation. While our work with undergraduate students has provided a preliminary view of how learners might think with the platform, we are expanding the dataset to include younger learners to examine how engagement with the platform develops across a broader range of learning conte xts. Future work will therefore explore how younger learners interact with the platform over longer periods of time in more naturalistic learning environments.
