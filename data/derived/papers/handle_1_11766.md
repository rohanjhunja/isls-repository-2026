# Leveraging Symbolic Representations to Understand Young Learners’ Interactions with Computational Thinking Concepts

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Computational thinking (CT) holds significant value in STEM education because it emphasizes cultivating problem-solving skills mediated by computational technologies. This aspect of CT enhances the educational experience for learners and can open promising opportunities for supporting and assessing learning with new technologies. Using a 14-week curriculum designed to integrate CT into fourth-grade mathematics classrooms using a programmable robot, we explore how young learners use symbolic representations to translate across contexts. We investigate the potential of symbolic representations in developing CT skills, including decomposition and abstraction. We present vignettes to show how young learners’ use of symbolic representations in CT tasks can contribute to a deeper understanding of how students conceptualize and engage with computational problem-solving. This work advances our understanding of how integrating CT in early-grade classrooms can significantly enrich STEM education thus highlighting the importance of developing CT skills for early-grade learners.

## Introduction

The importance of understanding computational thinking (CT) skills to navigate our technologically driven world cannot be overstated. As Wing (2006) put it, CT skills are as important as reading, writing and arithmetic to every child’s analytical ability. There have also been efforts to bring CT into class rooms by researchers either through calls to broaden participation or through providing  opportunities by developing CT curricula where stu dents can learn and develop basic CT skills (National Research Council, 2010; Shute et al., 2017; Wing, 2006; Weintrop et al., 2021). The focus of K-12 CT activities has predominantly focused on developing programming skills, STEM skills, or the production and use of other computat ionally related skills (e.g., see the studies revie wed by Grover and Pea, 2013a). The present study draws on literat ure that has focused on other representational and epistemic ways of knowing and doing that can lead to the development of CT skills in cu rricular contexts (Sengupta et al., 2018; Bers et al., 2014). Specifically, this study investigates the role and contexts in which young l earners use symbolic representations during interactions in CT and robotics tasks. In this study, we define symbolic representations as using symbols or abstract entities to represent concepts, objects, or relationships in a structured and meaningful way within the activity (Goldin, 202 0). To understand how symbols embed ded into activities contribute to the development of CT skills and expl ore the impact of symbolic use on students’ ability  to tackle complex computational problems, we undertook a stud y in fourth-grade elementary school mathematics classrooms, where students interacted with a programmable robot to navigate CT-enhanced classroom tasks. The objective was to gain insights into various represe ntational aspects of student learning, engagement, and the expression of CT ideas. This study investigates a r elatively underexplored area in CT research by asking the following research question: How do symbolic representations support young learn ers develop CT skills in robotics-enhanced CT tasks?  We conceptualize symbolic representations as tools for expressing CT concepts, expanding the understanding of how CT skills can be developed beyond conventional programm ing tasks, and aligning with recent literature exploring alternative approaches to fostering CT skills in curricular contexts (Bers et al. 2014). The present study contributes to our understanding of the role symbolic representations play in young learners’ acquisition of CT knowledge by examining them within the context of both activity-based learning and computational abstractions.

Previous work Foundations of CT can be traced back to Papert's pi oneering work with Logo (Papert, 1980). Jeannette W ing popularized the term in her 2006 article, defining CT as the ability to "solve problems, design system s, and understand human behavior by leveraging core concepts from computer science" (Wing, 2006, p.33). Researchers have developed various frameworks to define CT adap ted to specific contexts or target age groups. Expr essing CT ideas requires students to articulate real-world problems in a manner that a computer or machine can interpret and execute (Wing, 2016). This process demands problem-solving proficiency, clearly articulating intentions and instructions, interaction with the learning environment, and engagement with the computer system (Papert, 1980). Sengupta et al. (2018) have argued for an epistemol ogical shift, suggesting CT be seen not simply as m astery of computational logic and symbolic representations but as a more nuanced and complex form of experience. Additionally, there have been calls to recognize le arners' experiences as foundational elements for promoting deeper learning and the development of di sciplinary expertise in STEM fields (e.g., Papert, 1980; DiSessa, 2000). To this end, numerous studies have explored the phenomenological dimensions of CT, including the significance of discourse (Grover & Pea, 2013; Farris & Sengupta, 2014) in the development of CT w ithin STEM curricula. To support the expansion and deepen ing of the focus on the phenomenology of computing and CT in K-12 STEM curricula, Sengupta et al. (2018)  emphasized the importance of anchoring CT in the representational and epistemic practices that are f undamental to knowledge acquisition and practice in  science and, more broadly, in STEM education. Thus, we set out to observe and understand the nature and use of symbolic representations learners engage with programmable robots and accompanying activities. Building on the kinds of symbolic representations we observed learners use, the focus of this work finds alignment with Papert’s (1987) critique of technocentrism and provides an understanding of the role of symbolic representations in CT learning and prob lem-solving, including technology as just one tool amon g many meaningful tools that can be designed into integrated learning experiences. Furthermore, we ground this approach in Merleau-Ponty’s (1962, P.61) phenomenological concept of experience, defined a s “that vital communication with the world which makes it present as a familiar  setting of our life”. Existing studies have not sufficiently addressed how symbolic representations function as mediating tools  that bridge students' experiences and computational abstractions in CT practices. This wo rk aims to fill that gap by examining the phenomenological dimensions of CT, specifically how symbolic forms are used by learners to support CT. By integrating technology into experiential learnin g practices that present multiple layers of built-in complexities into tasks, this work will specificall y explore how learners relied on symbolic represent ations as a pathway for CT development, aligning with Wing's (2 008) argument that “the essence of CT is an abstrac tion” (p. 3717), highlighting how abstractions can enable computer scientists to manage complexity within tasks. Description of symbolic representations In this study,   symbolic representations are associated with the use of symbols or abstract entities to represen t concepts, objects, or relationships in a structured and meaningful way within the activity (Goldin, 2020). Symbolic representations in CT activities refer to using sym bols or notations to represent information, concept s, or ideas (Wearne & Hiebert, 1988, p. 372). These symbols can  be letters, numbers, mathematical operators, or an y other characters with a specific meaning the learners ass ign. Symbols can be abstract, and their meaning ass igned by humans or machines in the context within which they are use d. As seen in mathematics literature, symbols can take the form of letters, numbers, images, or any o ther object that can stand for something else (Gold in, 2020). For example, symbols like +, -, x, and / r epresent basic arithmetic operations in mathematics. Similarly, in programming languages, symbols are used to represen t data types: (integer: int, string: str), operator s: (addition +; subtraction -; multiplication *), functions: (print print(), Input input()) (Moore et al., 2020). In certain contexts, symbolic representations may take the form of programming languages incorporating a fusion of language and symbols (Bau et al., 2017). For instance, block languages seamlessly combine both linguistic and symbolic representations (Lin &  Weintrop, 2021; Maloney et al., 2010). Using aspect s such as the block’s shape, color, and use of natural language inscribed on the blocks, learners were able to communicate block functions and break down programming vocabulary in ways that directly constrained the language for dir ect manipulation into the code.  From a conceptual standpoint, highly multimodal CT robotics programming environments, the problem-solving process can often seem ill-structured. Students must formulate problems that can be solved physically and virtually using computers and programming languages (F. R. Sullivan & Heffernan, 2016). These tasks frequently require students to t ranslate across multiple forms of representation, i ncluding concrete, symbolic, pictorial, motor, and linguisti c, and then communicate solutions with machines and  peers (Bers, 2018; Lesh & Doerr, 2002). Representational symbols are either built into the activities or can  sometimes be constructed by learners as they develop experience through language and symbols to help them navigate tasks (Moore et al., 2020; Fofang et al., 2021). These sy mbols may represent real-world objects or concepts. Using symbols effectively in learning can help students make stronger connections between these abstract symbols and the concrete ideas or objects they refer to (Uttal et al., 1997). Research on representations and other theoretical p erspectives has incorporated a situative approach t o learning (Brown, Collins, & Duguid, 1989). This perspective emphasizes learning and understanding as processes shaped by the interactions between individuals and their environment's material, symbolic, and technol ogical resources. Together with frameworks on representati onal fluency (Alibali & Nathan, 2012; Korzma 2003),  this approach enhances our understanding of how young learners engage with representations within a computational thinking-enriched learning environment. For this study, we u se the Lesh Transition Model (LTM) (Lesh and Doerr, 2003) to ground our theoretical observations  of symbolic representations. The Lesh Transition M odel focuses on how learners move or "translate" between  various representations. These representations are  usually not isolated but are interconnected, a nd transitioning between them can support understanding and reasoning through ideas and concepts. The Lesh model includes  five forms of representations: (1) representation through realistic, real-world, experience-based metaphors, (2) written symbolic repr esentation, (3) language-based representation (spoken or written), (4) pictorial o r graphic representation, and (5) concrete represen tation (concrete, manipulatable models). Symbolic represen tations in the Lesh Model refer to formal written s ymbols and notations used to represent mathematical concepts (e.g., numbers, equations, variables).

## Methods

This section presents the curricular context of the study, including a Math and CT curriculum and an overview of the lessons and the technology used. We also discus s details of the setting, participants, and methodo logical approach.

The Robot.Math project The Robot.Math curriculum was designed through coll aborations between researchers working on the proje ct, a technology specialist, an instructional coach, and  4th-grade mathematics teachers.  Robot.Math was designed to understand the feasibility of integrating CT in mathematics classrooms and how this cross-disciplinary integration can support student’s understanding of CT while dee pening their understanding of mathematical concepts. Each lesson includes materials for both students and teachers, with teacher-facing materials clearly defining the lesson objectives and outlining how the lesson aligns with the 4th-grade Common Core Mathematics Standards (CCSS, 2010), followed by the district. The lessons also specify CT concepts students will explore and, where applicable, identify connections to the district's mandated mat hematics curriculum.  To create an engaging and relevant learning experience, the curriculum frames each act ivity within an amusement park theme. For example, in one lesson entitled “Carousel Repair.” Learners were tasked to program Sphero to move to an animal on a “carousel” to fix it if needed and then build a program that i nvolved using functions to control the Sphero robot  to move around points on a circle in 45-degree increments. The Robot.Math curriculum contains a series of 14 l essons designed to engage students in integrated math and CT activities. Learners are tasked to program the Sphero robot (see Figure 1a) using tablets or smartphones (see Figure 1b). Sphero robots can be programmed using a block-based interface, which allows learners to build cod e by dragging and dropping visual blocks (see Figure 1c). Sphero programs are represented as block-based scripts (see Figure 1c) that can use basic programming elements such as loops and conditionals, as well as commands  to regulate the Sphero's movement or actions (e.g., ro ll, strobe). The fundamental movement command for t he Sphero is the roll command figure that can take inputs for heading, speed, and duration(time) (see Figure 1b). ## Setting and participants

The study took place over two years in 4th-grade mathematics classrooms in an urban public school district in the Mid-Atlantic region of the United States. Two schools were signed up for data collection for this study. T he first school (BM) from which I present data in this study, has a total enrollment of 286 students with the f ollowing racial breakdown: 63.7% Hispanic/Latino, 15.5% Blac k/African American, 13.2% White non-Hispanic, 7.2% Multiracial, <1% Asian, <1% Native American/Alaska Native, and <1% Native Hawaiian/Other Pacific Islander. Participants for this study were focal students ide ntified by their classroom teacher based on their a ctive engagement in classroom activities and discussions.  A total of three students (in one focal group) were observed and videoed as they used symbolic representations during their interactions with Sphero robots.

## Data collection

The primary data source for this study were videos collected during the Sphero.Math lessons and student artifacts, such as images of codes written in notebooks and images of their programs. We collected close-up video footage using phones, iPads, head-mounted cameras worn by students, and stationary camera s positioned throughout the activities.

## Data analysis

We used coarse-grained interaction analysis (IA). These methods are well-suited for this study due to their focus on understanding cognition and learning from 'in us e,' 'in action,' and 'in practice' perspectives (Ha ll & Stevens, 2015). We provide analysis  in the form of data captured in ways that provide o pportunities for repeated examination, allowing for alternative interpretatio ns supported by evidence, through transcripts from recordings (Sacks, 1995; Atkinson & Heritage, 1984). The activity for this study was the final activity explicitly designed as a culminating activity to engage learners to integrate all the computational practices, mathematical concepts, and ideas explored across all 14 lessons designed into the Sphero.Math curriculum. The activity involved e ngaging students to design an amusement park and program the Sphero robot to guide visitors around the park. Our data analysis was conducted as follows. Initial ly, team members reviewed all recorded videos to identify and segment video clips with instances whe re students used symbolic representations to naviga te tasks. This process was accompanied by group discussions to refine our observations (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). We viewed selected video clips and used the LTM framew ork to theoretically anchor instances when learners  used symbols such as letters, numbers, mathematical oper ators, or any other characters to  assign specific meanings to them. One team member documented descriptive and analytical memos based on transcripts from video segments where students used symbolic representations to complete tasks. We maintained flexibility in our analysis in order to uncover and explore forms of symbolic representation that learners introduced during activities, leading to fresh perspectives on articulating and interpreting the d ata in ways to provide opportunities, “space and pl ace for exploration and discovery” (Charmaz, 2006, p. 81 –82). One researcher compiled all the memos and vide o links into a shared spreadsheet, including specific evidence of observations. This structure enabled the team to analyze within and across memos. Finally, the research team analyzed these memos to highlight instances where students used symbolic representations in ways that supporte d their CT development and learning elements (see T able 1) and supported their understanding of new concepts as they engaged in programming robotics tasks. ## Discussion

The students demonstrated through this activity an understanding of and ability to use several CT conc epts including decomposition, algorithm design,  and abstraction, through their implementation and u se of symbolic representations either embedded within the activity  or designed by the learners into the activity. The  students’ amusement park final project helped them to express  their understanding of CT concepts using symbolic representations such as functions, color-coded hashtags, and color-coded manuals to create engaging and interactive experiences for park visitors. By empow ering park visitors with the freedom to select thei r preferred attractions, students demonstrated an understanding of user-centered design. They created engaging experiences for users using symbolic representations to support their translation between activities. The students used functions to decompose specific i nteractions for each attraction, such as making sounds, providing information, or guiding visitors.  This approach allowed them to create a more engagi ng and interactive experience for park visitors. For example, they used hashtags to represent functions, and each function represented an activity in the amusement park. We infer students drew on familiar social and cultural symbols to manage the task’s complexity. Though not prompted, Student 1 explained they used hashtags to make navigation easier, an intentional design choice. This suggests students repurposed prior knowledge from social media, where hashtags organize content, to symbolically represent and categorize park activities in their project. Students used dots to represent the taillight on the robot.  In this case, the learners assigned a novel symboli c meaning for dots, to represent the taillight of the Sphero robot.

The students’ use of symbols typically used in soci al media communication to highlight activities and dots to mark directions and starting points for the  robot supports Merleau-Ponty’s (1962) phenomenological concept of experience, highlighting how learners we re able to bring in experiential learning practices  to support their learning and problem-solving strategies. By repurposing familiar symbols like hashtags and d ots, students showed how everyday knowledge can become symbolic tools for thinking and acting within computational tasks projecting learning as both contextual and embodied Like all studies, this work has its limitations. We  observed a very small sample size of students inte racting with symbolic representations. The use of symbols may have varied amongst learners; thus, our focus on a focal group of learners makes it challeng ing to generalize or apply certain learning claims about symbolic representations in robotics contexts for all young learners. Comparati ve longitudinal studies, comparable to those conduc ted for standardized curricular subjects, could offer valua ble insights to the role of symbolic representations in CT sk ill acquisition.

## Conclusions

Examining complex contexts in which elementary-grade students learn CT concepts in robotics activities, we set out to answer the question: How do symbolic representations support young learners develop CT skills in roboticsenhanced CT tasks? Through vignettes, we provided evidence of instance s where learners used symbolic representations to bridge the gap between the virtual programming environment and the physical navigation space of the robot. Our findings demonstrate the critical  role of symbolic representations in helping young learners connect and transition between  representations, as framed by the LTM. In most robotics environmen ts, information is usually translated from the physical  to the virtual space; that means collecting data f rom the physical environment and translating them into form ats the computer can understand. The stu dents relied on symbolic representations to translate problems in w ays that a computer can execute as a means of abstr action. Then, they reversed the information process using symbolic representations from the virtual to the physical space by translating machine-readable information into human-readable formats. Focusing on symbolic representations within CT environments offers the potential for observing insights into how young learners acquire CT knowledge and their cognitive behaviors within authentic interactions that constitute CT learning experiences.
