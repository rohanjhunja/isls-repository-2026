# Evidence-Centered Design for AI-Supported Assessment of Scientific Argumentation

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This demo showcases an AI-supported, evidence-centered assessment design that scaffolds middle school students’ scientific argumentation in the context of ecosystems learning. Grounded in Evidence-Centered Design (ECD), the assessment links constructs of scientific reasoning and argumentation with AI-driven evidence detection and feedback generation. Students investigate ecosystem dynamics through a NetLogo simulation, construct Claim-Evidence-Reasoning (CER) arguments, and receive adaptive feedback from an LLM-based chatbot. Participants will experience the task, explore reasoning analytics, and see how ECD structures both assessment and AI design. The demo highlights how ECD can ensure a valid, interpretable, and instructionally useful AI system that advances students’ reasoning and supports classroom instruction.

Scientific argumentation is fundamental to how scientists construct knowledge and how students make sense of the world. Engaging learners in argumentation, including formulating claims, supporting them with evidence, and reasoning about underlying mechanisms, promotes deeper conceptual understanding and reflects the epistemic practices of science (Osborne, 2010; Wilson et al., 2024). However, research consistently shows that students struggle to construct well-reasoned arguments: they often state claims without adequate evidence, overlook causal reasoning, and rarely critique or rebut competing ideas (Sampson & Clark, 2009). Teachers also face significant challenges in supporting students’ argumentation, including limited time, insufficient training, and lack of resources to provide individualized, timely feedback at scale (Rapanta et al., 2021). Advances in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly large language models (LLMs), offer promising opportunities to address these challenges. When designed with pedagogical intent, AI systems can analyze students’ argument structure and content, identify reasoning patterns, and generate personalized feedback that guides students to strengthen their arguments (Kumar et al., 2024; Watts et al., 2025). Such systems have the potential to extend teachers’ capacity to provide timely, diagnostic feedback and create new avenues for investigating how students reason and revise their arguments. However, without principled design, such systems risk offering feedback that is oversimplified or misaligned with learning theories and pedagogical goals (ZawackiRichter et al., 2019). Our project applies an Evidence-Centered Design (ECD) framework (Mislevy et al., 2003) to the development of an AI-supported classroom assessment and feedback system that helps students construct evidence-based arguments while making sense of ecosystem phenomena. Grounded in scientific argumentation theory and principles of instructionally supportive feedback, the project integrates the Claim-Evidence-Reasoning (CER) framework (McNeill & Krejcik, 2008) to define high-quality arguments as those that coherently link claims and relevant evidence through scientifically grounded reasoning. The AI feedback system draws from research on scaffolding (Reiser, 2004) and adopts an epistemic agent perspective (Gonzelez-Howard & McNeill, 2020), encouraging students to view themselves as knowledge builders who evaluate the validity of their own reasoning (Stroupe, 2014). Rather than just identifying correct answers or missing components of an argument, the feedback engages students with reflective prompts such as “Do the data you used actually support the claim?,” “How does your reasoning explain the why?” The assessment task to be demonstrated captures and promotes students’ scientific reasoning through NGSS-aligned ecosystem scenarios, automated reasoning analysis, and adaptive feedback. By integrating ECD with AI innovation, the project advances both the validity and usefulness of science assessments, offering a model of how principled design can anchor AI systems in learning theory, evidence of construct validity, and pedagogical purpose. This demonstration showcases how an AI-supported assessment enables interactive exploration of s tudent reasoning in authentic ecosystem contexts, how AI models classify reasoning patterns and generate reflective feedback, and how teachers and researchers can use the system to inform instruction and learningscience research. Theoretical framing Research on scientific argumentation demonstrates that students’ ability to engage in scientific argumentation significantly influences their understanding of STEM concepts and their success in related courses (Bathgate, et al., 2015; Sampson & Clark, 2009). Research has shown that reasoning is challenging to students (Kuhn, 1991). Furthermore, students rarely rebut potential counterarguments, though some may be able to do so when prompted (Kuhn & Udell, 2007). Students also often lack criteria for distinguishing and evaluating ideas (Sampson & Clark,

2009) or rely on informal criteria (e.g., teacher’s authority, fit with personal inferences). Even advanced students

struggle to evaluate competing explanations, assess the quality of evidence, or identify underlying assumptions in others’ arguments (Klaczynski, 2000).

Like scientists, children begin developing intuitive explanatory frameworks early in life, drawing on everyday experiences to make sense of the natural world. In ecosystem contexts, students tend to interpret changes in the organisms of a food web in linear ways, focusing on simple, one-step relationships (e.g. how changes in prey population influence changes in predator population) rather than complex multivariate causal interactions (Gotwals & Songer, 2010; Leach et al., 1996; Wennersten et al., 2020). These intuitive patterns of reasoning or facets of student thinking reflect deeply held “direct-causal schemas” (Chi et al., 2012) that help students make sense of observations but can also constrain their reasoning about systemic phenomena. Research suggests that building on these intuitive ideas through reflection and guided exploration can foster more sophisticated causal reasoning (Grotzer et al., 2017).

Supporting students’ argumentation practice requires teachers to surface and work with students’ ideas and engage with them to build scientific understanding. In typical classroom practice, however, students’ nonnormative ideas are often viewed simply as misconceptions to be corrected, rather than as productive starting points for reasoning. Teachers often lack time, training, or tools to analyze students’ reasoning in real time and provide feedback that leverages their ideas to foster the development of scientific reasoning. Innovative, AIsupported assessment design aligned with instructional purposes can bridge this gap by eliciting and analyzing the reasoning patterns students use to make sense of scientific phenomena, enabling feedback that supports conceptual and epistemic growth.

Building on this premise, the demonstrated work applies principles from the learning sciences and argumentation theory to model common reasoning patterns in students’ arguments and to design AI-supported assessment tools that both evaluate and scaffold argumentation. Using existing student response data from NGSSaligned ecosystem assessments, the project identifies recurring reasoning structures, automates their annotation through machine learning, and embeds AI-generated feedback to guide students in strengthening their arguments. Evidence-centered design (ECD) of an AI-supported argumentation task The ECD framework provides a principled approach to ensuring that assessment and AI systems embedded within them generate valid, interpretable evidence about student learning. As Mislevy et al. (2003) emphasize, ECD strengthens validity by linking the student model (what we want to infer about learners) with the evidence model (what behaviors and responses constitute evidence for those inferences) and the task model (the situations that elicit that evidence). Applied to AI-supported assessments, ECD extends beyond traditional psychometric design to also guide AI model development to ensure that the design, evaluation, and feedback algorithms remain tightly coupled with the learning constructs and pedagogical intent. In this demonstration, ECD has served as the organizing framework for designing, implementing, and refining the AI-supported argumentation task, which integrates the scientific practice Engaging in Argument from Evidence with the disciplinary core idea of Ecosystem Dynamics, Functioning, and Resilience from the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS). The development process followed three key phases aligned with ECD principles: target construct identification and domain analysis, domain modeling and AI-integrated evidence model construction, and task design with the embedded AI feedback system.

Target constructs and domain analysis The construct focus of the classroom assessment is student reasoning within scientific argumentation in the context of ecosystem sensemaking. The domain analysis unpacked relevant NGSS performance expectations to define the knowledge and practices students should demonstrate: Engaging in Argument from Evidence: Engaging in argument from evidence in Grade 6–8 builds on K–5 experiences and progresses to constructing a convincing argument that supports or refutes claims for either explanations or solutions about the natural and designed world(s).

• Construct an argument supported by empirical evidence and scientific reasoning to support or refute an

explanation or a model for a phenomenon or a solution to a problem. (MS-LS2-4)

• Evaluate competing design solutions based on jointly developed and agreed upon design criteria. (MS-LS2-5) Ecosystem Dynamics, Functioning, and Resilience: Ecosystems are dynamic in nature; their characteristics can vary over time.

• Disruptions to any physical or biological component of an ecosystem can lead to shifts in all its

populations.

• Biodiversity describes the variety of species found in Earth’s terrestrial and oceanic ecosystems. The

completeness or integrity of an ecosystem’s biodiversity is often used as a measure of its health. Domain modeling and AI-integrated evidence model construction The domain model operationalized these constructs into observable evidence features that could inform both human annotation and AI classification. Drawing on established argumentation frameworks (McNeill & Krajcik, 2008; Osborne et al. 2004) and existing assessment data from state assessments and prior research (Liu et al., 2024), the team identified four reasoning patterns: relational, cause-effect, hypothetical, and analogical reasoning. The assessment scenario positioned students as members of a school environmental club investigating a corn rootworm infestation phenomenon. Students interacted with a customized NetLogo predator-prey simulation (Wilensky, 1999), manipulated population variables, collected data, and interpreted outcomes in light of ecological principles. After the simulation activity, students constructed arguments using the claim-evidencereasoning (CER) structure (McNeill & Krajcik, 2008), explaining whether introducing a predator (harvest spiders) could help restore ecosystem balance. In the evidence model, these student-generated responses were represented as linguistic and conceptual features, serving both as analytic inputs for automated reasoning classification (via GPT-based models) and as interpretive basis for AI-generative formative feedback. Task design with the embedded AI feedback system The task model informed the design of the context, iteration flow, and feedback opportunities within the task. Students’ interactions with the simulation and constructed responses were embedded within an AI-supported environment that provides context-sensitive scaffolding through an integrated chatbot interface called SPIN-Bot. The SPIN-Bot, implemented using OpenAI’s GPT-4o was accessible through a “Need Help?” feature and was equipped with conversational memory and item-level awareness in the assessment task. To ensure alignment with ECD principles, the SPIN-Bot system prompt was developed iteratively through human-in-the-loop prompt engineering, explicitly embedding constructs from the student and evidence models (Liu et al., 2024; Watts et al., 2025). Three design iterations progressively refined the prompt structure: Version 0 focused on general tutor role; Version 1 included detailed task context and alignment with dialogic scaffolding principles (Mercer & Howe, 2012); and Version 2 focused on theory-grounded, bulleted prompts emphasizing argumentation scaffolds (e.g., prompting use of evidence, reasoning justification, and selfreflection). A corpus of 162 student responses to synthetic student prompts was evaluated using a four-dimension rubric (adapted from Duschl, 2003; Nicol & MacFarlan-Dick, 2006; Panadero & Lipnevich, 2022) assessing relevance, clarity, engagement, and reflective prompting. The iterative analysis informed refinements to both the system prompt and the AI evaluation criteria embedded in the evidence model. In addition, the project embodies the ECD principle of design-evidence-interpretation cycles through iterative empirical studies spanning cognitive labs, pilot testing, and field studies. Each iteration strengthens the fidelity between what the AI detects, what evidence it interprets, and what pedagogical feedback it provides. Design principles for ECD-aligned AI systems Drawing from the above iterative design process, we derived several guiding principles for integrating AI into ECD-based assessment design in support of the development of scientific argumentation:

• Embed learning-theoretic constructs in the AI system prompt. Define the student model (e.g.,

argumentation components and the CER structure, ecosystem core ideas) within the AI system prompt so that generated feedback explicitly targets conceptual reasoning and argumentation goals.

• Translate evidence rules into algorithmic features. Operationalize evidence model indicators (e.g.,

relational reasoning or cause-effect reasoning cues) as input features for AI classifiers or conditions for generating feedback.

• Desing tasks as evidence-eliciting interactions. Ensure that each student action or response yields

interpretable data aligned with the reasoning and argumentation constructs.

• Scaffold student reasoning and reflection, rather than focusing on correctness. Prompt reflection and

explanation rather than providing direct answers to prioritize students’ epistemic agency.

• Iterate through human-AI calibration cycles. Use human-in-the-loop evaluation to validate AI responses

against expert reasoning and pedagogical quality.

By coupling ECD with AI development, this demonstration will show how validity arguments can extend to algorithmic systems to ensure that both assessment and AI design reflect the same structure of scientific reasoning and feedback purpose.

Demonstration plan The demonstration will guide participants through the following several interactive segments showcasing how our work integrates simulation-based assessment and AI feedback within the ECD framework. First, it will begin with a live walk-through in which the presenter demonstrates how students explore the NetLogo ecosystem simulation, construct CER arguments, and receive real-time AI feedback. Next, in-person attendees can participate in an interactive exploration through the on-site laptop setups to experience both student and teacher perspectives; and virtual participants can join Zoom breakout rooms to interact with the demonstration. Embedded polls will collect reflections on assessment design, AI feedback usefulness, and improvement suggestions. A behind-thescenes view then illustrates how the AI pipeline was designed to process student input, classify CER components and reasoning patterns, and generate feedback grounded in the evidence model. Finally, during the post-session engagement, participants will receive author contact information for potential future applications for research or classroom use.
