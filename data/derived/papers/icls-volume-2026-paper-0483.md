# Co-designing a Teacher Learning Community to Counter Demoralization for Early Career Elementary Teachers

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper focuses on a math teacher learning community co-designed to nourish, empower, and sustain early career elementary teachers in their commitments to justice. We investigate the moral dilemmas teachers encountered as they tried to bring their commitments into their everyday math teaching in contexts that did not align with these commitments. We find that the co-designed learning community helped teachers renew their sense of agency in their school contexts and counter forces of demoralization.

New teachers often enter the profession motivated by a desire to make a positive impact in the lives of young people. Once they begin working in schools, teachers encounter a wide range of dilemmas as they try to maintain their commitments to young people while working within existing systems and structures (Acton & Glasgow, 2015; R. Gutiérrez, 2009; Louie, 2016). These dilemmas can feel particularly fraught when teachers are attempting to disrupt longstanding social hierarchies and inequities to create more just possibilities for learning (Kokka, 2015; Morales-Doyle, 2019). The moral and ethical dilemmas teachers face as they navigate systems of schooling can lead to a sense of demoralization when teachers find that they are unable to do what they believe is right for young people and encounter “consistent and persistent frustrations in accessing the moral rewards of teaching” (Santoro, 2011, p. 3).

This paper explores the ICLS conference theme of “partnering with purpose,” with a focus on collaborating with teachers to design spaces that nourish educators in their commitments to justice and provide support for the moral and ethical dimensions of their work. We explore the questions 1) What types of moral dilemmas do teachers encounter as they try to bring their commitments to justice and equity into their everyday teaching? and 2) How can a co-designed teacher learning community support teachers with these dilemmas?

## Theoretical perspectives

Santoro (2019) identifies “causing harm to students” and “degrading the profession” as two common sources of teacher demoralization. When teachers are asked to engage in practices that are “developmentally inappropriate, pedagogically ill-advised, or damaging to students’ social-emotional well-being” (Santoro, 2019, p.62) they can experience a sense of demoralization as they try to keep their jobs while also doing their best not to cause harm to their students. Santoro (2019) offers strategies for what she terms “re-moralizing” to support teachers to remain in the profession and to nourish their wellbeing. Professional community forms the foundation of these strategies which include taking student-centered actions and engaging in teacher leadership opportunities. Teacher solidarity co-design offers a methodological approach for partnering with teachers that is well suited to addressing demoralization because it explicitly counters the de-professionalization of teaching  and treats relationships and mutuality as a central concern of design (Philip et al., 2022). Teacher solidarity co-design acknowledges how teachers are multiply positioned in systems of power, attending to the complexity of partnering with teachers in ways that honor teachers’ perspective and needs while maintaining a vision of justice for young people. Building on this work, we examine the moral dilemmas early career elementary teachers face and how a co-designed teacher learning community can provide support for teachers to navigate their dilemmas in ways that align with their moral and ethical commitments to children and to the teaching profession. This paper focuses on a group of six PK-4th grade teachers who chose to be part of a teacher learning community formed around shared commitments to justice stemming from common experiences with ethnic studies pedagogy. The group was formed to support teachers to figure out how to bring these commitments into their math teaching while navigating school systems and structures that were not always aligned with their commitments. All six participating teachers attended the same multiple subject credential progr am and chose to opt into an ethnic studies-focused cohort for their credential program. During their program, principles of ethnic studies pedagogy were emphasized across coursework and field placements, with a focus on designing learning experiences for children that are culturally relevant, community responsive, anti-racist, decolonial, intersectional, and humanizing 2877 (Reyes-McGovern & Buenavista, 2016; Tintiangco-Cubales et al., 2015). The first author, who was the instructor for the math and science methods courses in their credential program, served as the group facilitator. Participating teachers all work in schools in California that serve immigrant communities that have been impacted by the recent surge in anti-immigrant policy and rhetoric. The teacher learning community draws on teacher solidarity co-design (Philip et al., 2022) to inform the ongoing co-design of joint activities, with a focus on creating space for grappling with tensions and dilemmas (K. D. Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016) as teachers attempt to bring their shared commitments into practice. The group meets once per month over zoom with periodic in person meetings  and classroom visits from the facilitator. Activities have evolved over time from getting to know each other and sharing stories about teaching to building a shared vision for math teaching and discussing artifacts from the classroom that align with that vision. Data sources include video recordings of monthly meetings, video recordings of one-on-one conversations with the facilitator, artifacts from the classroom (e.g. student work, math tasks), and fieldnotes from classroom visits. The moral dilemmas that teachers brought to the group fall into three categories, 1) dilemmas involving school/district curricular materials or decisions that conflict with teachers’ commitments to children, 2) dilemmas involving school policies that undermine their work, and 3) dilemmas about how to navigate differing political views of colleagues or administrators without jeopardizing their job or collegial relationships. The dilemma that came up most frequently across meetings and participants was how to navigate assessment policies in ways that aligned with teachers’ moral commitments to children. Teachers repeatedly brought up the disconnect between the assessment systems used at their school sites and their goals for making math relevant for their students. During one breakout room discussion of upper elementary teachers, 4th grade teacher Sarah, described the challenge of “trying to square making math meaningful, fun and purposeful with the requirements in the district of hav ing to get the goddamn iReady done.” In the ensuing discussion, teachers compared notes about how assessment systems were being utilized at their school sites, the challenges they were facing, and how they were holding onto their principles in the face of these systems. The group became a place where teach ers could remember their commitments and grapple together with how to live up to them in the everyday realities of their  contexts. Sarah described the function of the group as, “the biggest thing is just a reminder of who, not even who I am, but who I really want to be. And not get pulled too far into the sewer.” This theme of the group reminding them of who they wanted to be as teachers was echoed by other participants, with PK teacher Caroline commenting that the group was supporting her to “follow my pedagogy in my heart,” 3rd grade teacher Maria saying she didn’t want to “become an old lady who lacks empathy,” and 3rd grade teacher Tina commenting that the group helped her remember that “I’m not crazy.” commitments, but they also eventually led tonew actions such as speaking up to administrators or trying something new in their teaching. For example, Sarah began the year teaching math predominately from the district math curriculum, even though she did not think it was working well for her students. Over time and with the support of the group, Sarah began to try different approaches in her math teaching to make math more meaningful for her students and to use her curricular materials more creatively. In addition to math teaching dilemmas such as those described above, teachers also brought dilemmas related to the fraught times our country is experiencing, for example supporting each other with how to navigate immigration raids that were creating a cult ure of fear for children and families. Future analyses will further examine the different types of actions teachers took in response to their dilemmas and specific design choices (such as co-constructing a list of core commitments and related meeting topic s) that helped teachers hold onto their commitments and bring them into their practice as part of a process of collectively re-moralizing their work. For the teacher learning community discussed here, “partnering with purpose” involved co-constructing a space where teachers could dig into the moral dilemmas they faced and find solidarity and new ideas in the commitments that are closest to their hearts.  During these fraught times, finding ways to support teachers with the moral and ethical dimensions of their work may be especially needed. Learning more about teacher learning spaces that deliberately counter demoralization can offer new design ideas that  help teachers feel nourished and empowered to provide children with the rich educational opportunities they deserve. 2878 Acton, R., & Glasgow, P.
