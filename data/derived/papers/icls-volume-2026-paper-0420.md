# Rural Primary Students’ Early Conceptions of Artificial Intelligence

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Young learners’ initial conceptions of artificial intelligence (AI) shape how they engage with AI and have implications for instructional design. 23 Grade 4–5 students in a rural region of China completed a paper-based survey examining how they defined AI, identified it in everyday technologies, and perceive d their interest and confidence in learning it. Students often described AI in functional or robotic terms, with some associating it with automation or simple responsiveness. Students’ interest was generally high, but confidence varied. These findings suggest that early AI education should support both the development of more refined conceptual understanding and learners’ confidence.

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has been increasingly integrated into education and everyday life, making AI literacy an important topic in K –12 education. Currently, educators have established frameworks and outlined core competencies for AI education (Long & Magerko, 2020; Touretzky et al., 2019), and proposed design principles and classroom practices (Zhou et al., 2020; Lee & Perret, 2022). However, the successful design of AI instruction goes beyond these efforts and requires an understanding of young learners’ initial conceptions of AI to ensure that instruction is appropriately designed and aligned with their cognitive and affective development. Even before formal schooling, children are already exposed to various AI-powered technologies due to their widespread use. Research shows that children hold mixed conceptions of AI, often associating it with robots or automation, while also expressing both curiosity and concern (Bewersdorff et al., 2023; Mertala et al., 2022). These initial understandings influence how students engage with AI and, therefore, should be taken into consideration.

Most prior studies have been conducted in well-resourced settings, such as urban areas and more developed countries (Mertala et al., 2022; Walan, 2025). Research tends to overlook how rural students understand AI. At the same time, AI-powered tools are already part of everyday life in these regions. Looking at both well-resourced and less-resourced contexts gives a clearer sense of students’ starting points and can guide more inclusive teaching.

To bring in perspectives from rural regions of China, this study examines three research questions: how students conceptualize AI (RQ1), how they identify AI in everyday technologies (RQ2), and how they perceive their interest in and confidence in learning AI (RQ3).

We recruited participants from a four-week summer camp in a rural region of China. 23 Grade 4 –5 students participated voluntarily, with parental consent obtained prior to the study. Students completed a paper-based survey that explored how they understand artificial intelligence. The survey asked them to define AI in their own words, identify AI in everyday technologies, and rate their interest and confidence in learning about it. The open-ended responses were analyzed using an inductive coding approach, producing categories such as functions, robots, and automation. Because many students expressed more than one idea, multiple codes were allowed. Two researchers coded the responses independently and reached strong agreement (κ =.86). Students generally described AI in concrete and familiar terms. Figure 1a shows that they most often defined AI in terms of its functions  (57%) or as robots (43%). O nly a few students mentioned  intelligence or thinking. For example, some described AI as something that “explains problems” or as a robot that can “do many things.” These patterns suggest that students’ early conceptions are grounded in tangible and familiar forms. This reflects how learners build understanding from intuitive ideas (diSessa, 1993). Such conceptions are developmentally appropriate but also highlight a need for instruction that supports the gradual refinement of understanding (Vosniadou, 1994).

Figure 1b shows that students demonstrated some awareness of AI in contemporary technologies, including smartwatches (83%), drones (78%), and recommendation apps (70%). At the same time, many students attributed AI to devices that do not involve AI, such as electric fans (61%), toy cacti (35%), and cassette recorders (30%). These responses suggest that AI is often interpreted as general automation or responsiveness. This pattern points to a key conceptual challenge: distinguishing between rule-based automated systems and adaptive, data2689 driven AI. Therefore, instruction needs to make this distinction more explicit, for example by contrasting simple automated devices with systems that learn from data.

Students showed strong interest in learning AI, but their confidence levels varied. Figure 2(a) shows that 87% reported being interested or very interested, with responses clustering at the higher end. Confidence was more varied in Figure 2(b), with responses spread across levels. About one-third of students were unsure whether they could learn AI. Among the 11 students who were very interested, only 5 believed they could definitely learn AI, while the others expressed lower confidence. This difference suggests that interest does not necessarily lead to confidence and may relate to variation in self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997). From an instructional perspective, it is critical to sustain curiosity while also creating opportunities for success that support confidence development throughout learning experiences.

In this study, we found that rural primary students often anchor their understanding of AI in concrete terms, such as its functions or robots. Some also attributed intelligence to simple forms of automation.
