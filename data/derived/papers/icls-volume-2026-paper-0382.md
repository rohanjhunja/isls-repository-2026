# Democratizing Algebra: Advancing Conceptual Understanding Through Purpose -Driven Research-Practice-Industry Partnership

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This RPIP study refined From Here to There (FH2T), a game-based algebra application, by positioning educators as pedagogical architects. Through iterative feedback loops, practitioners directly influenced critical features like the Teacher Console, ensuring classroom-readiness and scalability. Results demonstrate that FH2T democratizes math by decoupling conceptual logic from computational barriers. By grounding learning in perceptual engagement, the game transforms students from passive rule-followers into agentic "Doers," dismantling traditional gatekeepers to algebraic success.

In alignment with the ISLS 2026 theme, “Partnering with Purpose,” this paper explores a research-practiceindustry partnership (RPIP) between Digital Promise, Graspable Math, Worcester Polytechnic Institute (WPI), and K12 educators. This cross-sector colla boration advanced research on From Here to There, a game-based application that replaces rote memorization with discovery-based puzzles. Our study’s goals were twofold: (1) to understand how digital tools candemocratize the learning of complex mathematical concepts, and (2) to center practitioner feedback to ensure FH2T is ready for sustainable, context-aware impact at scale. By democratization, we mean shifting the instructional focus from procedural execution to the active manipulation of mathematical structures. This ensures that access to high-level algebraic reasoning is no longer gatekept by computational anxiety or prior numeracy gaps, but is instead fostered through intuitive, perceptual engagement. FH2T achieves this by grounding its design in perceptual learning theory (Goldstone et al., 2017), which posits that how students interpret sensory information influences how  they process new concepts. Specifically, the game makes abstract symbols concrete through tangible virtual objects and gesture-based manipulation (see Figure 1). By providing fluid visualizations of mathematical transformations and immediate feedback (such as a "shake" for invalid moves), FH2T helps students transition from passive rule-followers to agentic "Doers" of math. This paper describes year one of this study, detailing how iterative feedback loops between teachers, researchers, and developers —faci litated by Digital Promise as a "knowledge broker" — transformed the product into a more equitable tool for conceptual understanding (Baker et al., 2022). Figure 1 Sample Problem in From Here to There Research-practice-industry partnerships The RPIP model exemplifies "partnering with purpose" by shifting power dynamics so that industry, researchers, and practitioners have an equal voice (Farrell et al., 2021). Unlike traditional processes where efficacy research occurs long after a product enters the market, the RPIP integrates practitioner expertise into the early development cycle (Peppler & Schindler, 2021). This model moves beyond basic user testing, positioning educators as pedagogical co-architects who directly influence the product’s technical roadmap. In this framework, Digital Promise acts as a knowledge broker, creating a translation layer between the pedagogical realities of the classroom and the technical constraints of software engineering (Rechsteiner et al., 2024). By bringing the learning sciences into product development, the RPIP ensures that FH2T is not just mathematically sound, but "classroom-ready," optimized to be used effectively across diverse and complex settings (Pautz Stephenson et al., 2022). This collaborative loop acknowledges that  while industry provides the platform, practitioners provide the essential context required to scale interventions for marginalized learners. 2572 The study employed a participatory design approach and mixed methods to explore how digital tools candemocratize learning complex math and how practitioner feedback supports implementation at scale. The RPIP utilized four pillars: (1) professional learnin g (PL) for educators, (2) collaborative research design, (3) iterative feedback loops, and (4) product/practice improvements. Five PL sessions transformed 10 K-12 educators (RPIP phase) into ethnographic observers of their own students. Rather than reporti ng general engagement, teachers documented specific conceptual friction points —moments where a student’s algebraic logic was sound, but the interface’s feedback (the “shake”) or gamification caused confusion. These insights were fed into iterative loops where researchers and developers presented wireframes for rapid co-design and pedagogical stress-testing. Following the RPIP, a confirmatory study with 11 new educators validated these findings through independent classroom implementation. Data across both phases included self-report tools (measuring usability and feasibility), educator surveys, and semi-structured interviews. Qualitative data underwent thematic analysis (Kiger & Varpio, 2020) to refine emergent themes regarding student agency and product-market fit, while survey responses provided descriptive measures like the System Usability Scale (SUS) and technology-task fit. The RPIP and confirmatory studies established the utility of FH2T as a unique tool for making algebraic concepts accessible across a broad spectrum of learners. A primary case study of the co-design process in action was the evolution of the Teacher Console. Initially envisioned by developers as a metric-heavy dashboard, iterative feedback loops with practitioners revealed a need for a tool that supported real-time, process-oriented instruction. Based on educator recommendations, developers implemented a “common struggles” view that identifies specific mathematical points where students reset or struggled. Furthermore, the shift from Dashboard (traditionally associated with evaluative surveillance) to Console  (reframed as an instructional support tool) ensured the tool aligned with a supportive, non-threatening classroom culture. These co-designed features directly facilitated the democratization of algebraic learning. Educators reported that FH2T’s intuitive drag-and-drop interface and automated arithmetic ensured that numeracy gaps did not gatekeep grade-level conceptual understanding. By providing a safe-to-fail environment where students "can't break math," the game fostered a sense of mathematical identity and belonging. Students were transformed from passive math rule-followers into agentic "Doers of Math," responsible for making strategic decisions rather than merely executing procedures.

Educators across diverse contexts—ranging from gifted elementary to high school remedial programs — valued the game’s ability to promote student collaboration. When implemented collaboratively, the quest for efficient, "three-star" solutions sparked peer discourse about mathematical structure, further shifting the classroom dynamic from teacher-led instruction to student-led sense-making. By centering peer-to-peer discourse, the game moves the authority of the right answer away from the teacher and into the shared logic of the student group. This paper has demonstrated how "partnering with purpose" can transform the development of educational technology from a top-down process into a collaborative, equitable endeavor, replacing isolated design cycles with a collaborative ecosystem that aligns technical innovation with the lived realities of student-centered pedagogy. By utilizing the RPIP model, we moved beyond traditional efficacy testing to a co-design framework where educators served as pedagogical architects. This partnership directly informed the evolution of From Here to There, ensuring that the tool's design responded to the real-world needs of diverse classrooms. The participatory nature of this study confirms that digital tools grounded in perceptual learning can effectively democratize access to mathematics. By decoupling high-level algebraic reasoning from rote computation and creating a safe-to-fail environment  for exploration, FH2T empowers students to move from passive rule-followers to agentic "Doers of Math." Ultimately, this research suggests that when researchers, developers, and practitioners work in a virtuous cycle of feedback, they can create technologies that not only scale across varied cultural contexts but also dismantle the historical gatekeeping of algebra, ensuring that mathematical agency becomes a universal right rather than a privileged outcome.
