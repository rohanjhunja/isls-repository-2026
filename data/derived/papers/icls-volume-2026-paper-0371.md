# Co-Designing GenAI Reading Technologies: Scaling Practitioner Expertise Within a National R&D Initiative

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This case study examines the U-GAIN Reading R&D Center’s national leadership cohort, a practitioner-centered initiative exploring how generative artificial intelligence (GenAI)-enabled learning technologies can enhance early reading instruction in grades 1-3. The cohort brings together 11 educators from 10 school districts across seven U.S. states to collaboratively co-design resources for integrating GenAI-enabled reading interventions in schools. Using participatory and sociocultural approaches, we studied how educators participated in seven co-design meetings to explore effective uses of GenAI-enabled learning technologies in classrooms. Issues addressed U-GAIN Reading R&D Center is a research and development hub dedicated to exploring how educators can effectively use generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) to enhance elementary reading instruction in grades 1-3. This initiative aims to better understa nd how GenAI-enabled digital reading interventions can be used to give students feedback and support as they read decodable text out loud. Additionally, U-GAIN Reading seeks to build an evidence-base for the benefits of implementing this knowledge at scale, as well as to offer national leadership on how the development of GenAI-enabled technologies for reading can be co-designed with educators. To accomplish these goals, U-GAIN Reading leverages two strands of work: Focused Research and National Leadership, the latter of which is the focus of this case study.

## Potential significance

U-GAIN Reading National Leadership co-design activities beganduring the 2025-2026 school year and include collaboration between educators and researchers to co-design reading interventions and resources with a focus on Amira, a popular GenAI reading technology serving millions of students across the United States. Students in participating school districts include many developing multilingual readers in grades 1-3, providing a strong opportunity to advance a national research agenda for co-design at scale. As such, we are developing a national leadership cohort of educators to explore how school districts can better design GenAI-enabled learning technologies to meet a broad range of needs among emerging readers. We are interested in better understanding how the National Leadership cohort’s engagement in co-design activities inform the development of GenAI-enabled reading technologies and resources within the context of a large-scale R&D center. Theoretical approach Research on practitioner learning suggests that opportunities to collaborate and co-design with colleagues are essential for exploring new ideas, sharing successes and challenges, and expanding professional knowledge (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017). The co-design activities being explored in this case study align with co-design elements identified by Roschelle et al. (2006), who define co-design as team-based processes where members work together to design innovations. The work of the national leadership cohort is exploring three areas of inquiry: Shaping the conversation around the ethics and use of GenAI-enabled learning technologies for young readers, meeting with national experts and sharing key research in the science of reading, and gaining leadership experience through national event participation and presentation opportunities. We adopt sociocultural approaches (Nasir & Hand, 2006), draw on funds of knowledge research (Gonzalez et al., 2005), and position members as collaborators and knowledge holders (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016), to explore cohort co-design activities in relation to these areas of inquiry.

Methodological approach 2539 We adopt an embedded case study design (Yin, 2014) incorporating participant design research approaches to codesign (Roschelle et al., 2006). We invited a purposively sampled (Etikan et al., 2016) educator cohort consisting of 11 educators from 10 distric ts across seven U.S. states to participate in seven virtual co-design meetings over the course of the 2025-2026 school year. Data stemming from these meetings include educator and researcher artifacts, meeting notes, and survey and 1:1 check-in responses. These data are being analyzed using directed content approach (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005) integrating first and second cycle coding (Saldaña, 2021), as well as analytic description (Miles et al., 2013) to identify approaches, strategies, and practices from co-designed activities and artifacts. We also note our resulting thoughts, reflections, and analyses through analytic memoing to surface insights  from the data (Birks et al., 2008). We use constant comparative strategies to discuss data Sandelowski, 1993). We also use respondent validation (Long & Johnson, 2000) and triangulation (Kuper et al.,

2008) to comment on whether initial findings accurately re flect member experiences and to arrive at more

comprehensive findings.

## Initial findings

We are exploring emerging findings across three iterative co-design phases: Backgrounding, Ideating (exploring and prototyping to create resources), and Refining (selecting promising ideas for further implementation). The Backgrounding phase illustrates how cohort members’ expertise as educators informs their assessment of problems of practice and feasibility during initial co-design processes, as expressed by a cohort member: I tend to think about how ideas will actually play out in real classrooms, especially in rural schools (Title 1) where teachers are balancing a wide range of student needs, time constraints, and different comfort levels with technology. Because of my background in instructional coaching and technology integration, I often focus on how resources can be implemented in ways that support teachers rather than overwhelm them.

Ideating: Educators collaboratively co-creating resources with partners Ideating is an intermediate co-design phase in which cohort members engage in capacity building through collaborative co-creation of resources with each other and researchers, as explained by another cohort member: Collaboration on co-design activities is centered around discussion about the practices currently in place in our elementary classrooms across the country. We are building perspectives, sharing ideas, and offering solutions. At the same time, educators are  getting information on current research that helps inform our instruction through guest researchers sharing their work. Refining: Integration and implementation Refining is a reflective process marked by the cohort’s critical evaluation of whether co-designed activities and resources are usable in practice, as shared by the following cohort member: The biggest challenge in developing and implementing co-designed ideas like the National Toolkit is moving from a shared vision to consistent, real world, practice. We need to ensure that the [resource] is practical, adaptable, and supported with ongoing professional development. We do not want teachers to see it as “one more initiative.”

## Implications

This case study seeks to contribute to research-practice partnership and co-design literatures by exploring promising educator co-design activities for effective adoption of GenAI-enabled reading technologies, including the challenge of translating local e xpertise into national impact. As such, we seek to offer a framework for discussing how practitioner-researcher co-design work, often occurring in the context of small and mid-size groupings (see Lee et al., 2024), can be extended to support large-scale national R&D initiatives at the intersection of technology, innovation, literacy, and education.
