# Fostering Learners’ Environmental Literacy through Augmented Reality and Virtual Reality: A Meta-Analysis

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This review addresses the dearth of meta-analyses investigating the effects of augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR) within the domain of environmental education. The analysis of thirty peer-reviewed journal articles encompassing fifty-two independent studies indicated that the implementation of AR and VR technologies, in comparison to traditional educational methods that do not utilize these tools, resulted in small yet statistically significant improvement in knowledge achievement and student dispositions. Furthermore, the technologies exhibited a large effect on skill acquisition and a minimal effect on environmentally responsible behavior. Additionally, the influence on dispositions was found to be moderated by educational level and learning time.

Research objectives Environmental education (EE) seeks to empower indiv iduals with the essential knowledge, skills, and at titudes necessary for transformative action as active participants in addressing environmental issues (Hollweg et al., 2011; UNESCO, 2024). Despite the growing awareness of env ironmental challenges, EE encounters difficulties due to natural phenomena that are macro in scale, complex in causation and effects, and temporally lagging (Foss & Ko, 2019). Consequently, these issues may appear invisible and imperceptible to learners and could not be effectively addressed through using traditional lecturing and m ultimedia (Plutzer et al., 2016). In recent years, augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR) have increasi ngly been employed and empirically examined within the context of EE, due to their unique immersive and interactive features (Fauville et al., 2014; Radianti et al., 2020), which embedded experiential learning (Kolb, 2014) and embodied cognition (Barsalou, 2008). Nonetheless, there has been no compr ehensive meta-analysis synthesizing the effects of these technolo gies across environmental education studies. Previous meta-analyses focusing on the application of AR and VR h ave predominantly been conducted within general educational contexts (e.g., Coban et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2020). Furthermore, they d id not separately examine learning outcomes that are distinct in their nature  (e.g., knowledge  encompassing facts and principles related to the learning topic versus dispositions  referring to attitudes, v iews, self-efficacy, etc.) (Garzon & Azevado et al., 2019; Merchant et al., 2014; Villena-Taranilla et al., 2022).

To address the limitations of prior reviews, this s tudy undertakes a comprehensive meta-analysis of empirical studies concerning the utilization of AR and VR in environmental education  by investigating the following questions: RQ1. What are the overall effects of using vs. not using  AR and VR technologies in environmental education on learners’ knowledge achievement, skill  acquisition, dispositions, and environmentally res ponsible behaviors?

RQ2. How might the impacts of using vs. not using AR and VR technologies in environmental education on learners’ learning performance be influenced by potential moderators (i.e., educational level, research design, treatment technology, control format, learning time)?

## Methods

This study conducted a systematic literature search for peer-reviewed English papers published between 2010 and 2024 in the Web of Science and Scopus databases using the search string (“augmented reality” OR “virtual reality” OR “mixed reality” OR “simulat ion”) AND (“environmental education” OR “pro-environmental” OR “climate change”) AND (“teach*” OR “learn*” OR “educat*”). A  hand search was also performed for (1) journals in  the fields of EE, science education, and educational technologies, and (2) previous reviews in EE and educ ational application of AR&VR. Research among the 1,571 undu plicated records was excluded if it was not a peer-reviewed study, not published in English, not related to environmental topics, not involving virtual or augmented reality, not empirical studies recruiting school learners, and not controlled-experimental studies. A total of 30 articles comprising 52 individual studies (i.e., on e contrast comparing the effects of using versus no t using AR&VR in EE) were identified and coded with respect  to learning outcomes (knowledge achievement, skill acquisition, dispositions, and environmentally resp onsible behaviors), educational level (K-12, higher education learners), research design (quasi-experimental design, randomized-controlled test), treatment technology (AR, VR), control format (traditional methods, multimedia such as slides or video), and learning time (formal, informal). Effect sizes for each independent study were synthe sized for the four learning outcomes based on Lipse y and Wilson’s (2001) practical guidelines. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test. Four outliers were identified and excluded based on the principles suggested by Viechtbauer and Cheung (2010). Moderator analysis was conducted for specific types  of learning outcomes, revealing significant result s from homogeneity analysis, wherein the variability observed among the studies could be explained by substantial study features mentioned in the coding framework.

## Results

The results presented a small yet significant effect of using AR and VR o n knowledge achievement (𝑔 = 0.35, 95% CI [0. 20, 0.50], k = 21) and student dispositions (𝑔 = 0.35, 95% CI [0.20, 0.50], k = 37), a large effect on skills acquisition (𝑔 = 0.80, 95% CI [0.20, 0.50], k = 8), and a minimal effect on environmentally responsi ble behavior (𝑔 = 0.05, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.17], k = 23).

Homogeneity analysis results for dispositions sugges ted moderator analysis w as required to explain significant variances in ESs across included studie s. For the ESs for dispositions, there was no signific ant variability among Randomized Controlled Test versus quasi-experimental design  (QB = 0.967, df = 1, p >.05), using AR versus VR in treatment (QB = 0.967, df = 1, p >.05), or employing traditional methods versus multimedia in control condition (QB = 0.005, df = 1, p >.05). However, the effects for students’ dispositions were moderated by educational level and learning time, with learners of K-12 level (𝑔 = 0.61, 95% CI [0.32, 0.90], k = 12) reporting higher dispositions than those of higher education (𝑔 = 0.21, 95% CI [0.04, 0.37], k  = 25), and formal environmental education programs (𝑔 = 0.66, 95% CI [0.34, 0.97], k = 10) generating better outcomes than informal programs (𝑔 = 0.21, 95% CI [0.06, 0.36], k = 27).

## Discussion

The positive effects of cognitive and affective learning performance associated with environmental education may be attributed to the affordances of AR and VR. Howe ver, these positive effects should not be overstate d, as numerous individual studies report negative to mini mal impacts of the intervention on knowledge achiev ement and dispositions. These outcomes may be due to unexpected technological issues (Hurrell et al., 2024), cognitive load imposed by the technologies (Soekarjo & Oosten dorp, 2015), and the failure to account for learner s' prior experiences (Plechatá et al., 2022). Furthermore, the positive effects on skill acquisition wer e identified in only eight studies, a number smaller than that observed for knowledge achievement and dispositions. This li mited number may be attributed to an absence of problem-solving teaching methodologies in these studies or the unavailability of appropriate measures or instrumen ts to assess task-related cognitive or social skills. Another notable finding is the minimal effect on environmen tally responsible behaviors, which may be explained  by the distinct nature of various behavior types (e.g., habit-driven behavior such as food choice versus stimulating behavior such as donation) and the lack of reliable or valid  instruments for evaluating behavioral change (Dunn et al., 2021).

The application of AR and VR within K-12 educational contexts yielded significantly larger effect sizes than those observed in higher education, corroborating findings from previous meta-analyses (Coban et al., 2022; Garzon & Acevedo, 2019; Wu et al., 2020). This may be attributable to K-12 students, who may lack life experiences and knowledge of unobservable phenomena, being more susceptible to the influences of immer sive experiences offered by VR or AR (Fokides & Chachlaki, 2020). The favorable impact of environmental programs conducted in formal settings compared to those in i nformal settings may be explained by a deficiency i n appropriate lesson planning and instructional suppo rt during informal learning periods (Schneider & Sc haal, 2018).

## Implications

This study synthesized the effects of utilizing AR and VR in the specific domain of environmental educ ation, focusing on four aspects of environmental literacy and the factors influencing these effects. The synt hesis provided substantial evidence regarding the impacts of AR and VR on environmental education and offers implications for the design and implementation of AR and VR in both research and practice within this field. Key recommendations include: 1. Considering learners’ p rior knowledge and life experiences when determinin g the learning content presented by AR and VR; 2. Identif ying behaviors that may be influenced in the short term through emotional engagement and developing more re liable and valid behavioral measurements for environmental education; 3. Placing greater emphasi s on assessing learning performance across multiple dimensions, particularly in terms of skills acquisi tion; and 4. Acknowledging various factors (e.g., e ducational level, learning environment) that may affect the efficacy of AR and VR in shaping learners' dispositions.
