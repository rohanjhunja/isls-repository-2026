# Kites in the Wind: Response Cries as Evidence of Listening

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Ingold (2007) argues that we hear in sound just as we see in light. The immersive media of sound and activities of everyday listening have largely been neglected in the field of the learning sciences. Reflecting on Ingold’s metaphor of a kite in the sky, I discuss the importance of Goffman’s (1978) concept of response cry in beginning to show us listening in learning. This paper describes three qualitative studies that all center sound as the primary medium for meaning-making. Findings demonstrate the ways in which response cries constitute evidence of listening and the tension of doing the complex work that is being a listener, particularly in the setting of listening together. Despite the similar immersiveness of these media, our world has been dominated by the hegemony of the visual, as well documented by media scholars (Levin, 1993; Cox, 2011). I have a personal stake in making this claim, as I believe worthwhile research should be personally meaningful: I am a life-long musician and someone who has spent hours being formally trained to listen (as many of us have with our current school systems). I have  been struck by how beautifully complex listening i s both in my own experience as a listener and in my study of it as a researcher. I believe we have an incredible opportunity to better understand how listening is constructed as a social practice. In this way, the phrase “active listening” is redundant.

Listening in the literature Listening, which is distinct from hearing in that i t is a conscious process of meaning-making rather t han a physiological one (Low and Sonntag, 2013), is notor iously complex to understand, accounting for its ne glect (Rowley-Jolivet, 2002). Certainly, the complexities  of listening became apparent and even visceral in the COVID19 pandemic and the shift in modalities of hea ring and listening from classroom co-presence to di gital and long-distance. Historically, findings describe listening is the primary communicative activity of students, accounting for much more time than writing, reading, or speaking (Barker, Gladney, Edwards, Holley, and Gaines, 1980; Emanuel, Baker, Daufin, Ellington, Fitts, Him sel, Holladay and Okeowo, 2008). Overwhelmingly, ea rly research praises the teachability of listening as d emonstrated through comprehension exams, in which s tudents who were instructed to summarize, draw inferences, and recall facts accurately and in order (Devine, 1978). This, however, reduces the nuances of sonic communication to a version of spoken text, a reduction that colors most of the past century’s work on listening and leaves stu dents to “prove” their listening by the rote regurg itation of information that plagues reading comprehension. Whi le both listening and reading involve internalized, interpretive thinking, they are two very different activities (Bostrom, 2011). Other research has theorized listening by comparing it to mathematical communication via signals and noise (Shannon and Weaver, 1949), to computers via inform ation processing (Brown, Anderson, Shadbolt, & Lynch, 1985), and to the evolution of language via hun ters and gatherers (Barsalou, 1999). Some have even offered theories of listening that are one-way as opposed to two-way, proposing that listening to an instructor ‘transmit’ knowledge is a mono-directional transaction (Lynch and Mendelsohn, 2010). Even in the case of diminish ed or lost hearing, embodiment of hearable interaction li ke the movement of lips, singing, facial expression  is an essential mode of communication (Friedner & Helmreich, 2012). ICLS2022 Proceedings 711 © ISLS Much of the interactive process of both formal and informal learning environments relies on listening: lecturing, discussion, conversation, music, ambient  sound, as well as environments that leverage audio performances that are subject-specific (Shapiro, Ha ll & Owens, 2017; Hall & SLaM, 2020; Silvis, Taylor  & Stevens, 2018). If we did not assume both the power  and potential of listening, we would not have the study of rhetoric, discourse analysis, conversation analysis, and communication studies. Since making and atten ding to sound is an interpretive, meaning-making process, w e as a research community must seriously examine experiences of listening, including song and story.

Yet how do we know that someone is listening? This is a problem in daily life, in education broadly, and in research on the processes of listening. What is hea rable is wider than what is heard, and what is hear d is wider than what we might have listened to. We know there are certain performances associated with listening: making eye contact with a speaker, nodding our head, or repeating a certain phrase that was just heard. These, however, do not constitute sufficient evidence that what was  said (the hearable) was actually processed and und erstood (listened to). How do we study listening, then?

Theoretical approach This paper examines three studies which are linked by the centrality of auditory media to make an argument for the value of examining listening in learning. Here,  listening is defined as a complex, meaning-making activity that leverages sound and the perceptual apparatuses of hearing. Listening provides similar opportunities as looking in the sense that there are ways of attuning one’s perception. Listening to something is similar to looking at something, a stance of observation. Listening for something, however, is akin to looking for something, a stance of searching. The negotiation between these two mod es of listening constitutes the experience of liste ning that Ingold describes when he writes against soundscape (2007): If sound is like the wind, then it will not stay put, nor does it put persons or things in their place. Sound flows, as wind blows, along irregular, winding paths, and the places it describes are like eddies, formed by a circular movement around rather than a fixed location within. To follow sound, that is to listen, is to wander the same paths. Attentive listening, as opposed to passive h earing, surely entails the very opposite of emplace ment. We may, in practice, be anchored to the ground, but  it is not sound that provides the anchor. Again, t he analogy with flying a kite is apposite. Though the flyer’s feet may be firmly planted on the spot, it is not the wind that keeps them there. Likewise, the sweep of sound continually endeavors to tear listeners away, causing them to surrender to its movement. It requires an effort to stay in place. And this effort pulls against sound rather than harmonizing with it. Place confinement, in short, is a form of deafness. (p.2) While I disagree with some of Ingold’s other arguments around soundscape conceptually, his identification of the inherent movement of sound and his analogy to a kite are useful. Flying kites, proverbially, also allows us to learn something about both the construction of the kite and the wind with which it interacts. While the wind, like sound, is invisible, it is made evident through its interactions with other objects. So too is listening to sound made visible through interaction. This visibility is key since listening is uniquely a private sense-making experience: we cannot listen precisely like anyone else yet listening happens in social contexts. Here, Goffman’s work on response cries (1978) becom es helpful in understanding the internalization of a social other: we move throughout public life with a n awareness that others might see or hear us. In Go ffman’s example, to trip on a sidewalk while sober and of sound mind often produces an utterance like, “They really ought to fix that sidewalk” not because there is a direct recipient of the utterance, but because the tripper lives in a world where clarifying to any onlookers that he is sober and of sound mind is socially valuable. Response cr ies demonstrate a figured world (Holland et al, 2001) of public perception which Goffman calls “response presence”; importantly, the winds of sound are an ever-present  part of this world, its weather. It is in this wor ld, then, that we might design activities to better understand listening’s role in learning. The kite is the response cry that we toss up in the air to twist in the wind, be upheld by it, and demonstrate the wind’s very existence. The string between the k ite and the person flying it is sense-making, the a ctivity of staying in attuned connection with the motions and qualities of the wind, being in productive tension with time and place. It is our own tugging on the string and the wind’s tugging back that co-produces a felt con cept of “windiness”. This tension is felt against the ground where we stand, our existing identities and experiences we as learners bring to the act of listening. The wind is that which is hearable all around us and, in the case of the studies in this paper, the eddies that we designed to represent place and time. The entire process of flying a kite constitutes listening (Figure 1).

In this paper, all of the designs for listening are such that no one is listening alone. While listening is an internal and private experience, it is always socially contextualized and most often happens amongst people as a part of communication. Using Hall & Ma’s lens of ensemble learning (2018), I posit that ensemble listening— listening that we do together—is a different animal than solo listening. In short, there are things that we can learn when we listen together that perhaps are unavailable when we are listening alone: there are no solo kiteflyers in these studies. Designing for listening Three Studies Let me orient us to the three studies in this analysis. These studies come to us as a part of ongoing design-based research (Barab & Squire, 2004) by the Space, Learning and Mobility (SLaM) lab. The content in the first case is quite different from the other two in that a single  song is the focus of the session. In the latter tw o studies, a collection of digitized songs and oral histories is embedded along a mapped route. In all three, however, the idea of controversial or contested narratives arises. While separate analysis has been conducted for each study with its respective set of research questions and findings, this paper presents an analysis of the studies’ shared interest in centering listening and presents findings that cross-cut all three.

## Context for study 1: listening session

In the summer of 2015, sixteen high school students enrolled in a summer course at an elite Southern university program for rising high schoolers nominated controversial songs for analysis by the whole class. One nomination that was selected by facilitators was the song “Blurred Lines” by Robin Thicke and Pharrell. Since its publication, the song has garnered appropriate critique of its lyrics and themes regarding consent in sexual encounters and the gendered power dynamics of inter-personal relationship. In short, the singer comes across as someone who does not respect women. Using two cameras set on tripods  and four microphones distributed across the four-p erson table groups, a record was compiled to show how students responded to listening to the song together.

## Context for studies 2 and 3: listening on the move

The SLaM lab has been engaged in a long-term partne rship with a community-based music museum wherein efforts have focused on preserving under-told stories about Black history in the area. Since music and oral history is at the core of our projects together, we had beg un to ask questions about how students made sense o f the recordings in relationship to the places about which the music and historians talked. Through design-based research, we built a series of activities related to learning on the move (Marin, Taylor, Shapiro, & Hall, 2020) where students would encounter a qualitatively themed map with digital archival material of songs and oral histories while walking along a p ath of points of interest on the street where the m useum is ICLS2022 Proceedings 713 © ISLS located. A majority of the students participating in these walks were white, middle class, and had characteristics of identity and experience which were disparate from those that were represented in the DSSLs. The tension that Ingold identifies can be leveraged in design to amplify the experience of palimpsest, of being simultaneously here and then (Hall, R. & Space, Learning and Mobility Lab, 2020) which was of interest to our research team. In the spring of 2019, thirteen master’s students in followed two modes of a digital spatial storyline (DSSL) (Hall, R. & Space, Learning and Mobility Lab, 2020). DSSLs are a form of qualitative thematic mapping that attach multimedia sources to points in space as a way of telling (hi)stories about place. The master’s course was oriented around the goal of developing a deep understanding of designing experiences for social studies learning. Our lab’s interaction was positioned as an opportunity for students to be participants in a design-based research study in order to gain insight into how they might conduct their own future studies. The DSSL contents were identical: both involved digital media of oral histories by local experts, songs related to themes from the neighborhood’s history, and a map which coordinated real time GPS data and the view of the corridor. The first version of the DSSL involved a web app designed by our lab, called Storyliner, which serves up map-located media that requires users to opt-in by clicking on a map icon. The second version of the DSSL is in an app called Echoes, which uses the media from Storyliner but involves geofenced circles that automatically play the sonic content.

The design of this particular study called for the students to be paired up, with half of the pairs using Storyliner on the first part of the walk and the other half using Echoes. Both DSSLs were used while students were walking down a historic Black music and civil rights corridor in the South. We later coordinated the chest-mounted GoPros that students wore with some iPhone and iPad screen captures in order to examine how students connected their interaction with the media with the interaction within the pair. In the third study, during the summer of 2019, two  high school SLaM interns participated in a new iteration of the second study (see Figure 3) with an ear toward what they could glean from the design of the story walk in terms of strategies to make their own story walks. As a pair, they used a shared iPad and connecting t heir headphones through a splitter so that our record captured what they heard and what they said in perfect sync.

## Data Analysis

For all three studies, I collaborated with colleague researchers, advisors, lab members, and participants to conduct interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) of the rich audio-video records through our standing Interaction Analysis Lab. Following the theoretical basis of re sponse cries, we identified ‘hot spots’ in the reco rd, paying close attention to how listening was being made evidence through gesture and utterance (Hall & Stevens, 2015). Additionally, we analyzed the video record through the lens of a question: “what resources do students leveraging in making sense of contentious or counter-story aud io media?” In the case of both the 2019 studies, me mber checking was performed with participants to ensure that our analytical understanding of their experience was true to their memory and their own understandings.

## Findings

Kites Students across all three studies used utterance, embodiment, and other methods to perform their private listening experience of song or story to those within their response presence, constituting response cries. We noticed kites or response cries of two main kinds related to the stances of listening as observation or listening as searching. Listening To One set of response cries can be grouped as more mo ment-to-moment reactionary and demonstrated a broad attendance to the sound. This connects with the obs ervational stance of listening: listening to something. In the case of the high school students, the recording of ‘Blurred Lines’ sustained a tension between the students’ existing social positions and their presence with one another while the song was playing. Alignment against the singers of ‘Blurred Lines’ became visible as students responde d to hearing lyrics which they disagreed by shaking  their heads, raising eyebrows, and hugging their knees to their chests (see Figure 3).

Response cries often precipitated each other, with one student exclaiming “oh man!” and ma king eye contact with a peer who then said “yikes” to the same lyric. Masters’ students performed their priva te experience of listening into the response presen ce by walking in time to the beat of a song or consulting  with their partner, asking, “Did you hear that abo ut the interstate?” referring to an oral history clip.

Listening For The other category of response cries relates to the  searching stance of listening: listening for something. In the masters’ class, a student returned to an audio clip to listen for the interstate story that her partner mentioned. For the summer interns, the idea of listening for the future emerged in their utterances about what a historic club must have been like overlapping with ideas of what it could be like “with some Christmas lights” and a band with Tim.

The shift between these two modes of listening is obscured when it is not the explicit framing of the activity, but is evident in how learners are able to filter out audio information irrelevant to their pursuit (e.g. for the pair of masters’ students, content not related to the Interstate was ignored). In identifying these two modes and their accompany ing response cries, the categories fall short of accounting for the ways in which each person has a different approach to listening, which likely includes some predispositions for certain types of listening for in a first listen (or a listening to) stance. Someone with a musical background may naturally gravitate toward noticing the instrumentation of a song; someone with a background in politics may ICLS2022 Proceedings 715 © ISLS hear the story about the interstate through that pa rticular lens. This is where strings come in to rep resent the connections between individual listeners and their response cries. Strings Students drew on a range of resources to connect wh ere they current were (both literally in space and time and conceptually in their own positionality) to what they were listening to. The quality and texture of these strings is likely innumerable. Some brought in personal stories tied to themes in the music and others brought in  original sources related to the sonic content like court records and news articles. One masters’ student related a colorfully muraled overpass to a similar site she had visited in New Orleans despite the vast difference between the communities’ respective use of these two spaces. Another student drew on a story a classmate had told her about the neighborhood to make sense of the oral history clip about the neighborhood’s once-vibrant music scene. These connections create and sustain tension between the performance of listening and the personal history of the listener.

## Discussion and Implications

Being moved by sound Earlier, Ingold described the possibility being swe pt up by sound, noting the effort it requires to st ay in place when pulled by the sonic wind. Now, we can consider the difference between where we started when we unspooled our thread and launched our kite to where we stand as the wind whips it around. It is unlikely, though  not impossible, to stay still. Ingold decries standing still: you might as well not have heard the sound to begin with. Then a change in position must reflect being moved by the sound. Listening produces learning in this way. We are not where we found ourselves before we listened and responded. This movement and tension shapes us as learners, strengthening us and bringing us tonew places. Listening is both the means and product of constructing new information in conversation. Doing this work of  listening, however, might lead to entrenchment if we are only listening to that which already aligns with our personal experiences and understandings: we will see slack in the line and feel no windiness. It is essential to have a critical perspective of listening that honors the undeniable socio-and geo-politics of listening. Songs, counter stories, erased or undertold stories, public histories, personal narratives specifically of women (as ‘Blurred Lines ’ goes to show) and Black people in the U.S. (as se en in DSSLs), and other forms of historically undervalued information must be revisited with the new commitment. Listening together Embedded in these findings are three clear methodological, epistemological, and ontological implications for what it means to listen together. Firstly, that kites do fly (so to speak) is a phenomenal thing. Because of  response presence, listening inscribes itself in the record through interaction. Methodologically, this means t hat while noticing or proving listening as an activity is difficult, examining listening from an ensemble learning perspective makes visible the otherwise invisible personal expe rience of listening. It also provides the opportuni ty for understandings of sound to be expanded and challenged by co-listeners. For the high school students, it was pivotal that their utterances and accompanying takes on the song’s meaning were so contrasted (see Figure 4).

While it will take future work to better untangle the transitions between these modes and what prompts them, epistemologically, this open s the door for future investigation of how these mo des constitute ways of knowing within the medium of sound.

Lastly, the context of listening together means that students draw on many resources to make sense of a song or story’s meaning. It is a context where narrative  and counter-narrative, personal experience and evi dentiary documentation sit side-by-side as legitimate interpretations. This togetherness foregrounds the diverse ontologies, building intersubjectivity and co-constructing a me aning that otherwise—having listened alone, as is o ften our practice—would be less rich. Initial understandings of the song or story’s meaning were complicated and made rich by the context of listening with others. Toward the end of each record, students express an idea of what was learned through listening that is specific to the c ontext in which they listened, namely the meaning c onstructed between their own listening and the listening of their peers collectively. A sky full of kites I am reminded of a county-wide festival that was held near my hometown each year called Art and Wind. Beyond the regular festival offerings of snacks and folk art, the central feature of the day was collective kite flying. Nearly every person at the event had one or more kites: a purple octopus, a rainbow, a classic yellow quadrilateral. Flying a kite was its most fun when yours was one of thousands dotting the sky with fantastic color, all of them sweeping about like dancers in the air. The visual impact of seeing the kites together was the main attraction for Art and Wind Fest. With listening, I believe the sa me may be possible. Listening is a practice that st retches us outside of ourselves, to attune our hearing to the sound around us and make meaningful connections to what we hear. The main event could very well turn out to be  how powerful it is when we reflect on our experien ces of listening in a social context, when we listen together.
