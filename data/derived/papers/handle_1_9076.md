# Computational Thinking in Middle School Science

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We designed a lesson about Urban Heat Islands (UHI) that features Snap! programming challenges. The lesson is designed to enable students to leverage their computational and scientific understanding synergistically to further their learning in each area. The lesson also features fictitious automated college student mentors to enhance students’ sense of belonging as they engage in the programming challenges. We report on students’ level of success with three increasingly complex programming challenges as well as their level of understanding of how UHI occur after completing the challenges. We also provide brief case studies to illustrate how students used their scientific knowledge to debug their code and solve the challenges. Despite varied success with the challenges, most students were able to demonstrate an understanding of UHI. Many reported feeling interested and nervous when starting the programming challenges and shared they did not feel alone after engaging with the college student mentors.

Objective Computational thinking (CT) is vital for designing and analyzing models of environmental systems and is a skill increasingly valued by middle school teachers. Participation in the public discourse about environmental stewardship involves questioning and interpreting computational models. Enabling students in middle school science classrooms to engage with models of complex environmental dilemmas such as Urban Heat Islands prepares them to explore phenomena that impact their lives. By leveraging science knowledge, students candetect bugs in their code and make sense of model output. By developing programming knowledge to test their conjectures about remedies for urban heat islands, students can investigate questions that interest them, such as the impact of planting more trees. Further, middle school is a critical time to bolster student identity as a computational thinker and to foster a sense of belonging while engaging in computational activities. To assess the potential of Snap! programming for engaging students in computational thinking in science, we designed, and pilot tested a lesson about Urban Heat Islands (UHI). The lesson features three progr essively more difficult programming challenges that support students to explore the heat island phenomenon and generate a solution for a fictitious neighborhood (see Figure 1). Students can evaluate the trade-offs necessary to achieve personal goals such as “s pace for skating” or “hidden hangout places” by designing a Snap! model to reduce urban heat islands in their city. We explored students’ feelings while programming using  Snap! for the first time and investigated ways to suppor t their sense of belonging.

How do students feel when attempting computer programming and how do they feel after engaging with automated college student mentors? What is the  teacher's perspective on the value of integrating CT with science?

Rationale Integrating CT with science understanding The UHI lesson in this study aims to support studen ts to leverage their science understanding to suppo rt the development of their computational thinking skills, while also using computational thinking to deepen students’ understanding of the scientific phenomena. This app roach of viewing CT and science learning as mutuall y reinforcing builds on prior work showing that when students learn graphing in the context of science t hey gain proficiency in interpreting graphs and also increas ed ability to understand the related science concep ts (Wiese, Rafferty, & Linn, 2017; Boda et al, 2020). This pri or work suggests the potential of curricula and ass essments designed using the Knowledge Integration instructional framework (KI; Linn & Eylon, 2011) to support students to integrate and mutually develop knowledge in computational and scientific domains. As such, the UHI lesson featured in this study was designed according to the KI framework. A KI approach to curriculum design recognizes that learners hold varied ideas that reflect their lived experiences. It advocates for supporting students to develop coherent understanding by eliciting students’ prior ideas, providing opportunities to discover new ideas, using evidence to distinguish among existing ideas and new ideas, and guiding students to make connections among their ideas to form an explanation or argument (Linn & Eylon, 2011). To create learning experiences that support student s to deepen and integrate their CT and science understandings, we apply the KI framework and prior  research in computer science and science education  to design activities that: elicit students’ relevant ideas from prior science instru ction and relevant everyday experiences as starting points (Hammer & VanZee, 20 06); guide discovery of new science concepts and programming patterns by analyzing and modifying computational models (Basu et al, 2017; Sengupta et al, 2013; Wilensky et al, 2014); promote distinguishing among ideas by annotating peer responses and comparing solutions (Buffum et al, 2016; Lewis et al, 2012; McElhaney et al., 2015; Vitale et al, 2019); and facilitate links among CT and science ideas by guiding use of CT to inform sc ience and science to inform CT, such as for debuggi ng (Hutchins, Biswas et al, 2020). In the UHI lesson, students first use and then create Snap! programs where they explore scientific models to connect their ideas about CT and climate. Students deepen their science understanding by first testing the impact of a single variable and then identifying interactions between variables. Students use their science understanding to debug their code and check if their output matches their expectations based on their knowledge of the UHI phenomena. Students distinguis h ways to reduce the UHI effect by analyzing tradeo ffs between alternative solutions. Ultimately they connect their ideas in a proposed solution. Developing a sense of belonging Students often report feeling unwelcome in classes involving computer programming (Garcia, 2020; Gorson & O’Rourke, 2020) and benefit most from learning en vironments that support them to develop a sense of belonging (Jagers et al., 2019; Trujillo & Tanner, 2014). We endeavored to build a sense of belonging where students identify as part of their classroom community and eventually, the computational community by creating automated mentors. Students often feel like imposte rs or think they are the only ones who are confused, when really many students feel that way. In one study, a cross three university introductory programming cou rses, students tended tonegatively self-assess in progra mming situations, like having a syntax error or str uggling to debug a program, that are common in professional pr ogramming practice. These moments of negative selfassessment were correlated with low self-efficacy and inability to imagine success as a programmer (Go rson & O’Rourke, 2020). Thus, students might not have a cl ear understanding of the experiences to expect whil e programming and might benefit from assurance that their feelings are held by others. These patterns of negative self-assessment and frustration impede student purs uit of computer science beyond middle school and ca n be remedied by collaborative, engaging computational experiences that are personally meaningful (Kafai & Burke, 2013). We designed the automated mentors by asking Computer Science undergraduates who serve as Teaching ICLS2022 Proceedings 840 © ISLS Assistants in the non-majors introductory programmi ng course at a large research university to respond  to frequently expressed beliefs of middle school students. The automated mentors elicit and affirm the beliefs of the novice programmers and help them connect their experiences to the mentors’ similar experiences.

## Context and Participants

The UHI activities were implemented at a public K-12 school located in the United States. This study occurred in one teacher’s three 7th-grade science classes (N=72). Students had two 70-minute class periods to complete the lesson. The teacher expressed a strong commitment to environmental science and engaging students in exploring local environmental issues but had no prior program ming experience. Students had limited prior program ming experience as well; only 11 students mentioned othe r prior coding experience at home or at school. Dur ing the study, students worked in groups of two or three; e ach group had one laptop. Placing students in pairs  allowed them to ask each other questions, problem-solve, and take turns typing responses to questions. When students got stuck and raised their hands for help, the teacher encouraged them to approach their challenge like a scientist; ask good questions, be curious, and experiment. After o ffering this advice, the teacher walked away and ob served that, in most cases, students would turn to each other to discuss ideas and methods for solving the problem. Curriculum The lesson is designed in two parts, informed by th e KI Framework. The first focuses on developing stu dent understanding of UHI through programming challenges. The second part tasks students with applying thei r learning to do a city planning activity. The first part starts by introducing students to the phenomenon of Urban Heat Islands by eliciting their prior knowledge about climate change and the effects that result from a changing climate. Students then build on their lived experie nces by considering how different surfaces in a par k feel and where they would sit if they were having a picnic i n the park on a hot day. Next, students discover mo re ideas through a series of progressively more difficult block-based programming challenges that are designed to scaffold student understanding of and ability to apply progr amming control structures. In the first activity, s tudents use Snap! blocks to set the variables in a model that tests t he effect of varied amounts of grass coverage in th e neighborhood and the impact on temperature over a m onth. Before engaging with the next, more difficult programming challenge, students annotate a sample program using pre-authored labels to identify the function of each of the different blocks in the program. Next, students are tasked with filling in a few missing blocks in the conditional if-statements that control the model and then use their code to test varied amounts of tree coverage in the neighborhood. In the third programming challeng e, students fill in the gaps to create the majority  of the program by writing conditional if-statements to tes t varied amounts of reflective roofing (See Figure 1). After using code to program the model, students observed the changes in the temperature of a city using a temperature graph, which updated in real-time as the model ran.

In the second part of the lesson, students apply the coding and modeling skills they learned to design a city that could reduce the ambient temperature. Stu dents consider their favorite parts about their own neighborhood and how they would like to improve it. They then map out the city design, considering tradeoffs as they select surfaces that maintain functionality and prevent the city from becoming a UHI. In the fina l activity, students are asked about how they felt when they be gan writing computer programs with Snap ! in the lesson. Students were then introduced to fictitious automat ed college student mentors (Figure 2) who shared ho w they felt when they started programming. The mentors’ re sponses are based on ideas shared by the undergradu ate teaching assistants described above.

## Results and discussion

Science-CT learning During the first programming challenge, 60% of stud ent groups were fully successful, using the Snap ! coding blocks to test varied amounts of grass coverage ver sus asphalt in the model. All of these students gen erated the intended solution. Observations revealed how the ch allenge elicited students’ science thinking about e nergy transfer and transformation. As one student remarked, “Less grass has more heat energy and when there was more grass it was just solar radiation.” Students observ ed that when they programmed the model to have more  grass than asphalt, more of the solar radiation was absorbed and transformed into heat energy. In the second, more difficult programming challeng e, 21% of students were able to program the intended solution and another 11% were able to manipulate or hack the code in ways that allowed them to run the  model and test the appropriate conditions. The flexibilit y of Snap ! enabled the students to experience programming success even when they had not fully developed an u nderstanding of how to write if-statements or how t o efficiently combine a series of if-else-statements. A partial solution was achieved by 18% who experimented with moving the Snap! blocks and tested one condition.  The remaining 50% of students did not manipulate the blocks. By the third activity, 39% of the students designed the conditional and output statements to test reflective roofing, with 21% completing the activity as intend ed and 18% successfully hacking the code to create an unanticipated solution. Several student groups who had a partial coding solution during the second activity were able to hack the code and run trials with the model during the third activity. Their experience experimenting with the blocks in the second activity supported them to  keep experimenting until they found a solution in the third activity.

Following their programming investigations of the m odel, 71% of students were able to accurately describe the types of surfaces that contribute to a n area becoming a UHI. Of those students, 40% were able to connect their ideas about the surfaces to the energ y transformation mechanism. Thus, despite strugglin g to complete the programming challenges, students gained understanding of the science concepts underlying UHIs. Case studies Drawing on observation notes, video data, and stude nt written work of the three observed pairs, we des cribe instances that illustrate how these students leveraged their science understanding to support their computational practices or used their CT understanding to support their sense-making around science concepts.

Using observations of the model to troubleshoot programming challenges When starting the first programming challenge, one pair of students we observed worked together to fig ure out how to move Snap! blocks into their program to control the model. The y noticed that even when they dragged over the 25% block to set the “grass coverage” variable, they still couldn’t see any grass on the model. They were able to use their program to run the model, but their observations indicated that the model did not behave as they expected. With some prompting from the researcher-o bserver, they noticed they needed to click on the c ode to broadcast the value they had set for the variable t o the model. With a little bit of prompting, they l ooked more ICLS2022 Proceedings 843 © ISLS closely at the Snap! blocks and realized the block where they set the variable was connected to another block (line of code) that said “When I am clicked.” Students then clicked the block and saw that the amount of grass visible in the model changed. Once the appearance of the model reflected their expectations, they continued to use their science understanding to make sense of the graphical output from running the code: “When I set it at 25%, I saw more heat. When there was more grass there was less heat energy”. The students recognized that as they increased the amount of grass, less solar radiation was conve rted to heat energy. This helped them to interpret the graph generated by the model and make sense of why the temperature on the graph was lower when they set the amount of grass higher than 25%.

Connecting coding and science by making predictions After writing the code for the third programming challenge, one group of students closely examined the graph to determine whether their model output matched their predictions. After setting the variable to a high percentage of “reflective roofs”, they ran their model. One stude nt in the group remarked, “Oh wait it [the temperat ure] just went down a bunch!” The other student commented back, “Yeah I was thinking it’d be 105 or something” as the first student observed “It’s staying below 100 and above 80.” Students were able to use their observations of the connections between the model and the graph to deep en their understanding of the impact that more refl ective roofs would have on a city’s temperature. Students did not initially predict that reflective roofs wou ld lower a city’s temperature and were surprised by what they observed. They were confident in their interpretation of the graphical output from the model controlled by their  code. This supported them to revise their initial prediction and deepen their understanding of the role that reflective roofs could play in a solution for UHI. Applying science understanding and annotated code for debugging After successfully completing the second programming challenge, one group of students quickly got to work on the third challenge where they were to test the imp act of reflective roofs. They begandragging blocks  into the missing components of the if-statements without attending closely to what was missing in the statement s. They followed their approach from the previous challenge, making sure that everything in each if-statement matched. For example, they wrote lines of code that read, “I f 0%=0%, broadcast 0%, else if 25%=25%, broadcast 2 5%”. They began running trials at various amounts of reflective roofs. However, they noticed the graphical output for each trial looked the same and the amount of reflective roofs depicted wasn’t changing. Because of their science understanding, the students believed that varying t he amount of reflective roofs should result in diff erent temperature graphs. This led them to revisit their code and look for issues. After some time grappling  with the code, the researcher asked them whether they had any resources they could go back to help them think about what each part of the code does. The students clicked back to the part of the lesson where they had annotat ed sample code. Almost immediately one of the students exclai med, “Aha! I found out!” and clicked back to the co ding challenge. They replaced blocks in their if-statements so that it read, “If reflective_roofs=0%, broadcast 0%, else if reflective_roofs=25%, broadcast 25%”. They realized the if-statements were the part of the code that changed what was broadcast, depending on what value they set for the variable “reflective_roofs”. They tried running the model again and while looking at the model output e xclaimed: “There we go!” and “Yay! It works!” Stude nts’ science knowledge cued them that something was not working correctly in their code and the annotated c ode helped them to fix their code and figure out the purpose of the if-statements they were writing. Looking across all three cases, we see that student s drew on both their science and computational understanding to work through the programming chall enges. When they struggled with programming tasks o r interpreting their computational artifacts (i.e., graphs) students could draw on their developing knowledge of UHI. Similarly, as students succeeded with programming challenges and graph interpretation, they were able to advance their understanding of how different surfaces contr ibute to UHI. Working together with a peer and havi ng the occasional prompt from a researcher supported stude nts to think through their resources and leverage t heir understandings in helpful ways.

Promoting belonging All of the students were using Snap! for the first time. They reported feeling interested (64%), confident (40%), excited (16%), nervous (12%), and afraid (24%) when they began the first programming challenge. While twothirds of the students expressed interest in writing code, many were also experiencing complicating emotions, like feeling nervous or afraid. After engaging with the role models, several themes emerged in student responses. 44% of participants expressed that they felt like they were not alone in their experience or that they identified with the college student mentors. For example, one student shared, “I felt that I wasn't alone and there are people who are thinking just like me.” Another participant wrote t hat the mentors are “like us who were interested bu t also get confused on how”. Several students expressed which of the mentors they most felt alike to: “I think I’ m kinda ICLS2022 Proceedings 844 © ISLS like Amina.” Students also reiterated how they were feeling about their own programming (32%). Some students wrote statements like I feel “interested”, “I think the program was pretty fun” or that they felt “good and inspired to help people.” 20% shared that it was helpful to learn what older computer science students think. For example, one student wrote: “I feel way more informed and no w I know that everyone has a different perspective about there first day on computer science” and another wr ote, “I think that it is interesting because I lear n what older students are taught and I think I will have fun learning this.” The automated mentors helped students to continue to articulate their own feelings and to envision what it might look like to continue learning to program. Importantly, the mentors also provided reassurance to the students that their feelings were valid and not unusual when learning programing.

## Teacher Perspectives

Overall, the teacher valued the activity as an oppo rtunity for her students to develop new programming  skills while leveraging their science knowledge. The teacher observed, “At least for my students, I think they understood how these different variables [in the Snap ! model] can have an impact on the city because we’v e talked about [climate change]." She believed that their science understanding helped them to overcome frustration o r discouragement when coding challenges proved to be difficult. The teacher also reported high student engagement in persisting through the challenges. She remarked, “It was neat to see them being challenged in that way. Some of them found it really cool and interesting and sp ent a lot of time playing with the different tools. ” She also identified strategies she would use to support students through those difficulties the next time she taught the lesson. She noticed common student questions that indicated  the instruction supported students to seek connect ions between the activities in the lesson and their own lived experiences. She reported students asked questions such as:  What could we do to improve our neighborhood?;  How fast is the world's climate changing?; How is this information useful for [our state]?; How can we implement green solutions as a community?; What would happen if the world did not have trees or grass? Thus, the lesson prompted students to consider how they could use their science and CT skills to make changes in their own communities. The teacher built on these questions by asking students to consider where there might be UHIs within their own community. Because their school is in an area with high vegetation, the teacher helped students t o distinguish between their school setting and othe r parts of their community. She shared that students really de epened their connections when she got them thinking  about the area surrounding their local airport. When reflecting on what was most challenging for her students, the teacher reported that, in general, students struggled the most with the initial programming challenges. However, she also noted that when asked at the end of instruction abo ut what aspect of the lesson they enjoyed the most,  an overwhelming majority of students selected the codi ng and modeling section. Although challenging, once  they understood how to properly use the model, it became  their favorite part of the lesson. They liked the idea of designing a city that is, “literally cooler”, as one student stated.

## Conclusion and Significance

Overall, we found preliminary evidence that the des ign of the UHI lesson activities prompted students to use science and CT ideas synergistically to integrate e nvironmental science and computer programming. Taki ng a Knowledge Integration approach to the lesson design supported students to connect their science and CT ideas in ways that strengthened their understanding of both. We observed that students’ science knowledge and practices supported them to stay engaged and to sustain their  efforts while trying to solve the programming chal lenges. Even when struggling to find full solutions to the programming challenges, students were able to deepe n their understanding of UHI phenomenon.

Our findings also suggest the promise of using Snap! as a programming language to introduce CT into middle school science. With some guidance from thei r teacher, many students were able to engage in Sna p! programming with only two days of classroom instruc tion. Further, the flexibility of the Snap ! programming language and interface enabled multiple operational  solutions to the programming challenges. This supp orted students’ creativity and allowed students to experi ence success even if they did not come up with the intended code solution. This experience communicates to students that they have valuable computational ideas and reveals to students the creative nature of computer program ming that is not always visible during early progra mming experiences.

Two thirds of students expressed interest in progra mming when they began the coding challenges, although many also held complex feelings like nervo usness or fear. After engaging with the automated c ollege student mentors, students felt validated in holding multiple, sometimes conflicting feelings. The mentors helped them feel as though they were not alone in their ex perience by offering reassurance about their feelin gs. This mentoring practice could be valuable for more complex modeling as well. Taken together, our findings s uggest ICLS2022 Proceedings 845 © ISLS the value of using the KI-design approach to introd uce students to CT through relevant middle school s cience topics and using mentoring modeled on undergraduate  teaching assistants to support a sense of belongin g and community.
