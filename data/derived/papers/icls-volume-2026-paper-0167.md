# Europeans Activists are Learning by Comparing the Holocaust and WWII to the Gaza and Ukraine Wars

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores how comparisons between the past (the Holocaust and WWII) and the present (the ongoing wars in Gaza and Ukraine) mediate different types of learning. Thematic analysis of interviews with young activists in four European countries reveal s that these comparisons mediate understandings of past and present, moral orientations, and associations with familial, religious, national, and universal identities. By empirically demonstrating how these controversial analogies mediate cognitive, moral, and identity-related processes, this paper poses urgent questions for scholars of history education. Key Words: Holocaust education, history education, historical consciousness, Gaza war, Ukraine War.

Historical consciousness can be broadly defined as "a process by which people understand the links between past, present, and future" (Popa, 2022, p. 172). Understanding these links primarily involves negotiating the presence of the past; inquiring about the past; and/or building a sense of historical being. In terms of learning, historical consciousness can be tied to cognition, morality, and identity. Cognitively, engaging with the past can become a valuable resource for developing learners’ knowledge and thinking skills, which advance as they corroborate evidence, question reliability of historical sources, and generally speaking, read "like a historian" (Reisman, 2012; Wineburg, 2001). History is also inextricably linked to moral reasoning, because inte rpreting the past is central to the process of applying moral values to actionable dilemmas in the present (Rusen, 2004). Finally, Wertsch (1997, p. 5) reminds us that history can "serve as a kind of 'cultural tool' in 'mediated action' that creates and re-creates identity." Indeed, it would be impossible to determine the boundaries of any social category here-andnow, and associate (or disassociate) oneself with it, without historical consciousness. The Holocaust is one of the most prominent examples of a past event that has shaped collective memory, meaningful engagement with the past, and history education in Europe. The Holocaust was a systematic, state-sponsored campaign of persecution and genocide carried out by Nazi Germany and its local collaborators during WWII, ultimately resulting in the murder, across Europe, of six million Jews and other groups: Roma and Sinti, disabled people, LGBTQ+ individuals, and political dissidents. Since 1945, the Holocaust has profoundly shaped Europe's consciousness and identity, often serving as a moral reference point for understanding contemporary mass atrocities. The collective memory of the Holocaust and the imperative to prevent another global conflict like WWII have transformed Europe's darker past into a unifying force, embodied by the European Union and other international institutions.

In line with its centrality to European identity and discourse over the past half century, the Holocaust has had a massive impact on the historical consciousness of present-day Europeans. Due to limitations in space, we cannot review all of the ways in which this has manifested itself, and can only highlight several points. First, previous studies have demonstrated that for European learners, knowledge and understanding of the Holocaust can be linked to contemporary national identities and dominant narratives (Bjerg, 2011; Gross, 2014; Welzer 2011). Second, educators and curriculum designers have long recognized that Holocaust education has many goals tied to knowledge, ethics, and identity (Totten & Feinberg, 2016). And finally, the Holocaust is perceived by many Europeans as a formative event in their region (Suchman). In this study, we seek to make an incremental contribution to what is already known of the ways in which comparing the Holocaust and WWII to the present can mediate acts of historical consciousness, when viewed through European eyes. This is accomplished by investigating the memory of these events in tandem with reflections on the current wars in Gaza and Ukraine. In light of all of this, we posed three main research questions:

(1) What types of understandings of the past, present, and the connection between them are mediated by the

comparison between past (WWII and the Holocaust) and present (Gaza and Ukraine wars)?

(2) What types of moral orientation are mediated by the comparison between past and present?

(3) What types of identity construction, reflected in any associations with groups and social categories, are

mediated by the comparison between past and present?

This paper is part of a larger study called Holocaust Memory as a Cultural Code that is currently underway in nine European countries: Ukraine, Romania, Poland, Germany, Spain, The United Kingdom, France, Hungary, and The Netherlands. Led by an international team, including scholars from Israel and from Ukraine, this project is employing a variety of quantitative and qualitative methods to investigate how the Holocaust is invoked in various political contexts in different locations, and to identify the role s that Holocaust memory plays in socio-political discourses across Europe.

As part of the larger study, our team is in the process of conducting 60 interviews with university students who are political activists, and who represent a broad spectrum of political views, in four countries: Romania, France, The Netherlands, and Ukrai ne (15 interviews per country). We defined "political activist" broadly to include anyone officially associated with an organization with a clear social mission, including political parties, environmental organizations, gender-related activism, and more. Interviews are being conducted by local researchers in each country, transcribed, and uploaded to a shared repository. The interviews are semi-structured, lasting 60-90 minutes, with the interview protocol touching on (1) respondents' personal backgrounds, (2) their activism, (3) their views on history, specifically WWII and the Holocaust, and (4) their views on contemporary global events including the wars in Gaza and Ukraine.

At the time of submission a total of 51 interviews were conducted and 15 were coded using the Dedoose software. Hence, the findings presented herein are preliminary and will likely develop further as more interviews are analyzed. We coded the available interviews thematically, meaning that we followed the six steps outlined by Braun and Clarke (2012): fami liarizing ourselves with the data to seek out any utterances that drew a direct comparison between the Holocaust (past) and the Gaza and Ukraine wars (pre sent), generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and writing this paper. What types of understandings of the past, present, and the connection between them are mediated by the comparison between past and present? The comparison between past and present was instrumental in furthering respondents' understanding of the past and the present. In some cases, respondents described how watching events in Gaza or Ukraine unfold helps them develop a better grasp of what transpired during WWII: I found [the war against] Gaza very interesting because I always [hesitates] I could never imagine how something like that [hesitates] how something like that could happen. That such a large part of the population would want to go along with it. But the interesting thing about Gaza is that you kind of see how the demonization of some people can be so intense that you can get a large part of the population to go along with "yes, kill them all."  (respondent NL5) In other cases, the relationship between past and present was inverted so that the past was invoked as a lens to construct a more sophisticated view of the present: "Yes, when people criticise the pro-Palestinian protest, for example, and say that it is anti-Semitic. That comes from WWII, from the fear that hatred of Jews will resurface. I can see that." (NL1) Our analysis identified three ways in which respondents' understanding of the present was enhanced by their knowledge of the past: justification and meta-justification, sensitivity, and historical rhyming (Zerubavel, 1995). Justifications included people who invoked the past to justify their beliefs about the present, while Meta-justifications explained how the past is invoked by others to justify opinions or policies that are observable in current events: 1318 It seems to me that the Holocaust is used very cynically, by a lot of people who are not very connected to it, in the sense that they use it cynically for their political ambitions, for their interests. For example, comparisons with the Nazis, comparisons with the Holocaust, and accusations of genocide are thrown around very easily, because the Holocaust is the most significant point of alluding to atrocity. (RO5) Sensitivity is an awareness in the present of unforeseen dangers that may be lurking in the future, given what has happened already in the past: "I mean, you see it happening in front of you, and everything you learned in high school, in history, should set off some alarms in your head. And [you] see exactly the patterns you learned there." (RO4) Presumably, without knowledge of the past, these dangers would be impossible to foresee. And finally, Historical rhyming is the understanding that history repeats itself in some way or another: "And of course I see WWII in [the discourse surrounding the Gaza war], now also with all the things about anti-Semitism and that kind of thing." (NL1) Some respondents identified a direct line connecting past and present: "it's almost happening again and we're all standing here saying 'Never again' while we are complicit, again, in Gaza" (RO5), while others spoke of discontinuity, as knowledge of the past prevents it from recurring: I think that we are articulating [the history of Holocaust] better. But I think it's not enough. I still think it's not enough. But again, given the context of today, in terms of how we are experiencing all these tragedies [in Ukraine] it seems to be clear why this [i.e., genocide] is not happening now, why it is not happening anymore. (UA2) Finally, for other respondents, the comparison between past and present led them to develop a nuanced view. For example, one respondent compared resistance to the Vichy government in the 1940s to a lack of resistance to Hamas and its supporters, which led him to raise a troubling question that he felt unable to answer: There was Vichy France and the collaborationists. But there were also a lot of people who resisted, resistance fighters who opposed it. And that's also what I was telling you earlier, that I don't understand why there aren't people today who don't feel concerned, for example, by this conflict… Nor were there any resistance fighters in Gaza who stood up against Hamas and tried to save hostages. (FR2) What types of moral orientation are mediated by the comparison between past and present?

We distinguished between three types of value orientations that were described by respondents, or that were implicitly implied: orienting themselves, orientations towards the Other, and moral ambiguities. Orienting oneself may mean taking responsibility for atrocities that are currently ongoing: "it's almost happening again and we're all standing here saying 'Never again' while we are complicit, again, in Gaza." (RO5) In other cases, it can mean being vigilant to ensure that atrocities such as the Holocaust  never recur: "For myself, it is being vigilant so that history does not repeat itself... I think that is why that vigilance is very much a part of me." (NL1) Vigilance can be collective, and translate into a society-wide effort, to "try to create your wor ld and your laws around the idea of how to prevent this from happening again." (RO4) Orientations towards the other  comprised two themes that might be viewed as complementary: identifying with the plight of the Other, and calling out Others for their transgressions. Comparing the Holocaust and World War II to the wars in Gaza and Ukraine led some respondents to identify with minorities such as Muslims and Jews, and recognize their suffering, fears, and anxieties. In other cases, respondents called out the g roups they saw as oppressors —Israel, Hamas and its supporters, and modernday neo-Nazis. Finally, moral ambiguity meant complex orientations that simultaneously identified with multiple and contradictory claims: And after World War Two, yeah, who had the position of power within the world? Well, the United States and then Europe. Then, yeah, as compensation for the pain we [Europeans] caused, and the extermination of 6 million people, the country [Israel] was then  given. Which is also more than justified. I don't deny it either. Israel has a right to exist. And it is good that Israel is there. Only yes, I do think 40,000 dead [in Gaza] is a lot for self-defence. So, I am critical of that. And when I look at Hamas, I also think that [hesitates] it's one big [hesitates], I don't like Hamas, it's a terrorist organisation. But I do have a nuanced view on that, I always think. Because it's about people and 40,000 people have already died since October 7th. And there have been thousands of deaths before that too. Respondents associated themselves with four types of social categories amid comparisons between past and present: family, religion, nationality, and humanity. One Ukrainian respondent recounted her family's past, which she feels she is reliving in the curr ent historical moment. For her, the past is repeating itself in a very personal sense: I looked at those photos [of my great-grand-parents] and I just, for me, it's such a, you know, vivid memory, which is something that haunts me. I live on, but I remember it and I understand, of course, it's very hard to think about it, that it's also part  of the family history, and it's also hard to think about the fact that, well, when I was in the 11th grade, we studied all this [Second World War history], and the case I mentioned when I was little, I was just leaving my grandmother's house, looking at the sky, and thinking that "Oh, my God, how people were going through that?" I mean, I think we all understand everything they were going through, but it's just such a shame that even now it's happening again. (UA1) According to another respondent, memory of the past can become entangled with religious identities in the present moment: "But I also see —and I try to pay more attention to this —[Dutch] Jews who stand up for Palestinians." (NL1) In other words, given their history of persecution during the Holocaust, these people's Jewish identity should be foregrounded when interpreting their views of the Gaza war in the present. Unsurprisingly, the national lens was most commonly invoked. For instance, the comparison between past and present led one respondent to highlight Israel's identity as a Jewish State: "I also see the Israeli state using that—not the guilt, but the feeling —to oppress [Gazans]. So I see that as an almost direct consequence of Israel itself also being a consequence of WWII." (NL1) In contrast, the same comparison led another respondent, along with a group of friends, to foreground their Dutch identity: "So, when we visited Auschwitz, we did notice... it did something to us, seeing those shoes and seeing those people there. And being in the Dutch barracks and seeing the pictures of the Dutch Jews. That did have an impact. And we also talked about [the Israeli-Palestinian conflict]" (NL2). Finally, while none of them said it explicitly, comparing the current wars to WWII and the Holocaust led several respondents to imply that all humanity shares a collective responsibility to prevent contemporary or future genocides, for instance in the following statements: ● "it's almost happening again and we're all standing here saying ‘Never again’ while we are complicit, again, in Gaza." (RO5) ● "any society, especially since we see this now, no matter how civilized we think we are, can fall into that darkness and is capable of those atrocities." (RO4) ● "Yes, I do feel that they are connected. When I think about what I said earlier, that you help people to fight injustice, I immediately think about what happened, or didn't happen, during WWII. So yes, they are connected." (NL1) Comparisons between the Holocaust and contemporary events are a controversial matter among historians and scholars of memory studies. Some have claimed that “irresponsible comparisons candistort understanding of both contemporary phenomena and of the Holocaust” (International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance, 2021), while others have argued that such practices are unavoidable and that any attempt to oppose Holocaust comparisons would be futile and absurd (Rothberg, 2009). Another ongoing debate, about the purpose of history education, has been between the scholars who emphasize individual knowledge and thinking skills, and those who focus primarily on national identity (Seixas, 2017).

The preliminary findings presented in this paper demonstrate how complex—and urgent —both of these debates are. Empirically, and whether we like it or not, it is apparently the case that young European activists are drawing on the comparison between WWII and the Holocaust and the current wars in Gaza and Ukraine. Additionally, these comparisons can simultaneously mediate cognitive, moral, and identity-related learning. As learning scientists who regularly collaborate with practitioners and policymakers, we must think carefully about how to respond to this reality: Should Holocaust comparisons be encouraged or discouraged? Should the primary goal of such comparisons be cognitive, moral, identity-focused, or some kind of fusion between the three?
