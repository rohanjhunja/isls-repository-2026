# “Pick a Partner!”: A Qualitative Study on University Students’ Motives to Choose a Specific Partner for Collaboration

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Students at university are often required to collaborate on tasks. However, collaboration processes are not always efficient and productive. Many problems can be attributed to group composition. Students are often free to choose a partner, but little is known about the underlying factors contributing to their decision-making process. In the current study, 30 students filled in questionnaires and were interviewed on their group formation process.

## Problem statement

The use of computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) has been extensively  documented in the last decades (Johnson et al., 2007). University students are frequently assigned col laborative tasks, such as collaborative writing tasks, through which they learn about a topic, gain writing skills, and develop collaboration skills. Prior research indicates that CSCL-processes are not free  of issues. Common issues are interpersonal conflicts, uncritical group discussions that lead to surface level processing of the learning material, and uneven participation (Johnson et al., 2007; Le et al., 2018). These issues can be attributed to  a variety of CSCL aspects, such as group composition. Professors are often insufficiently aware of their students’ strengths and weaknesses, making it difficult to form groups based on this information. This, in combination with the observation that students prefer to choose their partners, results in students often being allowed to pick their own partners. Earlier research discussed the impact of group composition on the subsequent c ollaborative writing process and its effects on learning. However, this research centralized di fferences between homogeneous and heterogeneous groups, (e.g., Schuitema et al., 2019), or group size and gender (e.g., Monson, 2019). Little is documented on students’ motivations when they are free to choose their partn er. Therefore, we put forward the following research question: “Which factors influence university students’ decisions to select a specific group partner for a collaborative writing task?” We hypothesize that three main factors impact their decision: familiarity, prio r experience, and status. The first, familiarity, refers to the degree to which students know eac h other (Janssen et al., 2009), with total strangers on one end of the spectrum, and friendship on the other. Group member s who are familiar with each other are more prone to engage in critical discussions and have more posit ive perceptions of their collaboration (Janssen et al., 2009). Friends who collaborate on a task risk to engage more in off-task behavior (Le et al., 2018). The second factor, prior experience, refers to students’ prior experience regarding collaboration and working on similar tasks (Zambrano et al., 2019). Students may have developed certain  effective or ineffective strategies as a result of prior experiences. They may transfer these to similar collaborative tasks (Yang et al., 2012; Zambrano et al., 2019). It is plausible that students who have previously succes sfully collaborated would like to repeat that experience and therefore select the same partner (Yang et al., 2012). The third factor, status, reflects how students perceive their group members. Cohen (1994) distinguishes between academic and peer status. Academic status is defined as students’ perceived cognitive abilities, i.e., how well they can perform a specific task according to their peers. This statu s thus reflects the perception of fellow students and does not necessarily correspond to the actual competencies. Pee r status concerns perceived popularity, or the degree to which a fellow student wants to collaborate with a particular student. Peer status impacts interaction and participation in group processes. For example, low-status  students tend to show lower levels of participation then high-status students. High-status students’ ideas are more likely to gain approval than the ideas of low-status students, even if they are not necessarily better (Cohen, 1994; Le et al., 2018). Plausibly, students carry other motivations for picking a partner for collaborative writing. However, this is a black box in the literature and is therefore the focus of the current research.

## Methods

36 students in the first year of their master’s degree completed a collaborative task. More in particular, they wrote a source-based text on the same topic over the course of nine weeks withi n the context of a course at a Flemish university. Students were instructed to write this task in dyads and to choose their own partner. 30 of these students voluntarily participated in an interview and 33 filled out a questionnaire two weeks after the task instruction. The main questions were: “How did you decide to partner up with each other?” and “If you can freely choose a partner, which aspects are important in your decision ?”. Further questions focused more in depth on their perceived importance of the degree of familiarity regarding their partner, the perceived importance of prior experience with a specific partner, and the benefits and drawbacks of collaborating with student s with whom they have personal or work history. The responses are currently thematically analyzed. In the questionnaire, students had to indicate the extent to which they thought each of their fellow students was capable of writing a good paper, in order to estimate the aca demic status of all students. Next, students were asked to mark the extent to which they would like to write a paper with each of the other students, to estimate peer status. Students indicated this on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much). Differences in how group members estimated each other were explored.

## Results & discussion

At the moment, all interviews are conducted and the analysis phase is ongoing. So far, the following findings can be reported. In the questionnaire, the majority of the students indicated that they were friends or at least more than acquaintances with their partner. Most students appeared to have simply chosen a friend with whom to collaborate, implying that a high degree of familiarity was a deciding factor. During the in terviews, many students reported that knowing their partner was critical. The reason for this, however, was that they preferred to know beforehand the strengths, weaknesses, and strategies for completing a writing task. This is reflected in the questionnaire data too, as many students perceived their partner as a good or very good writer. For some students, being friends was less important than knowing for sure that the task would be successfully finished. Many students stated that while prior collaboration experience with their partner was preferred, it was not required. Even if they had to write with a complete stranger, knowing that a partner would contribute equally to the task and was a good writer would be enough. Some students said that they avoided to work with a friend, because they knew this friend was great fun, but was not good at writing or following through on agreements. Another student never collaborated with friends out of fear of conflicts within the group spilling over into their personal lives. These preliminary findings suggest that knowing a student as a hard worker is far  more important than knowing a student as a friend to these students. So, prior experience and academic status are more important than familiarity when selecting a partner. Results of a more in-depth analysis will be reported at ISLS 2023.
