# Knowledge in New Pieces (KiNP): Intuitive Experiences of Social Media Viral Growth as Building Blocks for Understanding Advanced Mathematics and Network Theory

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Youth today navigate complex systems—technological, natural, and social—that profoundly shape their cognitive development. These interconnected environments foster intuitive knowledge, such as understanding exponential growth from everyday interactions with social media and “viral” content. The Knowledge-in-Pieces framework highlights these fragmented but functional knowledge units (p-prims) and their evolution into organized concepts. Through a series of case studies involving youth in semi-clinical, task-based interviews, this paper extends KiP to modern contexts, exploring how youth interactions with technology impact their understanding of exponential mathematics and network theory.

## Introduction

Youth are increasingly immersed in complex systems,  including technological, natural, and social envir onments that shape their daily lives and cognitive developm ent. These systems, ranging from networked internet-enabled devices to climate ecosystems and g lobal supply chains, create larger and more interco nnected environments. Modern technologies such as smartphones and other d evices integrate deeply into youths’ physical and s ocial activities, making some new understandings essential to their lives and social interaction, such as the mechanisms of information dissemination, the phenomenon of “things going viral,” and the architecture of networks. Similarly, societal conversations about resource conservation and global supply chains are now prominent in coll ective discussions, influencing how youth perceive and nav igate their worlds. The interplay of these experien ces generates intuitive knowledge that could serve as a foundation for formal learning. Rooted in everyday activities, such knowledge may encompass  an implicit understanding of concepts traditionally  considered advanced, such as exponential growth, gleaned from experiences wit h viral content, or insights into energy cycles and  resource conservation from discussions about recycling and c omposting. This type of knowledge challenges the assumptions underlying current educational standard s and curricula, suggesting that they may need upda ting to reflect and build on these new “pieces of knowledge ” inherent in contemporary youth experiences. To ad dress this issue, this study explores the intuitive knowledg e youth acquire outside formal schooling and its po tential to support formal disciplinary learning.

## Theoretical background

The theoretical foundation of this study is grounde d in the Knowledge-in-Pieces (KiP) framework developed by diSessa (1988, 1993). This framework provides a nua nced perspective on intuitive knowledge, highlighti ng its diversity and contextual nature. Central to KiP are phenomenological primitives (p-prims)—small, intuitive units of knowledge that arise from everyday experiences. Examples of p-prims include “more effort produces greater results” or “forces act in balance.” While these intuitive concepts can sometimes be unproductive in formal settings, they endure because they are often logica l within their original contexts. Moreover, KiP rej ects the idea that misconceptions can or should simply be elimina ted. Instead, it acknowledges that learners frequen tly use pprims unconsciously, and that these knowledge pieces can remain useful in specific situations as formal understanding progresses. KiP also illustrates how these pieces evolve into coordination classes—more organized and coherent conceptual systems —as learners’ understanding advances. For instance, novices might focus on irrelevant features when making inferences, while e xperts learn to consistently attend to conceptually  significant features.

Since its inception, KiP has transformed the study of conceptual change, particularly in science education. Traditional theories compared learners’ cognitive development to the historical progression  of scientific ideas (Wiser & Carey, 1983) or depicted it as a series of coherent, theory-like stages (McCloskey, 1983). In contrast, KiP suggests learners possess a fragmented but functional array of intuitive knowledge pieces drawn from everyday experiences. These pieces persist and  adapt within specific contexts, even as learners a cquire formal disciplinary knowledge. Subsequent research has extended KiP intonew domains, including mathematics (Wagner, 2006) and social justice (Philip, 2011). More recently, KiP has been integrated with interaction analysis to challenge deficit-centered perceptions of learners, emphasizing their competence and resourcefulness (diSessa et al., 2016). This combination provides a framework for understanding how learners draw on intuitive knowledge to navigate new conceptual domains.

However, modern youth inhabit techno-social environments that differ significantly from those of previous generations. Drawing on contemporary persp ectives in cognitive science (e.g., Hutchins, 2000;  Varela et al., 1991), researchers contend that the intuitive knowledge young people develop today may differ qualitatively from that studied 30 years ago. These differences are reflected in the new framework of Knowledge in New Pieces (KiNP) (Blikstein & Rosenbaum, 2023; Blikstein et al., 202 4), which aims to identify and validate the novel intuitive knowledge generated by youth in current contexts. Building on this theoretical lineage, the present study examines how youth’s interactions with complex tech nological and social systems contribute to their co nceptual development. Examples include children’s thoughts a bout and interactions with technology (Turkle, 1984), their understanding of the internet (Brodsky et al., 2021), and their trust in digital sources such as search engine results (Tong et al., 2022). Whi le previous research has documented youth’s technol ogy practices, the focus here is distinct: it investigates how intuitive knowledge from these interactions can serve as a springboard for disciplinary learning.

## Methods

We conducted interviews with youth aged 8 to 14 yea rs, collecting and analyzing video data and transcr ipts. We used convenience and snowball sampling, and all par ticipants resided within the United States. Intervi ews lasted approximately 45 minutes, were conducted via Zoom, and followed a semi-structured interview protocol developed from three rounds of pilot interviews previously conducted with youth (Blikstein & Rosenbaum, 2023; Blikstein et al., 2024). The youth participants sel ected the pseudonyms by wh ich they wished to be referred. Automatic transcriptions were generated for each interview and verified by the third author. The authors reviewed and coded the resulting transcripts to identify poi nts of interest across the interviews. We selected four foc us students who were representative of the dataset, in cluding one with significantly less experience usin g smartphones or social media. This choice aimed to h ighlight how experience with social media influence d the youth’s understanding of the tasks during  the interviews. ## Data and discussion

First question: How long does it take to go viral?

Sam does not have an Instagram account or any other  social media, but he has seen his mom’s Instagram and Facebook accounts and has experience watching YouTube content from major creators. When asked how long it would take for a post on a 100,000-follower account to reach 100 views, he said it wou ld take about 5 minutes. When asked about reaching 1 million views, he was unsure at first but eventually estimated it would take around 3 hours: “...just based off of how…many views my guess is uh  there’s kind of just like a curve…how quickly people get views. It’s like maybe within the first three hours to the uh…First three hours maybe would be like 10% of the views sent from three hours to three days, that's probably going to be like 80% and then after that, it's like 10%.” Kennedy (12 years old) explained why she thinks an influencer would take 20 minutes and 30 seconds to get 100 views, an hour to get 1,000 views, and “three hours closer to four hours though” to get 1 million views. She said, “I know that the math doesn’t match up pe rfectly, but it just feels right.” Kennedy demonstr ates that despite not having learned about this type of math yet, she possesses an intuitive understanding of ho w exponential, viral growth operates.

Miffy (14 years old) explains why she believes that an influencer would take around five minutes to get 100 views, 10 minutes to get 1,000 views, and when it comes to reaching 1 million views, she indicated  that it would depend on the topic and whether it is trendy,  adding that some topics, “probably get it in like a day, while others might take a few months or maybe even years. ” However, when considering one of her friend’s pos ts, Miffy thinks it would take them “a day or two” to r each 100 views and “five days to a week” to get 1,0 00 views. She mentioned that she did not think it would ever reach 1 million views, using the idea of shifting trends to explain how a post might experience rapid growth followed by stagnation, the point at which the curve approaches a limit: “I feel like it might not actually be able to reach  a million views because I mentioned before that things get like less trendy, so they start slowing down, and if they do slow down, it’s much harder to like reach that one million.” Sam’s estimates of post-growth (e.g., 100 views in 5 minutes, 1 million vie ws in 3 hours) reflect his understanding of exponential growth, influenced by his exposure to educational YouTube content. He des cribes viewership as following a curve, with rapid growth in the initial hours, followed by slower growth ove r the subsequent days. Sam’s explanation also incorporates network effects, reasoning that a follower’s larger network could amplify the reach of a post. Kennedy’s timeline for virality (e.g., 100 views in 20 minutes and 30 seconds, 1 million views in 3 –4 hours) also demonstrates an intuitive grasp of ex ponential growth. She explicitly acknowledges that her estimates were not mathematically precise but “felt right,” indicating a reliance on intuition. Kennedy does not expressly address broader network dynamics, instead focusing on follower-based viewership. Miffy introduces a new facet by linking the post’s popularity to trending topics and audience behavior s. She estimates that viral posts could gain rapid views (e.g., 100 views in 5 minutes) but note s that trends slow over time, leading to stagnation. Her explanation emphasizes the temporal nature of trends, aligning with the idea of a plateauing curve, where initial rapid growth slows as a topic beco mes less trendy. While all participants demonstrate an understanding of exponential growth,  Sam’s reasoning incorporates network amplification  (e.g., followers’ followers), whereas Kennedy and Miffy fo cus on individual viewership and trending topics. M iffy’s insight into trend saturation is also notable, while Kennedy relies on an intuitive, less detailed understanding. ## Conclusion

The interviews reveal a spectrum of reasoning among  youth regarding social media virality. They integrate network dynamics, exponential growth, individual be havior, trends, temporal decay, and content-driven factors. Participants demonstrated an emerging understanding  of exponential growth, evident in their ability to  estimate how posts gain views over time, with many emphasizi ng the importance of trends in shaping a post’s vir ality. They described how trending topics experience rapid growth before stagnating as interest wanes, highlighting the temporal nature of social media popularity. This reasoning introduced the idea of diminishing returns, suggesting an intuitive awareness of non-linear growth. Some participants also identified th e role of networks in amplifying reach, noting how content shared by a follower with  a larger audience could significantly increase vie ws. This reflects an understanding of interconnected systems, where secondary networks contribute to the dissem ination of information beyond the initial audience. Another factor that emerged from the interviews was the frequency of audience engagement and the quality of a post’s thu mbnail, which demonstrate an awareness of how conte nt design influences growth. Finally, participants identified the influence of “small accounts” as a barrier to growth, with limited initial reach and slower engagement.

These themes illustrate the sophisticated yet intui tive ways in which youth conceptualize social media dynamics and highlight the potential for leveraging  intuitive knowledge to deepen their understanding of formal concepts in mathematics and network theory. Even mo re importantly, these findings underscore how youth s’ experiences with social media and virality provided  them with new knowledge pieces that served as buil ding blocks for sophisticated predictions and explanations about virality and network effects, well before encountering such topics in formal education. If it is reasonabl e to assume that the same questions would have elic ited wildly different answers 20 years ago, it follows that today’s youth must be capable of understanding more sophisticated mathematical concepts than previously assumed, and that our expectations for what youth can learn should not be fossilized but continually updated to honor their brilliance.
