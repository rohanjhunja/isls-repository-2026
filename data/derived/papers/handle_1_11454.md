# Developing Scientific Literacy and Action-Oriented Skills through the “I and My Waste” Program: Empowering Youth as Changemakers in Sustainable Waste Management

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This practice paper presents the design and implementation of the “I and My Waste” program in Raigarh, India. The program empowers students to develop scientific literacy and socio-scientific reasoning through hands-on engagement with waste management. Using place-based pedagogy, fosters critical thinking, ethical decision-making, and collaboration. Students evolve from passive learners to active changemakers. The paper examines how lived experiences support socio-scientific inquiry and contribute to sustainable practices through action-oriented science education.

## Introduction

Global environmental challenges underscore the need for educational approaches that empower youth to engage actively and responsibly with complex, real-world issues. The "I and My Waste" program, situated in Raigarh, India, provides a model for how community-centered science learning can enhance students ’scientific literacy and equip them with the reasoning and action-oriented skills necessary to address pressing socio-environmental challenges. Through hands-on engagement in waste management practices, students develop critical thinking, problem-solving, and community collaboration skills, empowering them to act as changemakers. This paper is positioned as a practice paper and focuses on sharing insights from the design and implementation of a place-based science education initiative.

In this paper, scientific literacy refers to students' ability to apply scientific knowledge to real-world problems. Socio-scientific inquiry involves reasoning through complex, socially embedded scientific issues. The agency is the student’s capacity to take intentional action in their communities. These constructs are interconnected: socioscientific inquiry fosters scientific literacy and creates opportunities for students to exercise agency.

## Theoretical framework

The “I and My Waste” program is grounded in place-based learning and socio-scientific issues (SSI) pedagogy. Place-based education connects students with local issues to deepen understanding and engagement (Barnett et al., 2021; Barton, 1998). SSI approaches emphasize learning through ethical and scientific reasoning on social issues like plastic waste (Alcaraz-Dominguez & Barajas, 2021). Inspired by Barton’s (1998) framework on pedagogy and representation, we explore two questions:

1. How are socio-scientific issues like waste management represented to students in ways that foster critical

engagement?

2. How does participation in the program shape students’ understanding of their role as community

contributors and changemakers?

Objectives The primary objectives of the “I and My Waste” program were:

• To foster scientific literacy and socio-scientific reasoning by engaging students in meaningful waste

management practices.

• To empower students to act as change-makers, encouraging sustainable solutions to local

environmental challenges and strengthening community connections (van Eijck & Roth, 2010).

• To examine what kind of waste an individual or a school is generating.

• To investigate the journey of their waste and the possible impact of their waste on the nearby

environment.

• To design a possible solution for their school waste challenge.

Program design and implementation The first author started the ‘I and My Waste’ program 3 years ago. She completed a master’s in education from a social science institute in Mumbai, and returned to her hometown Raigarh to work with students aged 14–21. A government fellowship supported the program financially. The second author, a faculty member at the same institute, contributed as advisor.

The program took place in Raigarh, a culturally rich but industrially impacted city in Chhattisgarh, India. The Raigarh Municipal Corporation supported its execution.

Key activities included pre-program online meetings with school leaders to discuss expectations and logistics; formation of student teams in each school through consultation; diagnostic student questionnaires to gauge interest and prior knowledge; two-day workshops exploring definitions and personal experiences of waste; community waste mapping and project design based on local observations; and finally, public recognition of students' work through exhibitions and interaction with civic authorities. Students explored questions such as “What is waste?”, “Is a plastic bottle waste if reused?”, and conducted handson audits by visiting different areas within their schools like classrooms, canteens, and libraries to categorize and quantify types of waste.

Implementation cycles Cycle 1 in November 2021 included 75 students from one school aged 13–15 years. Cycle 2 in November 2022 involved 152 students from seven schools, including both government and private institutions. Cycle 3 in March 2024 focused on 19 college students aged 17–21. The fourth cycle is currently underway.

## Findings: Illustrative vignettes

Instead of traditional thematic analysis, we present selected moments that illustrate how students engaged with socio-scientific issues, developed agency, and enacted their learning in community settings. Each vignette is followed by reflective commentary on its relevance to design and practice. Understanding the socio-scientific nature of plastic pollution Participating students were aware of the issue of plastic pollution, it was part of their daily experiences. However, through the program, their perceptions of waste evolved from seeing it as a problem to understanding it as a complex issue with social, environmental, and health implications. Understanding environmental issues in a socioscientific framework helps students develop a holistic view, seeing science as a tool for understanding and improving community life (Gruenewald & Smith, 2008). Through mapping exercises and community engagement, students developed a nuanced understanding of waste production and its impacts, reflecting a foundational step in building scientific literacy (Alcaraz-Dominguez & Barajas, 2021). Agency as changemakers Participation in the program encouraged students to take on roles as environmental advocates within their community, fostering a sense of responsibility and empowerment. Activities such as distributing reusable bags and educating community members demonstrated that students could contribute to solutions. This shift—from passive learners to active agents of change—highlights the program’s success in fostering socio-scientific reasoning and action-oriented learning. Students recognized their capacity to make tangible changes within their community, reinforcing their roles as informed citizens and proactive contributors (Simmons, 2008). The street play vividly illustrates how students used performative satire to question cultural norms and raise public awareness. Their embodied protest critiques everyday practices and invokes collective accountability, with the voice of “Mother Earth” (line 6) urging a moral and emotional call to action. These vignettes were chosen not for their representativeness but because they demonstrate the potential of practice-based learning to foster socio-scientific reasoning and agency. Practitioner reflections and design principles The key takeaways from the program suggest that anchoring learning in lived experiences significantly increased relevance and motivation for students. By physically moving to waste-related sites—such as classrooms, school canteens, or nearby ponds—students developed a deeper understanding of environmental challenges. These site visits enabled students to engage in embodied, authentic inquiry. Real-world engagement allowed students to refine their initial questions and to co-design and test context-specific solutions. Over time, their roles shifted from problem identifiers to solution designers, community researchers, and even environmental performers—especially when they organized street plays to raise public awareness. Practitioner reflection on role The practitioner’s role was to support student-led inquiry even in unfamiliar problem spaces. Initial goals like “reducing plastic waste” were reframed to suit authentic local contexts, e.g., investigating e-waste in a college lab or drainage blockages due to plastic. Learning happened together.

## Conclusion

The “I and My Waste” program exemplifies how science education can empower youth through local, actionoriented engagement. It fostered scientific literacy, civic agency, and collaborative solution-building. The program’s emphasis on student-led inquiry and real-world relevance contributed to meaningful engagement and community impact. Future work will explore scalability, adaptability to other socio-environmental issues, and longer-term outcomes across diverse educational contexts.
