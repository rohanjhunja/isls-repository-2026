# Developing Approaches to Racial Justice in the Learning Sciences

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this symposium, we build on the legacies and ongoing work of scholars across fields who have made room for research and teaching involving literacies and practices of racial justice. In recent years, we have seen even deeper engagement with these conversations within the learning sciences, as the shift toward addressing the political and ethical dimensions of learning has come into focus. Through this structured poster session, we expand theoretical underpinnings and attend to the ways that emerging scholars from different areas of the learning sciences are taking up the mantle.

What does racial justice look like in the learning sciences? What does it look like, feel like, and sound like to advance racial justice in the learning sciences? What has racial justice meant? For whom? And, to what end?  In this symposium, we build on the legacies and ongoing work of scholars across fields who have made room for resea rch and teaching involving literacies and practices  of racial justice. The session explores what it means to advance racial justice globally in this era, where the backlash against movements for racial repair and remediation has gro wn. Hostility against racial justice movements is e vident in the systematic censorship of speech in education, i ncreases in xenophobic policies, and escalating  incidents of racialized violence interpersonally and by various state apparatuses. Moments like these require schol ars of learning to mobilize our teaching, research, and se rvice to advance justice projects (Politics of Lear ning Writing Collective, 2017), and to think carefully about the  role of learning in addressing historicized power relations (Esmonde & Booker, 2017). However, racial justice h as largely remained on the periphery of learning sc iences work, and so in this moment of racialized backlash,  we  look to scholarship and praxis from racial justice movements and literature to anchor our collective work in support of racial justice within the learning sciences. Our structured poster session invites participants to reflect on diverse racial justice traditions and interrogate if and how these theoretical keystones might generate new insights into learning toward justice. Since the learning sciences’ founding, some learnin g scientists have always been attentive to relation s of power embedded in historicity and identity. From the earl iest days, our predecessors have compellingly argue d that racialization, ethnicity, class, and culture are key facets of learning ecologies tha t must be addressed analytically (Lee 1995; Rogoff, 1981; Gutierrez 1992, Nasir, 200 2; Nasir & Hand, 2006; Rose 2001, Saxe, 1984). Thes e foundations have created a fertile ground for further engagement across educational research fields that centrally address racialization, including critical race theo ry in education (e.g., Ladson-Billings & Tate, 1995), ethnic studies (e.g., Cammarota, 2016; de los Rios, et al., 2015), critical Indigenous pedagogies (e.g., Tach ine & Nicolazzo, 2020), decolonial studies (e.g., Bajaj, 2022; Battiste, 2002; Zavala, 2016), and other specialized fields. In recent years, we have seen even deeper engagemen t with these conversations within the learning sciences, as the shift toward addressing the politi cal and ethical dimensions of learning has come into focus (Esmonde & Booker, 2017). This growing scholarship emphasizes that learning is cultural and embedded within current systems of injustice and that all conceptualizations of learning are connected to political systems (Madkins & McKinney de Royston, 2019). Within the learning sciences, attention to racial-ideological tensions in teaching and learning is generating important directions for learning scholarship. For instance, Philip (2011) and  SenguptaIrving et al. (2021) reveal a need for racial literacy development, arguing that these skills are both distinctive from other disciplinary learning and needed in the conte xt of deep, historical racial injustice. Another ke y focus of scholarship observes how learners imagine possibili ties for racial justice through organizing and desi gn (e.g., Pham & Philip, 2021; Arada et al., 2023). These app roaches draw our collective attention to how learni ng and racial justice can and should be imbricated in our field to theorize learning in situ, where racial in justice is hegemonic and where calls to action from communitie s, movements, educators, and young people have urged us to take substantive action to remake racial relations of power. As we work to build racial justice scholarship in t he learning sciences, we remain attentive to the relations, power, and processes that shape learning. We recognize that many scholars actively make space for discussions of race and racial justice within ICLS itself (Curnow, 2021; Marin et al., 2022; Jones et al., 2023; Rodgers, 2023; Shaw et al., 2023; Tzou et al., 2023; Leibovitz et al., 2024), contribute to ongoing dialogue about how we do this work, what counts as knowledge, and make way for alternative ways of being that are de-colonial, non-extractive, and meaningfully anti-racist. We affirm that such methodological considerations about how, why, for, and with whom we do research, are critical for adva ncing and understanding approaches to racial justice. As evidenced by the overlaps in our thinking, learning  is inherently reflexive, community-centered, intersectional, and taking action toward an otherwise (Tachine & Nicolazzo, 2023). In this structured poster session, we take up criti cal perspectives, expand theoretical underpinnings to include new-to-the-field approaches to theorizing race, racialization, and racial justice, and attend to the ways that emerging scholars from different areas of the learning sciences are taking up the mantle. Collectively, we position our work within the learning sciences’ long-standing engagement with diversity and equity goals  while actively integrating theoretical contributions from the broa der fields of education, critical ethnic studies, B lack studies, and other fields attuned to racial justice. We aim to offer a generative space to push ourselves and participants to make a deeper commitment to understanding racial re lations of power across space, place, time, and con dition, and centering these concerns in our design, theorization, and knowledge mobilization. Synergies within our work emphasize that racial justice is ultimately a relational endeavor. Through collective designs that center minoritized ways of knowing and being and pay attention to relations as both process and product (Bang & Vossoughi, 2017), we challenge hegemonic narratives and transform what learning could be toward justice. Facilitated process The symposium will be organized as a structured pos ter session, inviting participants to engage with v arious approaches to racial justice in the learning scienc es and synthesize across a range of geographic, the oretical, and sociopolitical diversity. Our session will begin with initial framing by emerging researchers engaged with racial justice literature within and beyond the learning sciences. Each poste r presenter will provide a brief overview of their work, then participants are invited to visit and engage more thoroughly with each of the eight posters at their own pace. Each poster will feature a raci al justice research design collaboration and respon d to a series of questions rooted in racial justice theory and practice, including invitations to 1) Address the positioning of the community partnership and researcher(s), highlighti ng the role of relationality and identity in making  specific knowledge production possible and productive, 2) In troduce the communities that the design collaborati ons are accountable to and describing the shared work in community, 3) Lay out the research questions and design goals,

4) Orient to particular traditions and lineages of racial justice theory and practice, and 5) Elaborate on how racial

justice is prefigured through the communi ty design processes. Taken together, these posters demonstrate the diversity of racial justice work and bring a pedago gical stance to integrating racial justice scholars hip into the learning sciences in theoretically grounded ways.

We invite participants to engage the dialogue throu ghout the symposium. To this end, we will ask participants to consider: 1) As you engage with various explorations of racial justice research in this poster session, how are you conceptualizing racial justice in the learning sciences? 2) How might different conceptualizations of racial justice i nfluence and impact your work and the communities y ou serve? 3) What questions do you have about racial justice research or how you might better facilitate racial justice in your own work? We will close with a synthesis of the overarching themes, offer insigh ts on how we envision support within our field to f oster meaningful and generative spaces for racial justice  work and ignite discussion on what racial justice work might look like in participants’ research and design work.

Working in good ways to prefigure abolition as racial justice Joe Curnow, Anny Chen Recently in Winnipeg, we have seen a proliferation of policing in everything from public libraries to grocery stores, justified through racialized language. We’ve seen similar “crime wave” discourses across North America, though in Winnipeg they have a distinctive anti-Indigenous framing (Wilt, 2024). However, a coaliti on of organizers have come together to fight for a city w here investment in communities might create antirac ist and anticolonial paths to community safety that do not rely on policing, prisons, and family separation. This coalition has struggled against mai nstream attitudes where safety means securitization  and surveillance. How, then, do coalition organizers expand the imaginations of Win nipeggers beyond carceral solutions and design for abolitionist futures?

The Abolitionist Futures Collective is a collective  of prison and police abolitionist organizers in Wi nnipeg, MB, Canada. Our group includes people working on defund ing police, prison rideshare, sex worker activists,  library activists trying to remove secu rity screening from public libraries, Métis soverei gnty groups, and others. We are a multiracial group with a plurality of non-binary/genderqueer folks. Joe (she/her) is a cis wh ite woman with European and Latinx heritage. Anny (she/her) is a cis Canadian-born Chinese woman. Our research asks 1. How do we redefine safety toward community and away fro m colonial models of punishment, 2. How do we keep us safe, and 3. How do we facilitate politicization that enables participants in our workshops to imagine abolitionist futures?

The Abolitionist Futures Collective works in Treaty 1 t erritory, the lands and waters of Nêhiyawak, Inninew, Anishinaabek, Oceti Sakowin Oyate, Michif, Anishininiiwak, and Denesuline people. This relationship calls on us to be in right relationship and attend to the ongoing colonial violence in Winnipeg. Racia l justice is read through the long histories of Indigenous-led anticolonial resistance in Winnipeg (Sinclair, 2024), as well as Black feminist scholarship within prison and police  abolition traditions (Dav is, 2011; Gilmore, 2007; Kaba, 2020).

Racial justice shows up in our research design in two significant ways. First by drawing on lessons from Indigenous Elders, Knowledge Keepers, and community  helpers, we have organized our work together throu gh the Working in Good Ways framework (Ferland, Chen & Villagrán Becerra, n.d.). This approach offers resources for building strong relationships rooted in Indigen ous ways of knowing and being while addressing hist oricized colonial power relations. Second, in our  design we work to prefigure (Maeckelbergh, 2005; Cu rnow & Uttamchandani, 2022) abolitionist politics (Gilmore, 2007), where building relationships and community  is a central outcome and process, where we plan for acce ssibility and attempt to integrate Dis ability Justice into our approach, where we strive to be in anti/decolonial relationship, and where we work to center racial justice. The early stages of this work were born out of a desire to more closely conduct analyses of how race and racialization shape the experience and design of le arning environments. As an early career learning sc ientist, I struggled to find literature that talk ed about race and racism as shaping technology educ ation. For e xample, overwhelmingly practitioners said “no” when  asked if race had ever been discussed in computing classr oom environments-and how this lack of instruction led to moments of uncertainty in how to approach times of great contention, daily activities, and tokenization. At its core, this work examines the contradictions and possibilities of transnational identity and intergenerational learning toward building coll ective power and engaging education as a practice of freedom (hooks, 1994). Becoming racially literate as racial justice praxis Talia Leibovitz I conducted a four year ethnographic study analyzin g a multi-racial group of middle-class parents organizing for racial justice in Oakland, California as a cis hete ro white woman with eastern European heritage who engages in anti-racist racial justice organizing in the K-12 education system. I utilize an interdisciplinary  framework of critical policy analysis and socio-cultural theories of learning to consider how people learn and strategize toward racial justice amidst the local political economy t hey are organizing within. My research places educa tion policy in conversation with the learning sciences, pushing  the boundaries of both disciplines to demonstrate that education policy scholars and scholars of learning must understand how and what policy actors learned about education policy and racial justice.

Much of the existing research on middle-class parents across racial groups organizing for r acial justice in education illustrates how despite the best of intentions (Lewis & Diamond, 2015), these parents often reproduce the very inequalities they aim to ameliorate. Build ing from this research, I consider how middle-class parents across racial groups engage in moment-to-moment interactions that shape their learning for racial justice in ways that afford a rearticulation of racialized ideologi es. I draw from Stuart Hall’s understanding that id eologies are constantly contested and at times, transformed (Hal l, 1996). Societal transformat ion occurs via rearticulation where ideological connections are disrupted, and new ones are created (Hall, 1996). Hall’s framing of articulation is particularly relevant given this study’s focus o n how a group of parents came to contest commonly h eld racialized ideologies of school quality in ways that not  only shaped their learning of racialization but al so their organizing strategies for racial justice. I also or ient to racial justice through Scott and Bajaj’s (2 022) use of racialization to analytically consider how people or groups come to be classified beyond identity, and how these categories are connected to political, social, and economic dynamics that have consequences for people ’s life opportunities.

I developed an original conceptual framework that i ntegrated theories of political economy and socio-cultural approaches to learning in order to capture the macro conditions that shape inequality in an urbandistrict, while also documenting and learning from the moment-to-moment interactions through which people make sense of inequality and organize toward racial justice. T his interdisciplinary framework allowed me to ask h ow the socio-political context of a school district informs the discourse and policies of educational efforts and how people learn about racial equity through their organizing.

The design of my study as an ethnographic case study allowed me to analyze learning over time as organizers for racial justice responded to political events that unfolded in the school district. Latina testimonios of failure: Trenzas of failure, learning, and survivance in the Latina experience Beatriz Salazar Over the last 20 years in the field of learning sciences, there has been an increasing amount of research surrounding failure and the experiences of failure as opportunities for learning. However, wha t has often been left out of the conversation, until more recently, has been the ways identity impacts the ways learners experience failure. In the United States, girls and young women of color often receive negative consequences as a result of asking questions in the classroom (Annamma et al, 2019). Instead, perfection and a lack of questions are rewarded in the education system, despite the research supporting opportuniti es for failure, mistakes, and questions (Kapur, 2008; McNamara, 2001; Van Lehn et al., 2003).

Guided by the framework of trenzas and greñas (Mont oya, 1994) and implementing testimonio (Delgado-Bernal, 1998) as a method, this dissertation explor es how Latinas in the United States experience self-identified failures and the learning that stems from these experiences. Much like Montoya’s experience in trenzas and greñas, Latinas and people of color, wear masks  that hide our greñas, our imperfections in our bra ided lives. This is a notion that is especially true for those who are children of immigrants,  who often believe that failure is not an option after their parents gave up so much f or them (Romano, 2022). Together, the researcher and the collaborators learn how to explore this racial justice issue through a collaborative, decolonized methodology. Without these masks, our true and whole selves migh t not be accepted by others. This work seeks to challenge the notion that to be imperfect means you  are a failure. Without mistakes and the room to se lf-reflect, process, and re-mediate our errors in true vulnerability, people of  color are robbed of the opportunity to learn, while the system is robbed of the opportunity to ac knowledge its wrongs. As a Latina daughter of immig rant parents growing up in the US, I challenge the notion that “failure is not an option” or that to fail means to become a failure that youth of color and children of immigrants carry. We are not perfect, nor are we meant to be. We too deserve the opportunity to learn and grow.

In our codesign effort, two teachers (Kenn edy & Folger) engaged with data specialists, curric ulum developers, and learning scientists to build data-focused addendums, including curating and customizing datasets, for existing curricula that blend Social Studies and ELA content. Our work serves students who are att ending Marbles Expeditionary, a charter K-8 school located in the midwest U.S. serving predominantly Black low-and middle-income students, and Oak Park Expeditionary, a publ ic K-12 school located in the northeastern U.S., serving predominantly Latine American students, and  students from immigrant families. Across their mid dle school classrooms, we observed how  instructors introduced and scaffolded data practice s that were grounded in and entwined with their commitment to promote racia l equity and racial justice. We co-developed an interest in exploring the sociopolitical implications of teachi ng, learning, and creating with data. Specifically,  how may classroom educators facilitate learning with data for racial justice? Our approach to racial justice issue focused on 1) constructing high-quality curricula that center and serve our own students to unsettle a normalized reality in which Black and Brown youth are seldomly prioritized in learning materials (Dumas, 2014; McKinney de Roy ston et al., 2021) and 2) interrogating and counter acting the systemic neglect of Japanese American injustice and Asian Americans across K-20 curricula (An, 2016). We describe two teachers' adaptations and enactments o f a curriculum module that examines the mass incarc eration of Japanese Americans during World War II. In Folger’s class, students studied the history of immigration to the US in the 20th century, the role immigration and ci tizenship played in the experience of Japanese inca rceration, and current trends in migration and detention, engaging multiple datasets to explore the question “Who gets to be an American? ” Kennedy’s data lessons delved into “sense of belong ing” as an enduring dilemma affecting generations of immigrants and racially marginalized  communities. Students worked with datasets to unve il the stories of Japanese American prisoners before, duri ng, and after incarceration and the current-day experience of refugees in students’ local state; furthermore, students collected their own data embracing the sense of belonging at their own school community. In this poster, we e xamine the range of activities and  interactions with data that our classroom educators and their students employed to challenge systemic violence and deficit narratives on the Japanese and Asian American communities. We explore  the role that youth's intersectional identities pl ayed in their emotional responses and perspective-taking regarding various racial injustices, includi ng those affecting immigrant, refugee communities, and their own racialized school communities.
