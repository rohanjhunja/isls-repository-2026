# Socially Shared Metacognitive Regulation (SSMR): Comparative Analysis of Strategies and Adaptations in Teams with Divergent Outcomes during CPS

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Collaborative problem-solving (CPS) for ill-structured problems is an integral part of various learning settings, where teams apply varied SSMR strategies and perform necessary adaptations. The effective application and adaptation of SSMR strategies contribute to CPS success, making their investigation crucial for understanding SSMR's in CP S. This study undertakes a comparative analysis of SSMR strategy application and adaptation in teams with divergent outcomes during ill-structured CPS. Five engineering teams (N Learners=20) completed four-hour programming-related ill-structured CPS tasks in a closed lab setting. Teams were classified as more and less successful outcome teams (MSOT and LSOT) using an evaluation rubric. Verbal interactions (of 20 hours) were analyzed using a mixed-methods approach combined with comparative thematic analysis. Considerable differences were found between MSOT and LSOT regarding SSMR strategy application and adaptation. These differences are presented through comparative frequency analysis and thematic representations, revealing distinctions in how MSOT and LSOT strategize and adapt during CPS.

Collaborative Problem Solving (CPS) is a dynamic process where teams must develop a shared mental model and collective scheme of cognitive interdependence to ensure high-quality participation and shared understanding (Järvelä, 2021; Vogel et al., 2023). This process requires learners to regulate their own cognition, metacognition, and motivation through shared metacognitive monitoring (Järvelä et al., 2013). However, challenges frequently arise from differing prior knowledge and task interpretations, varied task familiarity, and diverse socio-cultural backgrounds (Sobocinski et al., 2021). In such contexts, Socially Shared Metacognitive Regulation (SSMR) becomes critical, as it enables teams to collectively recognize their regulatory needs (Vauras et al., 2021; Seiradakis & Spantidaki s, 2019; Iiskala et al., 2011). Socially shared metacognitive regulation (SSMR) involves participants working together in a goal-directed, shared, and reciprocal manner to regulate shared thinking processes during collaborative learning context (Iiskala et al., 2015; Iiskala et al., 2011). SSMR plays an essential role in CPS by guiding teams' cognitive activity through monitoring and control processes to ensure CPS progress toward shared goals (Shin, Lee, & Kim, 2025; Schoor, Narciss, & Körndle, 2015). During ill-structured CPS, teams formulate and apply various SSMR strategies targeting task and content understanding, planning (goal setting), and task performance (Sobocinski et al., 2021). However, due to the paucity of studies investigating SSMR strategies in ill-structured CPS contexts, more empirical evidence is needed to understand their  dynamic application (Lobczowski et al., 2021). Moreover, existing studies investigating SSMR strategies have not adequately considered the performance levels of collaborating teams, highlighting the need for research that accounts for both performance outcomes and the dynamic emergence of SSMR across various contexts (Lobczowski et al., 2021). Beyond strategy application, CPS demands 'on-the-fly' adaptations in SSMR strategies during implementation, as team members negotiate adjustments to cognitive, metacognitive, emotional, motivational, and behavioral states as needed. Such adaptation is considered an efficient form of regulation (Hadwin et al., 2018). Adaptation is described as small-scale when it occurs "on the fly" and aims to optimize the current learning process in response to shared metacognitive monitoring indicating a need to adapt (Hadwin et al., 2018). In contrast, large-scale adaptation refers to transferring regulatory knowledge gained in the current task to future situations (Winne & Hadwin, 1998).

Regarding small-scale adaptations in SSMR, the limited number of existing studies necessitates more empirical evidence across various contexts (Sobocinski et al., 2021). Furthermore, available evidence on small-scale adaptations has predominantly focused on well-performing teams, warranting detailed investigation of SSMR strategies in both more and less successful outcome teams (Sobocinski et al., 2021). Additionally, since existing evidence for small-scale adaptations derives from studies of short duration, investigating these adaptations in longer-duration studies would be valuable (Sobocinski et al., 2019). This study investigates 109 SSMR strategy application and small-scale adaptations in dive rgent outcome teams specifically, more successful outcome teams (MSOT) and less successful outcome teams (LSOT) during ill-structured CPS. We classified teams as MSOT and LSOT based on the premise that SSMR is crucial for collaborative success (Malmberg et al., 2015; Ucan & Webb, 2015) and to explicitly consider the performance factor in our comparison. The ill-structured nature of the tasks increased difficulty, requiring enhanced SSMR strategy application for effective collaboration (Tucker et al., 2020; Iiskala, 2011). Our analysis employed comparative thematic analysis and quantitative measures to examine SSMR strategies and small-scale adaptations between MSOT and LSOT. The research questions (RQs) guiding this study is: 1) how  do the SSMR strategies differ between teams with divergent outcomes dur ing ill-structured CPS? and 2) how do the small-scale adaptations in SSMR strategies differ between teams with divergent outcomes during ill-structured CPS?

## Participants

Five teams comprising 20 undergraduate engineering learners (Mean age = 19.2 years, SD = 0.67; 65% Male, 35% Female) from a state university participated in this study with prior informed consent. Participants were recruited from two affiliated engineering colleges and registered voluntarily, forming teams based on familiarity with classmates. Learners were primarily third and fourth-year computer science engineering majors.

## CPS Task and study design

The ill-structured problem scenario for learners was: "A 9th-grade student from a vernacular medium school in India struggles with understanding C++ syntax and error messages in English despite having strong mathematical and logical thinking skills. The student needs a programming interface for understanding programming errors in their local language." A dummy user was available throughout CPS for guidance. The CPS required learners to apply software development and programming skills including coding concepts, debugging and troubleshooting (Çoklar and Akçay, 2018). The ill-structured nature allowed teams to choose aligned problem statements, encouraging diverse thought processes, brainstorming, and decision-making. The CPS study design was structured around three major milestones with corresponding tasks, as illustrated in

Figure 1. Each milestone contained a heading serving as an objective, indicating tasks and major actions for

teams. Separate closed rooms were allocated to each team to ensure an isolated working environment. Figure 1 The Ill-Structured CPS Process Has Three Major Milestones, Starting with CPS Orientation and Progressing Clockwise, with Bidirectional Links Allowing Teams to Move Back and Forth. Milestone 1 focused on data gathering and problem analysis tasks. Milestone 2 required reviewing existing solutions and finalizing one idea. Milestone 3 involved developi ng a low-fidelity prototype and  user 110 evaluation. The four-hour CPS task included comprehensive instructions, tasks, and a self-evaluation rubric (1) used to evaluate team performance. All teams successfully completed the task with varied performance levels.

The study began with a CPS orientation session. Teams were informed this was not a competition and could use any programming language or framework combination but were strictly prohibited from using LLMs, Co-pilot, or AI-based tools, and mobile phones as this could undermine externalizing SSMR strategies during CPS. Participants could consult websites like Google or Stack Overflow for technical details and debugging but not for ready-made solutions. Team members worked in face-to-face, co-located settings using individual laptops with options to share work. A physical whiteboard supported ideation and drawing like system design diagram creation. The facilitator explained the ill-structured problem theme, introduced dummy users, and discussed milestone-wise deliverables, all provided as handouts. The CPS session spanned maximum four hours with soft milestone time allocations as guidelines. Teams retained flexibility to divide responsibilities, work sequentially through milestones 1-3, and revisit previous milestones. Each team submitted deliverables as a single document at session end and demonstrated their low-fidelity prototypes to the dummy user. Data was collected from five teams (N = 20) through videotaped verbal interactions and low-fidelity prototype solutions created using different programming languages. Each member worked on a personal laptop with options to contribute on a shared screen. Verbal interactions were captured using a camera, yielding five team session videos of approximately four hours each, totaling 20 hours. Teams used Google Docs and physical whiteboards to create shared representations such as system design diagrams. Verbal interaction data was primarily analyzed to examine SSMR strategies and adaptation traces, while solution prototypes, Google Docs submissions, and shared representations provided additional contextual clarification when required. Progressive solution prototypes were considered in evaluating teams' performance against the self-evaluation rubric. Coding scheme and strategy A three-phase coding process was implemented for data analysis. In the first phase, episodes showing application of SSMR strategies were identified. Verbal interaction containing enactment/application of predefined SSMR strategy by two or more members is considered as an SSMR episode (Isohätälä et al., 2018; Järvenoja et al., 2020). The SSMR strategy episode reflects continued patterns of collaboration and topic, ending with a clear shift in either area. In the second phase, to examine micro-level application of metacognitive skills, SSMR strategy episodes were further analyzed at the statement level using a deductive categorization method of content analysis (Mayring, 2015, p. 95; Ader et al., 2023). A statement, defined as a single conversational turn, represents a thematically consistent verbalization of a metacognitive action or skill by an individual team member (De Backer, 2015). Each statement was coded for the metacognitive skills in SSMR (such as orienting, planning, monitoring, and evaluating) along with corresponding sub-skills (e.g., comprehension monitoring or strategic planning) (Meijer et al., 2006). In the third phase, to understand and map SSMR strategies applied by team members, statements situated in SSMR strategy episodes were coded using four strategy codes: monitoring/ controlling task understanding, monitoring/controlling content understanding, planning task performance, and monitoring/controlling task performance (Lobczowski et al., 2021). Finally, coding was concluded by marking traces of small-scale adaptations (either adaptive or maladaptive). The trace of small-scale adaptation in SSMR strategy was marked as adaptive if the monitoring event revealed the need for regulation and team members used the information gained to strategically control their learning (Winne and Hadwin, 1998), either through making adjustments to enacted strategies or by strategically adapting their task perceptions, plans, and actions (Sobocinski et al., 2021). The trace was marked as maladaptive if the monitoring event revealed the need for regulation but team members failed to use the information to strategically control their learning by making desired adjustments to enacted strategies or failed to adapt their task perceptions, plans, and actions. The target of the small-scale adaptation is marked in the strategy code (Sobocinski et al., 2021). The comprehensive coding scheme used for deductive categorization of statements is shown in Table 1. Two examples (respectively for MSOT and LSOT) of episode identification and statement coding are presented in Table 3 a &b. CPS performance was evaluated by experienced educational technology researchers. The rubric consisted of three milestones, nine objectives and tasks, aligned with 13 deliverables. Each deliverable and process carried a maximum of 10 points, totaling 130 possible points. A 60% cutoff (78 out of 130) was used for classification: teams scoring 78 or higher were categorized as MSOT, and those below as LSOT. Teams 1, 3, and 4 were classified as MSOT with scores of 93, 86, and 98 respectively, while Teams 2 and 5 were classified as LSOT with scores of 74 and 63. ## Data analysis

We analyzed 20 hours of videotaped verbal interaction data from five teams using a mixed-methods approach. The analysis employed deductive content analysis combined with comparative thematic analysis (Joffe, 2012) to examine SSMR strategies and small-scale adaptations between divergent  outcome teams. For RQ1, we compared SSMR strategy applications between MSOT and LSOT through three themes. Themes 1 and 2 focused on quantitative differences in SSMR strategies related to planning and task performance, comparing average statement occurrences (illustrated in Figure 2) and supporting findings with verbal interaction excerpts. Theme 3 examined qualitative differences in monitoring approaches for task progress and deliverables, supported entirely by verbal interaction evidence. For RQ2, we compared small-scale adaptations in SSMR strategies between MSOT and LSOT through two emergent themes (Themes 4 and 5). Both themes centered on 112 quantitative differences in adaptation frequency related to task planning and performance, as well as adaptation quality marked by occurrences of maladaptive traces (illustrated in Figure 3). Comparisons were strengthened through relevant verbal interaction excerpts demonstrating adaptive patterns. Descriptive statistics The descriptive statistics of five teams show that teams applied various SSMR strategies, resulting in 85 SSMR strategy episodes containing 763 statements verbalized by team members during CPS (Table 2). The statistics reveal that SSMR strategies such as monitoring/controlling task performance and planning task performance were widely practiced, whereas monitoring/controlling task understanding and monitoring/controlling content understanding were less frequently observed. A similar trend emerged for adaptation traces: those targeting planning and executing task performance were widely exhibited, while adaptation traces targeting task and content understanding were less frequently exhibited.

This study offers empirical evidence on SSMR strategy application and small-scale adaptations in divergent outcome teams during long-duration CPS tasks, advancing SSMR literature.  These insights into SSMR strategies and adaptations highlight important implications for scaffolding comprehensive strategy use and minimizing maladaptive SSMR. However, limitations include the small, closed-lab sample size, potentially limiting generalizability, and a narrow focus that excluded the influence of individual mediating variables and the interplay of multiple regulatory forms. Future research should address these by using larger-scale, multimodal designs.
