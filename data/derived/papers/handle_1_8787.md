# Writing to Rewrite Dominant Discourse(s): Some Design Dilemmas From Global South

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster considers some design elements that can potentially work through the double bind of English education for culturally marginalized students in post-colonial contexts. English writing in such contexts often plays out as an overwriting on native consciousness. Using the framework of counter stories, I look at the Facebook compositions of Sangita, a young indigenous girl from India. Her composition illustrates the potential to rewrite dominant notions around indigenous food systems and offer some possibilities for design.

## Introduction: Double bind of english language learning

In postcolonial settings, learning of the English Language presents one of the most complicated double binds. The historical origins of English language education in India emer ged primarily as a tool for governance. Almost two centuries later, language scholars argue that the colonial tendency of the English language is far from over, as it con tinues to play a strong imperialistic role in the global south (Phillipson,  2009). On the other hand, in a caste ridden societ y like India, English is pitched as the only language of emancipation for the marginalized (Ilame, 2020). In a society where educational opportunities were reserved for some; English became a tool to by pass the upper caste hegemony. In such a situation,  English is both empowering and disempowering for learners from cult urally and linguistically marginal backgrounds. Thi s calls for a culturally responsive English language learning framework that works through this double bind. Writing counter stories What do great writers who come from marginalized communities do and should do? For pragmatic reasons they emulate the structures of successful writing and earn economic and social recognition. But often they engage in co unter storytelling, which means presenting the world from a position of  marginality. They write into the discourse, that w hich resists being written. They write in ways that it challenges the contours of what writing is supposed to look like. At a level of abstraction all great artists produce that which is not a part of the dominant conversation or that which may subvert common sense. Thus, I argue, that counter stories are a good format on which writing may be modeled. The concept of counte r stories has been evoked under similar names like ‘critical storytell ing’ and ‘counter-narratives’, and it emerged in v arious disciplines like law, social work and critical pedagogy (Miller et a l., 2020). The concept was eventually more formally  grounded in the domain of education (Solórzano & Yosso, 2002). Over the years, some studies have tried to integrate counter storytelling as powerful way to engage with literacy and language development (Kelly, 2017).

## Research context and analyses

In this poster I provide an illustration of writing by an 18-year-old Santhal girl Sangita. Santhal community is an indigenous community in India. She is one amongst the many Santhal youth who are part of on online language learning project. When Sangita started posting on Facebook, she made all k ind of posts. For my analyses I focus on the posts that centers indigenous/local food systems. We do so, because lo cal food systems are a central node to understand v arious layers of identity, culture, and knowledge. In addition to this, there is an urgency to the health and nutrition crisis in the region. At a social level, Santhal food items are threatened because of the dominant discourse around ‘civil food’. Dominant stories around what is ‘good food’ dissuades young indigenous peop le away from a whole range of local food practices,  which are categorized as ‘backward’ (Bose, 2016).

In one of her posts (Image a) she e xplains the context of rat hunting in terms of the agriculture cycle. Hunting of field rats is play, pleasure, group activity, learn ing and a form of nutrition that kids (boys) genera te for themselves. She centers her world and normalizes it by asking her n on-Santhal Facebook friends if they have ever done rat hunting. The children are placed strategically, holding the bunc h of rats in the middle through a stick along their  shoulders. In the background one can get a good glimpse of a path and some younger children, who over time would also learn this traditional practice. The children are not hunting in the picture, but rather a thumbnail of the practice is stylistically choreographed. Thus, these posts do not just re-produce, but rather re-s tyle and reinvent the cultural connections for thei r audience and for the Santhal youth themselves. I want to highlight two key counter story elements to this post. First, it unsettles the hierarchy of food in the region, where rat eating is usually som ething that Santhal community considers a guilty pl easure. Second, this ICLS2022 Proceedings 2202 © ISLS image runs in contrast to images that national news papers carry about the community and nutrition cris is. Generally, rural children are portrayed as recipient of free meals, looking up in desperation of relief. In another post (Image b) she asserts the significance of local food systems in the times of Covid. There has been a huge movement to get rural ingenious communities integrated with markets, and then the lockdown enforced the closure of all markets for many weeks. Within such a context, she makes a case for the sustainability of locally available ‘wild’ food. Apart from the content, her Facebook composition is noticeable for translangauging and multimodality.

## Implications for design

What can we learn from such online compositions of indigenous youth? What implications it has for designing writing spaces for culturally marginalized students in postcolonial contexts? One element that needs to be incorporated and engaged with is the practice of translanguaging. This comes fraught with the tensions and hierarchy that exist within multiple languages and the explicit desire to move to a ‘pure’ English space. Another major component that needs to be part of the design is a critical engagement with community knowledge. One way to do that is using mentor texts that illustrate counter narratives. Another way to do that is to build on the aspect of multimo dality. By encouraging students to use images and visual expression, we may allow for community knowledge and practices to enter classroom discourse. I think another important aspect of writing on community practices, involves integrating commun ity experts in the process of writing. This may be done as part of student’s research into an issue, that has the power of remediating relationships in the community. A major challenge would be to design novel criterion for assessing such writing artifacts. Standard ways of assessment and standard writing methods (5 paragraph essay) may undermine some of the innovative aspects of composing counter narratives in this way. Finally, to really engage with the double of english language, it would be crucial that writing designs are informed by and generative of theories from the global south, rather than confirming to frames set by the global North.
