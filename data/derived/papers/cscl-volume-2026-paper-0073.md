# A Case Study on the Effect of TAs’ Awareness of Pre-class Report Submissions on Scaffolding in Small-group Discussions

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In recent years, Teaching Assistants (TAs) have been expected to play a key role in facilitating small-group discussions. However, prior studies have shown that TAs often struggle to provide appropriate interventions, frequently offering task-related explanations without first diagnosing students’ understanding. This study examined whether awareness of students’ pre-class report submissions supported TAs’ diagnostic scaffolding during small-group discussions. A 100-minute class session was video-recorded, and all TA utterances were transcribed and analyzed based on the framework of the Model of Contingent Teaching (MCT), focusing on the diagnostic and intervention phases. The results suggest that although both TAs were aware of students’ pre-class report submissions, only one TA engaged in diagnostic questioning to assess students’ understanding before intervening, whereas the other tended to respond with empathic support without diagnosis. This study extends the MCT to TA-supported learning environments by emphasizing the importance of recognizing pre-class reports and highlighting the interpretive processes that mediate between information awareness and adaptive intervention.

The role of the facilitator  is important for successful  collaborative learning. Teachers ask open-ended questions to promote group progress and support  students’ metacognitive engagement and collaborative knowledge construction (Hmelo-Silver & Barrows, 2008). During collaborative learning, teachers adapt the level and timing of scaffolding to facilitate social, cognitive, and metacognitive engagement (Kaendler et al., 2015). The Model of Contingent Teaching (MCT), based on Wood et al. (1976), highlights diagnosis as the first step in providing cognitive scaffolding  (Vande Pol et al., 2012). Recent studies have further demonstrated that contingent scaffolding effectively promotes students’ uptake and understanding when teachers diagnose before intervening (Vande Pol et al., 2019). Moreover, it is important to assess each student's level of understanding within the group before deciding whether to provide collaborative scaffolding  or cognitive scaffolding  (Calor et al., 2022).  Still, teachers face the challenge of insufficient diagnostic strategies to assess students’ learning progress during collaborative learning (Bohm et al., 2025).

In recent years, Teaching Assistants (TAs) have been expected to play an important role in supporting students’ small-group discussions (Wadams & Schick-Makaroff, 2022). TAs who  assist classroom activities alongside teachers can bridge the gap between teachers and students by providing immediate, context-sensitive feedback and recognizing learning difficulties that teachers  might overlook (Wilson & Varma-Nelson, 2016). In this regard, it is important for TAs to support students’ collaborative learning by providing scaffolding that promotes shared understanding through dialogue.  However, most TA–student interactions focused primarily on task-related explanations and problem solving rather than understanding students’ thinking, indicating  there are challenges with the TAs’ scaffolding (D’Angelo & Rajarathinam, 2024). Thus, developing methods that support the diagnostic phase of scaffolding is essential for improving the adaptiveness of intervention. Understanding students’ information beforehand is effective for teachers and TAs in  providing appropriate scaffolding during whole-class discussions and collaborative learning. An experienced teacher orchestrated whole-class sense-making discussions by adjusting her in-the-moment pedagogical reasoning according to students’ confidence, participation patterns, and classroom relationships (Krist,  2023). Similarly, teachers drew on contextual knowledge —such as knowledge of individual students and of prior lessons —when noticing and interpreting students’ strategies in order to support collective reasoning and discussion (Jarry-Shore & Borko, 2025). Fujikawa and Morita  (2025) suggested that TAs’ awareness of students’ pre-class reports can help them decide when and how to intervene appropriately in collaborative learning. Based on these previous studies, we hypothesized that TAs could use students’ submission status of preclass reports as a diagnostic cue to scaffold effectively during group discussions.  Therefore, this study addresses the following research question: Does awareness of pre-class report submissions promote diagnostic scaffolding by TAs? This study extends MCT to TA-supported learning environments by focusing on TAs’ use of pre-class information.

## Method

The research field was a class in a Japanese university called “Information Media Education Theory,” conducted from April to July 2025.  In this class, students engaged with educational technology research using information media through discussion and presentation.  It was a flipped classroom in which students watched preparatory videos, submitted reports, and participated in face-to-face classes. There were 68 second-to fourth-year undergraduate students, one teacher, and two TAs. TA1 was in the second year of a master’s course at a graduate school, and his specialty was educational technology. He had not previously taken or served as a TA for the class. TA2 was a fourth-year undergraduate student, and his specialty was  also educational technology. He had experience taking the class but he had no experience acting as a TA for the class. The analysis focused on the 12th session of 14 sessions, which explored new learning methods using AR (Augmented Reality). The discussion topic was to summarize the possibilities and challenges of AR-based learning and propose new learning methods and materials. The discussion time was 60 minutes. In this class, all students were given access to a real-time collaborative editing tool (Google Slides, n.d.) and instructed to summarize the slide numbers and key points of the discussion for each group. Students were instructed to support their ideas with references. During a one-hour meeting before class, the teacher  gave the TAs three instructions. First, the teacher explained the content of the pre-class report, in which students compared VR  (Virtual Reality)  and AR technologies based on prior research. Second, the first author provided a seating chart indicating groups by report submission status (Submitted groups vs. Non-submitted groups). As shown in Figure 1, the cells of Submitted groups are indicated in yellow  and Non-submitted groups are shown in gray. The Index of the Learning Styles (Felder & Supurlin, 2005) is listed to the right of each students’ name, but they were excluded from the analysis this time. Finally, the teacher instructed the TAs to provide support for students while referring to the tool and the seating chart. Both TAs viewed the tool and the seating chart on a tablet device (iPad) during discussions  at the same time.

The data collection and analysis was conducted as follows. First, the discussion utterances of all 14 groups were recorded using a 360-degree camera (PIXPRO SP360 by Kodak), with the exception of one group that did not give consent. Next, the recordings were transcribed verbatim, and all TA utterances were extracted. Utterances referring to report content or submission status were analyzed, and TA interventions were classified. Utterances were categorized into “Diagnostic strategies,” “Checking of diagnosis,” and “Intervention strategies” based on MCT (Vande Pol et al., 2012), with all coding conducted by the first author. Utterances expressing affective support were coded separately as “Empathy.” Finally, we compared the two TAs’ scaffolding based on one submitted and one non-submitted group, analyzing differences in strategies and alignment with the MCT. During the first 20 minutes of the small-group discussions, both TAs provided interventions that reflected whether they were aware of the students’ pre-class reports. As shown in Table 1, TA 1 demonstrated Diagnostic Strategies, Checking Diagnosis, and I ntervention Strategies in the submitted  group, and both Diagnostic Strategies  and Intervention Strategies in the non-submitted group. On the other hand, TA 2 exhibited intervention utterances in both submitted and non-submitted groups, while no Diagnostic Strategies and Intervention Strategies  utterances were observed. In addition, empathic utterances, which were not included in the MCT framework, were observed in TA2’s interactions.

The results revealed that awareness of students’ pre-class reports submissions alone does not necessarily lead to diagnostic scaffolding, as the two TAs used this information differently during group discussions. TA1 engaged in diagnostic processes to infer students’ understanding, whereas TA2 tended to provide affective support without diagnosis. This difference may reflect their familiarity with the course: TA1, unfamiliar with the class, inferred students’ understanding from dialogue, whereas TA2, a former participant, anticipated difficulties and intervened without diagnosis. Some of TA2’s utterances were categorized as empathic responses, suggesting that TAs may sometimes prioritize socio-emotional support over diagnostic scaffolding during group discussions. These awareness into diagnostic reasoning may be necessary. This highlights the importance of supporting TAs’ ability to diagnose students’ understanding during group discussions.
