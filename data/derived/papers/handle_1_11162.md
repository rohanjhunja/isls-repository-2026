# Teachers Learning to Make Space for Student Agency and Authority in Literature and Math Classroom Discourse

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents case studies of one high school ELA teacher and one middle school math teacher who, through reflecting on their practice with researchers, learned to support students in taking up agency and authority for building disciplinary knowledge. This learning reflected a shift in their understanding of their discipline, of their role as teachers, and of students’ capabilities to do the intellectual work of the discipline. How teachers made these shifts was different in the two cases but in both cases, collaborative reflection on the design and implementation of instruction was the critical mechanism of change.

## Introduction

Recent national standards in multiple disciplines shift the emphasis from simply memorizing, and mastering facts, routines, or procedures to inquiry orientations that involve students engag ing in disciplinary practices and knowledge construction (e.g., CCSSO, 2010; NGSS Lead States, 2013). Accomplishing thes e shifts involves making fundamental changes in classroom discourse, specifically from monologic to dialogic (Wells & Arauz, 2006). Previous studies have provided images of classroo m talk when students engage in productive student-tostudent disciplinary discourse or reported on the types of instructional moves that might facilitate such discourse (e.g., Cobbet al., 1997; Langer, 2011). However, few studies have empirically investigated teacher learning related to the facilitation of student-to-student talk. Here, we explore the question of how teachers learn  to move from monologic to dialogic discourse through two teacher cases. The cases illustrate contrasts in learning processes yet similarity in achieving shifts towards increased student agency in taking authority for knowledge building.

## Conceptual framework

Sociocultural theory contends that learning occurs through interactions with others (Vygotsky, 1978). Accordingly, communication and dialogue are core me diating factors for facilitating learning. Learning  is both relevant in and a product of the community and cult ure in which learners are participating (e.g., Rogoff, 1990; Vygotsky, 1978; Wenger, 1998). Discourse that is productive for learning uses language in service of constructing, exploring, testing, and/or verifying disciplinary c ontent and practices (Wells, 1999). We refer to suc h discourse as productive disciplinary discourse (PDD). From th is sociocultural perspective on learning in classro oms, students are agentive community members who collaboratively build their disciplinary understandings (Damşa et al., 2010; Hmelo-Silver & Barrows, 2008; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 200 6). As such, students need to see themselves as actors whose ideas are central to bui lding knowledge and understanding, a process that a rises through reflection on how learning is (or is not) h appening (Cobb et al., 1997). Likewise, for teachers, learning involves interactions, and reflection on what and how they are learning. I n the ideal case, reflection leads to iterative cycles of inquiry that build knowledge co llaboratively. In the cases presented in this paper, teacher-researcher dyads engaged in iterative inquiry cycle s focused on the design, planning, implementation, reflection on, and redesign of instruction intended to foster PDD in the classroom community. Conceptualizing teacher learning as sociocultural requires a model of teacher learning that goes beyon d accounting for what’s in an individual teacher’s he ad. It must consider interactive experiences —with students, with colleagues, with coaches, with researchers—and recognize as learning the learning that takes place in those interactions. In the present context, we focus on c ollaborative reflection between teachers and resear chers in the context of iterative design cycles. Dewey (1933/1960) defined reflective thought as “active, persistent, and careful consideration of any belief or supposed form of kno wledge in the light of the grounds that support it and the further conclusions to which it tends” (p. 9). That is, reflection provides the basis for a meaning-making process, and  reflection over time is made possible through video-stimulated recall of past lessons (Geiger et al., 2016; Hollingsworth & Clarke, 2017). In this study, the inquiry-cycle process situates learning in teachers’ experiences of planning, implementing and then reflecting on le ssons (Stein et al., 2021). To capture the sociocultural dimensions of teacher learning, Goldman et al. (2022) expanded the Clarke and Hollingsworth (2002) individual teacher professional growth model to reflect collaborative interactions at multiple points in an instructional design process. Dubbed the Interconnected Interactive Model of Professional Growth (IIMPG) (Figure 1), collaborative reflection occurs in designing, implementing, analyzing the implementation, and revising based on noticings and insights that arise through cycles of collaborative reflection. Figure 1 The Interconnected Interactive Model of Professional Growth (IIMPG) The IIMPG shown in Figure 1 portrays two contexts o f interaction: design spaces and implementation spaces. Design spaces depict two contexts of interaction—professional learning communities (PLCs) and teacherresearch dyads —although there are others (e.g., research-practice partnerships, collaborative design groups) which are external to the classroom. Key in the des ign space is that various individuals contribute di stinctive knowledge, beliefs, and attitudes (personal domain)  in discussions that explore co gnitive and social aspects of what is being taught and why, how it is being taugh t, how students learn, and effective teaching strat egies to produce instructional designs that are then implemented with some consequences in the domain of practice, which in this case is the classroom. Teachers may reflect  themselves on their implementations or there may b e others who collaboratively reflect with the teacher. These reflections are the basis f or consideration in the design space as reflection may lead to redes ign of instruction. Exchanges among participants cr eate opportunities for shifts in knowledge, beliefs, and attitudes, making such contexts valuable for understanding the dynamics of change. The cases of a high school ELA teacher Jennifer and a middle school math teacher Crystal (pseudonyms) presented in this paper compare Jennifer ’s and Crystal’s journeys through cycles of collaborative reflection as they designed and implemented instruction. Both teachers aimed to create classroom spaces for students to discuss their own ideas and collaboratively build their kno wledge and understandings in the disciplines. The cases illustrate the trajectories of teacher learning for  Jennifer and Crystal as well as provide empirical evidence of increasing student agency and distributed authority evolving in their classrooms.

## Methods

The two cases approached teacher learning from two different vantage points. In the ELA case, the lear ning process that occurred over a three-year period was examined retrospectively in collabo rative reflection sessions between the focal teacher and a researcher. In the mathematics case, collaborative reflection took pla ce in the context of cycles of inquiry that occurred over a 3-year period. The cycles of inquiry focused on ident ified problems of practice in design, planning, and implementation.

## Context, data sources, and analytic approach for the ELA case

Jennifer taught 11th grade English in an urban high  school serving a diverse student population (47.9%  Black, 43.1% Hispanic, 3.8% White, 1.9% Asian, and 3.3% mi xed race; 87% free and reduced lunch). She worked closely with researchers across three years to design and implem ent instruction supporting students in literary reading while participating in bi-weekly meetings with the design team, which consisted of researchers and other teachers, and in quarterly cross-disciplinary professional development sessions. During the first-and third-year units, researchers observed the classroom most days collecting video and field notes and debriefing with the teacher after c lass. During Year 2, the teacher kept a journal doc umenting implementation and her reflections, with occasional classroom observations by researchers. This process yielded an extensive data set: classroom observation fieldnotes and videos (35 from year one, 12 from year two, 111 from year three), teacher written reflections, researche r analytic memos, and reflective conversations during and after implementations. Three years later, Jennifer and a researcher analyzed and reflected on her classroom videos and learning experiences from the previous project. The se teacher and researcher retrospective reflect ions informed our understanding of the learning processes she experienced during and after the project. In the collaborative video reflections, the teacher and researcher selected classroom videos that contained extended classroom discourse segments and were at s imilar points in each year’s curriculum. N oticings in the classroom discourse led to potentially useful  or interesting points to stop and reflect on what was happening. These reflections focused on  identifying shifts in teacher practices over time and experienc es that may have contributed to these shifts. We then sampled whole class student discussions from each year and applied a coding system (described below) to ascertain if and how classroom discourse had changed from the first to third year.

## Context, data sources, and analytic approach for the math case

The middle school math teacher, Crystal, taught all subjects in a self-contained sixth grade classroom. She worked in a high-needs school in a district outside of a large Midwe stern city in the U.S. He r classes included students with Individual Education Plans (IEPs) and English Language Learners. Crystal’s work on the project in volved developing better formative assessment practices in algebra and on activating students as resources for each other. The data sources include classroom video and field notes from 25 lesson observations over three years, over 30 hours of audio and video recordings and not es from the teacher-researcher reflective conversations as they engaged in inquiry cycle iterations. In addition, Crystal produced informal teacher written reflections. The data analysis that supported unpacking the teachers’ learning began with generating lesson synopses, based on field notes and video from lesson observations, highlighting the instructional practices that seemed to foster opportunities for student agency and that tr acked teacher-student or student-student interactions. The synopses guided our selection of teacher-researcher discussions for closer examination to ex plore the what and how of Crystal’s learning processes and to identify lessons for a deeper analysis of teacher and student talk moves. Coding discourse To understand how the classroom discourse shifted over time, we developed the Agency and Authority Relationships among Teachers and Students (AARTS) c oding system based on our iterative analysis of the functions of student and teacher talk moves. See Pitvorec et al. (2019) for details. The AARTS codes identify the approximate location of functions of talk moves on two continua—first, with respect to who does the intellectual work (age ncy), and second, with respect to who or what deter mines the important disciplinary ideas to learn (authority). The codes include a set of six course-grained codes for agency (function of student talk codes) and six course-grained codes for authority (function of teacher talk codes). See Figure 2 for an overview of the AARTS coding system.

Figure 2 AARTS Coding System Student and Teacher Continua The order of codes reflects talk moves that at one end are consistent with monologic discourse (T1, S1) and at the other with dialogic discourse (T6, S6). Teacher codes T1 and T2 indicate talk moves directing students to the right answer as defined by the teacher. T3 a nd T4 support students in beginning to offer their ideas about what the text says and what it means. T5 and T6 moves facilitate student sensemaking through student-to-student talk, thereby distributing authority for what counts as i mportant disciplinary ideas. Student codes S1 and S2 are responses to teacher-posed questions or directives. S3 moves advance stu dents’ ideas into the discussion. S4 indicates student talk that takes up and engages with the ideas of other students. S5 and S6 are student moves that build on and challenge each other’s disciplinary ideas, taking up agency for guiding their own learning.

In both cases, the teachers evolved similarly with respect to their conceptualizations of (1) What it means to learn in the discipline;

(2) The expectations for teacher and student roles for learning in the discipline; and (3) Students’ c apacity for

taking up ownership of their learning and collaboratively deepening their understandings. See Table 1. Our analyses indicate that the shifts for teachers in these three dimensions are interrelated. Changes  in any one dimension are likely to affect change in another. For example, changes in co nceptions of the discipline can lead to changes in conception of one’s role in the classroom as changes in one’s role can lead to changes in one’s view of student capacity. The interrelatedness of changes in these dimensions is illustrated by excerpts from the collaborative reflections in each case.

For Jennifer, having the opportunity to reflect on and across three years of instruction afforded insi ght into how changes in the classroom correlated with learning opportunities from both professional development and cycles of collaborative reflection (Goldman et al., 2022). For example, Jennifer reported that she experienced a pivotal change in her conception of the discipline and how she wanted her students to engage with it as a result of a particular professional learning activity: teachers read a short, unfamiliar literary text and reflected on their own disciplinary reading practices, which they then dis cussed. As a result of this experience, Jennifer indicated that she realized a disconnect between how she read literature and how she was teaching her students to read literature: “I realized I wasn’t apprenticing them into any of the ways I paid attention to literature.” This real ization led her to shift her instructional focus from comprehension  and character traits to literary analysis and reas oning. This change in her epistemic commitment led to essential changes in the design and implementation of her instruction: Shifting the purpose of classroom discussion to app renticing students into the kind of thinking that I do as an expert reader in the discipline shifted the way I planned for discussions… Now, I'm thinking about unpacking my own moves and figur ing out texts, participation structures, opportunities for reading, writing, and talk that will invite students into thinking in  the discipline. I'm also thinking about what places in the text will invite students to have these kinds of disciplinary conversations—where in and across the text will invite students to consider how they are making sense. I'm thinking now about how I  am asking my students to approach the text—asking students to select and explain/investigate e vidence and its relationship to a larger question. This shift has allowed me to unpack the d ifferent ways that students are reading text, surface what they think is important and where they might be "stuck" in the text. Although the input for this change in Jennifer’s co nception happened in the design space of the IIMPG in a professional learning community, it impacted the wa y she designed instruction and enacted it in the implementation space. Reflecting on that enacted in struction allowed her to make significant changes t o future instructional designs, which were then brought back into the implementation space. She explained, how needed it was to have those texts and structures and ways of doing in that 9-week module, how necessary it was to engage in those and have the chance to think about them and reflect on them, and how I might have gotten lost in what I was trying to do had I just tried to superimpose that thinking on what I had already been doing. Bei ng deep in that work and reflecting on it, then saying what have I learned and how do I lift that onto a [longer] curriculum [was essential]. Opportunities for reflection were pivotal in her ch ange. These changes in Jennifer’s conception of wha t it meant to teach literary reading also correlated with significant differences in how she facilitated classroom discussions, including making changes in her role and in the mov es she was making during discussions that had a pro found impact on the disciplinary nature of discussions (Hall & Goldman, 2022) and on students taking up agency and authority in the classroom, as shown by the coding excerpts below. For Crystal, collaborative reflective cycles were e qually impactful on her learning over time. In thes e cycles, the teacher and researcher first identified a relevant problem of practice—an area to investigate that might contribute to enhancing opportunities for students to engage in PDD. Then they engaged in iterations of a lessonfocused sub-cycle: 1) T-R dyad planning lessons, 2) Crystal implementing the lessons, 3) T-R dyad reflecting on the lesson implementations in relation to the problem of practice. After multiple iterations of the sub-cycle, when the teacher and researcher identified significant progress on the original problem, they summarized and reflected on the journey and then returned to Step 1 to begin  the next cycle with identifying a problem of practice. Being focused on individual lessons and specific problems  of practice in the classroom, the reflective cycle  remained more proximal to the implementation space of the IIMPG. As an example, one of these inquiry cycles began with identifying the problem of how to increase student engagement in asking each other questions about and  commenting on each other’s strategies. The first i teration of Plan/Implement/Debrief sub-cycle involved: 1) Planning —Sequentially present four student work samples selected to illustrate different solutions with onl y the last one as correct. Ask students to evaluate  the work. 2) Implementing—Included the planned activity but no student questions or discussion resulted. 3) Debriefing—the teacher and researcher, through reflecting on the v ideotaped lesson, realized students anticipated that the last sample would be the correct one (as this was typica l in the class). This was followed by a second iter ation: 1) Planning—Simultaneously present four student work samples—samples that are different, but all of which could be interpreted as correct. Ask students to evaluate the work. 2) Implementing—Included the planned activity and an unanticipated prompt for students to “figure out  how your classmates might have calculated,” where students then discussed the work, responded to each other’s ideas, asked questions, and contributed new ideas. 3) Debriefing—the teacher and researcher, through reflecting on t he videotaped lesson, recognized that Crystal had made progress on the problem. The lesson had engaged students in the desired ways. Finally, in the summarizing and reflecting phase, the teacher and researcher an alyzed across the iterations of the lesson-planning sub-cycles and concluded that not only the planned task (of simultaneously showing four pieces of student work with multiple interpretations that could be correct), but also the unplanned prompt Crystal used (inviting students to “figure out” their classmates’ strategies) had resulted in shifting student engagement and the resulting classroom discourse. After the classroom research ended, Crystal described how her vision of teaching and learning math had shifted, highlighting not only how aware she was of  the changes she had made but also illustrating tha t what she valued had shifted towards privileging student thinking above all else. I got up there during the launch of the lesson [early in the project] and I showed them how they would fill out this table with what was given…and t hen all the students went and they did it. And so, when we got to the summary, there were no strategies for filling out the rate table. They all did the rate table the way I was thinking. I thought about how much I was talkin g…I need to stop doing all the talking because I’m doing the  thinking…they’re being told how to think and how to solve it instead of using multiple strat egies…So then the next year when they did the rate table, a lot of them were able to come up and share their strategies…there were so many ways of thinking that weren’t exposed the year before.

Correlated with teachers’ shifting perceptions, our  classroom videos in both cases indicated changing instructional practices that resulted in new emerge nt discourse patterns —discourse patterns that revealed that authority for what counted as important knowledge a nd understandings in the discipline was distributed throughout the community, positioning students as capable of collaboratively deepening their understandings and building knowledge together. To understand how the learning during cycles of collaborative r eflection in each teacher’s case impacted the classroom discourse, we coded sample segments of discourse from different points in time: Jennifer’s from years 1 and 3 and Crystal’s from years 1, 2, and 3. Analyses of Jennifer’s year 1 classroom videos indi cate emerging student agency and distributed authority. Students often talked during class discussions, but their talking was mostly in response to and directed at the teacher rather than to other students. She would revoice student contributions and ask o ther students to contribute by building on what other students said or presenting different views. However, even though  she listened to and summarized students’ ideas aloud, i nstead of pushing on and exploring those ideas, she  would move the discussion to the ideas that she already had planned and wanted students to discuss. ## Discussion

Our analyses of the learning trajectories and changing classroom discourse patterns of teachers in two disciplines afford insights into what teachers need to learn to  create such classroom spaces and possible processe s by which they learn it (Walkoe & Luna, 2019). Analyses indicated that learning in three areas supported teachers in shifting their practice to support student agency and author ity in sensemaking and knowledge construction media ted by and visible in productive disciplinary discourse. T eachers learned to view students as bringing valuab le prior knowledge and experiences to the work of engaging in complex and deep learning, deepened their understanding of the discipline they were teaching, and changed t heir views of their roles in the classroom, shiftin g their instructional strategies and practices so that the intellectual work was taken up by the students as e videnced by their engagement in PDD.  As teachers learn ed to make space for students to engage in disciplinar y work,  the authority for deciding what counts as important ide as got distributed within the community and student ideas became the focus for collective reflection (Cobb et al., 1997). In addition, students increasi ngly took up agency in directing their own intellectual work and in bui lding knowledge collaboratively (Damşa et al., 2010; Hmelo-Silver & Barrows, 2008; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 200 6). With respect to learning processes that supported t hese teachers, the opportunity to reflect on lesson implementations and designed units was critical. This process was enabled by research team members serving as thought partners. The findings underline the importance of creating equitable opportunities for teachers to learn through interaction with others who bring additional perspectives.
