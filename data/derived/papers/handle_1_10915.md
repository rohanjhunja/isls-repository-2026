# Redesigning Tabletop Games as a Culturally Sustaining Pedagogy in Canadian Middle School Classrooms

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We present our study of middle school students in Canada exploring tabletop game redesign as a culturally sustaining pedagogy. The teachers and the researchers co-designed the activities and data was collected on the students’ engagement with the activities and each other. We describe the design of activities and preliminary findings.

Our project is drawn from research on how learners engage creatively and critically with disciplines when playing and designing games, considering how tabletop games  are played across generations and reflect socio-cultural contexts and world     views. Tabletop games’ use of physical objects encourages communication beyond verbal or textual forms that suit players of different age s and linguistic backgrounds (Kim et al., 2023). We  present our study of how tabletop game redesign could help middle school learners with varying cultural backgrounds might see individual differences as resources for social learning (Ladson-Billings, 1995). Cultural practices and tabletop game redesign Tabletop games are and have been culturally signifi cant (Crist et al., 2016) and have often been adopt ed across cultures. In our past work on mathematics learning, we suggested that redesigning activities could enrich linguistic and cultural diversity (Kim et al., 2023) by allowi ng students to create their own ways of learning (C ivil, 2002). By redesigning, students collaborated with a common and deep understanding of the original game and its rul es. Students adopted rules of their cultural games and used their origins’ languages with friends. This pr ocess embodies the essence of culturally sustain ing pedagogies, i.e., the pursuit of cultural and l inguistic pluralism (Paris, 2012). We see cultures as meanings and prac tices inherited and formed individually and collect ively in social groups and believe that learners could bring in diverse cultural resources by our approach. ## Research design

The researchers worked with teachers at a culturally and linguistically diverse Canadian urban middle school. We started with co-design workshops in September 2022. To facilitate s tudent learning and our data collection, we created a model (see Figure 1) a nd a student workbook to support the redesign proce ss based on our previous work (Jaques et al., 2019). We used Forbidden Island® as a suitably cooperative game with simple rules. The codesign workshops were focused on exploring the ways  of thinking and learning in game redesign and how to integrate it with the teachers’ current teaching. W e collected data from two Grade 7 classes during bo th Humanities and Science/Mathematics from December 2022 to April 2023. We explored how the game redesign approach was impl emented and taken up by both the students and teachers in this project. Students were assigned to  groups by the teachers and then we selected groups  for data collection based on the group members’ informed consent. To maintain the anonymity of those students who had given consent, researchers kept the appearance of collecting data across all groups. At least one researcher visited their classes as a participant observes. Students d ocumented their ideas in workbooks, and the researc hers took photos and videos of their in-progress designs. Students showcased their final ga me design to their families and peers in a game night event. After this event, we conducted group interviews with students. Tabletop game redesign and culturally sustaining learning In the ongoing analysis we are engaged in thematic analysis to review our notes, classroom recordings, and learner artifacts (i.e., games, photos, workbooks). Our analysis aims to identify how students construct ways to draw and connect with their interests, cultural practices and experiences, and personal stories. We are also investigating how redesign ideas formed and evolved, and how we could better support this process. In this poster presentation, we will focus on three major themes that have been identified thus far. Slippage of multiple selves of students. Our findings show that students expressed their personal interests (e.g., game characters, their country of origin) and engaged in conversations related to their cultura l practices (e.g., women’s roles in their households) and stories from their extended families (e.g., an uncle’s difficult journey as a refugee), regardless of such conversations directly impacting game design. This slippage showed us a glimpse of their cultures, interests, and relationships within and beyond the school boundaries as they became part of their school life. One of the students, for example, was constantly at the boundary of being a fun friend and a serious student. He was playful with teammates, playing with toys and talking about video games, while he would talk in a mature way with the researchers and the teachers about their game designs. Inventiveness of students’ redesign process. Some students found completing the redesign workboo k a source of disengagement rather than a tool for brainstorming. Other students still engaged in brainstorming while diligently filling it out, while others came up with new methods of articulating their ideas, reflecting their interest in media production. For example, two students in a  group started recording videos of themselves inter viewing each other about their game design ideas while anot her group pretended doing a YouTube instruction vid eo for their game looking at our camera.

The role of the “intruders.” Our observations indicate that teachers or researchers’ uninvited visits could interrupt activities (regardless of their legitimac y). The classes had similar structures, but what ha ppened after their visits were starkly different depending on the adult’s pedagogical approach. When the students were deeply engaged in discussions of their ideas, the teacher/ researcher could interrupt their flow, regardless o f their intentions. On the other hand, an intervention from  the same goal-oriented adult helped other students who were not engaged to find new directions for their design. After interacting with these adults who focused o n listening, students often continued with their previous process and found new insights to improve their ideas.

## Discussion and conclusions

We are exploring how students invent ways to draw o n cultural practices and differences while redesign ing tabletop games. Individual students negotiate redesign ideas and learning topics while making sense of their own and each other’s interests, culture, and personalities. The preliminary analysis showed what might be difficult for teachers to know, such as students’ conversations r elated to their cultural practices, and what happen s when teachers and researchers introduce the ideas deemed  helpful to students. As participant observers, res earchers’ presence and occasional visits to groups’ work coul d also influence learners’ design and thinking. Not ably, researchers’ design involvement is a significant re sponsibility – we cherish the slippages that occur in  students’ chatter that may not find ways into their design, a nd their invention of new ways of learning, thinkin g, and designing. At the same time, we continue to design new structures to help students learn and engage with decades of established public education. Striking this delicate balance is an important agenda for our on-going work.
