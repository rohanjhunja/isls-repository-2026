# Constructing Relations Between STEM Researchers and Publics: A Case Study of Empathy in Action

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
How academic researchers engage with non-researchers is critical to supporting the public’s understanding of complex socio-scientific and sociotechnical challenges. In this paper, we examine the learning of one scientist, Murphy, in the STEM Liaison Program (SLP), a project that trained university STEM researchers to design public engagement events. Focusing on the ways “empathy” was represented in trainings and enacted by Murphy as a kind of emotional configuration (Vea, 2020), we track accompanying shifts in how Murphy saw youth in a residential treatment facility and his own role in conducting public engagement efforts with them. We argue that tracing the co-constitution of emotional configurations with “relationship schemas” (Polletta, 2020), cognitive and cultural representations of the nature of relationships and what obligations they entail, can expand the learning sciences’ understandings of relationships as a learning outcome with important societal implications.

Public understanding of scientific and social scientific phenomena is crucial for addressing massive public crises like climate change and pandemic diseases. How academic researchers engage with non-researchers is important to supporting people’s learning of these complex and collective problems. People are radically dependent on the actions of each other in approaching these problems. They require cooperative behavior and ways of seeing our dependent relations that support us to take action.  How we learn to be in relationship is a critical q uestion, and survival depends on how we collectively respond.

In this paper, we present a case study of the path way one scientist traveled with incarcerated youth towards realizing the capabilities members of the p ublic have for engaging with complex science. The s cientist was a participant in the STEM Liaison Program (SLP), a project that trained and supported university S TEM researchers to design public engagement events for groups underserved by informal STEM opportunities a nd institutions such as those who are incarcerated, people with disabilities, or seniors in constrained living situations. The program combined training in science communication methods with practices from human-centered design. In a human-centered design approach, designers enga ge in empathy-focused practices such as observation  and interviewing to gain understanding of the perspectives and needs of those for whom they are designing (Goldman & Zielezinski, 2021; Hasso Plattner Institute of De sign, 2013). With the incorporation of these empath etic practices, the program provided a novel approach to building relationships between STEM researchers and public groups around science exposure and understanding. T hrough the case study, we explore how a physical anthropologist named Murphy conceived of and shifted his relationships with non-scientists as he tried to bridge the gap between science and the public. We ask:

1. How does the STEM researcher come to enact and represent “empathy” in the design of his public

engagement activities?

2. What kinds of social relationships are cultivated through the design processes as he practiced them?

In the following sections, we provide background on  empathy and navigating it within STEM professional contexts, describe our approach to understanding emotion and the construction of relationships using sociocultural learning theory, articulate our methods in relation  to SLP, and present findings from the case study o f Murphy and his learning as a scientist doing outreach. Fin ally, we present implications from the case for the  learning sciences and public engagement efforts in STEM, and we discuss future directions for this research. Navigating empathy in STEM The word “empathy” has multiple meanings, but it ge nerally indicates a complex of feeling and knowing (Verducci, 2000). It involves a form of intentionality directed at the experiences of another (Stein, 1989). Empathy has been considered to have two major roles, one ep istemological and the other social and relational (de Vignemont & Singer, 2006; Song, 2015; Verducci, 200 0). Epistemologically, empathy provides a basis for intersubjective understanding of the experiences and subjective point of view of another person. This understanding aligns with an interpretivist epistem ology in which knowledge is constructed by people i n relationship with each other. In its relational and social role, empathy can be seen as a form of sharing of another ICLS2022 Proceedings 575 © ISLS person’s “inner experience,” fostering a sense of c onnection with the other. Empathy in this sense fac ilitates effective social communication and motivates cooperative and prosocial behavior.

The study of STEM disciplines is a social system it self (Latour & Woolgar, 1986). It is governed by norms that are socially and interactionally achieved, and that develop and change through their enactment. They are also powered and determine who gets to be where in relation to science and STEM (McDermott, 2013; Medin & Bang, 2014). Part of the norms of Western science  is what forms of emotion are considered appropriat e (Davidson et al., 2020). In the dominant, Western w hite ways in which STEM is instantiated in universi ty departments, empathy is seen as running counter to its practices and epistemologies. Hegemonic STEM epistemologies are positivist, holding that all tru e knowledge can be scientifically verified (empiric ism) or is capable of logical proof (rationalism) (Reynante et al., 2020). A discipline’s underlying epistemology or theory of knowledge has significant ramifications for how the discipline determines what counts as legitimate knowledge. We see this problematic as a central challenge both  of SLP in its preparation of STEM researchers for public engagement and for the larger question of relationa l development between STEM researchers and publics in approaching global sociotechnical and socio-scientific challenges. Learning sciences researchers have previously examined how learners in STEM can come to navigate across multiple epistemological systems (Bang & Medin, 2010; Rosebery et al., 2010). In this paper, we aim to build on this and similar work by focusing on the relational and emotional dynamics that are central to researchers’ navigation of empathy and STEM in SLP. Conceptual resources: Empathy as relational and emotional configuration Sociocultural theories of learning have long supported a softening of individual autonomy, figuring the individual within social relationships that support learning (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Vygotsky, 1978) and adopting u nits of analysis that extend beyond the individual learner (Engeström, 1999; Wertsch, 1998). An ongoing line of research examines how relations develop between actors in learning settings as a kind of learning outcome (Nasir & Hand, 2006). For example, DiGiacomo and Gutiérrez (2016) argued that relational equity, marked by more symmetric relations between teachers and learners, should be considered as “both a tool and an outcome of designed learning activities” (p. 141).

There is an opportunity to further explore the interplay of cognition and emotion in the achievement of relation as a learning outcome. In social movement studies, Polletta (2020) explored how “relationship schemas,” cultural and cognitive patterns that shape our expectations and norms for interacting with one another, shape what kinds of participation are possible. She describes the formation of relationships as a process of “relationship work” (p. 11)—a matter of “craft” and “invention” (p. 9).  A relationship schema is a constructed understandi ng of the ties between self and other that also carries with it implications for who we can be and how we can act together. In light of these bodies of work, we attend in our analyses to the ways being together and cultural materials support people’s developing understandings of how they are in relation to one another. Due to the specific focus on empathy in SLP, we als o place analytical focus on the role of emotion in these developing understandings. While many models of emotion in affective science assume that emotion is an internal and individual phenomenon, a sociocultural and social constructivist view of emotion is gaining traction (Clore & Ortony, 2013; Mesquita & Boiger, 2014). In  this view, emotions emerge from social interaction s and relationships, which they in turn constitute, shape, and change. They are also situated—embodied, enacted, and experienced in particular situations which give them particular definition and meaning. Therefore, while “empathy” has particular meanings in psychological research, we defer specialist definitions in favor of seeking how empathy is emotionally configured (Vea, 2020) within the specific cultural settings and practices of SLP. Just as relationship schemas point to the importance of attending to the relationship work that people do as they make sense of their commitments to one another, the notion of emotional configurations directs attention to the ways emotion becomes meaningful in the conduct of social activity. The ways humans configure emotion, such as how a concept like “empathy” becomes enacted in social practices and interactions, have implications for what is possible to achieve together. In this sense, both relation and emotion are processes and outco mes of learning. Furthermore, emotion and relation are co-constituting. How we see our relationships helps de termine how we feel toward one another, and how we come to feel for one another helps determine how we relate (Dewey, 1975; Jasper, 2011). As a result, the form that scientists’ designs for public engagement take emerge in part from the interplay of emotional configurations and relationship schemas that participants in social activity construct.

## Research context

We conducted research in SLP, an initiative that fa cilitated a multi-faceted engagement model for STEM researchers working with communities and people in the public who have historically lacked access to S TEM ICLS2022 Proceedings 576 © ISLS research and STEM researchers. The goals of SLP included helping STEM researchers gain new understandings of public groups, supporting public learning in STE M, and connecting public groups with STEM fields and researchers in new ways. In other words, SLP involved refiguring the social relationships between scientists and the public. The SLP was innovating a combination of  three engagement models: a model of science communication informed by learning research, an out reach mentorship structure to support researchers a s they engaged in the planning and execution of public engagement, and practices and principles from human-centered design. The human-centered design components of SLP  provided a structure, a set of practices, and ways  of thinking that supported the construction of a different kind of social arrangement between researchers and public groups. Human-centered design’s “empathy” focus encourages designers to see beyond their own expertise and understand what the situation looks like from someone else’s perspective. These insights can then help  the designer develop interventions (in this case, publi c engagement programming) that meet their needs. Hu mancentered design also entails taking a different emotional and relational stance compared to Western science. Each year, the SLP invited a cohort of university STEM researchers to participate in training and subsequent supports that covered aspects of the three models. Two of the three authors of this paper, along with other members of our research team, developed and l ed workshops on the use of human-centered design fo r developing public engagement in STEM. This training  included understanding the empathy basis of humancentered design and how making sense of empathy can  lead to more effective design outcomes than proces ses that center on designer expertise alone. The training began with a focus question: “How might we design engaging and effective science outreach events for non-tradi tional outreach participants, about whose life expe riences, interests, and concerns we know very little?” This question emphasized that public engagement in STEM is “for” the public and should serve their interests and nee ds. Yet it also emphasized that most STEM researche rs were starting from a position of incomplete understandin g about the public, and that building a better unde rstanding was key to designing successful public engagement.

Here we explore “empathy” as a kind of emotional configuration that brought together ways of feeling about members of the public with particular ways of  making sense of their needs and the goals of publi c engagement. This configuration also included sets of practices aimed at understanding the other, distilling what was learned, and acting on that understanding through design. “Empathy” was explained as a way to understand the situation of public engagement from the public’ s point of view. Enacting empathy in this context r equired “lingering in ambiguity,” immersing oneself in the problem space of trying to understand the needs of the public before jumping to design solutions. The training included  opportunities to practice developing and enacting empathy with people for whom the STEM researchers w ould be designing public engagement programs, first through a prerecorded video interview and then with in-person interviews that the STEM researchers themselves conducted. In attending to other people’s perspectives through these exercises, the researchers were encouraged to “Listen carefully,” “Ask questions about the per son’s ideas and experiences,” and “Never judge the person even if you do not agree.” In this way, the enactme nt of empathy included interactional practices and habits of thinking in regard to those for whom the STEM researchers were designing. Collecting information was only half the picture. After taking notes and accounting for what they had heard, the STEM researchers were trai ned in strategies for sense-making around the information they gathered. This process involved reflecting on what they learned and trying to distill synthetic statements about what the members of the public they heard fro m might need to accomplish through engaging with STEM. Thes e needs might be very different from the STEM researchers’ own goals for public engagement. Finally, the STEM researchers were explicitly encouraged to think beyond the lecture-and-Q&A format of public engagement and to develop ways of relating to public groups that were more equitable and multi-directional in the flow of expertise and knowledge. Data sources We report on research that examined the experience of the STEM researchers who participated during the SLP’s first two years. This analysis takes an in-depth ca se study approach aimed at examining the pathways a nd experiences of one researcher. A case study is an empirical inquiry that investigates a contemporary phenomenon within its real-life context by drawing upon multiple sources of evidence (Yin, 2013). The emphasis in performing case studies is primarily on interpreting the data (Stake, 1995). In this paper, we take a descriptive and exploratory approach, describing the data as they were generate d in context and developing explanatory proposition s for further inquiry (Yin, 2013). The goal was to deeply  understand the experience of a STEM researcher in the program. Case studies are generalizable to theoretical propositions, but not to populations of cases (Yin, 2013). The goal of a case study is not usually generalizat ion but rather particularization—emphasizing its un iqueness (Stake, 1995). We use the case of Murphy to help reveal the analytic contours of what is possible in shifting STEM researchers’ relationships with the public, revealing theoretic propositions for further study. Each case study subject was interviewed in a 20-40-minute interview prior to SLP training. They completed pre-program surveys, including open-ended written responses, that were administered to the entire cohorts. They were observed during the training, and observational field notes were written for each outreach event they completed. We recorded their initial meetings with program s taff in which each answered a question about their commitme nts to public engagement. Evaluation feedback was collected from the public participants at their eng agements, and the SLP researchers were asked to com plete a reflection survey within 24 hours of completing an engagement event. Each SLP researcher also participated in an end-of-program personal interview and a post-program survey. Analytical approach All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. We developed a preliminary list of codes conducting an open-coding process (Emerson et al., 2011; Strauss & Corbin, 1994) and a grounded approach to data analysis and theory building. To ensure reliab ility, we co-coded data, checked for any discrepanc ies, and resolved disagreements through discussion. Of particular interest in this paper were codes and data so urces that tracked the STEM researchers’ developing practices and representations of empathy in the preparation f or, execution of, and reflections about their public engagement activities. We paid close attention to constructions in language that documented the ongoing sense-making that STEM researchers were accomplishing about the role of empathy in developing relationships with public groups, how representations of empathy were related  to the work of STEM research, and how researchers’ constructions of STEM research itself shifted through the relational work of designing public engagements. In tracking h ow the emotional configuration of empathy was taken  up, we also attend to the ways its enactment was associ ated with shifts in relationship schemas, the cogni tive and cultural representations of the relationship between the STEM researchers and the public. We present the case of one participant, Murphy, because it is indicative of growth in understanding and change in thinking about what people in the public know about science and how to approach engaging the m. Rather than arguing that Murphy’s experience in the program was representative of all SLP participants, we make the case that his experiences illustrate processes of shifting relations in ways that can be helpful for theory building and future design work.

## Findings: Murphy and the public

Murphy was a graduate student in physical anthropology, studying the connection between genes and variation in the structure of brains of both humans and non-human primates. Based on prior experiences, Murphy had the idea that the general public has a very poor understanding of science, influenced by both his previous long career as a lawyer working with juries and his graduate studies in physical anthropology. During the pre-program interview, Murphy revealed a view of the general public as having poor understanding of science: “Because I came to science late in life, after another career… what I learned was—is that, um, people on juries—the level of thei r science knowledge is somewhat shockingly low.” Also before the SLP, Murphy participated in a science outreach program at a local museum where his experiences might have reinforced that thinking. Murphy surmised that the attendees of his public talk at the museum were not very enga ged because the adults did not ask many questions and they seemed to want to be entertained instead of to engage deeply with the science. When asked what he would think if the public at an engagement event had a great ex perience and were excited but left with inaccurate scientific understandings, Murphy shook his head back and forth: “Again, I sup pose it accomplishes something, it puts a human face on scientists, but I think that there’s…  yeah, that’s not good. Inaccurate information is n ot good. I think that that ought to be fundamental. Everything else ought to flow from that.” In the interview, Murphy said it was important to bridge the gap in public science knowledge: What’s happening in science today, especially in br oadly speaking the field of genetics is astounding—it’s changing people’s lives. And will do much more so in the future, over the next ten or 15 years. But really the gap between the regular person who’s not in science and what’s happening is becoming broader and broader and broader.

Speaking of his previous STEM engagement experiences, Murphy said describing complex science to people who “don’t know what cells are” made the talks ineffect ive. “People said, ‘Wow, that’s amazing,’ which I g uess is somewhat flattering, but I don’t know how much it accomplished.” ICLS2022 Proceedings 578 © ISLS Murphy’s enactments and representations of empathy Murphy’s thinking about the potential for the “public” to become engaged and think deeply about science shifted during his participation in the SLP. During his SLP  participation, Murphy held engagements in two resi dential centers for youth. One was a medium security juveni le justice center that aimed to prepare youth to re join the wider community. The second was a residential treat ment center for girls 12 to 17 years old where girl s enter based on referrals from the juvenile justice system or Child and Family Services. Murphy’s most prominent enactments of empathy occurred during his first SLP engagement event, when he learned about the teenagers and their interests. He reported a change in opinion and increased empathy about what teenagers knew and how to approach science wit h them after the visit. Murphy started by mentionin g that he was an anthropology student. Almost immediately,  a girl asked, “What is anthropology?” which led to  an explanation of the branches of anthropology and their differences. Murphy went on to show the youth a picture from the science lab where he worked, displaying th e equipment and explaining what it does. After movi ng to explain how DNA sampling works, more questions started coming. Surprising Murphy, the teenagers backtracked to asking about how being a “PhD student works.” Th ey expressed interest in Murphy’s previous career a s a lawyer, and they wanted to know how the move from that profession to being an anthropology student happened. Murphy then moved into asking the youth questions: “What is a gene? Do you know what an MRI is? Have you had one?” Murphy explained brain structure s and asked them how brains recover from trauma. Th e youths’ responses showed quite a bit of brain knowledge. This line of questions sparked a girl to ask about how brain changes can “make a person act differently, b e more susceptible to drug or alcohol addiction.” T he youth also raised questions about Zika virus and microcep haly and how viruses can affect the brain and brain development. These questions and tangents had the participants involved. After Murphy answered them, he forged ahead to describe a set of topics more closely related to his personal work: different brain structures, the functions of neurons, risk and reward, different reactions of mice to stimuli, optogenetics, and algae. In each case, Murphy presented information, asked questions, and was ask ed questions. Finally, Murphy showed a demo of a ch ance and risk game that a few students got to play. All told, Murphy covered at least twelve sub-topics. Th e youth raised another six or seven topics. They asked a steady stream of questions that Murphy answered even when the questions were related to connections the youth were making instead of those that Murphy had planned to present. This experience was surprising for Murphy. Speaking  of it in his post-SLP interview he said, “They ask ed questions constantly throughout the whole thing, a few of them did. If anything, it was... I would have guessed I would have been asked three or four questions. I was probably asked 30 or 40.” On the post-engagement survey regarding the first event Murphy wrote about what he learned about the youth in the facility and how he would apply his new empathic understanding in his future public engagements: I addressed some topics that I thought would be a big hit and I was wrong, but I’ll learn from it all. These are young people that respond well when the subject matter is made directly applicable to their lives and their futures. I woul d be better prepared to address their career in science-related questions, and I would make more ex plicit the connections between different aspects of the presentation. The highlights were each of the several occasions when a significant portion of the students were amazed by the subject matter I was addressing. In the way Murphy spoke and wrote about his experie nce in this event, we can see him enacting the emot ional configuration of “empathy” presented in the SLP training in particular ways. Though he did not attempt to meet and learn about the teenagers before his first engagement event, he made note of his surprise at the questions they asked and how they wanted the science to be “made d irectly applicable to their lives and their futures.” In the statements he made about what he “would be better prepared to address” in a future event, we see evidence of his sense-making about these young people and what it meant for productive public engagement. Murphy’s shifting relations with the public Murphy treated this first SLP engagement as a proto type to learn from and to help him redesign the nex t engagement at the second youth facility, with 15 girls in attendance. We examine the second engagement event and Murphy’s discussion of it to understand how his relations with youth in residential facilities were shifting. In this session Murphy brought physical models of the brain and showed them to the youth, passing them around so they could feel them. Murphy started by explaining the career change from law to studying anthropology. Next, Murphy showed a slide on “what is anthropolog y,” explaining what the word means then outlining t he different branches of the discipline. Finally, when  he got to biological anthropology, Murphy talked a bout the components of the brain, DNA, and genes. A girl asked a question about genetic traits, and Murphy talked about ICLS2022 Proceedings 579 © ISLS eye color and height. Murphy showed an MRI image, c ompared it to a physical model of the brain, and pa ssed around a cast of a homo habilis skull. During this time, Murphy fielded questions. Murphy then moved to discuss the specific gene of interest to his research and s howed videos of mice activity. The students asked q uestions about the use of mice in research. Murphy showed them how to take a DNA swab from the cheek. The participants offered explanations and asked questions through th e talk and demonstration. Murphy concluded again wi th a game about chance and risk.

Murphy left this second event feeling like the girls were engaged, even though not everyone understood every piece of the material. From the event itself and from Murphy’s discussion of it, we analyze several ways in which the relations between himself as a science communicator and the girls in the facility were shifting. In his post-program interview, Murphy described how he had  learned from the previous engagement event to plandifferently for the second one: After the first one, I had a lot of ideas myself. Now at least I had some experience to know what worked and what didn’t. The addition of these things that I passed around I think was important. I focused more on things people could relate to. Just that your brain weighs about three pounds and it’s kind of a flimsy thing if you tried to hold it in your hand and it would leak through your fingers. Just things to make it more real.

In the first event, the girls had asked about brain changes that might make people more susceptible to addiction, and Murphy saw these questions as evidence that the girls were trying to understand what studying the brain could tell them about their own experiences. The model br ain he brought to the second event was another thin g he thought “people could relate to.” Having learned that adolescents had some grounding questions at the beginning of the first engagement, Murphy also began the seco nd one with those very questions: What is anthropol ogy? What does it mean to be a Ph.D. student? Murphy’s d esign for the second event further involved a diffe rent relation to the girls in the way he positioned his own goals. In the post-program interview, we asked: Could you talk just a little bit in general about how you fou nd a balance between your goals and your interests,  what you wanted to communicate, and the needs and interests of the youth who were there? He responded: I hope I abandoned my goals on the second one. I think that that’s one of the wonderful things about this. I don’t have a curriculum. I don’t have to make sure I’m leaving no student behind or any of this or that they’re going to do well on standardized testing. This is a tremendous advantage. You can go there, try to stay flexible, and be responsive to tell people what they’re interested in. […] You also respond to questions and listening to the question carefully will take you a long way… I tried to be more responsive and to improvise more. We take Murphy’s words as evidence of a shift in hi s relationship with the public. He began to differentiate his relationship to the public in the SLP from the relationship between instructors and students. While Murphy kept key science messages in mind, he emphasized that adapting to the participants’ particular questions and interests was the heart of his approach for the second and fu ture engagements. His new relationship carried dist inct expectations for his actions. His evolving relationship with the public required him to listen “carefully” to what people say and to be willing “to improvise more” rather than follow a unidirectional lesson plan. Relating to the public required that Murphy position his knowledge and practice more humbly. Murphy also mentioned in the post-program interview that going into these events he did not much “like” teenagers. We see evidence that Murphy improved his  regard for the youth at the first engagement, whic h then became consequential in the second engagement. On t he post-engagement survey for the second engagement, Murphy wrote that he saw the girls as “engaged and attentive, mostly” and that they expressed “their appreciation at the end.” At the same time, he saw how “the [gir ls’] participation can be encouraged by asking ques tions, answering questions even if off-topic and altering the presentation outline as participant areas of interest emerge.” Murphy’s perception of the girls as “engaged and at tentive” contrasts with his earlier dislike of teen agers. His remarks also indicate a recognition that the qualities of the girls’ participation were not innate to them, but rather at least partially a result of the way he related with them. Murphy sees his own role in fostering an engaged and interactive atmosphere, including altering the plan  as participants’ interests changed during the even t. Murphy came to see the adolescents as engaged, curious, and insightful learners who thought scientifically and critically about the content at hand. Through interacting with them, Murphy learned about their knowledge, interests, and identities and seemed to have come away with a new appreciation for adolescents in terms of who they a re and their capabilities. He recalled their “stunningly i nsightful” questions about genotype heritability, g enes’ effects on risky behavior, and the role of nature versus nu rture. He said these questions “really illustrated a kind of ICLS2022 Proceedings 580 © ISLS scientific thinking in my mind, and these are people who had gotten it and were trying to move a step ahead.” He also saw them as “better questions, by the way, than I got when I did my four […] presentations up at the museum.” One of the goals of the SLP training was to emphasi ze the role that empathy could play in STEM communication (e.g., learner-centered activities, understanding the needs and interests of the public, and finding points of personal connection with non-scientists),  and Murphy’s experience seems to have provided an opportunity for growth in empathy. Responding to the question about the SLP’s human-centered design training on empathy, Murphy said, “It helped me focus on the needs and desires of the participants, rather than on what I may think is important.”

## Discussion and conclusions

Murphy’s case illustrates what may be possible in shifting STEM researcher’s relations with the public. He came into the SLP thinking that members of the public had poor scientific understandings and that the most important thing in science communication was to deliver accurate information. In his first public engagement event in the SLP, Murphy’s experience went off-course from his plans. He was surprised by the youths’ interest in his personal trajectory and the overall sophistication of their questions. He worked to derive insights from his em pathy experience in that first event and apply them in the design of the second event, in which he focused m uch more on grounding the presentation in aspects he thought the youth would relate to. He also tried to relate in ways that were more responsive to emergent interest and to be  improvisational. The experience ultimately shifted  how Murphy regarded the youth in the residential facilities as scientific, intelligent, and engaged. From this case, we highlight several areas of sign ificance for the learning sciences. First, while empathy has particular technical definitions in psychologic al and affective research, the case shows the impor tance of attending to how empathy takes shape as an emotiona l configuration, socially achieved through the fram ing enacted in the SLP training and in Murphy’s uptake and enactment of empathy’s practices and ways of thinking about the other in his public engagement events. We see evidence of Murphy’s attunement over time to how the youth he encountered, despite being in a residential treatment facility, were engaged scientific thinkers who were interested in what biological anthropology might have to say about their life experiences and futures.  We argue that the emotional configuration of empathy in the SLP provided structures for Murphy’s thinking and p ractice that enabled him to improve his regard for these youth and for members of the public more generally. This case is also instructive as an example of how emotional configurations can be instructive outside the stron gly politicized social movement context in which the theory was initially developed (Vea, 2020). Second, building more generous interpretations of the youth and other members of the public was associated in this analysis with shifting ways for Murphy of seeing himself in relation the public. In  his early interviews and surveys, he emphasized the central importance of providing correct scientific facts to uninformed publics. He saw himself as the holder of knowledge that should be transmitted to passive audiences. In contrast, by the end of the SLP, Murphy was articulating a revised relationship schema of public engagement. In his new understanding, he saw his role as connecting with what people see as meaningful to their lives—something that could not occur in a unidirectional transmission of  his expertise to the public. Rather, this new rela tionship required Murphy to take up new ways of practicing in relation to the public. He saw the need to let go of his own agenda, be responsive to emergent interests, and im provise. If relationship schemas involve expectatio ns and norms for how people should interact together (Poll etta, 2020), the emotional configuration of empathy  in the SLP helped Murphy shift his expectations for his own behavior as a STEM communicator. We see these shifts as productive for societal needs. Meeting collective sociotechnical and socio-scientific challenges will require STEM researchers to relate with public groups in new ways, and focusing public engagement work on what members of the public see as relevant to their lives and futures can help all of us work together toward collective solutions. We see the potential for future work in the learni ng sciences to continue to analyze the relationship between emotional configurations and relationship s chemas to understand how they co-constitute one ano ther over the course of social interaction and unfolding activity. Interaction analytical approaches (Jordan & Henderson,

2011) may be especially useful for building on the work presented here by illuminating the moment-to-moment

ways that people work together to shift their understandings of each other and their obligations to one another’s wellbeing.
