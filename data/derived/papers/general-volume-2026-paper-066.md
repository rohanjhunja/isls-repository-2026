Developing Digital Humanities Pedagogical Content Knowledge Nichole Nomura, University of Wyoming, nnomura@uwyo.edu Abstract: This research investigates the pedagogical affordances of digital humanities learning outcomes using co-design, classroom research, and qualitative interviewing. My work examines how visualizations of literary texts facilitate computational and literary thinking in English Language Arts classrooms and can cultivate data skepticism. While the field of digital humanities has developed pedagogical practices for training future digital humanists, less attention has been paid to how digital humanities methods, especially computational text analysis, can support learning outcomes in other disciplines. This research statement overviews my work in education at the intersection of digital humanities, English Language Arts, and data science, exploring how computational Digital humanities pedagogy for humanists and data science My research in this area centers on the pedagogical opportunities afforded by digital humanities methods, especially digital humanities methods related to text, and their implementation outside of digital humanities contexts. I study the ways in which digital humanities pedagogy can be leveraged in support of other disciplines’ learning outcomes, including traditionally humanist learning outcomes and data science learning outcomes. My work is deeply indebted to constructionism (Papert, 1993) to understand critical making within digital humanities pedagogy, and it is committed to the principles of data feminism (D’Iganzio & Klein, 2020). Since 2024, I have worked on Building STEM Skills by Integrating Data Literacy and Text Analytics in English Language Arts (National Science Foundation Grant No. 2241483). The project aims to develop and research the potential of integrated data literacy and text analytics instruction in middle school English Language Arts (ELA). Through co-design, classroom research, and participatory professional development with practicing English Language Arts teachers, we developed and tested text analytics tools, visualizations, and pedagogical techniques for use in ELA middle school classrooms. Within this project, I frequently find myself in the role of translator—working to bridge digital humanities, data science, and ELA concepts—and I have worked on pedagogical design, classroom research using video analysis, instrument design for qualitative interviews, and data analysis. We find that visualizations built on human-readable scales of text (e.g. a single novel, short story, or poem) afford productive computational and literary thinking, with students able to challenge, revise, and understand the computations using textual evidence and their own linguistic-experiential reservoirs (Nomura et al, 2025).

Developing this kind of pedagogical content knowledge also requires surfacing the existing meaningmaking protocols students use when looking at visualizations of text—including their misconceptions, assumptions, and critical frameworks. My work, therefore, seeks to understand—through experimental conditions and through close reading of digital-humanities argument—what happens when an expert or when a student looks at a visualization of literary text. Existing work on graph comprehension highlights that domain knowledge interacts with graph literacy (Freedman & Shah, 2002, Shah & Hoeffner, 2002)—it is likely, therefore, that literary-critical and linguistic knowledge interact with graph comprehension for visualizations of text. We have adapted existing graph literacy instruments (e.g. Lai et al, 2016) to see if students interpret frequency differently when literature is involved, or if they understand ratios of lines in a play differently than ratios of seeds that sprouted. Our preliminary findings suggest that students do draw on literary funds of knowledge when they interact with text-based visualizations. In addition to supporting more robust humanities pedagogy, this work also seeks to further develop and understand the mechanisms by which humanities data practices support broader data skepticism—the development of perspectives that consider the subjunctivities, contexts, and diverse meanings of data (D’Iganzio & Klein, 2020; Wild & Pfannkuch, 1999)—in data science and statistics education. How is working with text, especially literary text, different from working with other kinds of data in learning environments? What kinds of pedagogical content knowledge can we develop to support more robust data skepticism in both teachers and students?

In all of my classroom-based work, I explore the possibilities of minimal computing (Risam & Gil,

2022) for digital humanities pedagogy—recognizing that computing under conditions of constraint may be not

o nly necessary, but beneficial. My recent work in this vein includes an analysis of how erasure poetry can be a low-tech way to teach the relationship between text-mining and text-encoding (Messarra & Nomura, 2025). This approach, which blends the calls of data feminism to visceralize data (D’Ignazio & Bhargava, 2019) with the pedagogy of Tukey’s Exploratory Data Analysis (1977) and Papert’s constructionism (1993), has implications for humanities pedagogy, data science pedagogy, and digital humanities pedagogy at both the secondary and postsecondary level.

Future work I plan to build on my work looking at the outputs of digital humanities research by diving into deeper questions of algorithmic and computational literacy, focusing on methods of operationalizing by hand or with a computer. In particular, I want to know how digital methods and tools can change, support, and develop metacognition in humanist rules of notice and signification (Rabinowitz, 1986) and literary-critical epistemic cognition (Lee et al,

2016) in specifically pedagogical contexts. Professional digital humanities practice has done significant work in

the development of such rules to the ends of research-related utility, and some work in the vein of pedagogy designed to train future digital humanists, but less in the area of pedagogical utility for other learning outcomes. I hypothesize that the act of operationalizing a literary-critical concept, and the work of moving back and forth between computational outputs and text, can help students develop better humanist and algorithmic metacognition.

The work described in this paper has been on visualizations that are built on roughly-human readable scales—a single novel, a short story, a poem, a collection. Following calls to develop pedagogy that supports postdigital literary literacy, including corpus and database literacy (McLeandavies et al, 2020), I have piloted some work aiming to bridge these scales with those of professional digital humanities, including experimental protocols to surface the differences between single-text and corpus-level visualization for unfamiliar text. This work will help us better connect secondary and postsecondary digital humanities pedagogy, supporting more robust pipelines in humanities disciplines.

Fostering Agentic and Actionable Collaborative Discourse in Analytics-Enhanced Learning Environments Yeonji Jung, Texas A&M University, yjjung@tamu.edu Abstract: My research explores how analytics-informed learning environments can foster agentic and actionable collaborative discourse in higher education. Drawing on sociocultural and dialogic theories, I conceptualize discourse as a core mechanism of knowledge coconstruction and epistemic agency as learners’ capacity to initiate inquiry, build on peer ideas, and shape shared understanding. Using a design-based, mixed-methods approach, I integrate computational discourse analysis with participatory design to trace epistemic engagement and develop analytics-based interventions that scaffold learners’ discursive moves. Projects such as EntryPoint illustrate how analytics-based scaffolds and AI partners can support dynamic participation trajectories and amplify student voice. Ongoing work advances temporal modeling of discourse, expands human-centered analytics tools, and promotes inclusive design for AIsupported collaboration. This agenda contributes to computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) by advancing theory, methods, and tools that center learner agency and dialogic engagement in dynamic, data-rich learning environments.

## Overview

My research lies at the intersection of CSCL, learning analytics, and human-AI interaction, with a central focus on how analytics-informed learning environments can foster agentic and actionable collaborative discourse. Grounded in sociocultural and dialogic theories of learning (John-Steiner & Mahn, 1996; Stahl et al., 2014), I view discourse not simply as evidence of learning but as the core mechanism through which learners articulate ideas, negotiate meaning, engage in knowledge co‑creation, and construct shared understanding (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2014; Van Aalst, 2009). A foundational commitment in my work is the conceptualization and scaffolding of epistemic agency, defined as learners’ capacity to initiate inquiry, articulate ideas, take up peers’ contributions, and shape the trajectory of joint intellectual work (Damşa et al., 2010). My research examines how epistemic agency is enacted and constrained in learning experiences mediated by analytics-based scaffolds and AI-powered agents, and how carefully designed interventions can support dialogically rich and agentic participation. To advance this agenda, I pursue two interrelated lines of inquiry, each reflecting a distinct orientation to learning analytics in CSCL contexts: (1) using learning analytics techniques as an analytical lens to model the structural, semantic, and temporal dynamics of discourse; and (2) designing and implementing analytics-informed interventions that serve as learning partners to scaffold dialogic participation and epistemic moves. Together, these lines of work position learners not only as recipients of information but as epistemic agents who actively shape knowledge co-construction in collaboration with both human and artificial partners. Through this framing, my research explores how analytics-informed interventions can be designed and implemented to support agentic and dialogically rich participation in authentic collaborative learning settings. Theoretical approach My research is grounded in sociocultural and dialogic theories of learning, which emphasize the social, situated, and mediated nature of meaning-making. Learning is conceptualized as a collaborative process of knowledge building in which understanding emerges through interaction with others, tools, and cultural artifacts (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2014). In this view, discourse is not merely a vehicle for sharing ideas, but a generative space through which knowledge is co-constructed, contested, and transformed (Oshima et al., 2012; Van Aalst, 2009). Within this theoretical framing, collaborative discourse is positioned as the central mechanism of learning in CSCL contexts. Building on foundational work in collaborative knowledge building (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006; Van Aalst, 2009), my research examines how learners pose problems, elaborate ideas, and engage in sustained inquiry within discourse communities, as well as how dialogic exchanges foster shared understanding and collective cognitive responsibility. A central construct anchoring my research is epistemic agency, defined as learners’ ability to take initiative, contribute substantively, and shape the direction of joint inquiry during discourse (Damşa et al., 2010; Nieminen & Ketonen, 2024). Epistemic agency is conceptualized as a distributed and emergent property, co-constructed through participation in sociomaterial activity systems. This perspective foregrounds how agency is enacted not only through individual cognition but also through relational, temporal, and material conditions within learning environments.

As collaborative learning increasingly unfolds in analytics-enhanced environments, my research extends these theoretical commitments to account for emerging sociotechnical actors. Specifically, my studies examine how AI agents (Jung, in preparation), analytics-based suggestion systems (Jung & Wise, 2025), and orchestration tools (Booth et al., Under Review) mediate learners’ epistemic positioning, dialogic moves, and participation trajectories during knowledge co-construction. Within a design-based research framework, these perspectives guide the development of analytics-informed interventions that scaffold learners’ discursive engagement (Jung et al., 2026). For example, tools such as EntryPoint (a discourse suggestion system for social annotation) are designed to prompt re-engagement, introduce epistemic contrast, and support temporal coordination, enhancing learners’ capacity to participate meaningfully in discourse (Jung et al., 2026). These interventions are grounded in the belief that well-designed technologies can amplify dialogic space, invite student initiative, and sustain epistemic engagement over time. Ultimately, this theoretical approach bridges CSCL’s foundational emphasis on discourse and knowledge co-construction with the emerging realities of AI-augmented learning environments such as MACIE (a real-time multi-agent orchestration engine for idea creation; Booth et al., Under Review). This provides a lens for critically examining how learners navigate human-AI collaboration, how tools can be designed to support dialogic learning, and how epistemic agency can be sustained within increasingly complex sociotechnical ecologies.

Methodological approach To investigate collaborative learning in analytics-enhanced environments, my research adopts a design-based, mixed-methods approach that integrates computational learning analytics with qualitative and participatory design requires both fine-grained temporal analysis of interactional processes and context-sensitive insight into learners’ perspectives, routines, and participation trajectories. At the core of my methodological approach is computational discourse analysis, used to trace how learners co-construct knowledge over time. Techniques such as natural language processing (NLP) and social network analysis are employed to examine how ideas emerge, evolve, and dissipate in digital discourse spaces. These methods enable the identification of patterns of epistemic engagement (e.g., anchoring, elaboration, and integration) as they unfold in environments such as asynchronous social annotation and generative co-authoring tools (Jung & Lee, 2026). In addition, my research employs temporal analysis to unpack the sequential nature of discourse moves. For example, prior work has examined how learners respond to analytics-informed suggestions, take up peer ideas, and shift participation roles in relation to analytic scaffolds (Jung et al., 2026). These analyses support the development of temporal models of learning that move beyond static indicators to capture evolving epistemic trajectories across time and context.

Complementing these computational approaches, my research incorporates human-centered design methodologies to develop and evaluate analytics-informed interventions. Through co-design workshops, prototype testing, and participatory evaluation, my studies actively engage learners in shaping systems such as EntryPoint (Jung & Wise, under review). These activities surface students’ needs, values, and concerns regarding analytics-mediated learning and ensure alignment between theoretical commitments and lived experience. Additional methods, including think-aloud protocols, reflective interviews, and interaction trace analysis, are also used to examine how learners interpret and appropriate analytics-based scaffolds in practice (Jung & Wise, 2025). Across projects, my research aims to develop methodological frameworks that are both rigorous and responsive, capable of capturing the complexities of human-analytics interaction, the situated nature of collaborative discourse, and the pedagogical considerations of analytics-mediated learning. Future plans My future research will advance analytics-informed interventions that support epistemic agency and actionable participation through three interrelated directions. First, my work will deepen temporal modeling of epistemic activity by leveraging NLP-based semantic and trajectory modeling to examine how learners co-develop ideas over time with peers and AI agents. This direction aims to move beyond static metrics toward responsive analytics that support real-time scaffolding of dialogic participation. Second, my research will expand the design and implementation of analytics-informed interventions that are personalized, context-aware, and aligned with learners’ routines. Building on systems such as EntryPoint and MACIE, future work will develop and test adaptive scaffolds that respond to discourse dynamics and support re-engagement, epistemic contrast, and sustained inquiry in authentic learning contexts. Third, my research will extend into curricular innovation by integrating analyticsinformed learning into educational data science and AI literacy initiatives, particularly for non-STEM learners.
