# Whole Scientist Education in an Authentic-Inquiry Laboratory

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This report argues for broader implementation of authentic-inquiry courses defined by Buck’s rubric of inquiry as those in which undergraduates pursue their own research goals and process. Addressing this gap in implementation may prepare students for careers as self-directed and transformative researchers by facilitating multidimensional learning outcomes. This study assesses student outcomes from authentic inquiry using a Whole Scientist framework encompassing autonomy, belonging, competence, and purpose. Seven consenting students released the recordings from 15-minute semi-structured qualitative interviews conducted during the final week of the semester. I de-identified and deductively analyzed transcripts for evidence of Whole Scientist learning outcomes and found participants consistently reported outcomes across all domains. The potential of authentic inquiry to facilitate transformative intellectualism is discussed. Broader implementation and assessment of authentic inquiry is recommended.

## Introduction

A diverse, competent, collaborative, science workforce empowered to manifest social change is needed to tackle the problems of the 21 st century (Committee on the Call to Action for Science Educat ion et al., 2021; vanden Besselaar et al., 2012; Burgess & Patterson William s, 2022). To meet these workforce goals, I argue for augmentation of laboratory course-based research education with authentic inquiry, de fined by Buck’s rubric  as that in which the problem/question, theory/backgrou nd, procedures/design, results, communication, and conclusions are not provided by the instructor (Buck et al., 2008). Research education, or experiences that scaffold student participation in research and research practices, is essential to student development as a scientist. Involvement in research predicts for success in STEM (Chang et al., 2014; Graham et al., 2013)  illuminating their potential as a mechanism to reta in minoritized students (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and M edicine, 2017). Undergraduate research experiences (UREs) are the traditional  mechanism of involving undergraduates in research. UREs take a situated learning apprenticeship approach by including individual or small groups of undergraduates in an existing expert’s research program, typically a faculty member ’s. Therefore, UREs exist outside the formal undergraduate curricu lum. However, UREs are insufficiently scalable and thereby exclusionary (Bangera & Brownell, 2014). Students who cannot access a URE rely on the formal curriculum to devel op research competencies. Laboratory education is a required component of formal science, technology, engineering, and mathematics  (STEM) curriculum that aims to facilitate application of scientific knowledge t o practice. Traditionally, lab education relies on a confirmation model in which the process and goals are pre-defined. These courses provide students with step-by-step protocols and verify their results against a known outcome. These labs do not seek to investigate a knowledge gap  nor do they allow students to make decisions. Confirmation labs are the subject of extensive critique, and two coursebased innovations in laboratory education aim to address the limitations of confirmation labs: c ourse-based undergraduate research experiences (CUREs) and inquiry-based learning (Weaver et al., 2008). Dolan (2016) defines a CURE as whole classes of students address ing a research question or problem with unknown outcomes or solutions that are of inte rest to external stakeholders, typically the scientific community. Like UREs, CUREs are associated with faculty-led research. Thus CUREs expand access to research opportunities, including for students who are underrepresented or may not seek out research on their own (Dolan, 2016). CUREs are a key development in  evidence-based laboratory education as demonstrated by superior outcomes for students compared  to confirmation-based labs. Participation in (C)UREs predicts for retention in STEM, probably  because CURE students engage in active learning, collaborate wit h peers, network with faculty/experts, apply content knowledge to real-world problems, and demonstrate self-efficacy with activities that map directly onto the critical competencies of scientists (Dolan & Weaver, 2021). Engaging in these authentic activities of a scientist helps facilitate cognitive, social, and affective outcomes that strongly track with persistence in STEM (Dolan, 2016; Lockhart et al., 2022).

In comparison, i nquiry-based learning is an umbrella term for a spectrum o f classroom experiences intended to involve students in the processes of in quiry. Historically, use of the term “inquiry” is i nconsistently applied, inspiring Buck and colleagues’ development of a rubric to distinguish inquiry across a spectrum of levels (Buck et al., 2008). Each level is distinguished by degree of structure,  or whether the problem/question, theory/background, procedures/design, results, communication, and conclusions are provided by the instructor or open to a degree of student choice (Table 1). Level 0 refers to the absence of inquiry, exemplified by confirmation labs described above. Level ½, or structured inquiry, is exemplified by confirmation labs in which students may draw conclusions not provided in the lab manual and communicate them. At Level 1, or guided inquiry, students design methods, conclusions, and communication usin g a provided problem and procedures. In Level 2, or  open inquiry, students have some choice in which procedures or study design to pursue in order to arrive at an unknown result. It is only in Level 3, termed authentic inquiry, th at students are able to select the research direction  and associated theory and background. The typical level of student intellectual  ownership within a CURE would categorize them at level 1 or 2  (Dolan, 2016), though others argue CUREs may span a  wider range  of inquiry (Brownell & Kloser, 2015).

## Conclusions Provided Open Open Open Open

A significant gap remains in experiences that allow students to determine research direction. (C)URE at most may choose a sub research question within a broader research question defined by faculty (Dolan, 2016). In fact, a guide on designing and implementing CUREs direc ts instructors to predetermine course research goals— ideally in connection to an existing research labor atory (Dolan & Weaver, 2021)  to ensure students’ results are relevant to the scientific community. Further, in a  2008 survey of 22 laboratory course manuals and nea rly 400 experiments, zero authentic-inquiry labs were identified  (Buck et al., 2008). Since then, others have applied Buck’s rubric to assess level of inquiry within the ir own discipline and have identified no authentic inquiry labs (Ryker & McConnell, 2017). Several papers report positive outcomes from discipline-specific authentic inquiry. For example, gains in competence were reported among students af ter an authentic inquiry geology laboratory (Plenge et al., 2022). However, others who report implementing authentic inquiry implicitly or explicitly conflate it with open or guided inquiry (Goodwin et al., 2021; Srisawasdi, 2012; Wu et al.,  2021). Indeed, others argue authentic inquiry is not efficacious (Brownell & Kloser, 2015; Goodwin et al., 2021) or practical (Almeida Bauer Zytkuewisz et al., 2023; Nadelson, 2009). Conclusively, genuine student choice in research direction is rare. A couple studies indicate consequences of research ed ucation in which faculty determine the research direction. A study comparing a CURE and a traditional lab fo und no significant differences between degree of student agency over control and design of the resea rch project nor personal connections to scientific inquiry (Hanauer & Dolan, 2014). The authors interpret these findings as a reflection of the reality that in most (researchbased) lab courses projects are assigned rather tha n student-defined (Hanauer & Dolan, 2014). This curricular norm corresponds with a paper modeling minority STE M student persistence; this paper poses a compliance-based definition of student self-efficacy that, in conjunction with internalization of the values of the STEM social system, strongly predicts persistence  (Estrada et al., 2011). Given that research faculty and STEM professionals of highest power/status are more likely to be membe rs of dominant groups relative to the student bodie s and members of the broader public they serve (National Center for Science and Engineering Statistics Directorate for Social, Behavioral and Economic Sciences, 2023), unequal relegation of power between faculty and stu dents in determining course-based research goals is maintained across racialize d, gendered, and classed lines.  Moreover, a compliance imperative is in tension with the para digm of diversifying STEM that insists radical chan ge for social justice must target the status quo rather than solely expect historical ly marginalized students to comply or change their mindset to actualize as scientists (Suárez & Beatty, 2022; Thiem & Dasgupta, 2022). Indeed, students leave STEM not because they lack competence  or interest but because they reject the  STEM environment (Ong et al., 2011; Trujillo & Tanner, 2014). The compliance-inclusion tension implies a limitation to the recen t developments in lab education in which power to direct research is still held by faculty. Here, I argue not for eradication and replacement of comp liance-based STEM research education with an entirely student-driven model in which faculty fully foreclose on their power and expertise. Rather, I argue for supplementation of current research-education e fforts with more experiences where students share decision-making power for research direction with peers and faculty. Therefore, this project aims to contribute to expansion of research education, specifically targeting cohor ts of interdisciplinary STEM undergraduate s from non-dominant social groups, by implementing and studying a new authentic-inquiry laboratory course.

## Theoretical framework

Given the centrality of shared power to this work, the course studied herein is grounded in critical p edagogy (Freire, 2000). A critical pedagogical approach  encapsulates student-instructor partnership in learning and capacity-building, a critical perspective toward oppressive power structures, and empowerment of students to apply learning to personally meaningful social chan ge. Previous a pplications of critical pedagogy to science education have 1) facilitated transformative intellectu alism, i.e. student application of scientific knowledge to meaningful social change (Morales-Doyle, 2017) and 2) disrupted normative knowledge/power relationalities in classrooms (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020). This theoretical framework grounds this paper’s support for authentic inquiry, a research education approach in which stu dents possess power to direct the research goals and process. Because of its support for student choice and power, authentic inquiry may deliver on four hallmarks of equitable and just research education: providing space for students to pursue socially relevant goals (Dasgupta et al., 2022), be agents of change within STEM (Morales-Doyle, 2017), and belong to a community of learners (Rogoff, 1994) while still achieving standardized research competencies that prepare them for successful futures in STEM. These hallmarks inform definition and analysis of learning in this study. Learning is defined from what I term a Whole Scientist perspective, adopting from Whole Child (Diamond, 2010) and Whole Teacher (Chen & McCray, 2012) frameworks of learning which simultaneously position multidimensional outcomes as the goal of academic/professional learning. Efficacy of holistic learning models in science education is empirically grounded. Namely, Self-Determination Theory (SDT; Ryan & Deci, 2000)  demonstrates how multi-need-supportive learning environments enhance student success  (Núñez & León, 2015). SDT identifies fundamental needs for competence, relatedness, and autonomy  (Ryan & Deci, 2000, 2020; Baumeister & Leary, 1995). Given the importance of social change within critical pedagogy, I also include a fourth need: purpose (Yeager et al., 2014). As such, I define four Whole Scientist learning dim ensions: autonomy, competence, belonging, and purpo se. Definitions and mechanisms of learning are taken from the  literature and displayed in Table 2. These four dimensions map directly onto the four hallmarks of equitable and just research education identified above. My research question is, Does an authentic-inquiry laboratory course facilitate Whole Scientist outcomes? ## Methods

Institutional Context:  This initial cross-sectional study is part of a 3-year investigation of how choice impacts inquiry-lab students. I designed the authentic-inquiry course in partnership with students during a 2020-2021 pilot program and offered  it in 2022, leveraging the students as partners paradigm (Mercer-Mapstone et al., 2017). I teach this course through a 4-year interdisciplinary science certificate program at a large R1 university in New England. The certificate program integrates several evidence-based teaching practices: autonomy-supportive, collaborative, active-learning experiences applied to social good and emb edded within a learning community (Trujillo & Tanner, 2014; Thiem & Dasgupta, 2022; Lawrie et al., 2017). Curricular Context:  This course takes a guide on the side  mentorship approach to facilitate  student progress through the stages of authentic inquiry. Specifically, students collabora tively identify societal health problems suitable for scientific investigation, for m affinity teams around shared interest, perform a literature review, identify a knowledge gap, write a research question, design a pilot study to address their research question, write a grant proposal for internal funds supplied through the certificate program, pilot their study, record results, and report conclusions through a research brief and a communication produc t of their own selection. Student choice of methods are not constrained to a single d iscipline. Students in 2022  pursued projects on hospitalacquired infections, factors influencing biodiversi ty of the oral microbiome, protein-protein interactions connected to Alzheimer’s Disease, and detection of persistent organic pollutants in breast milk. Student-selected methods drew techniques from microbiology, molecular biology, biochemistry, and analytical chemistry. Participants and Data Collection:  The selection pool constituted 17 students  enrolled in the authenticinquiry course in  spring 2022. Students were  recruited after grades were submitted. Seven studen ts provided written consent to release for analysis video recordings of 15-minute semi-structured qualitative interviews conducted in the final week of the semester as part of the coursework. Interviews provided students space to make meaning of their cognitive processes in their own w ords and give direction to the interview process  (Brenner, 2006). Open-ended, non-leading interview questions elicit ed general reflections on learning so as not to bias responses toward a particular theme (Kerlinger & Lee, 2000). Interview questions asked what participants’ major takeaways were from the course, what were the most important experiences leading to these takeaways, w hat aspects of their learning experience supported or o bstructed their success, and how they felt in the course compared to how they feel in their other STEM courses. The Institutional Review Board of the study site approved the study protocol. All seven participants were women and most (n=6) were juniors. Participant races/ethnicities were White (n=3), East Asian (n=3), or Black (n=1) as documented by the author. Students’ primary majors were biology (n=3), microbiology (n=1), biochemistry (n=1), public health (n=1), or exploratory track (n=1). Data analysis: I transcribed, de-identified, and qualitatively analyzed all seven in terviews. I used structural coding (Saldaña, 2016) in the first round to extract data on learning outcomes and processes that fit the codes. I then used deductive pattern coding (Saldaña, 2016) to categorize learning descriptions into the four Whole Scientist dimensions (Table 3) organized in a case-ordered matrix (Miles et al., 2014). Excerpts with multiple themes were assigned a primary code  by the most salient theme, and secondary or tertiary codes were noted as synergy. Iterative back-checks with definitions from the literature ensured consistency in application of codes. ## Findings

Findings show undergraduate laboratory students enrolled in the authentic-inquiry laboratory course I designed report on multidimensional learning outcomes. Parti cipants shared key takeaways and learning experienc es connected to their autonomy, belonging, competence, and purpose. Autonomy All participants referenced autonomy. Daisy reflected on an experience of failure in which the team realized they did not have an instrument sensitive enough to dete ct dioxins in parts per trillion. The team then had  to decide together how to carry their p roject forward, and they arrived at detecting per-and polyfluorinated substances instead. This experience arose in interviews with Daisy’s teammates, Lily and Violet, as well. In reflecting on her takeaways from the course, Lily pointed out her “learning how to be more flexible when a problem arises… the confidence in knowing-hey, I overcame that, and I took a new direction.” Violet also described her team’s investment of effort, and how these cycles of troubleshooting “could have kind of hindered our way into, like you know, our study overall, but we kept going, and it was nice just having a team overall to like just kind of push us and keep going to, you know, make our study successful.”  This latter quotation in particular indicates evide nce of interdependence and connection with teammates supporting autonomy. Compared to her other courses, Rose indicated she felt “definitely more like in charge… more active” and that she spent less time “listening to someone talk and taking it in or being told exactly what to do. Whereas this was very unique in that we had to figure out w hat to do ourselves with guidance, of course, but d efinitely very different.” Iris also compared her experience of autonomy in the lab to other classroom experiences: “I didn't feel like I was going to class… I felt like I was j ust like going th ere to do my own thing, to do my own like research, to do my own project versus other classes, like I go there to watch lectures, like to listen to professors.” Jasmine compared the lab course to her experience working in a research lab, stating “[my independence] totally like exponentially increased through this lab, because I was able to kind of decide what I wanted to do and do it.” Similarly, Dahlia indicated that being asked to make research decisions was “really important for me to like grow past…” and that not having an expert in the room  supported her autonomy: “Having someone there who like is just there to like support you but not necessarily give you all the answers or maybe doesn't have all the answers is like helpful to… push us forward.”  Overall students described gains in autonomy result ing from facing challenges unique to driving their own research study.

Belonging Most (6/7) participants commented on connection and building r elationships. Most commonly, participants discussed learning how to collaborate, communicate, connect, or learn with classmates. Rose stated, “The biggest thing that I learned to do is work in a bigger grou p.” Iris described how flexible instruction “really hel ped us improve and grow as a team.” Daisy referenced how tackling the novel challenge of designing and c onducting research for the first time with her peers supported the team-based learning component compared to other courses: “in this class it actually worked because we learne d it together.” Lily stated her unique lab experiences made her “feel like a real scientist”  and allowed closer connections with students from d ifferent backgrounds. Here she describes relationship-building: With support of my peers, my teammates, we all made it happen…. I feel like this class allowed more closer connections with students because of that we are all under the same kind of pressure to come up with something, do something meaningful,  figure out h ow to overcome obstacles, and really like keep a close communication, continu e like this optimistic energy amongst the group to keep us going.

Her anecdotes aligned with her teammate Violet’s takeaway about closer connections resulting from uniting over a shared project that their team started together: Being able to just be in a lab setting that you actually get to enjoy-like just talk to people you kind of connect with after you know-it’s just nice so when you walk in and you’re just like, oh hi guys, like let’s get started, let’s do this, we’re all tired, but you know it ’s just it’s so nice to just be able to like you know come together and just finish what we started in the beginning because we all get invested at the end of the day a nd we just what to get it done, you know? That that was nice about this class.

Four participants also reported new/deepened connec tions with faculty outside the classroom. Daisy reported that her team’s research project required them to “contact so many professors,” and Dahlia reported that the course experience “pushed me to like make more connections and be more comfortable talking to professors.” Lily reported, “learning how helpful it can be to r each out to professionals and how to  collaborate with them to receive their guidance.” Iris shared a specific anecdote of reaching out to professors: The first experience was when we reached out to Dr. Oak and then Dr. Maple. The first response was from Dr. Oak. And when she said that we couldn ’t work on this topic at all, I was a little devastated, but then we got the response back from Dr. Maple and like the two different responses kind of made me think like it's not only one pathway that I could take to explore this project. There are multiple ways and multiple routes that I can take, so that was one experience. Student gains in belonging spanned both new and deepened connections with peers as well as new skills and experiences building relationships with or collaborating with faculty. Competence Most (6/7) participants commented on novel gains in research competence. Most commonly participants commented on learning generalized scientific research competence like learning the research process, how to design a study, and how to write better. Lily reported “learning how to ultimately do research.” Jasmine reported, “Learning how to design an experimental procedure.”  Violet reported learning how to draw from literatu re to justify study design: “being able to also like get data from different literature and use that as evidence and just put it into a study and be able to e xplain that just really made us like stronger scien tists.” Dahlia indicated that the course “pushed us to … think about things critically before actually doing our experiment.” Iris and Daisy both indicated that learning the pro cess of conducting research was new to them. Iris reported, “Learning how research is done, learning the process of developing the research question so that it's feasible for your project, doing a literature review, and reaching out to professors, and all that process was all new for me.” Similarly Daisy stated, “I had no idea how  researchers designed their own study but-I had to read a lot of different papers through this class and, uhm, I have a sense of how to read research papers now and also… how to read the methodology section especially.” Importantly, Jasmine perceived the course activities as mapping onto the authentic activities of scientists: I think there's so many different things that you’ll have to write as like a scientist doing research and whatnot. And I think writing the grant proposal with the guidelines of the like, I forget the name of it, but there was like a specific kind of grant proposal that you modeled ours off of like with certain like rules and stuff [NSF]. And I thin k that that was really helpful to learn how to write one following the requirements of what like a normal grant proposal would require. Lily indicated that learning the research process w as not just a hypothetical exercise: “to bring it i nto something physical… this was really the exciting part, doing something in person, seeing your results… it defini tely is meaningful to learn about that  process.” Some students also reported on gaining discipline-specific competence. Dahlia, Lily, and Jasmine commented on learning gains with laboratory or analytical techniques. Dahlia reported on gains in underlying understanding of laboratory techniques: I learned a lot like lab-wise. …it was kind of cool to like learn new techni ques and kind of actually understand what was going on. Also kind of  cool to see some overlap because the genetics class the last… lessons were on molecular technologies and stuff and so we were going over like PCR and transformation and everything like that and so it was kind of cool to actually be like oh I’ve done that. Lily reported on learning about atomic absorption and spectrophotometry. Jasmine stated the lab provided a new opportunity to apply statistics to a real situation: “Determining like P values and graphing our data  … I haven’t done a whole lot with statistical analysis, but I know that it definitely is a part of research. You know, I read about P values and like stats in like physics, but I’ve never actually like applied them to any situation.” However, Rose lamented that constraints limited her learning new techniques: “I personally didn ’t get to do much that was new for me. And a lot of what we did… was something I ’ve done before, which is like why I really wanted t o do the sequencing stuff because that would have been new a nd cool.” Taken together, students’ reports of competence span several scientific practices including familiarity and experience with the full arc of research design, scientific literacy, and use of  tools and technologies.  These findings indicate competency outcomes compara ble to other research education experiences.

Purpose Most (5/7) participants commented on pursuit of their own goals, interests or passions that transcended the course. For example, Iris commented, “in the beginning of t he semester, we got to brainstorm like all the topi cs that we were kind of passionate about, so that like I had a  chance to explore the research that I really cared  about throughout the entire semester.”  Rose stated, “The, like, flexible nature of the cla ss made me feel more comfortable in, like, adapting what we were doing and working with what we were interested in.” Lily described how pursuing a passion allowed her to be flexible a nd persevere during troubleshooting: “if you’re rea lly passionate about something  – how to make compromises that are helpful but also n ot deterring you from the passion you are researching.” Violet and Dahlia noted the importance of shared pu rpose within their team. Dahlia discussed how she, along with Iris and another teammate, planned to co ntinue the research they started in the course for their senior thesis: “Iris, Poppy, and I knew like we were going to work on it further. I think it kind of became a little difficult for us to separate like this course from the future but also like prepare ourselves for the future.” Violet specifically discussed their team’s goal of tackling a global health issue affecting local nursing mothers: Not only just working with teams, working with people that really want to tackle the same topic and issue as you… I was able to really dive into the whole multidisciplinary team based projects and with the research being in a team it was really  nice being able to view other people's perspectives and ideas and really just combine it into one big topic and ideally it was some sort of like global health issue and we kind of just made it into a more specific, …in our case it was breast milk for women in [our state]… Jasmine indicated that the course did not necessari ly support her to identify her research interests, but that it did help her teammate in that regard: “I think if I had not been currently writing my thesis, I think it would have been a really great way for me to figure out what I want ed to do for my thesis, like that-I know that that was the experience for Azaelia.” Data coded with purpose demonstrate the learning im pact of students getting to choose the research direction. Sense of purpose contributed to student persistence through challenges in research.

## Conclusion

This study offers insight into the value of an authentic-inquiry (Buck et al., 2008)  interdisciplinary laboratory course for undergraduates. This implementation resulted in learning gains indic ated as meaningful and unique compared to other STEM courses  and comparable to previous reports (Dolan, 2016; Pl enge et al., 2022). The Whole Scientist framework described outcomes well. Frequently, participants indicated support in one dimension led to outcomes in another,  particularly among autonomy, belonging, and purpose, indicating synergies. Participants also indicated the opportunity to purs ue coursework aligned with their own goals for community health justice, indicating transformative intellectualism  (Morales-Doyle, 2017). Tradeoffs, particularly the absence of greater discipline-based competence reported by Rose, confirm the need  for multiple pedagogies of research education. Still, this work supports the practicality and effi cacy of student-driven research education. Authentic inquiry may contribute to diverse student  retention and equity and justice in research educa tion by allowing students’ socially relevant goals to influ ence the direction of research and support their ba sic needs for self-determination. Future study iterations will leverage mixed methods  and recruit comparisons groups. Wider implementation and assessment of authentic inquiry is suggested across varying lab contexts and demographics.
