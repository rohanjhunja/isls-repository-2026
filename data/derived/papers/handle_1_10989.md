# Healing Justice Practice Professional Development Program for Black K-12 Educators

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Buffalo-Niagara is one of the most racially segregated regions in the nation. The majority of students in Buffalo city schools are Black while the employees are predominantly white. Black communities in Buffalo live in constant fear exacerbated by a deteriorating quality of life and mass shooting. K-12 schools have the potential to become sites for healing regimens, self-care, and protection led by Black educators trained in Healing Justice practice-based curriculum.

Buffalo-Niagara is one of the most racially segregated regions in the nation. The majority of students in Buffalo city schools are Black while the employees are predominantly white. Black communities in Buffalo live in constant fear exacerbated by a deteriorating quality of life and mass shooting. K-12 schools have the potential to become sites for healing regimens, self-care, and protection led by Black educators trained in Healing Justice practice-based curriculum. Summary of project The “Healing Justice Practice Space (HJPS)” (Brown et al., 2012) curriculum is for teachers working in K-12 Buffalo city schools who are the descendants of enslaved Africans in America. Healing justice work has historically been led by Black, Indigenous, and People of Color (Brown et al., 2012). The curriculum is grounded in three central tenets: History, Identity, and Agency. The HJPS professional development (PD) curriculum’s goal is to positively transform the narrative and environment for Black teachers. Theoretical frameworks The term Healing Justice as used within our program, “refers to an evolving political framework shaped by economic, racial, and disability justice that re-centers the role of healing inside of liberation; that seeks to transform, intervene and respond to generational trauma and violence” (Brown et al., 2012, p.1). Through the lens of Afrocentricity, which “questions the imposition of the White supremacist view as universal and/or classical”(Asante, 1990, p.173), HJPS teachers will study the Africandiaspora and the World (ADW) program from the historically Black college (HBCU) Spelman College, and African-centered education which “builds on the work and struggles of radicals, activists, poets, scholars, and regular people in Africa and the Diaspora who have fought against oppression in their own ways” (Dei & Kempf, 2023, p.163). History The ADW program prepares students to develop a perception of themselves as citizens of a changing and increasingly compressed world, of sharpening the awareness of diverse cultural and historical experiences, and of promoting the association between learning and social change. These studies will provide interdisciplinary insight into the histories and cultures of Africa and the diaspora, creating connections between the various communities of Africandescent globally. According to Shuster (2014), Black people make up an immense multitude of ethnic groups and belong to one of the world’s most influential cultures. Therefore in education, Black history cannot be relegated to one month, and must be taught within the larger American story. We will anchor our program with the fundamental historical textbook used at Spelman, Africandiaspora and the World. Weekly reading assignments will include in-depth discussions that will culminate in our own journal to be published in schools, updates/newsletters, and the state education association magazine. Identity Black self-love is impossible when grounded in European standards and ideals. HJPS teachers will identify how Africa and African communities of the diaspora have shaped the modern world. As a result, HJPS curriculum professional development attendees will analyze distinct categories of identity, especially in relation to difference and the construction of gender, race, ethnicity, class, and citizenship. In her investigation into the study of internalized racial oppression, Pyke (2010) explains how we all have a personal responsibility to confront stereotypes. Members of the majority need to help and support those in the minority to see their personal value and to know that our worth is not related to the prejudice in our society. Members of the minority must listen to those who challenge the view of the majority and challenge it ourselves. In “Afr ocentric Curriculum: A Paradigm for Healing and Education,” Ameer Akinwale Ali (2016) uses Afrocentric mental health practices and curriculum as the groundwork in his analysis. In his study he revealed how the development of a personal identity, leads to the development of a group identity, and thus transforms the individual, the group, and the community, despite the complexity of problems. His findings also suggest that  the Afrocentric curriculum provides the mediating link between an individual's racial identity and self-esteem. 2307 Agency Freire (1970) shows us how to engage in the battle for liberation by perceiving the reality of oppression not as a world with no escape, but as a situation with limits that can be transformed. After carefully analyzing and identifying how American standards have been instilled and manifest in our personal identity, each member will create their own standards, core values, and processes. A daily regimen will be created where new narratives, internal dialogues, and manners of matriculation through society will enhance the perspective of Black people in public education.These modifications will be anchored in Dei et al.’s (2000) nine spheres of implementation for Afrocentric education: representation, language, family/community, and school partnerships with a focus on shared responsibility, co-operative education, equity and values education, agency for transformation, Indigenous/community knowledges, spirituality, and learning for climate justice. Over the years, development and education in Buffalo city has endured some changes, but very few improvements regarding the rehabilitation and authentic inclusion of Black people. Black educators are forced to navigate through a system which promotes the total erasure of their culture, history, and stereotypes over facts. Once these modifications become incorporated into our routine, we will extend our experience to at least one other teacher who did not participate in the professional development program. Within ever y Buffalo city school, a collective must be created which works to ensure that the unique needs of Black and marginalized employees are met. A collective for Black educators who will in turn share their healing strategies with their Black peers is imperative. These HJPS profession development programs catered to the descendants of enslaved Africans in America working in K-12 education, will honor the grief, pain, talents, beauty, and humanity of Black people. “There is no Black person here who can afford to wait to be led into positive action for survival. Each one of us must look clearly and closely at the genuine particulars (conditions) of his or her life and decide where action and energy is needed and where it can be effective. Change is the immediate responsibility of each of us, wherever and however we are standing, in whatever arena we choose” (“Audre Lorde, Learning from the 60s, 2012”). References Ali, A.
