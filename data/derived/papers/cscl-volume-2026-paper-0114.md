# ClassMosaic: Leveraging Solution Diversity to Teach Computational Problem- Solving

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
A key challenge in K-12 Computer Science (CS) education is teaching computational Problem-Solving (PS). PS instruction research emphasizes the importance of structured whole-class discussions to reflect on solution strategies to foster PS competences. How ever, orchestrating whole-class discussions is challenging for teachers. As such, we drew on PS-and dashboard research to design ClassMosaic, a classroom orchestration tool that supports productive PS-teaching practices. ClassMosaic provides real-time insights into student work to facilitate discussions and promote collaborative learning by enabling collective comparison and reflection. We present ClassMosaic’s design principles and the first user study focusing on perceived pedagogical value with 9 CS teachers. Results show that ClassMosaic aligns with existing CPS instruction practices, and fosters new CPS-teaching strategies, including heterogeneous strategy-based grouping and structured post-discussion adaptations. Future work will further automate PS-based code clustering, and adapt ClassMosaic’s UX/UI to reduce teacher cognitive load, before in-class experiments.

PS is a core competence in CS that students struggle to acquire (Medeiros et al., 2019; Pechorina et al., 2023). As research on explicitly teaching Computational PS (CPS) has produced mixed results (Craig et al., 2019; Denny et al., 2019; Pechorina et al., 2023; Prather et al., 2020), how to design and structure CPS teaching to foster learning and transfer of effective CPS competences remains an open question. While research on teaching PS in K-12 CS education is still emerging, mathematics education can provide relevant insight due to its decades-long research into teaching mathematical PS (Santos-Trigo, 2024; Silver, 2023; Stanic and Kilpatrick, 1989). A core finding from mathematics education is the importance of (1) having open-ended, cognitively engaging PS activities with multiple solutions (Lester and Cai, 2016), and (2) orchestrating whole-class contribute significantly to developing students’ PS competences (Silseth and Furberg, 2024; Stein et al., 2008) through (i) sense-making and reflection, (ii) consolidating knowledge, and (iii) deepening students’ understanding (Ackermann, 2001). However, orchestrating whole-class discussions is challenging for teachers (Evans and Dawson, 2017; Liljedahl, 2016). This is where teaching CPS provides an interesting opportunity: given the central role of digital  technologies in CS, it becomes relevant to study how technological tools enable and support effective PS teaching practices (Quintana et al., 2004; Santos-Trigo and Moreno-Armella, 2016). Despite increasing research on dashboards for education, there are frequently raised limitations:  (1) few dashboards are designed and adapted to K-12 pedagogical practices, (2) lack of theoretical grounding (Hundhausen et al., 2017; Jivet et al., 2017), and (3) focus on awareness (Jivet et al., 2017) and / or prediction, without a clear translation between the analytics and pedagogical actions, thus limiting “the educational value” Masiello et al., 2024, p.8 of dashboards. To address these limitations we developed ClassMosaic. ClassMosaic ClassMosaic is a web-based platform designed to support teachers in CPS instruction by facilitating productive whole-class CPS discussions and leveraging the diversity of students’ solutions to support collective sense-making and learning. To that effect, ClassMosaic makes students’ PS strategies and solutions visible, comparable, and interpretable in real time. ClassMosaic’s design draws from (i) mathematics PS research to provide a theoretical foundation, and (ii) dashboard research to adhere to best prac tices and address challenges that affect teacher use and adoption. The goal is to enable teachers to engage with student data to support pedagogical decision-making in real-time, and thus extend the role of dashboards in CPS education beyond awareness tools. ClassMosaic is compatible with Scratch (Resnick et al., 2009, development underway for python) and comprises of three main parts: (1) The task designer to create single or multi-task programming sessions where teachers can (a) limit the set and number of available blocks, (b) freeze selected code blocks, and (c) create custom code tests (assertions) to evaluate students’ solutions. (2) The automatic real-time collection of students’ codes in-development. (3) The interactive dashboard, which can be viewed by the teacher alone or together with  the classroom, enables real-time engagement with student data from the CPS activity in multiple ways: (a) An 662 interactive 2D graph view shows student codes arranged according to different code metrics to make visible the commonalities and rich variations in solution approaches. Teachers can assign codes manually to groups, or choose automated groupings of student code (based on clustering of Abstract Syntax Trees). (b) A code comparison view that shows manually or automatically selected codes side-by-side. These elements aim to support real-time exploration, identification, and analysis of students’ CPS approaches.

## Materials and methods

We conducted a preliminary user study with 9 K-12 and higher education CS teachers, all with extensive teacher training experience and ongoing CS teaching mandates, to examine ClassMosaic’s perceived pedagogical value. The user-study was organized as follows: (1) Questions regarding pre-existing CS and CPS teaching practices and needs; (2) Solving a pre-designed Scratch task with ClassMosaic; (3) Using ClassMosaic to explore a pre populated solution data set for the same task while following a think-aloud protocol; (4) Feedback on the user experience to identify emerging use cases, affordances and barriers; (5) Synthesis of key-findings for participant validation to minimize misinterpretations (Merriam and Tisdell, 2015). The data analysis was conducted by two researchers as follows (Saldaña, 2009): (1) A descriptive coding phase to classify the topics in each passage. (2) A focused coding phase to create more refined codes within each topic in terms of teacher practices, and features needed to support them. (3) Grouping teacher practices into highlevel categories (lesson preparation, classroom & data management, CPS instruction, analysis and feedback). Existing practices & needs to teach CPS.  Prior to seeing ClassMosaic, the teachers already engaged in some productive CPS instructional practices, but needed technological support, including (i) a system where students can solve the same problem in different programming languages, reinforcing CPS beyond language syntax; (ii) awareness of classroom activity through direct access to students’ codes and progress; (iii) automated grading. ClassMosaic in support of existing practices.  ClassMosaic was perceived as facilitating classroom management and orchestration by making student data accessible, easy to display, compare, execute, and edit. Teachers noted that this would enable them to (1) view student work in real-time without interrupting them, and

(2) identify and showcase examples in post-task reflections. The code analysis and grouping features were seen

as helpful to identify strategies and enrich whole-class discussions. However, teachers requested more automated grouping functionalities to streamline their workflow and minimize in-class time spent behind the screen. ClassMosaic in support of novel practices.  Beyond enhancing existing practices, ClassMosaic prompted teachers to consider new instructional approaches, particularly to analyse student work and adapt lesson strategies. Notably, after interacting with ClassMosaic more teachers explicitly mentioned the relevance of getting students to compare and reflect on the differences between them. Teachers also reflected on ClassMosaic’s potential to support CPS instruction by (1) creating heterogeneous groups based on P S strategies; (2) adjusting subsequent lesson designs using data insights; (3) providing individualized formative and summative feedback. By supporting established and emerging CPS teaching practices, ClassMosaic helps bridge the gap between theoretical best practices and real-world classroom needs. However, refinements are required to improve ClassMosaic’s potential utility and effectiveness to teach CPS by: (1) reducing teachers’ in-class cognitive load to analyse students’ strategies (e.g., fully automated code clustering); (2) preparing teachers for in-class use of ClassMosaic to ensure that the practices that teachers engage in align with pedagogical best practices that have been found to foster PS competences (Leeuwen et al., 2021; Molenaar and Knoop-van Campen, 2019). Limitations As for all studies, the current study has its limitations: (1) small sample of participants with a deep understanding of best practices and novel teaching tools; (2) focus on a single programming language (Scratch); (3) perception based on limited use as teachers’ feedback was based on unguided interactions with precollected data, not actual classroom use, which hindered their ability to anticipate student responses, increased the need for guidance, and reduced the effectiveness of data exploration with the tool. Novel technological approaches to CPS instruction with theory-informed design are perceived by teachers to facilitate existing teaching practices and enable new instructional strategies. Teachers considered that ClassMosaic could be valuable to support cla ssroom orchestration, whole-class discussions, and comparative analyses of students’ solutions. Teachers also identified features that could enhance ClassMosaic’s utility, such as automated solution grouping, cross-language comparisons, and integrated form ative feedback mechanisms. The variety of teaching practices evoked also reflects the value of designing for pedagogical flexibility, so a tool can be more effectively  and expand on different teaching practices.
