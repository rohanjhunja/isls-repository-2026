# How Adolescent Learners in a STEM Summer Camp Represent Astronomy Concepts Through a Drawing Exercise

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Learners in an informal, STEM-focused summer camp (n = 19) spent one week exploring hypothetical versions of Earth and known exoplanets in a customized version of the digital sandbox game, Minecraft. On the third day of camp, learners were asked to draw their own exoplanets and incorporate various scientific measurements used in the game (i.e. oxygen levels, temperature, etc..). A semiotics analysis was applied to the drawings to understand how learners use representations to explain scientific phenomena and convey specific contextual themes related to the content of the camp. Outcomes showed about 70% of learner drawings included multiple celestial objects, depicting planetary system relationships.

## Introduction

Informal spaces offer opportunities for learners of  diverse backgrounds to engage with and explore sci entific phenomena. One way to capture how leaners consolidate and make sense of what they experience in a simulation is to ask them to create phenomenological drawings of the representations they interacted with throughout. The research presented in this paper explores how learn ers in a STEM-focused summer camp represent knowled ge and understanding of basic astronomy principles and general scientific relationships through creative drawings of possible exoplanets. A majority of the time in the camp was spent exploring simulated hypothetical versions of Earth (i.e. Earth without a moon) and known exoplan ets in the digital sandbox game, Minecraft, and act ivities such as drawing exoplanets were used to augment the digital experience. Our research explores how learners take concepts from simulated worlds in a digital game and represent them in a generative drawing activity.

## Background Literature

Drawing serves as a means to consolidate learning o f presented material through choices of how to repr esent concepts, which allows learners to reason and repre sent like a scientist trying to communicate ideas t o a broad audience (Ainsworth et al., 2011). Drawing is seen as a generative exercise, which can result in deep learning for students (Fiorella & Mayer, 2016).

Visual representations are common in Astronomy tex tbooks, and learners often adopt features and sample existing conventions they see in textbooks in their own scientific drawings of phenomena (Galano et al., 2018). A semiotic framework created by Tang, Won, a nd Treagust (2019) took drawings from learners in h igh school Physics and Chemistry classes and deconstructed all relationships down into presentational, organizational, and orientational representations. To conduct our a nalysis, we adopted this framework to analyze how m iddle school learners take what they observe in a digital game setting, Minecraft, and translate those concepts into selfgenerated visual representations.

## Methods

Our summer camp took place at non-profit community center in the Midwestern United States for underrepresented middle and early secondary learners (n = 19, 47% female, all participants identified as Black or African American, age range = 11 – 15 years, all participants eligible for free and reduced lunch). Remote consent was obtained from at least one guardian of each participant. Participants attended a total of five camp sessions, with each session lasting 3 hours. Most of each camp session was spent exploring hypothetical versions o f Earth and known exoplanets in the digital sandbox  game, Minecraft. On the third day of each respective camp, participants were given a lesson on known exoplanets: how they are discovered, what we can infer about condit ions on specific exoplanets, and whether experts th ink they might be hospitable to biological life. Simulations  of three known exoplanets were then explored in Mi necraft. Following exploration, participants were given blank worksheets and asked to draw their own exoplanet.  Using the analytic framework from Tang et al., (2019) an undergraduate and graduate student looked at how the drawings were used to convey scientific information and represent relationships between objects. All aspects were discussed and mutually agreed upon.

For those that did include multiple objects, we then looked a t other categories within the broad frames of prese ntation, organization, and orientation. Included in Table 1 are the categories and relationships represented by many of the drawings. Considering categories of Distribution, R elative Scale, and Formality, we perceive that most participants drew a representation in earnest that closely resembled what they thought an existing exoplanet could look like and how objects around the planet might be oriented. One relationship we added under the category of Simplicity was “Layered”, which we define as using a mix of colors to represent diverse composition of planetary features or atmosphere. This feature was present in 15 (78.9%) of the drawings, and even when participants only drew a single object they often differentiated regions of the planet to represent changes in temperature, oxygen, weather, oceans, or landforms.

## Discussion and Future Directions

Most drawings attempting to depict what could be a realistic situation, absent elements of fantasy or desire, was an encouraging result from an open-ended exercise that did not have any explicit assessment of learner knowledge. This form of self-related information processing has shown promising evidence for triggering interest in science and offering opportunities for learners to develop their interest in STEM (Renninger & Hidi, 2021). Even students who did generate planets that represented personal interests did include some labels or features consi stent with themes explored in the informal camp experience.

Future analyses will consider fidelity to concepts conveyed throughout the camp and a more formal assessment of how drawings do convey knowledge and understanding of content presented through discussi on and in Minecraft. Scoring drawings could then be correlated with other indicators of student interest in STEM.
