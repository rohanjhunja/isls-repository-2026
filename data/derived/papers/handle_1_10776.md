# Play and Design: Faculty Perceptions on Playful Interventions for Collaborative Learning in the Design Studio

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The design studio is the cornerstone of art and design education where students learn the creative process through working on open ended design problems. These spaces have been critiqued for not reflecting the collaborative nature of the profession where designers work in interdisciplinary teams. Play and playful interventions are known to support creative exploration and collaborative learning. However, play in the context of the design studio has largely been unexplored. In this exploratory study, we interview instructors from different disciplinary backgrounds to understand how play innately manifests in their teaching practice. We synthesize the findings to present considerations for future works that incorporate the notion of play into the design studio model to support collaborative learning.

## Introduction

The design studio is an integral part of architecture, art, and design education at the university level and refers to the studio model of teaching as well as the physical teaching space. Sawyer (2018) defines the studio model as a cultural model where the “central concept is the creative process”. Studio spaces define communities of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991)  that focus on creative thinking. Students work on open ended design problems  and through frequent interactions with the expert (instructor or facilitator) and each other, they develop mastery of the creative process. These spaces can benefit from playful interventions and may themselves have practices of playful learning.

This study focuses on play and playful learning wit hin the design studio in undergraduate architecture  and design courses. Through semi-structured interviews with design faculty th is study aims to develop an understanding of the role of play in supporting peer-to-peer collaboration in the design studio. The insights from this study will be used to inform the design of playful interventions.

The study is informed by the following two research questions:

1) How do instructors define play and how are these no tions of play supported or hindered by practices or

structures within the design studio?

2) What does peer-to-peer collaboration look like in the design studio and how has or can play be leveraged

by instructors to support this?

## Theoretical framework: Play and collaboration in the design studio

Views on play are diverse, ambiguous, and even contradictory (James, 2022)  and definitions include play as ‘bonding cooperation, and community’ (Sutton-Smith, 1998); ‘social engagement’  (Henricks, 2008); ‘tending toward social affiliations’ (Huizinga, 1955); ‘engaging with others’ (Sicart, 2017); ‘diminished consciousness of self’ (Brown, 2009) and ‘forgetting self’ (Csikzsentmihaly, 2014). Thes e various definitions underscore the role of social engagement, and we initially defined play as “a deeply engaging activity, that allows one the freedom to express oneself within some constraints or rules, supports social engagement and may lead to creation or making”. However, as we explore in the next section, social engagemen t within play is variously construed in the design studio.

The design studio is both the physical space as wel l as the course that  art and design stud ents engage with that follows a studio model of teaching. A studio model of teaching involves students working on open-ended design or art prompts through an iterative process in close collaboration with the studio facilitator through various pedagogical techniques like demons trations, pin-up critiques, desk-based critiques leading to a final presentation or display of work (Sawyer, 2018). Dutton (1987) highlights two imbalances in the design studio-the hierarchy between instructor and student  that results in  the absence of real dialog between the two, and competition between students leading  to them working alone and not utilizing each other as resou rces. Collaboration in the design studio can support the ideation phase and reduce “individual effort in learning” (Wang et al., 2010a). Studies on collaboration in the design studio have focused on co-design during the ideation process, community projects, interdisciplinary collaboration s, workshops, and technological intervention s or on collaborative design environments  (Achten, 2002; Boudhraa et al., 2021; Emam et al., 2019; Kiernan & McMahon, 2011; Safin et al., 2021). This  reflects a growing interest in designing a collaborative studio environment that parallels professional design practice where designers work in interdisciplinary teams. Studies that reflected ideas of play in the design studio used games,gamification, or game design to support collaboration (Cassim, 2020; Lee et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2010b).

However, the idea of play and playful learning as it is embodied in the design studio is under-reported in the literature. Unstructured collaboration as described by Craig & Zimring (2000) as “collaboration without goals” where students openly share ideas and issues withou t fear is what this research intends  to support through the playful interventions, thus we redefine play in the context of a collaborative design studio as: “a deeply engaging activity that encourages individuals and groups to freely express and share their ideas and resources within given constraints, that values social engagement, and that may lead to creation or making”.

## Methods

To better understand the presence of play and playful learning in the design studio and its usefulness in supporting collaborative peer-to-peer learning, we co nducted semi-structured interviews with  6 faculty members at design colleges in two countries. Interview questions focused on the faculty members’ views of play in the design studio, their experience in designing playful interventions and unstructured peer collaboration. Respondents were chosen based on a convenience sample; however, we intentionally choose two countries since play is influenced by and closely related to culture. The interviews were transcribed and thematically coded. A total of 3 Design professors from India and 3 Art, Architecture and Art History professors from the US were interviewed. Interviewe es represented a range of experience from assistant professors to department heads. Sample questions that were used to guide the interv iew included: How would you define play ? What aspects of a design studio incorporate play or play ful learning? What is your experience of peer-to-peer collaboration in your design studios?  And what do you think about using a playful interac tion between students to encourage collaboration?  Each interview lasted between 35-50 minutes and interviews were transcribed for coding. Data were coded using an open coding approach and emergent codes and themes were identified.

## Findings and discussion

Emergent codes are illustrated below in two broad categories: Defining play through the design studio and Peerto-peer collaboration in the design studio. Defining play through the design studio Instructors defined play as activity that is “engaging”, “not really work”, “immersive”, and “joyful.” Being in a play state was likened to the flow state (P1, P3 and P4) where you aren’t self-conscious, lose sense of time and are intensely engaged (Nakamura & Csikszentmihalyi, 2002). All the instructors defined play through embodied activity in the design studio and their definitions of play varied. This relates to the difficulty in defining play that we highlighted earlier in this article as well as indicating that play in the design studio is instructor led. They also highlighted the need for play in the design studio and challenges faced in incorporating play. Play as creative exploration This theme is used to capture how play is defined and us ed by instructors  to support or describe the creative process in the design studio. For example, P4 says “You decide the only way out of this thing [design problem] is to be playful. Which means we have to approach it from … different perspective that is free from these bounds.” Similarly, P1 describes play as “… sort of like a dance. You interact with materials, with ideas and this whole process that they will document until their artwork evolves into a final piece from sketches to an in-progress mini mockup, to the final piece”. They also defined play as making, experimenting, and as the process of iterating. Play as instructor led This theme highlights how play in the design studio occurs through intentional actions of the instructors. Play was used to reduce the distance between the instructor and the students and to make students feel more comfortable. P3 mentioned the use of humor in changing the authoritative instructor role in the design studio - “undergraduate kids may feel that, okay, this guy cracks jokes on himself, maybe it's not so bad. So maybe we can chill out and relax.” Feedback provided to the students during periodic  reviews or one-on-one critiques were also used as venues for playful dialogue. P4 says “I don't just model it. I act so ridiculous doing i t that it puts, hopefully puts, the other person at ease. That we are getting rid of the hierarchy right. That we're speaking at as equal designers.” The design prompt provided at the beginning of the design studio was mentioned to be an important determinant of how playful the students were able to be. Prompts had to be ope n ended (P5), but with certain constraints so that a wide range of outcomes are possible while pushing them to think in new and innovative ways (P6). In the design studio, emphasis is on the process rather than on the end goal which is like many truly playful activities that “have no immediate practical goal” and “performance is goal in itself” (Bateson & Martin, 2013). Peer-to-peer collaboration in the design studio The interviewees’ ideas related to collaboration in the design st udio were primarily themed as challenges with and needs for such integration.

Challenges An idea that came up frequently in the interviews, related to Dutton’s (1987) evaluation of the studio pedagogy, is competition between students. Students feared their peers would steal their ideas if shared before the completion of the project. Viewing other students as their competition hinders the opportunity to learn from each other. P3 talked about how “in individual projects sometimes, people  are not willing to share, because they think, oh, if somebody takes my idea and runs away with it and do es a better job of that idea.”  Another challenge that P3 mentioned was ensuring all  members in group contributed equally to the team, “when it comes to the final evaluation and then the grade it's not easy to real ly figure out how much effort somebody put in. and how much of the effort translated int o some meaningful action or activity.”  Not having a mechanism to grade teams that reflected the possibly unequal contributions was a struggle that highlights interesting questions of how playful collaborative practice, an inherently informal acti vity, can be assessed within formal structur es o f higher education.

Need All the interviewees reinforced the idea of play as being necessary for creativity and innovative ideas. P1 described that “[Collaborations] force them to think in new ways and  innovative ways that push them out of their comfor t zone.” Collaborations were also described as aiding knowle dge and skill sharing and building a sense of community in the design studio.  P6 describes her experience of structuring peer-to-peer collaboration as leading to “a really tight knit community as well as brought all of their skill levels to a more consistent place… The quality of the work turned out amazing because of that too, and they really grew. And I’ve seen them stay close after that and that's felt really good.” This is important as students’ feelings of belonging and community are known to be linked to higher levels of motivation, academic self-confidence, academic engagement, achievement, and student retention (Pedler et al., 2022).

Peer feedback or critique A lot of the peer-to-peer interaction in the design studio was structured aro und the idea of feedback or critique. Providing each other feedback on their designs was a strategy frequently used by instructors. They supported this through structured time and space for feedback as well as providing guideli nes on feedback etiquette to ensure feedback was constructive and respectful.

Playful collaborations Playful group interactions mentioned in the intervie ws involved various ice breaker strategies  like doing introductions, getting to know each other and short  design challenges like the egg-drop challenge. There was an understanding that playful interventions would help reduce hierarchy or distance between instructor and students as well as create a more conducive environment for organic collaborat ion between students. P4 describes this playful interaction between students as “where the peers respect one an other, where they're on equal footing and they're both pointing in the same direction, I think there might be more of a freedom. To ramble off into a stream, like, I don't know, but what if it was this sort of thing.”

## Conclusion

The study uncovers three important features of play in design studios that can be used as principles for guiding future works in pedagogy and curricula. Firstly, it  is important to note that play takes place through various embedded activities in the design studio. Play is particularly well suited for implementation in a design studio model with its emphasis on active creation. Secondly, peer interactions and student-instructor interactions need to be structured around playful dialogues to break down traditional instructor-student hierarchies. Finally, playful interactions can encourage more peer-to-peer collaboration and support more collaborative creativity by creating a safe space for experimentation and expression. The design studio as a community of practice could support “modes of belonging” for its members through the in troduction of playful interventions for developing interpersonal relationships, shared experiences, “m utual engagement in shared activities”, know ing each other’s work, sharing stories, and exploring new perspectives  (Wenger, 1998). This study is intended to serve  as a backdrop for future research projects that engage in playful interventions in design studios to assess the degree to which collaborative learning is fostered as a result.
