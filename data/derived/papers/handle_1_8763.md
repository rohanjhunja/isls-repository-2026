# Designing for an Emerging Paradigm in Computer Programming - Towards Hybrid Human-Computer Creation of Artifacts

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Computer science education has experienced many noticeable changes since its inception. Today, students encounter considerably more user-friendly and intelligent IDEs, far less confusing error messages, and much simpler program compilation processes. Learners are also likely to encounter programming environments that can combine human created artifacts with programmatically created content. Even so, much of computer science education assumes a paradigm where only the computer directly manipulates the final artifact. This paper describes an emerging human-computer hybrid paradigm as seen through Scratch, Minecraft Education Edition, and the Dash and Dot. By thinking about these three platforms, the paper motivates more intentionality in how we design for learning in this hybrid paradigm.

## Introduction

Learning computer programming has generally made si gnificant advances over the past several decades. L ong gone are the days of paper punch cards that are fed  into room-sized machines. Today’s programmers can conceivably execute computer programs from anywhere  in the world. The environments for writing code ha ve also changed in dramatic ways. Today's integrated d evelopment environments provide countless tool-tips, automatic completion, and easy linking among source  files, libraries, and other resources. Moreover, w ith the emergence of block-based programming, users, kids, and adults alike, can author intricate threaded com puter programs without typing a single line of code. Broadly speaking, these advances have shifted who is able to create computer programs, and how the programs are used.

One thing that has seemingly remained stagnant in the world of computer programming, however, is the relationship between the human, the program, and th e resultant artifact. By and large, programmers wri te code for the computer to execute in the creation of a di gital artifact. The creation of the computer progra m is the responsibility of the programmer. The program subsequently creates the artifacts. If the artifact is not satisfactory the programmer adapts the code and reruns the programs. They repeat this process until they are pleased with the outcome. This process has worked well for decades and will likely remain a dominant paradigm. That said, this paper explores an alternative paradigm that involves both the human and computer program directly contributing to the final artifact. The paper describes three examples that demonstrate how this paradigm is enacted and serve to highlight ways that it differs from the traditional approach. These points of distinction inform some suggested considerations that may be unique to this human-computer-hybrid programming paradigm. Furthermore, the novel design possibilities that they afford may give rise to more programming experiences utilizing this paradigm. Case studies This paper uses Scratch, Minecraft Education Edition, and the Dash and Dot robot platforms as driving cases for this hybrid paradigm. The reader is encouraged to explore these three platforms at their leisure. Important aspects of these three platforms can be represented in terms of two dimensions. The first dimension considers how much the programming environment compartmentalizes the human created artifact (i.e., to what extent can the computer program permanently damage the human created compon ents). The second dimension is about the resultant artifact’s physicality (i.e., is it virtual or physical). Looking across Scratch, Minecraft, and Dash and Dot, we see a shift from compartmentalized environments to deco mpartmentalized, and from virtual artifacts to phys ical artifacts. The compartmentalized environment of Scratch meant that the code could minimally modify any student created artifacts. On the other hand, in both Minec raft and with Dash and Dot, the actions of the prog ram can cause extensive, seemingly irreparable changes to t he human-created artifacts. We also see a transitio n from virtual artifacts to physical artifacts. Both Scratch and Minecraft exist within virtual contexts where the artifacts created are digital. With the Dash and Dot SketchKi t, the artifacts are physical in nature. These shif ts will be important as we think about how to design for learning within this new paradigm.

## Discussion

With these three scenarios in mind, we now transition to discussing what it means to design for this paradigm by thinking about some of the challenges and opportunities they present. This includes sprites and backgrounds in Scratch, intricate buildings in Minecraft, and drawings with Dash and Dot (as derived from pushing a sequence of buttons to guide the robot), for example. This difference necessitates both intentionality and flexibility in the design of the experience. Intentionality is needed to authentically engage users in coding. Absent opportunities for programming to enable obvious advantages, some youth will invariably elect not to code. Hence, it is essential to consider how the benefits of coding are articulated to participants and the types of activity structures that make these benefits apparent. Errors are costly Whereas the dominant computer programming paradigm tends to be forgiving to errors, within these hybrid human-computer collaborations, errors can be quite costly, with the exception of Scratch. In Minecraft the propensity for significant and costly errors is quite high. Something as simple as running your code facing the wrong direction can lead to hours of time spent undoing that action. In the case of the Dash and Dot robot, an error can ruin a manually drawn sketch. In both platforms, there are various strategies to overcome this problem. For example, students might save a copy of their world before running their code, or they can use the agent to build and watch as the agent completes the different actions. Similarly for Dash and Dot, students can reduce the cost of coding errors by doing additional tests on unwanted materials and carefully watching the robot execute the instructions received.

Programmatic replication of human-created artifacts The high cost of errors is one motivation for havin g students complete their entire designs using codi ng. If all aspects of the artifacts are created with code, the re is increased reproducibility and easier ways to address any errors. For instance, if the program can create the entirety of the Minecraft structure, including the intricate details, users do not have to deal with recreating those parts if something goes awry. That said, one reality of these design environments is the ways that manual manipulation of the materials (i.e., Minecraft blocks, graphics, or sprites) can often spur student ideation. Hence, it is not s imply a matter of students creating their desired c omponents using coding. Without the manual design work, students may lose opportunities to realize their intended artifact. Instead, the high cost of errors might motivate additional innovation in computer programming where, presumably using artificial intelligence, a program can be gen erated to programmatically create what a user may h ave manually created. This would make it easy to redo existing artifacts. Taking a step back though, the high cost of errors disincentivizes utilizing coding and alters the debugging process. For example, we might see that students undertake better decomposition of their programs to be more certain that it will work as intended. Some  of these changes in behavior may be associated wit h better learning and programming outcomes, while others may  not. These are questions for further exploration. Either way, designers of these environments should conside r how some of the characteristics of this type of h umancomputer hybrid environment influences different ways of engaging the programming process.

## Conclusion

The field of computer science continues to see rapid advances in how we educate future computing professionals. From increasingly intuitive programming environments to developing more inclusive and culturally sustaining curriculum, to integrating wearables and e-textiles, many aspects of computer science education have significantly changed throughout the past few decades. As youth experience these learning innovations, they are regularly introduced to a relatively new paradigm in computer science that is typified by the combination of human and computer program generated artifacts. Operating in this paradigm challenges some of our typical assumptions and practices for teaching computer science. Nonetheless, this paradigm also holds significant promise for realizing the potential of human-computer collaboration. To best leverage these potential developers, practitioners, and researchers need to be intentional about designing for this space and create learning experiences and platforms that better reflect the unique constraints and challenges that exist along the spectrum of this paradigm.
