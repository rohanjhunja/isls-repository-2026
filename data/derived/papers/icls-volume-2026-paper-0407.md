# Linking Data and Community: Design Data-art Inquiry Programs to Foste Civic Engagement

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster introduces the MVP model, a transdisciplinary approach designed to foster civic engagement among upper middle and high school students by linking data science, art, and social studies, through the artistic visualiz ation of community data. Students will investigate locally relevant topics by designing data collection instruments, conducting analyses, and translating findings into creative, artistic data visualizations. Through situating data work within social and emotional contexts, MVP aligns with the civic mission of social studies education—to prepare reflective, informed, and active citizens.

In social studies education, a persistent challenge is how to engage students in meaningful civic learning that centers their voices and lived experiences while also grounding their understanding in data and evidence. To address this challenge, social studies educators need to emphasize the connection between civic reasoning and evidence-based inquiry, which is central to democratic citizenship education (Levine, 2015; Swan et al., 2013). While classroom discussions based on personal experiences can serve as a valuable entry point, structured and expressive approaches are needed to deepen students' inquiry. One such approach is to focus on community issues rooted in students’ lived experiences and enable them to move from seeing data in social studies classes as merely abstract numbers to understanding it as personally relevant societal phenomena. This poster argues that incorporating data meaningfully in the social studies classroom holds substantial promise for fostering civic engagement, defined as “individual or collective actions in which people participate to improve the well-being of communities or society in general, and which provide opportunities for reflection” (Innovations in Civic Participation, 2010). Specifically for upper middle and high school students, we introduce the MVP (Mathematizing, Visualizing, and Power) model to encourage educators to empower those students  to creatively explore and visualize personal and community data for  meaningful engagement with critical civic issues.

The MVP model Our research team at the University of Tennessee, Knoxville, in collaboration with local middle and high school teachers from eastern Tennessee, developed the MVP model —a set of curricula and activities designed to integrate data science, art, and social studies to foster civic engagement among upper middle and high school students. Grounded in design-based research (DBR), the MVP model involves educational researchers and teachers collaboratively designing and iteratively revising instructional activities. Students participate in a structured 12-to-14-week program that introduces foundational data science concepts and artistic practices. Learning Sessions are structured as  a series of 1.5-hour transdisciplinary sessions designed to integrate data literacy and art learning. Each session begins with a warm-up activity introducing an artistic technique, transitions into a small-group exploration of community-related data, and concludes with a reflective group discussion. 2) guides students from interpreting and analyzing existing artistic data visualizations to independently creating

visualizations based on local map-based data in subsequent weeks; the second half (Segment 3) emphasizes community inquiry, allowing students to identify local concerns, collect data, and present findings to their communities and enabling them to actively engage in civic action and democratic practices in real-world contexts and supporting authentic civic learning by centering lived experience and public dialogue (Torney-Purta et al., 2001). Community Learning Events (CLEs) are hour-long public gatherings held at accessible community venues and open to students’ families, peers, educators, and community members. During these events, students share their data collection processes, discuss the artistic and analytical decisions behind their visualizations, and articulate how their projects connect with broader social and civic contexts. CLEs create valuable opportunities for dialogue by linking students’ educational experiences directly to their communities and thereby enhancing their overall civic engagement. They also foster intergenerational civic learning by involving shared discussions about community issues, an approach shown to strengthen civic identity and social trust (Ballard et al., 2016). Call to action for transdisciplinary data science educators The MVP model is based on data-art inquiry (Matuk et al., 2022), which integrates principles from data science and the arts to help students build data literacy.  Our prior research found that students participating in the MVP program effectively used creative artistic data visualizations to communicate powerful, context-rich messages about their community issues (Zhao et al., 2025; Zhao & Hodge, 2026), which demonstrates the practical value of integrating art into data-focused social studies education.  While the MVP model has primarily been implemented as an afterschool program, it holds significant promise for use in formal social studies classrooms. As we continue to refine the MVP model, we emphasize the importance of decolonizing educational practices and building strong, respectful relationships throughout  the learning process (Bhattacharya, 2017).  Therefore, to support social studies educators interested in adopting the MVP model in their curricula, we offer the following practical recommendations.

First, we encourage educators to implement the MVP model, when  possible, on a semester-long basis rather than as a single or short-term event. In our experiences, most students need time and multiple iterations of data, and create compelling artistic visualizations. Allocating one or two class periods per week for MVP learning sessions will provide students with the sustained engagement necessary for deep and meaningful exploration of civic topics about their communities.

Second, we recommend providing students with diverse material options when creating their data visualizations. Although visual arts like painting and drawing may initially seem the most intuitive media for artistic expression, the MVP model intentionally embraces a broader range of artistic materials  to ensure greater accessibility and engagement. Recognizing that not all students feel confident or inspired by traditional visual art mixed-media creations like painted objects. Expanding material options  can enable students to express their data insights creatively and authentically, which respects and reflects the diversity of students’ experiences and perspectives.

Finally, it is crucial to actively involve local community members in the MVP process. Community participation significantly enhances the civic dimension of students’ learning experiences within the MVP program. When students engage directly with community members, they candeepen their understanding of local issues, become more thoughtful about the implications of their work, and learn to communicate their findings to the public effectively. Additionally, community participation provides an authentic audience for students’ artistic data visualizations and can also  raise awareness and promote dialogue around critical civic issues in local communities.
