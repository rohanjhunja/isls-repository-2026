# A Group Awareness Tool for Self-Assessment and Visualization of Participation: Its Effect on the Regulation of Unequal Participation

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We conducted a field study to examine how a Participation Awareness Tool (PAT) influences the regulation of participation during a group project. The PAT had two parts, a reporting part in which students were asked to self-assess their participation, and a visualization part in which students were presented with a graph representing the distribution of participation together with an indicator of the level of inequality in participation. Students used the PAT for four weeks. Results support a positive effect of the tool. Students benefit more from the visualization than from the self-assessment activity. The influence of the visualization on the regulation of participation seems to be more important for the most active students. Its influence on the behavior of less active students depends on when it is introduced during collaboration. Visualization motivates them to make more effort when it is introduced late. These results offer new possibilities for future research.

## Introduction

This research took place in a hybrid undergraduate course in adult education. This  course uses a project-based pedagogical approach: students are required to work in groups to design a training programme with the objective of changing students’ environmental behavior. This hybrid context was chosen because it is relatively common at the university, and because it requires groups to work in and out of the class room, alternating between synchronous (face-to-face or remote) sessions and asynchronous sessions. I n this context, the continuity of activities and exchanges may be more difficult to maintain, making it more difficult for students to coordinate and regulate their group activity. It is also common that during a group project lasting several weeks, some group members reduce their efforts and become less involved than others, partly bec ause they feel that the success of the group will not depend on their individual contributions. These phenomena of social loafing and free riding (Kerr & Brun, 1984) can result in an uneven level of participation during collaboration resulting in frustration (Capdeferro & Romero, 2012; Strauß & Rummel, 2021). One way to help students maintain their engagement in the task throughout the project is to have them regularly evaluate their participation, especially in relation to their collaborative partners. In this study, we examine the effect of a group awareness tool on students' participation in a 6-week collaborative learning project in a hybrid context. This awareness tool asks students to report their participation in different tasks of a group project. It also provides them w ith a graphical representation of the distribution of participation among group members, coupled with an indicator of the  level of inequality of participation (Gini coefficient). The aim is to encourage equal participation within the group. Participation in collaborative learning The scientific literature does not provide a single definition of participation. Participation can be described as the behavioral component of engagement in a task (Fredricks, 2013). In the context of  a group project, participation can be defined as taking part in different activities associated with the project such as discussing, reading, writing, etc. Much research has investigated participation in computer-supported collaborati ve learning by focusing on verbal exchanges, e.g., by counting and comparing the number of words produced by each gr oup member in a chat, forum and/or wiki (e.g., Adeniran et al., 2019; Strauß & Rummel, 2021). Results show that the equal contribution of group members to exchanges is a key factor in collaborative learning. The particularity of this research is that it focuses on students' particip ation in four activities necessary to carry out a group project, namely their participation in (1) "reading and s earching for information", (2) "producing", (3) "planning and coordinating", and (4) "collaborating". Thus, in order to move the project forward, all members of the group must be jointly involved in searching for relevant r esources, reading them to construct meaning and extract reliable information; producing different types of content  such as texts, figures and even videos; planning and coordinating activity, evaluating work in progress or completed; and co-constructing shared representations, proposing solutions, discussing ideas. These four components were  defined based on previous research (Adeniran et al., 2019; Strauß & Rummel, 2021). These four components were defined based on previous research (Adeniran et al., 2019; Strauß & Rummel, 2021) already focusing on the production and some parts of the planning and colla borating activities but also on field observations showing that these are not enough to go through a collaborative project. This led to CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 36 another particularity of this study: data were not automatically gathered from a learning management system but based on the self-report from the students themselves.

Group awareness tools to promote the regulation of participation Group Awareness Tools (GATs) are developed to provide learners with infor mation about their partners and the current activity. This awareness information can support group regulation and collaborative learning (Bodemer et al., 2018). GATs candisplay different types of awareness information, such as cognitive information, e.g., about what others know (Sangin et al., 2011), behavioral information, e.g., about what others are doing (Jermann & Dillenbourg, 2008), social information, e.g., about group dynamics (Phielix et al., 2011), and socio-e motional information, e.g., about the emotions of partners (Molinari et al., 2013, Avry et al., 2021). For a group to be effective, all members must contribute equally. However, groups regularly experience problems of social loafing, with some individuals being less motivated and making le ss effort than others. The reason for this social loafing problem is that individuals do not always make the li nk between their individual contributions and the success of their group, often perceiving their own contributions as dispensable, less valuable than those of their partners (Shepperd & Taylor, 1999). One way to reduce these problems is to make each group member aware of the responsibility they have for the success of their gr oup (Johnson et al., 2014), including assessing their individual performance and showing it to them and the group to enc ourage the regulation of participation at the individual (self-regulation) and the group (socially shared re gulation) levels (Järvelä et al., 2016). Group awareness tools are interesting tools to use to foster individual a ccountability within the group, as their role is precisely to help visualize individual contributions. Surprisingly, the  number of GATs specifically designed to promote equal participation in the group is not as large as one might expect. For example, Bachour et al. (2010) created and evaluated an interactive board that was able to indica te the level of participation of each participant in real time during collaboration via coloured LEDs. In Strauß and Rum mel's (2021) study, a GAT displaying the distribution of participation in the group (the average number of  words produced by each group member represented as bar graphs) was combined with incentives to bett er distribute the task within the group (adaptive prompts). In this research, the results do not provide evidence of a beneficial effect of the GAT combined (or not) with prompts. Feedback from participants after the study shows that they did not use or did not know how to use the information provided by the tools. The authors recommend that a scen ario for the use of GATS be designed or co-designed with the students so that they can use them appropriately and benefit from them. Research objectives and questions We conducted a field study to examine how a Participation Awareness T ool (PAT) influences the way students regulate their participation in a group project. The PAT had two parts, a re porting part in which students were asked to report their participation (self-reported participation) in four group activities (i.e., reading and searching for information; producing; planning and coordinating; collaborating) and a visualization pa rt in which students were invited to look at a graph representing the distribution of participation within their group associated with an indicator of the level of inequality of participation (Gini coefficient; Strauß & Rummel, 2021). The PAT was used by the students during four weeks of the group project. Our research questions concern the influence of the PAT on the evolution over time of two indicators of participation, the overall participation rate of each group member (individual level,  RQ1) and the level of inequality of participation (group level, RQ2). We expect the reporting part of th e PAT will encourage students to self-regulate their participation by focusing on what they did (Järvelä et al., 2016) and that the visualization part will encourage students not only to self-regulate but also have socially shared regulation by having them discuss what they could have done to participate more or what work needs to be done this week. The PAT should also encourage a more equal distribution of participation within the group. We are also interested in how the PAT is used and perceived by students (RQ3). PAT by reducing participation gaps, should increase student satisfaction; it should therefore be well accepted by students.

## Context and participants

The study was conducted between March and April 2022 with students of the hybrid bache lor course in adult education at the University of Geneva. We intervened during the first stage of the training design project in which the students were involved as a group. During this stage, the students participated in 5 lessons on the theoretical and methodological aspects of the design work, as well as in 11 planned group wor k sessions (face-to-face and in-class sessions). Students were also expected to work individually or with  their group between these sessions. CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 37 Twenty-eight students (21 women) were enrolled in this course. The groups we re composed by the students themselves, i.e., 5 groups of 4, 1 group of 3 and 1 group of 5. Description of the participation awareness tool As mentioned earlier, the Participation Awareness Tool (PAT) consisted of two parts, a reporting part and a visualization part. The reporting part consisted of an online questionnaire (15 questions) to be filled in by the students at the end of each week for a total of 4 weeks. Students were asked to identify themselves (name and group), then to evaluate their participation in four types of activities (reading and searching for information; production; planning and coordinating; collaborating), and finally to evaluate to what extent their contribution to each of these activities was useful or not to the group. A 10-point scale ranging from 0-10% to 90-100% was used for the self-assessment of participation, and a 4-point scale ranging from "not useful" to "necessary" was used for the self-assessment of the usefulness of the contribution. In addition, for each type of activity, they could express praise. They could praise their own work, the work of one or more members of the group, or the work of the group as a whole. An example of this questionnaire for the production activity is presented in Figure1. Finally, they were asked to estimate the amount of time they had spent on the project. At the beginning of the week immediately following the reporting phase, students were provided with a visualization of the collected data (as mentioned in the section below, only some  students were able to access these visualizations). We calculated two indicators on the basis of the data collected, a global participation rate (GPR) per group member and the Gini coefficient for the group (the difference between the participation rates of group members). We then created visualizations of these indicators as show n below (Figure 1). The GPRs were displayed as a bar graph, with the lowest in red and the highest in green. The Gini coefficient was presented below the GPRs, and students were told that this coefficient should be as low as  possible. These visualizations were designed based on those used in previous research on participation in collaborative learning, such as the study by Strauß and Rummel, 2021 or the tools designed and tested by Adeniran and Masthoff (2022)  called WC/GCMS (Word Count/Gini Coefficient measure of Symmetry).

## Procedure and design

Our study is a field study, which means that the design of the participation awareness tool (PAT) and the protocol for its use were done in a participatory approach, i.e., in consultation with the course instructors, i n order to take into account their needs and constraints, and not to add too much extra work for the students. It was decided that the tools should not require more than 30 minutes of use per week for 4 consecutive weeks, and that the researcher of this study would give a one-hour presentation and conclusion at the beginning and end of the course. The aim of the presentation was to explain to the students how and why to use the tool, a nd to answer any questions they might have. The conclusion aimed to describe to the students the first results regarding their actual use of the tool. CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 38 Our aim was to determine which part of the PAT had the most influence on students’ behavior, i.e., its reporting part to encourage self-regulation of participation and its visualization part to encourage socially shared regulation of participation. Two conditions were defined, one mainly focused on self-regulation of participation (condition A) and the other mainly focused on socially shared regulation of partic ipation (condition B). Groups were randomly assigned to one or the other condition. Week 1 of the study was identical for both conditions and was designed to familiarize students with the reporting tool. Only weeks 2 to 4 were subject to variation between the two conditions. For both condition, students were involved in the reporting phase for all 4 weeks of the study.

• In condition A (self-regulation, adding the visualization), students did not have access to the

visualization until the beginning of the last week (this visualization was created based on the data from the reporting phase at the end of week 3). Four groups were involved in this condition (groups 1, 2, 3 and 7, 16 students).

• In condition B (socially shared regulation, removing the visualization), students received a

visualization at the beginning of weeks 2 and 3 (these visualizations were generated from the data of the reporting phases at the end of week 1 and week 2), and this visualization was not sent to them at the beginning of the last week. They were unable to see their and the group’s participation for the final week of work. Three groups were affected by this condition (groups 4, 5 and 6, 12 students). As shown in Figure 2, this experimental protocol allowed us to compare the evol ution of the two indicators (global participation rate and Gini coefficient) between each condition, i. e., Condition A – report without visualization in Weeks 2 and 3 and report with visualization in Week 4 versus Condition B – report with visualization in Weeks 2 and 3 and report without visualization in Week 4. ## Measures and data analysis

GPR and gini coefficient The GPR (Global Participation Rate) is an individual-level indicator obtained by multiplying the different selfreported rates, i.e., participation, usefulness, praise and time. Each rate was  calculated by dividing the student's average by the group average. For example, the participation rate was obtaine d by averaging the contributions given by a participant for each of the 4 activities (production; reading and infor mation seeking; planning and coordinating; collaboration). This average was then divided by the average contribution of the whole group. The Gini coefficient (Adeniran et al., 2019; Strauß & Rummel, 2021) is a group-level measure that shows the deviation of group members' GPR from an equal distribution of GPR. It is therefore a way to see how equal the participation was in the group and to examine the effect of the visualization on the regulation of participation at group level. The coefficient can vary between 0 (equal participation) and 1 (unequal participation). Perceived use of the participation awareness tool We wanted to understand the students' experience with the PAT. To do this, in the middle and at the end of the study (i.e., end of week 2 and week 4), students were asked to give their opini on (1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree) on a series of 6 items that aimed at assessing the perceived use of the PAT at the individual level ("I used the tool to change my participation to the task") and at the group level ("We used the tool to balance participation within the group "), as well as the perceived usefulness of the tool (e.g., "It was useful to report my participation”; see Table 4 for full list of items).

## Results

For the present paper, we have chosen to remove Group 4 (condition B) from our  analysis because this group made an atypical use of the PAT. Indeed, the group members agreed to answ er the questionnaire together and identically during the reporting phase in order to obtain a Gini coefficient of 0. Evolution of the Gini coefficient (group level) The Gini coefficient was very different between all groups at the beginning of th e study. Values ranged from 6.88% to 31.99% after the first week. We therefore analyzed the evolution of this coefficient over the weeks.

• Between weeks 1 and 2. The Gini coefficient increased for the groups in condition A (reporting only;

+26.30%) while it decreased for the groups in condition B (reporting +visualization; -3.64%).

• Between weeks 2 and 3. The Gini coefficient decreased for groups in both conditions, and this

decrease was slightly larger for condition B (reporting +visualization; -12.95%) than for condition A (reporting only; -8.42%).

• Between weeks 3 and 4. A visualization was sent to groups of the condition A and the Gini coefficient

decreased by 10.68%. At the same time, the visualization was removed for groups of the Condition B and the Gini coefficient increased by 19.89%.

To sum up, each time the visualization was added, the Gini coefficient decre ased and when it was removed, it increased. This suggests a positive effect of the visualizati on part of the PAT on the regulation of participation at the group level.

## Discussion and conclusion

In this field study, students used a participation awareness tool (PAT) e ach week for 4 weeks. This tool asked them to self-assess their participation (reporting phase) and offered them  a visualization of the distribution of participation (visualization phase). We studied the effect of the PAT on the self-regulation of participation as well as on the distribution of participation within the group. Results regarding the evolution of the Gini coefficients show that asking student s to self-assess their participation is not sufficient to reduce the participation asymmetry within the group. The level of participation inequality decreases mainly when students receive a visualization of participation. More interestingly, the results show that removing the visualization contributes to an increase in participa tion inequality. This highlights the importance of showing individual contributions to all group members in order to support individual accountability (Johnson et al., 2014) and encourage the regulation of participation at the group level. The results concerning the evolution of the overall participation rate (GPR)  per student show that it increases with the introduction of the visualization in week 2 while it decrea ses with the introduction of the visualization in week 4. This suggests that the influence of the visualization on the self-regulation of participation depends on when this visualization is introduced during the group work. When the visualization is introduced late (week 4), it seems to cause the more active students to reduce their  participation and the less active students to increase it. In other terms, the decrease in GPR following the late in troduction of visualization would therefore support a more equal distribution of participation; it would increase the motivation of the less active students and reduce the problem of social loafing. When the visualization is introduced early (week 2), its influence on the less active students seems to be less important. This point should probably be analyzed further to understand why the less active students seem less inclined to regulate their behavior when they are confronted with a visualization of their contribution and that of their partners at an early stage of group work. Another interesting result is that when the visualization is introduced at the beginning of the collaboration, its later removal from the participation support system (week 4) does not affect the overall participation rates, which nevertheless continue to increase. Finally, although these results support a positive effect of the PAT on the regul ation of participation, students expressed that they did not have a positive experience of the tool. This raises some limitations that need to be recognized. In particular, as suggested by the students themselves, the information provided by the tool was not easy to understand and interpret, despite our intervention at the beginning of the study. Finally, some students were concerned that the PAT would lead to tension and conflict in the group. This is a legitimate concern because the visualization part could lead to students evaluating their abilities and performance in comparison to the other group members. This social comparison could have a detrimental effect on the gr oup climate and performance, as it could lead students to view their partners as a potential threat to their own competence (Darnon et al., 2007). To sum up, the results encourage the design of group awareness tools to promot e equal participation in the group. Students seem to benefit more from the visualization component of th e tool than from the selfassessment component. The influence of visualization on the regulation of partic ipation seems to be more important for the most active students, leading them to reduce their contribution and probably to delegate tasks. Its influence on the behavior of the less active students seems to be dif ferent and dependent on when it is introduced during the collaboration. Visualization motivates them to make more effort when it is introduced late. This last result probably requires further analysis. As Strauß and Rummel (2021) have suggested, it is questionable to what extent PAT could have a negative impact on students' sense of self-effic acy, especially when it provides information at the beginning of the collaboration that increases social compar ison within the group. We can therefore hypothesize that visualization, when introduced at an early stage of group wo rk, may not be well received by the less active and may lead them to develop defensive strategies (e.g., external attributions to explain the reasons for their lesser effort), or even reduce their capacity for action. This reinforces the idea that there is a need to better understand the relationship between self-efficacy and equal participa tion in groups, as well as to study the extent to which the effect of group awareness tools on particip ation varies according to students' selfefficacy. Finally, the negative feedback from students suggests that group awareness tools should be designed on the basis of a participatory and iterative approach in which students are included from the beginning.

In conclusion, this study has methodological limitations (e.g., small number of parti cipants and groups; data mortality since some participants were no longer motivated to report their data, atypical use of the PAT) and could be expanded in the future to provide objective data such as chat content or student learning analy tics (e.g., their production activity in the learning management system or a shared document). With a larger number of participants, statistical tests could be conducted to generalize the results. However, these limitations are specific to a field study and we believe there is a need to better understand the ef fects of group awareness tools in ecologically valid learning contexts, in our case a long-term group work conducted in a hybrid context, i.e., alternately inside and outside the classroom. Our initial aim was to use a single-case experimental design approach. However, we wanted to ensure that the experimental protocol wa s carried out in agreement with the CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 42 teachers of the course and according to their needs and constraints. The stre ngths of this research lie in the fact that it focuses on participation in four activities specific to a group project, in the attempt to disentangle the effect of self-reporting from the effect of visualization, and in the study of the extent to which the effect of visualization varies according to the time of its introduction during collaboration.
