# How Transnational Youth Make Sense of Multiscalar Citizenship Towards Climate Justice

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how transnational youth make sense of climate change across personal, local, and global contexts. Drawing upon critical participatory design-based methods, we bear witness to how youths’ embodied experiences, including transnationa l identity and cultural, ecological, and geopolitical positioning, become onto-epistemological resources for climate sense-making. Through a case study, we illustrate how one youth connected scientific climate knowledge to war experiences, framing war as climate catastrophe encompassing increased carbon footprints and ecological devastation, displacement, cultural erosion, and trauma. Youth draw on their vulnerability to deepen understanding of climate causes and consequences, while fostering empathy-driven solidarity and advocacy. They amplify silenced voices and call for collective responsibility in confronting intertwined climate and geopolitical injustices. Youth’s multiscalar experiences constitute legitimate knowledge that expands and critiques dominant climate narratives, offering onto-epistemological testimony that reframes climate justice scholarship and pedagogy.

Reconceptualizing climate change education as a justice issue is increasingly important given the disproportionate burdens of climate change on minoritized populations. School science, however, often privileges canonical knowledge and hierarchical, traditional approaches to teaching and learning climate change, with little discussion on whose  knowledge counts, how lived experiences shapes one’s understanding of climate change, and how minoritized voices can be centered. Science education remains silent about disparities that underlie social challenges (Lee & Grapin, 2022). By taking a “neutral ” stance and using “sanitized” and “settled” onto-epistemologies of socio-scientific issues (Bang & Marin, 2015), children miss opportunities to engage with the complexity of these issues to address the profound injustices in their causes, impacts, and pol itical response. Furthermore, climate justice education and research that center transnational youth voices, perspectives and experiences are minimal. These youth are expected to absorb western science uncritically, without recognition of who they are or what they bring. There is a greater need for cross cultural understanding and collaboration among students to create shared responsibility and solidarity among youth with different backgrounds (Datta, 2024). New pedagogical approaches are needed that call for interdisciplinary approaches that center equity, justice, and action (Lee & Grapin, 2025). When applied to transnational youth, these frameworks can reimagine climate justice education by positioning identities and testimonies at the core of pedagogy and scholarship. In this study we ask: 1) How transnational youths’ multiscalar ways of being inform their situated and embodied understandings to make sense of climate change; 2) In what ways does cri tical witnessing enable transnational youth to transform embodied experiences of geopolitical conflict into climate justice advocacy? Theoretical framing Climate justice education that expands what counts as legitimate science knowledge, challenges structural inequities, and positions youth as agents advances our understanding of climate justice pedagogy and scholarship (Clark, 2024). Bang & Medin (2010) use community-based ways of knowing and being as legitimate forms of knowledge, as necessary for minoritized students to engage in transformative science discourse. Building on this, Morales-Doyle (2017) used youth participatory research to demonstrate that minoritized students who challenge structural and environmental racism exhibit higher academic achievement, and learn critical academic skills that transform them into agents of change. Finally, welcoming and positioning students as legitimate members means giving them the authority to determine what counts as knowledge through their testimony to actively restructure power dynamics towards more just ends (Barton & Tan, 2019). Transnational youth identities and relationships are defined by mobility, language, and politics across multiple countries, allowing them to maintain social and affective connections and consciousness across multiple scales (Levitt, 2009; Skerrett, 2020). We draw on onto-epistemologies, which recognize that ways of knowing and being are intertwined and constitute legitimate forms of knowledge, frame learning as relational among persons, spaces, and across time (Vissoughi et al, 2023). Through this lens, knowledge is dynamic and hybridized. 1742 When applied to transnational youth, knowledge is made and remade across multiple scales. We understand multiscalar as referring to “sociospatial spheres of practice that are constituted in relationship to each other within and across various hierarchies and networks of power, while accounting for the complexity among a host of sociopolitical, cultural, and environmental issues” (Kubow & Jin, 2023, p. 2). We extend this definition through an onto-epistemological funds of knowledge lens to consider how transnational youth bring, interpret, and mobilize accumulated knowledge across global, national, and local scales. We also draw upon critical witnessing to foreground how youth seek to reclaim themselves by narrating their experiences as empowered storytellers to disrupt damage-centered narratives while also giving insight into how educators can support them (Adair, 2023). These testimonies allow for critical consciousness about social injustice, and challenge dominant narratives that omit minoritized stories. Hearing these testimonies allows listeners to develop political awareness and question injustice, leading to advocacy (Figuero & Barrales 2021). Taken together, we argue that transnational youth who engage in multiscalar, relational sensemaking can use their testimonies to produce knowledge about climate change, adding to climate discourse and fostering civic agency. Our study was completed in a large suburban middle school that serves an immigrant community, with over 95% Middle Eastern students, and 92% from low-income families. This study is part of a larger critical participatory ethnography design project where we collaborated with two 8th grade teachers, totaling seven classrooms of minoritized students and families to co-design and implement a climate justice unit. During the unit, students and parents engaged in various activities to explore their thoughts, feelings, and experiences related to climate change, including a “climate change map” using drawings to express what most mattered to them and what they wanted others to understand. Data were gathered through student and parent artifacts, interviews, field notes, and feedback conversations conducted in English and/or Arabic. Our research team acknowledges that this study was shaped by our shared commitments to equity and social justice, bringing experience as researchers and educators in justice-oriented science education. Two researchers are Arabic-speaking, one Lebanese Ame rican and one recent Palestinian immigrant. Data analysis employed open coding to bear critical witness to how transnational youth make sense of climate change by: 1) recognizing lived experiences as onto-epistemological resources that provide firsthand accounts of climate change; 2) integrating personal, cultural, socio-political, and scientific perspectives; 3) negotiating multiscalar (local, national, global) experiences while critiquing dominant narratives; and 4) fostering multiscalar citizenship to address inequities and take meaningful action toward justice. To illustrate these themes, we present “Husam’s story” as “information-rich cases that manifest the phenomenon of interest intensely” (Patton, 2002, p. 234). Born in the US but raised in Lebanon, Husam moved back during the previous year. In Lebanon, he attended a private school and developed an interest in science, supported by his mother’s work as a nurse. Husam was twice displaced, with his relocation prompted by the war in the Gaza Strip that expanded into a regional conflict involving Lebanon. Frequent missile attacks damaged his home in their village of ﺗﺒﻨﯿﻦTebnin), forcing his family to flee to Beirut. After further damage to his apartment in Beirut, they eventually resettled in the US.

Critical Witnessing of war as climate catastrophe In this section, we show how Husam links the destruction caused by war to multiple layers of climate catastrophe, including increased carbon footprints and ecological devastation, cultural erasure and heritage loss, and psychological trauma.

The carbon footprint of warfare. Husam’s climate change map (see Figure 1) illustrates how an imminent missile strike devastates the area, highlighting emissions from the missiles, labeled as “smoke.” He identifies missile smoke as a source of atmospheric CO 2 that intensifies the greenhouse effect and contributes to rising global temperature. H e explains, “[smoke] increases the climate change, like pollution, because there's a lot of smoke, that means there's a lot of carbon dioxide. So it increases the global warming[...]. Because the CO 2 levels are trapped in the atmosphere. So it's like a blanket covering the Earth, [...] So a huge amount of carbon dioxide can, like, overheat the Earth.” Husam uses his firsthand experience of war to describe its intersection with climate change. Through his narrative, he engages in critical witnessing that surfaces the unseen atmospheric consequences of the war, inviting his peers to recognize how missile attacks increase global warming. His testimony challenges dominant Western science discourse that predominantly ties carbon emissions to personal and industrial use. Husam reframes carbon emissions as products of warfare through bombs and military jets, revealing an interesting dichotomy where, for children in the west, carbon consumption is a choice. For Husam and many other minoritized youth, in the Global South, it is lived and imposed reality.

Ecosystem disruption, cultural erasure and heritage loss. Husam brings transnational, cultural, and ecological onto-epistemologies to demonstrate how war devastates  ecosystems and disrupts the cultural heritage of his homeland. In his drawing, Husam refers to “toxic chemicals” that c and damage local agriculture. Although he does not name the substance, his awareness aligns with documented reports of white phosphorous munitions used throughout South Lebanon, including Husam’s village. Such exposure causes physical harm, including burns, organ failure, and food and water contamination, and long-term ecological degradation  (Kallab & Mouawad, 2023). Husam explains, ‘We have a land, we have some زﯾﺘﻮن[olives] my dad went there and there is no more زﯾﺘﻮن[olives]...After one or two months he was supposed to start taking زﯾﺘﻮن[olives] and make olive oil, but the ground is polluted. [We] won't be able to get زﯾﺘﻮن[olives] for two or three years.’ For Husam, olive cultivation represents culture and continuity, and disruption through loss. Olive trees, native to the Levant region, are powerful symbols of identity and resilience, with some living up to 4,000 years, linking generations to the land and Mediterranean landscape. Through his narration, he bears critical witness to the intersecting losses of cultural and land heri tage, illustrating that knowledge emerges through embodied experience, and that being and knowing are deeply intertwined. Enduring psychological war trauma associated with airplanes.  Husam provides testimony that speaks to the psychological and physical trauma of war  as embodied forms of climate knowledge. He recounts multiple experiences of “very heavy rockets” attacks, including one after prayer services: “It [the ground] started to shake us. We had just left [the mosque], from night prayer, we were startled. ” In another attack,  Husam tells his experience with two supersonic planes, “we heard the ﺟﺪارﺻﻮت[sonic boom] it's going in a speed more than the speed of the air. So it cracks the air. Then the planes start throwing weird stuff, and we start running.” Husam’s experiences with aerial attacks are embodied, making his knowledge about emissions and noise pollution inseparable from his reality of missile attacks and sonic booms. The topic of airplanes carries opposing meanings for Husam as he moves between Lebanon and the US. D rawing on his transnational experiences,  he interprets the same phenomenon through different geopolitical and psychological lenses. In Lebanon, airplanes symbolized violence, death, and displacement. Thus, he struggled for two years to adjust to the sound of commercial jetliners in the US (Husam resides near a major city adjacent to an airport that functions as a regional hub). He shared, “when I'm like, asleep and I hear the airplanes coming down to the airpor t…I remember how I was sleeping in Lebanon and hearing the…fighting airplanes coming down to bomb in Lebanon.” This association triggered anxiety as he points out, “in the first two days, I used to get, like, nervous and stuff, but like, I got used to it.” Husam’s story reveals how critical witnessing of airplane emissions, noise pollution, and atmospheric disruption can mean forced displacement and loss, making climate science personal and trauma-informed. 1744 Multi-scalar sensemaking catalyzes empathy-driven solidarity Husam makes sense of climate change across multiple scales-geographical, environmental, social, explicitly demonstrating the global impact of climate catastrophe across borders. Husam sets his climate change drawings in Palestine to express solidarity with those still enduring violence, destruction, and displacement, he states, “I was describing Palestine, how it is getting bombed so frequently…and how people, like, can't live in Palestine no more.” His narrative, informed by his transnational identity and  embodied experiences of war, transforms his critical witnessing into empathy-driven solidarity. Through his map, he links the unlivable reality of war to atmospheric CO 2, inviting others to share and experience his reality, and empathize with those facing harm. To extend his testimony, Husam relates climate change causes to global inequities, emphasizing the disproportionate burden borne by minoritized populations. When asked if he could change his map, he replied, “I would have added… people sitting [in] their office and just pushing the button so that …[w e] can go down and destroy the house. And I could have put …the temperature is increasing, like now it is like 78 degrees  [and] after the missile drops, it will be 80 or 85. ” Husam links warfar e to rising global temperatures  while critiquing the asymmetry of power and privilege in modern warfare where destruction is remotely enacted through drones and airstrikes, leaving minoritized communities to face both the immediate human toll and the long-term ecological devastation.

Climate justice as transnational advocacy Husam transforms from witness to advocate for climate justice, connecting his testimony to a call for action that addresses the environmental toll of war, the conflict’s global climate impact, and the need to amplify silenced voices while supporting vulnerable nations. Husam wants his peers to understand the environmental and human cost of war, as he left behind family and friends who are still suffering. He contrasts his experiences of loss with his peers who, he feels, think briefly about war before turning back to their cell phone games. He explains, “Most [of] the kids don't even know what war is. They just think that people are dying, and [then] responding like their games and stuff. But…when I got, like, the first ﺷﮭﯿﺪ[martyr] from my family, I knew what real war was. He was my mom's cousin.” (In Islamic contexts,  ﺷﮭﯿﺪ[martyr] can refer to not only those who die in battle, but also those who die as a result of aggression or injustice).  Husam is indignant at what he sees as his peers’ performative concerns and shares his testimony to ensure they must confront the reality of war, transforming grief and memory into emotions, knowledge, solidarity and advocacy, bridging personal experience with civic awareness. Through his storytelling, Husam designates himself as a voice for the voiceless, recognizing how many are unable to speak their truths, “I’m showing how other people are getting affected by war. And like, I am saying, I am giving the things [speaking, giving witness] that a lot of people wanted to give but they couldn't give.” Understanding this silencing, he takes on the responsibility of bearing witness for those who cannot through multiple lenses: he reflects on the psychological toll of war, while also  emphasizing the unseen intersection of war and climate change. This testimony becomes both a personal and ecological plea to end the war for the sake of the people and the land. He emphasizes, “war isn't just something that you can pass through. It's really affected. People are dying from this thing.” He highlights that the resultant environmental destruction and emissions extend beyond Gazan and Lebanese borders, contributing to broader global environmental consequences: “it affecting every country around Gaza. Because air pollution doesn't stick in only one place.” Aware that the US holds significant influence over the duration and severity of the conflict, Husam hopes his testimony can catalyze solidarity within his community and inspire collective advocacy for peace and climate justice. Yet Husam's vision of climate justice acknowledges global pollution asymmetry and calls for wealthier nations to take responsibility, stating, “in Africa and in Middle East, we have a lot of pollution. That makes me feel sad, because the wealthy countries should send some money to the poor countries so they can get better.” of understanding climate change. Embodied experiences of war, ecological loss, cultural erasure, and trauma serve as onto-epistemic resources that challenge dominant notions of legitimate knowledge. By witnessing how war, displacement, and environmental instability intersect, youth reveal how minoritized communities make sense of global crises. Youths’ testimonies position them as active knowledge producers, underscoring the need for justiceoriented climate education that values relational, situated, and affective ways of knowing. Recognizing transnational youths’ lived experiences as legitimate resources can transform research and pedagogy through critical witnessing and collective action. Such critical witnessing fosters empathy, solidarity, and advocacy, advancing climate education that  decenters Western pedagogies that frame climate consequences as matters of choice, and foregrounds how climate catastrophes are unevenly inflicted on minoritized communities. 1745 Adair, L., Kim, G.
