# Visioning Schooling in 2050: Analyzing Pre-Service Teachers’ Futures Consciousness

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Amid global ecological and societal crises, the role of education and teachers in driving societies towards more sustainable futures is ever more crucial. To drive desired changes, one must first be able to vision them. This study explores pre-service teachers' futures consciousness by analyzing their visions of a desired school day as a teacher in 2050 through the 5 dimensions of futures consciousness: agency beliefs, systems perceptions, time perspectives, openness to alternatives, and concern for others. 100 pre-service teachers’ short essays, consisting of their desired future visions and backcasting tasks exploring the steps to their desired futures, were analyzed through an abductive thematic analysis. Findings indicate that pre-service teachers manifest all dimensions of futures consciousness. Agency beliefs and systems perceptions are manifested more frequently in the backcasting task. The study aims to develop teacher education to encourage teachers to drive societal change.

## Introduction

Given the pivotal role of education in driving societal change, teachers must serve as agents of change, th at is catalysts for fostering more responsible societies.  To achieve this, educators require both Futures Li teracy (FL) (Häggström & Schmidt, 2021)  and Futures Consciousness (FC) (Ahvenharju et al., 2018). FL encompasses the ability to understand, anticipate, and prepare for future transformations and challenges using imagina tion, ultimately aiming to promote more sustainable socie ties. There is a growing body of operationalization s and empirical work of FL  (Bol & de Wolf, 2023; Toivonen et al., 2021), inclu ding the model of FC (Ahvenharju et al., 2018). FC, according to Ahvenharju et al. (2018), is the skill to imagine the future, including one 's agency beliefs, systems perceptions, openness to alternatives, time perspectives, and concern for others. Previous research indicates that FL activities promote imagination of alternatives, navigation of uncertainties, and systems thinking, however, cultivating this is a complex endeavor (Hyy ppä et al., 2024; Laherto & Rasa, 2022). There is also a research gap in empirical investigations of educato rs’ FL. The aim of this study was to explore pre-service teachers’ FC by analyzing their visions of a desired future school day as a teacher in 2050. Bridging the academic fields of education and futur es studies, this study extends previous research into a teacher education perspective. To further understand how pre-service teachers vision desired futures and  how they understand their abilities to impact them, the concept of FC is here contextualized into educational settings. The five dimensions of FC; agency beliefs, systems perceptions, time perspectives, openness to alternatives, and concern for others (Ahvenharju et al., 2018), are in this study utilized to explore  pre-service teachers’ futures visioning and backcasting tasks abductively. The research question is: How do pre-service teachers’ visions of a desired future manifest different dimensions of futures consciousness?

## Theoretical framework

Futures consciousness, a core analytical framework in this study, is a way of applying futur es-oriented ways of thinking into daily use and both thinking of the fu ture and retaining a consideration of the future effects of all thought processes (Ahvenharju et al., 2021). This study builds on Ahvenharju et al.’s (2018) framework of five core dimensions of FC, exploring it from an educational context: Agency beliefs, at its core, centers around the question of how much we trust in our capability to impact the future (Ahvenharju et al., 2021). Agency beliefs are  defined by Ahvenharju  et al. (2018) as an individual’s self-efficacy and self-responsibility. The dimension also revolves around aspects of motivation for and control of the future, including action and proactivity in cre ating futures (Ahvenharju et al., 2018). Within an educational context, understanding the role of futures visioning allows teachers to explore their agency beliefs. Recent studies lay basis on FL as a core pre-requisite for transformative agency (Rasa, 2023; Rasa et al., 2023; Varpanen et al., 2022). Various aspects of t eachers’ professional agency ha ve been widely studied to understand, among others, the changes in teachers’ roles, but previous studies lack  the long-term futures-oriented scope (Varpanen et al., 2022), here explored through their FC.

Systems perceptions  circles around the key  thought of how we see the interconnectedness of var ious systems as well as the complexities and causalities  within them  (Ahvenharju et al., 2021). The dimension also considers matters of brea dthwise thinking and holism, as well as understandi ngs of both short and long-term consequences of actions. Systems perceptions are also considered  through wicked problems and the interdependence of broad  challenges (Ahvenharju et al., 2018). Systems perceptions and systems thinking have been recently increasingly explored in education al contexts, notably exploring systems thinking in sustainability and futures education  (Hofman-Bergholm, 2018; Hyyppä et al., 2024; Uskola & Puig,  2023), where systems thinking skills have been found to both increase during futures-oriented learning activities, and their application in learning has also been shown to benefit sustainability conceptualizations and transformations. Openness to alternatives revolves around the key concept of how fixed or open we are towards different possible futures  as well as how much we challenge norms  and assumptions  (Ahvenharju et al., 2021). The dimension considers aspects of certainty and uncertainty, skepticism, and anticipation towards futures, focusing on whether futures (or the future) are seen as sing ular or plural possibilities (Ahvenharju et al., 2018). Openness to alternatives considers alternatives both within the future itsel f, as well as in comparison to the present day. Within an educational context, openness to alternatives within teacher education a nd the teacher’s role can be perceived in studies on teachers’ adaptive  and flexible  expertise, requiring openness to alternatives as a pre-requisite to develop their professional role. This study relates the previously independent persp ectives of FC to frameworks of teachers’ adaptive expertise, perceived through lenses of  novel problem solving, creative and critical thinking, multidimensional observation, and innovating with stabilized routines (Munson et al., 2021; Pelgrim et al., 2022; Van Tartwijk et al., 2017).

Time perspectives entails how and how much we orient ourselves to the future (Ahvenharju et al., 2021). The dimension revolves around senses of time as well as temporal and situational awareness, understanding how time frames connect  and using forward thinking as a way of crisis preve ntion and management  (Ahvenharju et al., 2018). Time perspectives explores to what degree future visions are process or result oriented. Concern for others centers around our moral and ethical values in considering other entities around us, both physically and temporally in varying proximities (Ahvenharju et al., 2021). The dimension entails extents of participation, altruism, societal and cultural diversity as well as a broader care for both humanity as well as other living beings around us (Ahvenharju et al., 2018).

In this study, FC is analyzed from the context of two tasks: a future s visioning task and a backcasting task. Visioning desired futures aims to evoke alternative, critical, open-ended, and discussive texts and thoughts, similar to utopian methodologies (Levitas, 2013)  or pedagogies of utopias (Rajala et al., 2022). While related as approaches, this study refrains from using the conc ept of utopias due to their distant “fairytale” con notation, and instead, focuses on visioning desired futures to maintain a sense of possibility for chan ge through participants’ own action and agency. This study extends those results into a teacher education perspective (similar to Bacher et al., 2024), deepening previous research on how teachers’ futures visions exhibit changes in their ways of thinking. Backcasting (contrary to foreca sting) tasks intend to further explore futures visi ons through tasks on understanding the intermediate steps, actions, deci sions, or events necessary to achieve wished change s. By building on futures visions through backcasting tas ks, participants can position themselves into a fut ure they envision and look back on the steps that have led there (Dreborg, 1996; Rajala et al., 2022). Futures visioning and backcasting tasks have  been shown to increase students’ agency to change f utures, imagine alternatives, and navigate uncertainties (Laherto & Rasa, 2022).

## Methodology

This study analyzed 100 pre-service teachers’ short essays, written during a futures thinking workshop held in a compulsory first-year educational sciences course at the University of Helsinki, Finland. The essays consisted of responses to two prompts on Task 1; a desired future workday as a teacher in 2050, and Task 2; the changes and actors needed to achieve that future.  The futures visioning Task 1 prompted participants to consider pedagogical solutions, pupils and students, and potential conflicts, among  others. T he backcasting Task 2 prompted participants to write a text on the steps from their envisioned future back to the present day. Participants’ essays consisted of approximately one paragraph response s per futures visioning T ask 1 and backcasting Task 2. Participants responded in their native language and all citations used have been translated from Finnish to English. An abductive thematic analysis, similar to Thompson (2022), was conducted to search for manifestations of the 5 dimensions of FC apparent in the essays. The essays, consisting of both Task 1 and Task 2,  were coded and categorized based on the FC model in an educational context.  The FC framework (Ahvenharju et al., 2018), originally employed quantitatively, is here further explored through a qualitative ap proach on the 5 dimensions. Essays were coded into the 5 dimensions of FC in 1-4-sentence long segments that expressed a single continuing thought or theme. A segment may have been coded into multiple dimen sions, and as such one future vision or backcasting task can include multiple dimensions. The analysis first explored manifestations of FC in the futures visioning and backcasting combined. Finally, the analysis  searched for  differences and similarities in the manifestations of FC between the futures visioning Task 1 and backcasting Task 2 by comparing frequencies in the manifestations of each of the 5 dimensions between the two tasks.

## Findings

The 5 dimensions of futures consciousness Our results indicate that all 5 dimensions of FC are manifested in pre-service teachers’ desired futures visions to varying degrees. While themes varied, recurring perspectives on each of the dimensions were visible in the essays. Agency beliefs of pre-service teachers are manifested in how they see themselves as able t o impact and be responsible for one’s everyday tasks, yet also in how they place agency behind larger changes in broad political or technological developments. For instance, one participant wrote of high motivation towards the profession and towards impacting the surrounding community, “teachers themselves are motivated to work, want to b e in the profession and constantly contribute to the school culture”, while another hoped educators to be a part  of technological educational developments,  “...breaking down the language barrier will certainly b e influenced by technological pedagogical developments, so I hope that educators will be involved in thinking about what kind of tools are needed”.

Systems perceptions  can be seen through pre-service teachers’ considerations of the interconnec tions between school and society. Pre-service teachers also consider the systems of change, and the relations of different parties involved in creating changes in the future school. For instance, one participant wrote about socioecological systems, “[we have increased] understanding of the important connections between humans and nature”. Another participant wrote about overcoming geographical boundaries through technological devel opments, “pupils and students can study with smart glasses in Finland for example, even if they are physically in Australia. The world no longer seems to have boundaries.” Openness to alternatives is manifested when pre-service teachers either extrapolate current trends into the future leaving little room for change or describe changes to the school day of the future, showing consideration of other possibilities. Also, pre-service teachers manifest  topics pertaining to the flexibility and variety of decisions present within their everyday work, descr ibing varying degrees of freedom present in their w ork. For instance, one participant spoke of a need for open-mindedness within the modern world, “a changing world needs changing ways of thinking and acting, otherwise you  cannot succeed!” Another participant spoke of alternatives through a nostalgia for past ways of educating,  “…to go back to a more traditional way of teaching, where you observe and do things yourself a lot.” Time perspectives in pre-service teachers’ future visions are manifested when they describe the future either as a fixed result or an ever-changing continuum made up of the changes that could have occurred along the way. Pre-service teachers also manifest uncertainty of, and hope and trust towards, the future. For instance, one participant thought about changes along the way, and wanting to change even i n the future, “after more than 25 years, the world and the students will have changed and I would like to be informed even then, so that I can keep up with things.” Concern for others is manifested through perspectives of teachers’ psychological wellbeing, overcoming social barriers and inequities, and school as a saf e environment for all parties involved.  For instance, one participant wrote about students’ social relations,  “even though today's students are digitally savvy, I am still happy as a teacher that my students are also able to be present with each other”, whilst another believed that the school will be a bridging place for all people,  “students will come from many different cultures, and I hope that the school will succeed in building links between them.” Futures visioning and backcasting Drawing together the analysis of each dimension of FC, the study explored the differences in manifestation s of FC between the futures visioning Task 1 and the backcasting Task 2. ## Discussion and conclusions

As teachers hold a c rucial role in guiding generations to come, they mu st be encouraged to not only react and adapt to change, but also drive societal changes. In actively shaping the competencies of students and their future skills, teachers carry a vital responsibility in un derstanding how they themselves view futures and how they see themselves and others as able to impact more sustainable, desired futures. Paving way on a thus far undervalued intersection of teacher education and futures studies research, the aim of this study was to explore how pre-service teachers’ futures visions exhibit different dimensions of Futures Consciousness. Collectively, all dimensions of FC; Agency beliefs, Systems perceptions, Openness to alternatives, Time perspectives, and Concern for others, were manifested in pre-service teachers’ future visions. In line with findings by Varpanen et al. (Varpanen et al., 2022), the essays evoked broad considerations of teachers’ Agency beliefs in creating desired changes. Moreover, participants considered Systems perceptions through causal relationships and complex systems behind change, supporting the findi ngs of Uskola and Puig (2023). In line with Pelgrim et al.’s

(2022) conceptualizations of adaptive expertise, essays manifested Openness to alternatives through explorations

of changes to the teachers’ role, skills, and flexibility in their future profession. The findings point to advantages in employing futures visioning as a method to foster reflections on teachers’ adaptive expertise and agency. In exploring the differences between futures visioning and backcasting tasks, results indicated that Task 1 most frequently manifested Openness to alternatives, Concern for others, and T ime perspectives, while Task 2 showed most manifestations of Agency beliefs  and Systems perceptions. This study supports the findings of Hyyppä et al. (2024) in showing that Futures Literacy activities foster different dimensions of FC and help deepen those skills when future visions are developed with further tasks and reflections, such as backcasting tasks. Futures visioning indicates promising results in encouraging teachers  to step out of their ordinary thinking guided by a very single narrative media and instead focus on exploring their desired futures.  As indicated by Figure 1, while futures visioning exhibits all 5 dimensions of FC, it is crucial to highlight the strongly increased manifestations of agency beliefs within the backcasting Task 2, in line with findings of Rajala et al. (2022) expressing the importance of backcasting. Building on futures visi oning tasks with backcasting tasks allows  for more in-depth understandings of the systemic changes and the driving forces  of communal and individual agency in creating desired futures, supporting the findings of Bacher et al. (2024). The result points to the importance of not only creating opportunities for visioning desired futures, but more so taking the further steps in exploring how t hat change was achieved and by whom. Through the increased manifestations of pre-service teachers’ agency beliefs when further elaborating on their futures vision through backcasting, this study highlights the importance of futures visioning as a tool to foster pre-service teachers’ understandings of their roles as societal change agents. The results of this study are used to develop various activities to foster pre-and in-service teachers’ FL skills. Employing futures visioning as a tool to analyze teachers’ FC shows promising directions in emphasizing teachers’ voices in educational change.  By exploring the connections between pre-service teachers’ FC and the ways in which they see themselves as able to impact desired future changes, the research aims to develop teacher education to encourage teachers to act as societal change agents in and for the future. This requires long-term FL regarding the future role of the teacher and the school, thus far an undervalued area in teacher education.
