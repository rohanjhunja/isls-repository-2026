# Are We Still Teaching the Same Learning Sciences? A Ten-Year Follow-Up Analysis of Graduate Program Curricula

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study provides an updated overview of the concepts and methods in the Learning Sciences (LS) graduate programs, addressing the question posed by Sommerhoff et al. (2018) about what we teach in the LS. Analyzing 88 programs across institutions, we found a quantitative growth in programs. Most are concentrated in North America, with fewer in Europe and minimal representation in Australia and Asia. Key concepts include digital technology to support learning, learning in formal contexts, and the design of learning environments. Programs employ methods, including both quantitative and qualitative approaches, with design-based research and learning analytics being prevalent. Programs teaching non-parametric statistics, collaborative learning, and computational modeling showed the highest homogeneity. A decade later, we see that some former core concepts of LS have shifted to the periphery, while peripheral concepts, like diversity and equity, and methods are merging with the core, reflecting the LS as an innovative, dynamic community of practice.

## Problem statement

Interest in the Learning Sciences (LS) has substant ially increased  among academics, leading to the growth of university programs and the number of programs iden tifying themselves as LS (Sommerhoff et al., 2018). The Network of Academic Programs in the Learning Scienc es (NAPLeS) currently lists 39 universities globall y as part of its international community, which facilitates networking and collaboration am ong learning sciences programs to enhance the learning and teaching of the LS (International Society of the Learning Sciences [ISLS], 2024). The LS integrate  research on teaching and learning within multiple disciplines, such as mathematics, science, the arts, and computer science (Tabak & Radinsky, 2015), drawing on insights from psychology, instructional design, and cognitive science to offe r diverse perspectives on teaching and learning in real-world contexts and methods to support teaching and learni ng (Hoadley, 2018; Hoadley & Van Haneghan, 2011). A thorough understanding of LS concepts and methods i s essential for grasping both the current landscape  and potential future directions. These LS concepts and methods may ev olve depending on the evolving diversity of methods researchers apply across disciplines (Nathan et al., 2016). Previous studies have examin ed various aspects of the LS programs, including geographical locations (Packer & Maddox, 2016; Sommerhoff et al., 2018), curricula content (Sommerhoff et al., 2018), and challenges in mainta ining a common core in the LS (Nathan et al., 2016).

Furthermore, topics like promoting equity and diver sity have been raised within the LS (Famaye & Adisa, 2024). A document analysis by Sommerhoff et al. (2018) of 75 graduate programs (including findings until July 2015) indicated that these programs span 45 di sciplines, the most common being computer science, psychology, and natural sciences. Programs prominen tly highlighted concepts such as technology-supported learning, learning environment design and scaffoldi ng, cognition and metacognition, and methods like d esignbased research (DBR) and basic statistics. The high level of alignment on concepts across LS programs suggested a substantial coherence within the LS (Sommerhoff e t al., 2018). A more recent analysis of the International Society of Learning Sciences  proceedings highlighted a strong use of DBR and par ticipatory methods. Core concepts in the analyzed proceedings included proje ct-based learning, embodied interaction, and game-based learning, suggesting developments in the focus of the LS in the last years (Famaye & Adisa, 2024). Furthermore, the rise of digital and online learning, accelerate d by the COVID-19 pandemic, has transformed educational practices and demands. Emerging technological advan cements and methodologies, including simulations, augmented reality, virtual reality, and artificial intelligence, are reshaping the landscape of LS. However, further research is needed to understand the impact of thes e technological advancements and developments in th e LS community on the concepts and methods of the LS graduate programs.

## Research questions

In replicating Sommerhoff et al. (2018), we address  the following research questions to update our cur rent understanding of LS programs and their curricula and capture recent developments in the LS more accurately:

1. Where are graduate learning sciences programs locat ed, and which disciplines are involved in teaching

the LS?

2. What are the core concepts and methods taught in graduate LS programs?

3. What concepts and methods are explicitly emphasized by the graduate LS programs?

4. Is there a core of more homogeneous graduate LS programs def ined by specific concepts or

methodological approaches?

We compare the present study's findings to those of Sommerhoff et al. (2018). The present study was preregistered as part of a larger project. The preregistration, a nalysis scripts, coding scheme, and quantitative da taset can be found at https://osf.io/k4mhb/?view_only=47ca04a160dd4382b005e5319209e758. For RQ2, RQ3, and RQ4, we additionally conducted exploratory comparative anal yses of NAPLeS-member graduate programs versus non-member graduate programs.

## Procedure

First, we conducted an internet search using the keywords “(“learning science*” OR “sciences of learning”) AND (“master” OR “PhD” OR “degree program”)” across fou r standard internet search engines (Google, Bing, DuckDuckGo, Startpage). Second, we included program s associated with NAPLeS. We excluded programs (n =

2) lacking sufficient English descriptions to ensure a high coding quality. The final list comprised 8 8 eligible LS

programs, with program information downloaded in Ma rch 2024. We exported all pages from the program websites that included information about the struct ure and content of the programs and imported these files to MAXQDA (version 24) for coding. The sample included 41 master’s programs (47%), 46 PhD programs (52%), and one post-master program (1%). A majority (60%, n = 53) of the programs were offered at NAPLeS member universities, with 23 master’s degrees and 30 PhD programs. Analysis We conducted a document analysis (see Bowen, 2009), reviewing data on LS programs through a skimming, reading, and interpretation process. We focused on the disciplines involved, the concepts covered, and the research methods taught in these programs. Standard qualitative content analysis procedures  (see Mayring, 2014) were applied using an adapted version of the coding scheme developed by Sommerhoff et al. (2018). Following rater training, interrater agreement among the seven coders (six authors and one student research assistant) reached an acceptable level for 10% of the programs  with a Gwet’s AC1 =.63. Gwet’s AC1 accounts for the number of categories used and the overall number of codes  assigned (see Gwet, 2008). Programs were then evenly divided among coders. Quantitative analyses complemented the qualitative analysis: To answer RQ2 and  RQ3, w e calculated percentages of concepts or methods mentioned and compared NAPLeS with non-member graduate programs. For RQ4, we again calculated Gwet’s  AC1 (values between 0 to 1) to assess the homogenei ty of programs with the same concepts and methods. While this measure is common for interrater reliability, it can also be used to determine the homogeneity. We, therefore, interpret higher Gwet’s AC1 values as an indicator for more homogeneous core concepts and methods.

## Results

To address RQ1, our results revealed that the progr ams were located in North America (68%), Europe (24 %), Australia (5%), Asia (3%), and South America (1%). We did not find any programs in Africa. We found a total of 16 disciplines involved in delivering the program, led by education (43%), psychology (36%), computer science (24%), mathematics (23%), and sociology (16%).

To answer RQ2, we analyzed which core concepts or m ethods were covered by more than half of the programs. We identified five core concepts, namely digital technology to support learning (77%), learn ing in formal contexts (66%), design learning environments (62%), learning in informal contexts (59%), and equity and diversity (50%). In a more detailed analysis, we co mpared NAPLeS with non-member graduate programs. We found that learning in formal contexts, design of l earning environments, equity and diversity, learnin g to argue, collaborative learning, emotion, and motivation wer e addressed relatively more often by NAPLeS program s, indicating a higher degree of conceptual homogeneit y among the NAPLeS programs (see Figure 1). Looking  at the core methods for data analysis and data collect ion, programs stated data analysis in general (50%), quantitatively (33%), or qualitatively (27%) without further specifying. Specific methods that were mentioned by more than a tenth of the programs were DBR (28%), l earning analytics (19%), basic statistics (16%), mu ltilevel analysis (11%), and linear models (11%).  NAPLeS programs only show higher homogeneity than n on-members for basic statistics, linear models, and multilevel analysis. With RQ3, we investigated which specific concepts and methods are explicitly emphasized by the graduate LS programs. Using technology to support learning is by far the most emphasized concept (30%), followed by designing learning environments and scaffolding (14%), content and learning context (10%), equity and diversity (9%), learning in formal contexts (9%), learning in informal contexts (8%), and cognition and metacognition (8%). Regarding the methods, unspecified data analysis (28%), quantitative data analysis (18%), qualitative data analysis (11%), design-based research (6%), linear models (5%), learning analytics (4%), basic statistics (4%), and multilevel analysis (3%) were mentioned in more than 1% of the codings. To answer RQ4, we examined the homogeneity of progr ams that share a specific concept or method (e.g., multilevel or discourse analysis) to identif y potential homogeneous cores—indicated by a higher Gwet’s AC1 among these programs. The analyses revealed that programs teaching non-parametric statistics (AC1 =.63), collaborative learning (AC1 =.60), and computational modeling (AC1 =.59) show the highest and those teaching multilevel analysis (AC1  =.52) the lowest homogeneity. Aga in, we conducted a detailed  comparison between NAPLeS member and non-member graduate LS programs. The homogeneity of NAPLeS members was low er (AC1 =.51) than that of non-members (AC1 =.46). Furthermore, the homogeneity of programs that  teach DBR was lower (AC1 =.42) than of those that do not (AC1 =.52). Figure 1 Concepts Mentioned in the NAPLeS vs. Non-member Programs

## Discussion

We replicated the study by Sommerhoff et al. (2018)  with an updated dataset and a revised coding schem e that captures the core concepts and methods of graduate LS programs. We observed a growth in the number of programs identifying as LS, with an updated data set of 88 programs compared to  75 programs in 2015 (see Sommerhoff et al., 2018). While the geographical fo cus of LS programs remains in North America and Eur ope, the first program is now offered in South America.

Similar domains and key concepts were identified in  comparison to Sommerhoff et al. (2018) (RQ1). The majorit y of programs are still located in North America, with e ducation and psychology as dominating disciplines. The predominance of LS  graduate programs in North America can be explained by the fact that the LS were founded in North America in the 1980s, and funding programs dedicated to the LS are offere d in North America. The advantages in time and financial suppo rt compared to other locations promoted the growth of LS programs in North America (Fischer et al., 2023; Ho adley & Van Haneghan, 2011; Pea & Linn, 2020). Anot her explanation could be a more robust tradition in int erdisciplinary research and the application of tech nology in education in North America, fostering the developme nt of LS programs. Moreover, NAPLeS has developed into an institution in the LS. Its membership has grown from 43 programs in 2018 to 54 in 2024. NAPLeS memb ers share syllabi and discuss recent developments and i ssues in workshops and seminars during their conferences. Indeed, these programs entail more conceptual homog eneity than NAPLeS non-members. A further explanation is that they are not remarkably homogeneous concerning the methods they teach. Conceptually, the design of learning environments in secondary schools and higher education along with the role of educational technology  remain relevant to the LS programs (RQ2). The results sugg est that core concepts of the LS continue to be important, partic ularly in investigating learning processes facilita ted by emerging digital technologies like learning with AI (Fischer et al., 2023). While many of our results regarding the core concepts align with Sommerhoff et al. (2018), we found deviations concerning the use of concepts in the description of the current LS programs. We can see a decrease in the relevance of the concepts  of cognition and metacognition and an increase in the relevance of t he concepts of diversity and equity. The increasing emphasis on concepts related to diversity and equity reflects the call for more equity-based research in the LS community (Famaye & Adisa, 2024), addressing the demand for a paradigm shift towards a more just and inclusive education with learning sciences-based research (Gutiérrez, 2008). Methodologically, empirical and observation-oriented methods, as well as quantitative and quali tative methods, are still taught as the most relevant methods in the LS (RQ3). Our findings differ from those of Sommerhoff et al. (2018) concerning the relevance o f the research method of DBR. It appears that DBR i s no longer only a feature of North American programs that are similar to each other but that DBR is now a method that is taught in many programs that otherwise differ from each other (cf. Sommerhoff, 2018). LS programs seem to incorporate  a broader range of methods, while other programs ar e integrating DBR as a signature method because it is perceived as beneficial for complex educational contexts and as a means of establishing connections with more established programs.

While Sommerhoff et al. (2018) found DBR to be  the core method that highlights homogeneity across LS programs because programs emphasizing DBR would also focus on learning in formal and informal conte xts and using technology to support learning, for examp le, this implication seems no longer true for the c urrently taught programs. Instead, we identified that progra ms featuring non-parametric statistics, collaborative learning, and computational learning (RQ4) were the most homogeneous and no longer featured DBR. We faced limitations on two levels. As a replication, we faced design and methodological constraints, as Sommerhoff et al. (2018) noted, with LS program des criptions often being broad and superficial. The lo w percentage of programs mentioning specific methods reflects unspecific descriptions of the methods in the programs. Additionally, it is uncertain whether publicly available program descriptions truly reflect the program's content or if they instead serve as promotion mater ial. To address this issue, we  also examined syllabi, when available, to analyze the courses offered and the t opics covered, offering a more detailed understandi ng of the program structure. However, future studies may go d eeper by including other data sources as well, such  as interviews with program directors and students. Mor eover, by only considering programs with descriptio ns available in English, we thus potentially missed pr ograms that might classify and identify as LS progr ams but whose program descriptions were not in English and  might not be labeled as such.  This might create bias, especially concerning LS programs that are still developing and might not be represented in English. As a result, our findings could be skewed toward English-speaking countries, limiting the broader applicability of the results. Future studies should consider non-English-language sources to capture a more global perspective. Several years after Sommerhoff et al. (2018) posed the question of what we teach when we teach the LS, our study provides an updated overview of the conce pts and methods emphasized across various LS progra ms, contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of the field. The LS is viewed as a community of pr actice and includes a core of relatively coherent programs, suggesting an emerging discipline. The core is or bited by various multidisciplinary and more heterogeneous pr ograms in the periphery, shaping  and innovating the field (see Sommerhoff et al., 2018). Our findings confirm  that LS graduate programs still share a homogeneou s core of concepts and methods within the LS. However, some core concepts and methods have shifted to the periphery, while others from the periphery are merging with the core. The  LS have indeed experienced a dynamic growth within the higher education sector. However, this expansion remains largely localized, with no substantial shift in the balance between North American programs and those on other continents.
