# A Design Space for Dialogic Assessment in CSCL

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Dialogic assessment remains remarkably unexplored as a research and design setting in CSCL. We address this gap by developing a design space to support future partnership-based work in our field. Our design space reflects a 1.5-year investigation of dialogic assessment practices and perspectives conducted through literature review work, focus group interviews with practitioners, formal and informal discussions with colleagues and prototype development work with technology designers. First, we frame the design space concept before arguing for the relevance of dialogic assessment as a potential CSCL activity. Then, we present our design space through a cumulative distillation of four relevant perspectives and seven design dimensions. Finally, we conclude by laying out an agenda reflecting how this design space can inform future work and prompt innovative assessment practices.

With few exceptions, dialogic forms of summative assessment like oral exams have been perceived in opposition to movements towards digitalization and pedagogical innovation in education. Despite decades of pedagogical transformation efforts towards teaching and learning that is student centered, based on dialogue and collaboration, and supported by technology, the corresponding summative assessment practices have been surprisingly static and constrained (Boud & Bearman, 2022). We have a strong tradition in the learning sciences towards tools, activities, and concepts that support situated learning conversations, but the oral exam as a context for such learning remains almost entirely unexplored. The view of an oral exam as an un-mediated, technology-free, (and often decontextualized) discussion between learner and examiner with little relevance for learning persists. We see evidence of this tension in recent years with the responses to the rapid growth of Large Language Models (LLMs) driven by generative arti ficial intelligence (GenAI). Chatbots like ChatGPT are disrupting traditional exam methods at all levels of the educational system, enabling students to perform conventional assessments, such as scientific essays, with just a few keystrokes (McGrath et al., 2024). In response, we see renewed interest in “old-fashioned” assessments such as pen and paper tests, and naturally oral exams (Hartmann, 2025). A handful of studies adapt oral exams to online (Akimov & Malin, 2020) and video-based settings (Bayley et al., 2024). However, the main motivation to reconsider oral exams seems to be directly because of a lack of computer-support rather than because of an interest in bringing assessment methods into alignment with current perspectives on dialogic and collaborative learning.

This misalignment underscores a need to develop assessment formats that are fair, systematic, and following teaching approaches that value dialogue, participation, and digital possibilities. The central premise of our work here is that dialogic assessment should be (re)conceptualized as a computer-supported collaborative learning context and as a fertile ground for pedagogical and technological innovation. Dialogic assessment formats facilitate interaction between examiners and students and enable assessing students’ active use of knowledge. Unlike traditional assessments, which often test static memorization and reproduction of information (Collins & Halver son, 2018; Pea et al., 2025), dialogic formats assess skills like problem-solving, conceptual application, and adaptive thinking. However, a persistent challenge in dialogic assessment is that dialogue is inherently ephemeral, often leaving students unclear about how their grades are determined. Additionally, examiners, as active participants in the assessment conversation, require support to be consistent in balancing their roles as both participating and evaluating the conversation.  This also relates to validity in assessment, which concerns whether the tasks provide trustworthy information about what students know and cando, and whether subsequent  interpretations are well justified by theory and empirical data  While CSCL research has long explored technology's role in supporting collaborative learning, its potential for dialogic assessment remains underexplored. To date, we have not identified existing tools or practices designed specifically to support dialogic assessment.

It is important to discuss how dialogic assessment, as a summative assessment practice, might be positioned in relation to the notions of collaboration and computer-support central to the field of CSCL. We argue 234 that bringing this kind of assessment conversation into alignment with sociocultural learning perspectives invites considering the conversation as a learning activity itself and not merely an evaluation post-learning. The goal of this conversation should be for the student and examiner(s) to collaboratively elicit the student’s understanding. This is very much a joint activity with a shared goal of making learning visible. A CSCL activity is built on a triadic structure with a computational artifact mediating the interaction between two or more participants (Ludvigsen & Steier, 2019). We see many opportunities for such computational artifacts to enhance dialogic assessment which build on rich research traditions in CSCL. For example, teacher dashboards have emerged as tools to support teachers in visualizing, monitoring, and adjusting to the complexities of classroom group-work (Matuk, et al., 2019). How might the lessons from these kinds of orchestration tools be adapted to dialogic assessment situations to support examiners in managing the assessment activity in real time? Similarly, scripts, as support structures, have emerged as an important contribution within CSCL to support collaboration (e.g. Noroozi, et. Al., 2013). Could assessment scripts be developed to guide student and examiner in the kinds of knowledge production relevant to an oral exam setting which preserve the possibility for summative evaluation? We can also imagine that findings from CSCL research on  learning with simulations (e.g. Hontvedt & Arnseth, 2013) could help create assessment situations that are more authentic and contextualized in real situations. In developing the current design space, we are particularly interested in the ways that perspectives from video-and interaction design space is not to specify all the relevant avenues of exploration within CSCL, but to establish a syste matic and structured foundation inviting future work.

When it comes to dialogic assessment, one size does not fit all. We consider dialogic assessment as a format, that needs to be actively translated into a concrete assessment method. Against this backdrop, this study proposes a design space (MacLean et al., 1991; Coelho et al., 2025) for (computer-supported) dialogic assessment. We argue that this design space is necessary for aligning assessment practices with pedagogical goals, practitioner needs, and technological advancements. The overarching agenda is to create a foundation for exploring dialogic assessment like oral exams as a computer-supported activity that is also reflective of theoretical views of learning based in dialogic and collaborative epistemologies. We outline how this space can facilitate collabor ation among stakeholders, researchers, and designers. Within this design space, actionable methods tailored to specific subjects and local contexts can be co-created, tested, and eventually implemented with students. We pose the following research question: How can a design space for dialogic assessment be configured to effectively translate objectives and situational contingencies into actionable computer-supported and collaborative assessment methods? Approaches toward a design space for dialogic assessment in CSCL A design space refers to a conceptual and structured environment created through the configuration of resources, frameworks, and tools that enable the exploration of possibilities, constraints, and dimensions relevant to the design process (MacLean et al., 1991; Coelho et al., 2025). A well-developed design space enables researchers to demonstrate how they navigate challenges, engage with target communities, and deal with potential risks (Lee et al., 2025). A well-functioning design space relies on a set of shared  concepts and resources that facilitate productive discussion and framing of goals, challenges, and opportunities for innovation. Through structured dialogue and exploration within this space, participants can communicate effectively and co-construct share d objects (Lee et al., 2025). A design space analysis articulates the basis for design choices and evolves alongside the artifact being developed and can help learning scientists articulate: “Here’s what I plan to do today, and here’s why I’m making that choice” (Coelho et al., 2025, p.1680). As such, a design space is providing a foundation for reasoned decision-making and iterative refinement.

The dialogic assessment design space builds on a sociocultural perspective on learning and assessment, emphasizing how contexts are collaboratively shaped and re-shaped through the use of conceptual, cognitive, and physical tools (Daniels, 2010). W e thus build on the assumption that the specific configuration of dialogic assessment needs to be attuned to each specific course’s assessment criteria and objectives. For example, the technological needs as well as ways of eliciting questions would be different in a language class in secondary school, an upper secondary history course, or a pedagogy course in a teacher education program. The design space acts as a dynamic environment that encourages the investigation of unaddressed issues and overlooked perspectives. It is in this iterative process of exploration and reframing that oppo rtunities for innovation emerge. Importantly, treating the creation of the design space as both a part of—and an object of —the research process in this paper, makes the foundation of the design transparent and open for inspection. This helps position the f inal design solution as not merely a standalone artifact but as a sociocultural and sociotechnical outcome shaped by collaborative practices, contextual influences and technological affordances. In this paper, we develop a design space that comprises four mutually informed perspectives: "institutional," "practitioner," "interaction researcher," and "tool designer” that contribute to enabling a design space for dialogic assessment. This approach underscores 235 the importance of design spaces as environments for fostering creativity, critical engagement, and thoughtful problem-solving.

To create a transparent and research-based design space, we draw on several methods: literature reviews of existing oral exam practices and uses of interaction analysis methods, conversations with stakeholders, and focus group interviews with practitioners. Additionally, we draw on insights from the prototyping process of a theory-grounded and practitioner informed digital tool that combines video annotation with AI-based coding and feedback to analyze oral assessment dialogues. This on-going iterative design work involves working with students, educators, researchers, and stakeholders. We briefly specify methods for these various knowledge sources in relation to the four perspectives below. The analysis shows how technology affordances are enacted based on the theoretical and practical knowledge drawn from our investigations. This work represents 1.5 years of systematic investigation into dialogic assessment, and the purpose in presenting this design work is not to put forth a particular design solution but to present the lessons that are learned from these kinds of technology-focused conversations and how they can inform the emerging design space. The design space for dialogic assessment Dialogic assessment brings attention to the types of knowledge that emerge through interaction, problem-solving, and joint meaning-making in real time—forms of knowledge often missed in traditional assessment formats like written texts. This aligns with re cent learning sciences research, that emphasize the importance of assessment formats that capture interaction and the use of artefacts (Pea et al., 2025). In this section, we outline the design space for dialogic assessment by introducing four perspectives that each contribute valuable and productive insights. By “perspective,” we mean a particular lens for interpreting and addressing key considerations in designing this space. For each perspective, we provide an overview, discuss its research foundation, and illustrate its relevance using examples from our work in the Norwegian context. We then identify seven dimensions that guide and structure the exploration of this design space. We use the term dimension as an extension of the spatial metaphor; dimensions describe the design space and allow us to position new assessment methods in relation to one another and in relation to critical questions (Lee et al., 2024).

## Key perspectives on dialogic assessment

We describe four perspectives that are understood as cumulative and building on each other. We argue that these perspectives should be involved in design work and prototyping, with the goal of materializing concrete actionable Perspective 1: The institutional assessment practices Assessment, particularly summative  assessment methods, are key for students to progress in an educational system. While it is widely accepted that learning involves social and complex processes that develop over time with many possible trajectories, formal assessment situations tend to be individual and involve little interaction with others. Dialogic assessment formats exist but are often less easily standardiz ed, documented, and compared across students and contexts than written tests, which poses challenges for their integration into established assessment systems. Still, assessment is  given great authority in educational systems and shapes the students’ future opportunities. The summative assessments are usually regulated through policy documents at the school -, national-, and international levels. Therefore, any design process for dialogic assessment must build upon these existing institutional practices and regulatory frameworks to ensure relevance and feasibility. Accordingly, the institutional perspective becomes a vital foundation for designing innovative assessment methods. To illustrate the importance of this perspective and how it may manifest, we provide an example from the Norwegian context, a particularly relevant case due  to its long tradition of dialogic assessment through oral exams. Here, students undergo high-stakes oral and written exams in at least one school subject after year 10 in secondary school and potentially several oral exams in upper secondary school as well as in higher education. The institutional goal of these exams is to assess students’ competence within a school discipline, such as Norwegian, mathematics, or science. ‘Oral exams’ are standard within the education system from secondary school through higher education, especially in subjects like languages and social s ciences. However, oral exams are not standardized tests but take the method of a subject conversation between the student and an internal examiner (typically the student’s teacher). An external examiner, normally a teacher from another school district, als o attends the exam, and in the event of disagreement about the student’s grade, the external examiner is given primary rights to decide on the final grade.  While there are national guidelines on how to conduct an oral exam, most relate to the administration of the exams, with limited guidance on how teachers should assess students’ performance. The examiners have primary responsibility for designing the quest ions based on curriculum standards, as well as evaluating students’ performance, and guidelines concerning the actual examination are 236 restricted to the rather vague formulation that the examination should provide students with “the opportunities to show as broad competence as possible” (Norwegiandirectorate for Education and Training, 20 26). Research on oral exams has shown that the examiners’ management of the conversation creates the basis for the student to demonstrate competence (Sikveland et al., 2021; Skovholt et al., 2021; Vonen et al., 2022). Against this backdrop and the case of Norway, it is evident that the institutional practices and frameworks governing assessments needs to be included in a design space for dialogic assessment. Institutional practices set constraints and opportunities for designing new assessment methods. A design process of dialogic assessments needs to build on and acknowledge  the need to include the institutional perspective to see opportunities for variation and innovation. For example, in national guidelines for Norwegian oral exams it is explicitly stated that students should be able to present their work, engage in discussions, and demonstrate an independent stance. However, the use of technologies is not specified in detail within the guidelines, nor is it restricted. Students commonly use tools like PowerPoint, although this practice is not explicitly mentioned. We propose that technologies could be meaningfully integrated into dialogic assessments to enhance the conversation and support both students and examiners. Including institutional perspectives in the design space for dialogic assessment is crucial to identify paths for redesign and refinement in a design space. Perspective 2: The practitioners’ experience with conducting exams Building on the consideration of institutional practices and formal guidelines, we now turn to a less documented yet equally important aspect of dialogic assessment: the practitioners’ perspective.  Dialogic assessment is inherently relational, relying on the conversation between examiners and students. While general aspects of the assessment process are closely regulated, the actual practices  depend heavily on the professional judgment and expertise of examiners. As such, including the practitioners’ perspective and experiences are crucial for creating a design space for dialogic assessment.

In our work in Norway, we have conducted a first step of mapping practitioners’ perspectives through conversations with county representatives, with school leaders, and conducted group interviews with examiners in secondary school and in teacher education.  The focus group interviews were conducted  with lower secondary school teachers (N=4, 1 hour and 22 minutes over Zoom), secondary school teachers (N=3, 60 minutes) and teacher educators (N=4, 60 minutes). The teachers  had experience as examiners in the subjects of Norwegian, Social Studies, English as a Second Language, Natural Sciences, Mathematics and Religion.  All teachers were recruited based on previous work affiliations, and the interviews were audio recorded. We then transcribed the interview data and mapp ed general themes that occurred  across participants’ perspectives. At the county level, they acknowledged that the current institutional practices for oral exams need innovation to secure student rights and enable them to formally appeal and request re-assessment. The county we collaborate with will therefore experiment with recording the students’ oral exams. School leaders acknowledge that current practices need innovation but also point to the importance of involving examiners in this process, not new rules imposed topdown. In the focus group interviews, the practitioners in secondary school and teacher education highlighted oral exam situations as conducive for displaying students’ communicative competence in the subject. They acknowledged that oral exams allow assessing students’ analytic ability and the ways in which they engage with knowledge resources, e.g., concepts, images or questions asked. T hey described that the sensitive nature of oral assessment situations could influence both their mental presence as examiners, and the assessment process. The conversation between examiners after the exams were brought forth as a productive way to ensure a  high degree of objectivity for assessing the students’ competence, yet that the oral exam format had limitations: it provides only a "snapshot" of a student's competence. The teachers expressed a need for support that could provide oversight and evaluate students more objectively. Here, they suggested that ‘an extra set of eyes’ could be a meaningful way support the assessment process, and their own training as examiners. Video was suggested as a useful resource in relation to the latter; ‘look at recordin gs of a few exams afterwords to learn better myself’ together with a ‘teacher council’ for professional development. The teachers tend to focus on the most overarching skills, such as analytical abilities and the capacity to apply theory. Introducing student voices in a more systematic way in the partnership process will be essential. We have had initial conversations with students and are now preparing a survey in two counties to further explore students’ views on fairness, as well as the opportunities and challenges associated with oral exams.

Clearly, the examiners’ perspectives  in the design space  are key to identifying needs and gaining understanding for the complex, relational and situated practices of doing oral assessment. Engaging with local authorities, school leaders, and educators gives a deeper insight of the practical realities and relational aspects of dialogic assessments. This is in alignment with a partnership-based approach that facilitates innovation, while ensuring that new tools and practices are grounded in the lived experiences of those directly involved in the assessment practices.

237 Perspective 3: The interaction analyst’ strategies for conceiving dialogues The first two parts of the design space involve institutional practices and participant perspectives that both are key in an initiative to develop a design space to innovate dialogic assessment.  We move forward by proposing a specific line of research that has well-developed methods for empirically analyzing dialogues. Interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995), and more broadly conversation analysis (Sidnell & Stivers, 2012) and video analysis (Derry et al., 2009), enables the analysis of dialogue as it develops sequentially within social practices. Interaction as the key resource for conducting such analysis. Based on a literature review we conducted on interactive use of video in higher education, findings show that video recordings of talk can become meaningful tools for formative and summative assessment. In short, through a framework synthesis approach (Dixon-Woods, 2011) we mapped qualitative research addressing practices and applications in which video technology was employed as a resource for group learning and assessment.  Searches were conducted in Education Research Information Centre (Eric), Web of Science and Academic Premier in June 2023 and January 2025 by using the following parameters: 1. Video (in title), 2. OR “interactive video”, 3 AND collaborat* OR interact* OR teaching OR lecturing OR assessment OR feedback OR classroom OR dialogue (in abstract), 4. AND “higher education”. These searches resulted in 2578 studies. After removing duplicates and screening abstracts, 92 studies were included in the review. Studies were included if they examined activities where two or more students interactively engaged with video for learning or assessment purposes.  Most studies were excluded when solely based on quantitative data, video was only part of research method, focus was on individual learners interacting with video, or interactive use of video not being main phenomenon under study. Various kinds of video-based and interactive learning activities were shown to afford students opportunities to practice, assess and improve their communication and language skills (Chi et al., 2017; Greenblatt & McDonald, 2024; Roza & Rustam, 2023). Students have also reported that time spent watching video recordings of their own presentations helped improve their presentation skills (Ritchie, 2015).

In related work from the Norwegian context, recent conversation analytic studies have shown that the examiner’s conduct influences student performance (Sikveland et al., 2021; Skovholt et al., 2021; Vonen et al., 2022). It is shown how  oral exams can allow students to collaboratively construct and re-contextualize prior experiences, expanding on them in relation to the broader knowledge structures of a discipline (Hontvedt, & Solem, 2024;). Analysis of the grading conversations between examiners after oral exams shows that assessment tends to be based on vague interpretations of the assessment criteria, with little explicit connection to the students’ talk (Solem et al., 2023). This highlights the need to experiment with oral assessment methods that ensure transparency and anchor grading discussions in student performance. By incorporating interaction analysis perspectives into the design space for dialogic assessment, we candevelop methods that are better equipped to capture the complexity of interactions in assessment contexts. This involves recognizing the examiner's role in creating a context that allows students to demonstrate their knowledge. Another key aspect is the "next-turn proof procedure" for investigating how a participant's contribution builds on and responds to the preceding utterance. Lastly, but perhaps most importantly, interaction analysis has demonstrated the value of creating detailed transcripts and visualizations of conversations to enable thorough innovating dialogic assessment.

Perspective 4: The CSCL designer’s eye for technology affordances The fourth perspective builds on the previous three and emphasizes the potential contribution of technology to support dialogic assessment, through the lens of a CSCL designer. We use this term to include knowledge on the affordances of technology to enhance collaboration and interaction in learning contexts. As a research domain, CSCL is particularly well-positioned to contribute to methods and tools that emphasize both "discourse and interaction" and the "interactional mediation" of technology in teaching and learning (Stahl & Hakkarainen, 2021). In our case in Norway, we have collaborated with CSCL designers to develop a working prototype: ‘the traced dialogic assessment tool’. This work has helped us begin to concretize designer perspectives in a particular case. The tool is implemented as an Electron-based desktop application that combines speech-to-text transcription (using Whisper), natural language processing (through GPT models), and interactive interfaces for transcript editing and annotation. This architecture makes it possible to transform ephemeral oral exchanges into durable representations that can be inspected collaboratively by examiners. Rather than treating oral exams solely as individual performances to be scored, the tool foregrounds them as jointly  constructed interactions. Within the application, examiners can import recordings, generate and import transcripts, and review them in synchrony with the original audio. A waveform visualization supports navigation through the recording, while transcripts  remain coupled with the audio to preserve features such as prosody and hesitation that are often significant in dialogic 238 assessment. Using a combination of GPT-based analysis and locally implemented fuzzy matching, the tool highlights transcript segments in which students appear to demonstrate understanding of key concepts defined by the examiner or identified in the syllabus. These suggestions can then be accepted, rejected, or modified, ensuring that responsibility for evaluation remains with the human examiner. At the same time, an integrated editor allows examiners to insert their own annotations, highlight relevant passages, and build layered records that combine AIgenerated suggestions with human judgment. A central affordance of the tool is its configurability. Examiners are able to define the key ideas and assessment criteria that guide the analysis, set thresholds for the confidence of AI-generated suggestions, and adapt prompts to specific disciplinary contexts. In this way, the prototype accommodates local differences in curriculum design and assessment culture. Beyond the moment of examination, the tool also enables export of annotated transcripts and assessment reports, offering possibilities for feedback, documentation, and reflection. These outputs can make the rationale for grading more transparent to students and create new opportunities for formative use of summative assessments. In our pilot work, a class of 20 information science students conducted mock oral exams using the traced dialogic assessment tool and then reflected on their experiences in group presentations. Through these presentations, participants suggested new configurations, visualizations, and annotation functions, illustrating concrete directions for further development of the tool. Another take-away from these sessions involved students’ reflections on their participations in the exam – with the computational resources they started to identify patterns and traces in their dialogic participation as individuals and as a group. This process enabled us to collaboratively envision possibilities for traced assessment and to identify priorities for future work. For this study, the purpose of the prototyping and design work is to provide insight into (CSCL design) perspectives and not necessarily to advance a specific technical solution. Based on this work, we hypothesize that CSCL technologies can be harnessed to make the interpretive process of assessment more systematic, transparent, and accountable, without replacing professional judgment in assessment. This is a timely topic, since recent developments of AI-supported transcription tools, visualization tools, and annotation tools can support analysis of dialogues (Davidsen & Steier, 2024; Shapiro et al., 2025). This includes alternative visualizations of transcripts (e.g. bubble diagrams, turn-taking charts, spatio-temporal di agrams, etc.), learning analytics dashboards or multimodal learning analytics.  Furthermore, producing a material artefact from the dialogue can support transparency and object-oriented collaboration that goes beyond the concrete assessment situation. (Damsa, 2014). We carried these insights through to the development of design space  dimensions below. Dimensions of design space for dialogic assessment By looking across  findings from  the above perspectives, we inductively and iteratively developed a set of dimensions that can be used to characterize a dialogic assessment method (Table 1). We clarified dimension descriptions and possibilities  in relation to ex isting CSCL frameworks, reducing these to the seven below. As noted above, dialogic assessment in general is seen as a format and these dimensions support the translation work of moving to concrete computer-supported dialogic assessment methods. Note that some of the dimensions are linear whereas others are more categorical. For a given method, some dimensions may be more relevant than others. As the field of CSCL has paid so little attention to dialogic assessment contexts, it can be challenging to anchor new design ideas in relation to other emerging designs. These dimensions, as part of the proposed design space, open up useful questions for designing new assessment methods and their associated tools. We can also identify fruitful areas of exploration by combining dimension possibilities in new ways. For example, what might a student-group-focused dialogic assessment method look like that was highly disruptive to current practices, and aimed at long-term learning?

In Norway, a typical oral exam has a single student with 2 examiners which means that the post-exam evaluation conversation between the two examiners is also a kind of collaboration that could benefit a mediating tool. This paper has argued that dialogic assessment formats foster an interactive environment where students actively engage with and apply their knowledge. However, these benefits are contingent on the relational dynamics between students and educators. From a sociocultural and CSCL perspective, oral exams can be understood as assessments of students’ individual competence, that are undertaken within collaboratively organized, tool-mediated activities in which knowledge is distributed across students, exa miners, and artefacts. We do not claim to resolve this tension, but treat students' individual summative performance as an institutional requirement that needs to be negotiated within a distributed activity system. Our argument is that these interpretive processes, which are already taking place, can be made more transpar ent and systematic, rather than eliminating the inherently interactional nature of dialogic assessment.  For this purpose, we need  tools and strategies to mediate what examiners see and support their documentation of student achievement, thereby strengthening the validity of the assessment.  For dialogic assessment to be effective, examiners must acknowledge their role in shaping opportunities for students to demonstrate their understanding. This involves being attuned to students’ needs while maintaining a clear distinction between the students’ contributions and their own (Hontvedt et al., 2024). Another challenge is that dialogic assessment can appear unsystematic with choices being invisible and untraceable. CSCL as a field has much to contribute to these challenges by framing the relevant dialogues as collaborative, and by providing computer-mediated-support to the process. Framing dialogic assessment a s a computer-supported and collaborative activity will also bring summative assessment methods into alignment with innovations in learning activity design. This shift calls for support structures that help map emerging possibilities, along with a design space for innovating, refining, and testing methods through co-design in schools and teacher education. The design space provides a shared vocabulary for collaborative development and enables the joint (Lee et al., 2024). The design space for dialogic assessment builds on a cumulative and multifaceted approach to perspective taking from relevant knowledge areas. We have approached dialogic assessment as highly situated in local contexts and thus began with the relevant institutional perspectives. For us, this means we ground our local approach in the Norwegian education context, but we hope that insights from this perspective can be extended. We then turned to practitioner perspectives to see how examiners and school leaders conceive of oral exams a nd how they might be strengthened. Next, we introduced perspectives grounded in interaction analysis and video analytic methods. Adopting these lenses in the context of this design space provides insights about how we approach interaction in design work. We thus then turned to a CSCL designer perspective to see what lessons could be learned from engaging in a prototyping process. Here, questions about the relevant technology interfaces could be articulated, as well as questions about what aspects of the assessment process could benefit from designed computer support. Thus, working with a specific prototype created an opportunity to reflect on broader design possibilities. Finally, to create a shared focus a cross these four perspectives, we developed a set of seven dimensions that ensures progress and structure in design space for dialogic assessment. In the case of Norway, we find that the design space ensures that assessment configurations are researchbased, context-sensitive, and aligned with local goals. By explicating the design space in terms of perspectives and dimensions, the design process becomes transparency, avoiding black-boxing critical elements such as stakeholder involvement and pedagogical principles that underlie certain methods. Having a functioning prototype creates opportunities for conducting design experiments and iteratively refining assessment designs based on empirical results. Regarding generalization, the design space framework can be adapted by other practitioners and researchers to develop dialogic assessment methods that address their specific needs and contexts. This proposal has several limitations. The empirical basis consists of pilot investigations with a limited number of participants in specific contexts, and we acknowledge that the proposed design space may need to be expanded and revised when confronted with a broader set of relevant actors and contexts. While our empirical investigations are not thorough enough to generalize to the needs and perspectives of a larger group of stakeholders, they do confirm the validity of including these kinds of perspectives in the design space for dialogic assessment and point to their potential importance in future work.  As such, this paper provides an initial exploration of the potential to merge learning sciences, CSCL, and interaction analytic insights in order to create theoretically informed design principles and hypotheses about the future of dialogic assessment. Consequently, the design space outlined here should be considered a starting point for collaborative refinement within the CSCL community.

Finally, this partnership-based approach underscores the collaborative nature of the design process, where practitioners, researchers, and technologists work together to create assessments that are both innovative and practical. By expanding on the design space, we aim to bridge the gap between theoretical principles and realworld assessment practices, fostering the sustainable development of dialogic assessment across diverse educational settings.
