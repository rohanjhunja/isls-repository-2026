# Assessing Student Collaboration with Multimodal Data and Large Language Models

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We introduce a multimodal evaluation approach that leverages large language models to assess group collaboration quality in computer-supported collaborative learning environments. Using synchronized log data, dialogue transcripts, and human-coded annotations from undergraduate dyads working in the Graspable Math platform, we develop a theoretically grounded rubric capturing cognitive, social, and regulatory dimensions of collaboration, and examine the extent to which LLM-generated evaluations align with expert human judgments.

Collaborative learning (CL) supports deeper understanding through shared reasoning and knowledge co-construction (Dillenbourg, 1999). In computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL), researchers increasingly rely on multimodal data (e.g., logs, dialogue) to capture these complex processes (Sharma & Giannakos, 2020). However, evaluating collaboration quality remains challenging, as rubric-based approaches require extensive human coding and are difficult to scale (Lee et al., 2025). Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) offer a promising alternative for automating the analysis of unstructured educational data, including dialogue and log traces (Liu et al., 2024). While prior work has shown that LLMs can approximate human coding for well-defined constructs, their ability to evaluate group-level collaboration quality using multimodal inputs remains underexplored. To address this gap, this study explores how LLMs can be leveraged to scale the evaluation of pair collaboration based on log data, dialogue data, and human-coded annotations. Specifically: 1) RQ1:What aspects of collaboration quality should be considered when developing a group-level collaboration rubric grounded in the theoretical framework? 2) RQ2: How does the LLM perform in grading group collaboration quality using combined input data (logs, dialogue, and precoded annotations)? This study involved 22 undergraduate students (11 dyads) who collaboratively solved algebra problems using the Graspable Math platform  (see Figure 1). We recorded both log and dialogue data, providing synchronized multimodal records of student interactions. To address RQ1, we developed a theoretically grounded collaboration rubric based on prior frameworks, Mercer’s Exploratory Talk Framework (1995), Weinberger and Fischer’s (2006) Argumentative Knowledge Construction model, and Järvelä and Hadwin’s (2013) Socially Shared Regulation of Learning perspective, capturing cognitive, social, and regulatory dimensions of collaboration. Dialogue transcripts were manually coded using an established scheme (Zhang et al., 2024) with strong inter-rater reliability (κ > 0.75), and log data were processed into structured, time-aligned action sequences. Details about dialogue, and log coding schemes are available on the Open Science Framework: https://osf.io/mnxts/overview?view_only=6c0769b0bfcb44998b5a29b6eb184f42. To address RQ2, we constructed multimodal inputs combining logs, dialogue, and human-coded annotations, and evaluated LLM performance (e.g., GPT-5) in grading collaboration quality against expert ratings. Model outputs were assessed using macro F1 and Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK).

The rubric in Table 1 comprised six dimensions capturing key aspects of effective collaboration: Participation Balance, Responsiveness & Listening, Idea Elaboration & Co-construction, Questioning & Clarification, Conflict Resolution & Negotiation, and On-t ask Focus & Shared Goals. These six dimensions were organized into cognitive, social, and regulatory aspects, reflecting the multifaceted nature of collaborative learning. Each dimension was rated on a three-point scale, with an “NA” option when not applicable. Two coders jointly reviewed 20% of transcripts and video recordings to calibrate their understanding of the rubric. Inter-rater reliability was established at 0.84 QWK, after which one coder completed the remaining ratings. After scoring all 11 groups across their attempted problems (n = 49), we computed a total collaboration score per problem by summing ratings across all six dimensions and dividing by the number of applicable dimensions (excluding any marked “NA”). For RQ2, using these expert rating s as ground truth, we evaluated LLM performance under the input data of log, dialogue, and precoded. GPT-5 achieved an F1 score of 0.46 and a QWK of 0.31. This suggests that GPT-5 demonstrates some ability to clarify collaboration quality, but its alignmen t with expert judgments is still relatively modest. Our findings contribute to the ongoing discourse of using AI for qualitative analyses and provide evidence on the extent to which LLMs can approximate expert evaluations of collaborative processes.
