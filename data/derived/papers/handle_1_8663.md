# Machine Learning and Student Reasoning Patterns in NGSS Assessments

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
SPIN-NGSS (Student Reasoning Patterns in NGSS Assessments), an NSF funded project, responds to the national need to document various student reasoning patterns in threedimensional (3D) science assessments and tied machine learning techniques to automated diagnosis and feedback to support science learning. This presentation describes a framework of student reasoning patterns illustrated by example student responses, the development and evaluation of machine learning methods, and an exploratory study on how students reacts to the automated system.

## Introduction

The SPIN-NGSS project addresses critical challenges  faced in research and practice in the implementati on of NGSS or NGSS-like standards in many states. Capital izing on existing assessment data and machine learn ing techniques, the SPIN-NGSS conducted a proof-of-concept study to develop an automated diagnosis and feedback tool to support student science learning. SPIN-NGSS responds to the national need to document various student reasoning patterns in response to 3D science assess ments and support students’ 3D learning through rea l-time, automated diagnostics. This presentation describes the assessment material and data resources, a frame work of student reasoning patterns illustrated by example student responses, the development and evaluation of machine learning methods, and a discussion on potential uses of these AI models in science classrooms. Sense-making as Building off Student Reasoning Learning as sense-making of the world has been emph asized in the Next Generation Science Standards (NG SS Lead States, 2013) and various National Research Council reports (NRC, 2008; 2012). Sense-making should build on student reasoning about science. There has been abundant research on analyzing how scientists reason (e.g., Kind & Osborne, 2017). There is less research, howe ver, on categorizing student reasoning patterns, pa rtly because the previous assessments do not necessarily elicit how students reason about science during their problem solving or sense-making. With ongoing NGSS-aligned assessment development efforts, it is possible to conduct a systematic analysis of student reasoning patterns. These NGSS-aligned assessments should provide evi dence about how students reason and inform characteristics in their reasoning. Assessment and data resources SPIN-NGSS investigates student data in constructed response items collected from a scenario-based ecosystems assessment task developed in an IES funded project (Liu et al., 2021). The ecosystems task requires students to use their understanding of the crosscutting concept of systems and system models, and knowledge of relationships among organisms in ecosystems to come up with an ar gument through engaging in the practice of model-based argumentation. The constructed response item followed a simulated activity of the corn rootworms invas ion on the corn field and how adding a predator (harvestmen) into the farm ecosystem impacted other system components including rootworms and corn yield. The constructed response item asked students to use data generated from the simulation and construct an explanation for why adding harvestmen into the system did not resolve the rootworm invasion issue. A total of 864 student responses we re used to develop and validate a student reasoning  pattern framework.

Student reasoning patterns Student reasoning patterns are typical ways of stud ent thinking when making sense of a natural phenome non. They are essential stepping stones to use and inter pret student data to inform evidence-based instruct ion. Leveraging existing NGSS assessment data, SPIN-NGSS  developed a framework that described four typical patterns, three of which were associated with 3D sc ience learning.  The first pattern represents unidi mensional reasoning, focusing on Science and Engineering Prac tices (SEP) only. In other words, student responses  only include data patterns or data points, but no mentio n of scientific principles.  The second pattern als o represents unidimensional reasoning, but only focuses on Crosscutting Concepts and Disciplinary Core Ideas. For example, student responses only mention system mechanisms li ke “rootworms eat corns” or “harvestmen get rid of ICLS2022 Proceedings 1960 © ISLS rootworms” without reference to data or data patterns.  Note that we combined crosscutting concepts and the third pattern represents integrated reasoning with a mix of practice and system mechanisms, a so called mult idimensional response. Text relevant to the practice  dimension will be highlighted in blue, and text re levant to system mechanisms in orange.  The fourth category (unidentifiable pattern) is for student responses that are too vague or irrelevant to identify their reasoning pat tern. Among the 864 student responses, the distribu tion of the above four categories were the following: 36.34% for unidimensional SEP, 16.32% for unidimensional DCI/CCC, 26.62% for multidimensional, and 20.72% for unidentifiable pattern. Note that we combined DCI and CCC due to much overlaps between the specific CCC of systems and system models with the specific DCI of interactions in the ecosystems, which would make it extremely difficult to disentangle them. AI Model Development and Evaluation Automated scoring tools are in high demand by K-12 educators to provide timely feedback to students. T he complex 3D learning makes it challenging for teachers to provide timely feedback. With high quality assessment data, it is possible to train the machine to learn about how students reason about science and identify what is still missing in their reasoning regarding the three dime nsions of science. The machine generated diagnosis can be used to define differentiated feedback to support individual student needs. However, to develop reliable machine learning based AI innovations, it is important that we have appropriate assessment data and reliable human codes to train the machine.

To achieve reliable human codes, iterative training  and revisions took place to reach high human interrater reliability (ranging from 0.67 to 0.83 of Cohen Alpha value). Then we deployed two phases of AI model building. Phase 1 model building focused on predict ing the reasoning pattern label and phase 2 model building aimed to predict spans of text correspondi ng to NGSS dimensions. In phase 1 AI modeling, we compared two models. The first model was a Suppo rt Vector Classifier (SVC) with features extracted from the words in student responses. The second model wa s a fine-tuned BERT transformer model, which took t he Google model trained on massive amounts of English texts and adapted it to the language in our student  data. We used 10-fold cross validation to evaluate the pr ediction in terms of accuracy and macro f-score.  T he transformer model outperformed the SVC model, achieving over 78% accuracy in label prediction (compared to 73% for the SVC). Phase 2 AI modeling aimed to automatically highlight relevant textual evidence corresponding to NGSS dimensions in student responses. We treated  this as a sequence labeling task and used a fine-t uned BERT with Conditional Random Fields to annotate thr ee types of evidence: SEP related evidence, DCI/CCC related evidence, and PROMPT evidence when students  copied texts in the assessment prompt into their responses. The highlighted texts could be useful in formation for teachers to interpret student assessm ent data associated with the three dimensions in science lea rning. Phase 2 modeling achieved over 80% accuracy at the word level compared with human annotations.

## Discussion and Future Work

There are critical challenges in the implementation  of NGSS or NGSS-like standards in many states. Providing a score only is insufficient to support l earning and teaching and, teachers need support to understand student reasoning. The SPIN-NGSS AI innovations hav e the potentials to help teachers hone the knowledg e of attending to student thinking by identifying student reasoning patterns. We just completed the data collection of 45 students to explore how students used the automa ted diagnosis information to revise their explanati ons and will report the results in the presentation. Our future work includes collaborating with teachers to optimize the AI tool use by incorporating their knowledge about ind ividual students’ needs, learning contexts, and oth er background information essential to their learning pathways.
