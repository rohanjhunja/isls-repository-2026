# Which Bright Side to Look at? Cultivating Goal-oriented Shame Regulation Under Challenging Problem-Solving Contexts

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Emotion regulation in authentic learning settings has traditionally been understudied. In the context of problem-solving, understanding how students perceive their emotional experiences and how teachers can effectively facilitate emotion regulation remains unclear. In this study, we introduce an iteratively developed coding framework to examine how students navigate their feelings of shame, a hedonically negative emotion, while attempting problem-solving tasks intentionally designed to be challenging. Our qualitative findings reveal a notable increase in goal-oriented behaviors in the condition where students receive emotion regulation tips to maintain and utilize their shame, compared to conditions where students are provided with hedonic regulation tips or no guidance.

## Introduction

Learning is a challenging endeavor (Bjork & Bjork, 2020). To learn means to put oneself under constant risk of gaining negative feedback, whether stemming from an unsuccessful attempt at problem-solving, a disheartening self-assessment regarding one's laziness, or an unfavorable comparison with peers.  Although learning does offer the potential for feelings of satisfaction, pride, and joy, the darker aspects of the learning process are inescapable and often appear stronger and last longer (Baumeister et al., 2001). While the cognitive aspects of learning have receiv ed significant attention, the emotional context oft en remains neglected or disconnected  (Aldao, 2013; Scheff, 2003). Interventions usually isolate specific cognitive elements, such as memory or problem-solving strategies, without fully exploring students' emotional experiences during challenging learning tasks. Currently, there is  no established framework available to effectively c apture and describe students’ emotional experiences as integral parts of their unique learning journeys. In this study, we use an iteratively developed coding framework to demonstrate how students navigated their emotions when  attempting challenging problem-solving tasks. The tasks were designed to provide an authentic learning context where students can learn from fail ures in a safe environment  (Sinha & Kapur, 2021). Specifically, we focus on the experience of shame, a self-conscious emotion (Silvia, 2009) with both cognitive and affective facets, that arises during adverse learning situations. We seek to understand how individuals perceive and manage their feelings of shame, either through their own efforts or external guidance.

## Theoretical background of goal-oriented shame regulation

In educational settings, challenging problem-solving scenarios frequently elicit a sense of fail ure, and in turn, negatively valenced  emotions, during the learning process. Educators use these challenges  to introduce novel concepts while simultaneously needing to create a s upportive environment that encourages students to p ersist (Kapur, 2016; Sinha & Kapur, 2021). Among frequently occurring emotions naturally present in such learning scenarios (Sinha, 2022), shame holds particular interest because it is a self-conscious emotion that bridges people’s subjective feelings and cognitive judgments based on self-identity. Unlike short-term emotions generated by the problem-solving context such as frustration, shame can persist longer and accumulate, necessitating greater efforts for its regulation and thus making it a suitable target for emotion regulation interventions. Shame arises from a perception of falling short of personal or societal  standards, resulting in an unpleasant experience characterized by negative sel f-evaluation, which can lead to low self-esteem, anxiety, and reduced performance  (Tangney & Dearing, 2002; Tracy et al., 2007). Exis ting research on shame regulation primarily focuses on individuals' natural responses  to shame, with typical responses identified  as self-attack, attacking others, withdrawal, or avoidance (Nathanson, 1994). The optimal strategy to cope with the hedonically negative emotion of shame has been under debate (de Hooge et al., 2008; Ryall, 2019). Commonly employed  strategies to regulate negative emotions at their onset include attentional deployment and cognitive change (Gross, 1998). However, individuals may be driven b y different motivations when regulating their negativ e emotions  (Tamir, 201 6). Hedonic motivations prompt individuals to seek avoidance of negative events – for example, by diverting their attention away from the source of discomfort, or nudging reappraisal of the situation to achieve emotional relief. In contrast, instrumental or goaloriented motivations encourage individuals to approach negative events or reinterpret the situation in  a way that makes them more willing to confront it.

When students instinctively employ hedonic regulation strategies in response to shame, seeking immediate relief and avoiding negative emotions, th eir learning experiences may be hindered or prematu rely terminated due to diverted attention or self-defensive reappraisal. However, when students posse ss the ability to proactively harness negatively valenced emotions like shame as a constructive tool for problem-solving, they are more likely to persist in problem-solving, invest greater effort, and have a higher c hance of meaningful learning outcomes (Leach & Cidam, 2015). Therefore, educators striving to enhance students' resilienc e and adaptability in challenging situations should explore techniques  for facilitating emotional regulation and guide students towards proactive strategies.

In this study, we qualitatively investigate student s' incorporation and implementation of emotion regulation tips provided during a challenging probl em-solving task, addressing a notable research gap in understanding how external shame regulation is adopted by students internally within authentic learning contexts. We integrate emotion regulation model (Gross, 1998) into the framework of hedonic and goal-oriented regulation (Tamir, 2016) to narratively describe and classify the types of strategies used and their effects.

## Participants

We recruited N = 132 participants with diverse ethnic backgrounds and undergraduate majors from two highly ranked European universities using a large pool of student  volunteers (>10,000) to participate in a lab study that lasted 1.5 hours. Each participant was reimbursed 35 CHF for their study completion. The study was approved by the university ethics commission.

## Procedure

Participants' learning experiences consisted of a failure-driven problem-solving prior to instruction design (Sinha & Kapur, 2021). First, they were given two attempts to solve an ill-structured data science problem on Anscombe's Quartet, where the problem involved rank ordering  the success of four car companies based on  four datasets representing units sold and employee satisfaction. The datasets were designed to have  identical descriptive statistics. This required participants to combine numerical and graphical representations to create a fitting ranking. Participants were encouraged to provide as many mea sures as possible.  In the first attempt, they generated solutions without any  scaffolds. In the second attempt, they were provided with th ree failure-driven scaffolds, which were suboptimal ideas related to the problem,  presented sequentially. Afterward s, participants watched a video lecture that consolidated the knowledge, comp ared and contrasted the suboptimal solutions, and demonstrated the canonical solution. Finally, parti cipants took a posttest to assess their learning ou tcomes. Because the problem-solving task was designed based on novel knowledge for the participants, and students exposed to failure-driven scaffolding were found to demonstrate exclus ive dynamics comprising shame (Sinha, 2022), a high failure rate and the incidence of negatively valenced emotions including shame were expected.

## Emotional context of shame

Prior to the first problem-solving attempt, participants were prompted to writ e about a university event that had made them feel shame. The problem-solving task itself was framed as an opportunity fo r participants to engage in a new experience that could re store their self-image. This framing aimed to elicit a self-conscious aspect of shame. Participants were also asked to imagine that their solution attempts would  be reviewed by another study participant who was aware of their previously recal led shameful event, adding a social evaluation aspect to the emotional context (e.g., not wanting others to know about one’s actions, worrying what others would think about oneself). Most strongly, due to the ill-structured nature of the problem-solving task  and the failure-driven scaffolding, participants were expected to experience negatively valenced emotions comprising shame during the problem-solving process. Collectively, these three aspects formed the emotional context of shame  in learning: recalled shame, imagined shame, and experienced shame. Self-report questions assessing participants' emotions after each problem-solving attempt were used as manipulation checks for the emotional context of shame. Three experimental conditions of shame regulation To investigate the differential effects of external hedonic and goal-oriented emotion regulation  scaffolds on students’ regulation of shame, participants were provided with different regulat ion tips between the first and second problem-solving attempts. They were randomly assigned to on e of three conditions  (n = 44 each): two conditions with tips for either down-regulating or maintaining shame and one condition without any regulation tips.

Participants in the down-regulating condition were guided to think about the monetary gain for the study, as an employment of hedonic regulation. They received the following instruction: “When other students in the past have attempted this task and encountered difficulties, they also felt shame. However, they did not dwell too much on it and instead felt happy that they were at least getting paid for the study.

TIP: So, you may also try to avoid acknowledging that you may be feeling shame and always put a positive spin on your experiences.” Participants in the maintaining condition were guided to  think about the utility of shame, as an employment of goal-oriented regulation. They received the following instruction: “When other students in the past have attempted this task and encountered difficulties, they also felt shame. However, they found shame to be useful because this led them to self-reflection and approaching the task with even more focused efforts to restore their self-image. TIP: So, you may try to cultivate and experience shame in pursuit of your task goals and not assume the worst.” Participants in the no-tip condition were expected to naturally manage their own emotions wit hout explicit external guidance. They received the following instruction: “You now have another opportunity to re-attempt the task. Please go  ahead and revise your solutions.” Prompts of the open-ended question about shame regulation After the second problem-solving attempt, participants were first asked to re-read the emotion regulation tip they received, rate its sensibility and their level of incorporati on into the problem-solving task. Following this, participants were prompted to briefly explain how they incorporated the regulation tip while working through the problem (for the two conditions with regulation tips) or how they manage d their emotions (for the condition without regulation tips).

Development of the coding framework To comprehensively understand responses to the open-ended questions and observe trends across three conditions, we first developed a qualitative coding framework. Subsequently, we quantified the coding results for analysis. The coding framework for the regulation of shame  was developed based on the verbal descriptions provided by participants in response to the questio n of how they incorporated the regulation tip while  working through the problem or how they managed their emoti ons. Our goal was to identify and categorize the different strategies and approaches used by participants to i ncorporate the regulation tip and regulate their em otions, specifically focusing on shame. We note that individuals varied in their experience of emotions during the study. Some participants did not feel shame at all, while others experienced it right from the beginning. Mor eover, participants held different perceptions regarding the sensibilit y of the regulation tips provided to them, choosing either to incorporate or ignore them. Even among th ose who chose to incorporate the tips, the specific regulation strategy employed was dependent on their personal understanding of the tip and their skill in emotion regulation. Participants also provided observations and reflections after attempting to manage their emotions. An interactive approach involving deductive and inductive methods was  thus employed in developing this framework. Theories of interest provided the initial scheme, and data served as a cross-check to determine the appropriate one. Further refinement of dimensio ns focused on theoretically significant aspects distinguishing hedonic regulation from goal-oriented regulation. Finally, the coding framework was consolidated into a 4-stage scheme based on the process model  of emotion regulation (Gross, 2015). The process of developing the coding framework is demonstrated in Fig ure 1. In this framework, we conceptualized participants’ regulati on of shame as a dynamic endeavor. It started with the  identification and evaluation of emotional experiences, where the presence of shame was contingent on whether self-image damage was perceived. Subsequently, in the two conditions with regulation tips, participants evalu ated and selected whether and how to incorporate the provided tips based on perceived benefits. Depen ding on their interpretation of the tips and person al backgrounds, participants might have implemented them to facilitate problem-solving or alleviate negative feelings. Ultimately, participants may also have monitored their emotion regulation efforts, assessing alignme nt or discrepancies with their intentions.

Specifically, in the design of the final monitoring stage coding, a relative benchmark based on individual intentions was used rather than an absolute standard. This was due to the fact that individuals might retain diverse intentions even after receiving regulation tips. For example, in the maintaining group, although participants were suggested to maintain their shame level, some still chose not to incorporate this suggestion because they thought it was too counter-intuitive or not helpful. Instead, they had the intention to regulate their emotions hedonically, which makes an absolute coding based on experimental conditions unreasonable. ## Results and discussion

Following Chi (1997), we coded responses for the em otion regulation question in our study to gain quantitative insights into the trend of thoughts demonstrated in the process. Two raters with different ethnic backgrounds first coded 20% of the data and resolved disagreements, a fter which one rater coded the rest of the data. Co ding was conducted blind to students’ ethnicity and condition.

Because the open-ended question allowed for varied responses without specifying regulation stages, and participants differed in their ability to articulat e their emotion regulations, responses often did not encomp ass all stages, resulting in totals not reaching 44 for each condition group in each stage. However, each response received at least one code across the four stages which comp rehensively covered the process of emotion regulation and provided strong explanatory power.  Double coding was allowed in the selection and implementation stages, as individuals may agree or disagree with the tip for different reasons and employ multiple strategies to regulate their emotions. The average Cohen’s 끫븊 across the four coded levels (Table 1) fell in the range of 0.69-0.75 before resolving disagreements. The disagreements were mainly due to the differences in participants’ verbosity. More tolerated self-image damage for maintaining condition In the identification stage (level 1), responses were coded by their explicit verbalization of self-image damage (Table 2). People in the maintaining shame condition (M = 0.227, SD = 0.424) reported less self-image damage compared to the other conditions (M = 0.375, SD = 0.487) from our one-tailed independent samples t-test analysis (t (130) = 1.713, p = 0.045, d = 0.316). One potential explanation could be that the maintaining regulation tip prompted participants to focus less on their emotions, leading to reduced acknowledgment of self-image damage. A more compelling explanation emerges from our exploration of the manipulation check. Despite underreporting self-image damage in textual answers, participants in the maintaining condition rated the highest levels of shame in self-report questions about emotions compared to other conditions. This discrepancy suggests that, rather than making people fee l less shame,  the maintaining regulation tip en couraged participants to focus more on the situation and act more goal-oriented instead of dwelling on their own shortcomings, making similar or greater levels of shame tolerable to them.

## More unideal regulation results for down-regulating condition

In the monitoring stage (level 4), responses were coded according to the verbalization of regulation results based on individual intention. Given the adoption of a relative benchmark, quantitative analysis of the code was not informative. However, upon coding the responses, a prevalent narrative emerged in the down-regulating condition-participants reported not feeling better even though they tried to. For example:

“It did help a bit to remember that I'm getting paid. However, I feel even more ashamed now because I feel like I failed even with the help on my 2nd attempt(s) so t he negative feelings definitely outweigh the positive. It's nice that I'm getting paid but I still feel very stupid.” Several potential explanations may account for this  pattern. First, the prompt in  the down-regulating condition encouraged a focus on emotional pleasure, leading p articipants to express more about their regulation outcomes. Second, the hedonic goals of emotion regulation, while intuitive, may have presented a conflict for the downregulating participants. Despite attempting to focus on the br ight side of monetary gain unrelated to problem-solving, the monitoring mechanism prevented self-deception when the problem-solving situation did not meet expectations. This finding reveals the multiple dimensions people use to evaluate thei r emotion regulation outcomes and suggests a potential downside to hedonic regulation when it results in dissonance between intentions and reality.

## General discussion and significance of this study

In the face of challenging situations and failures, conventional wisdom often suggests "looking at the bright side" as a means of seeking relief and focusing on the side gains from the experience. However, the true "brigh t side" can also encompass engaging with the challenge, eve n if it entails enduring negative ly valenced emotions for longer and struggling more, as it ultimately leads to valuable learning. The findings from this study indicate that encouraging students to engage in deeper reflection and leverage negatively valenced emotions can enhance their resilience and goal-oriented behavior. Students who received external goal-oriented regulation tips demonstrated greater tolerance for shame, directed more attention towards problem-solving, engaged in reappraising challenges to motivate themselves, and achieved more favorable  regulation outcomes. They also expressed a desire to learn more about developing the skills to effectively use  shame. This suggests the need for future investigations into specific goal-oriented emotion regulation strategies and the role  of teachers in explicitly facilitating emotions during problem-solving, as these aspects hold promise for enhancing students' emotional resilience and learning.

The study's significance spans both theoretical and methodological dimensions. The comparison between goal-oriented and hedonic regulation strategies illustra tes the immediate impact of  brief emotional instruction during learning. This challenges the traditional be lief about what is considered "positive and desirab le," suggesting that purely hedonic regulation of negati vely valenced emotions may not be as positive as commonly believed. On the other hand, enduring such emotions and actively engaging in chall enges can ultimately reveal their bright side. Methodologically, the coding framework developed in this study is adaptable for analyzing other self-conscious emotions in authentic learning contexts, as it considers both the individual and their overa rching objectives. Since this study was conducted during an authentic  problem-solving setting and gathered  rich information about emotional responses and thoughts,  it provides insights into previously unexplored em otional contexts when approaching problem-solving as a means of learning. Findings of this study need to be combined with mor e data on participants' learning outcomes and behavioral measurements to establish the efficacy of goal-oriented regulation on learning. A detailed exploration of the mechanisms underlying individual emotional p erception and regulation, using multimodal methods,  is recommended. Subsequent studies could investigate the long-term impact of goal-oriented regulation on learning and well-being, leading to a more nuanced understanding of interact ions among students, teachers, and the learning environment. Examining the applicability of the coding framework to diverse self-conscious emotions and learning contexts is essential for a broader understanding of emotion regulation in real learning contexts.
