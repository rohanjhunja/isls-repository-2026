# Building a Research Agenda on the Intersection of Art, Culture, and Education in Brazil

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster presents an ongoing research initiative in Brazil that seeks to generate empirical evidence on the intersections between art, culture, and education for the holistic development of children and adolescents. The initiative emerged from a formal partnership between government institutions and a philanthropic organization, intending to integrate educational and cultural data systems, evaluate the impact of arts and culture on academic and socioemotional outcomes, and mapping international policy experiences.

The initiative emerged from a formal  partnership between government institutions and a philanthropic organization, intending to integrate educational and cultural data systems, evaluate the impact of arts and culture on academic and socioemotional outcomes, and mapping international policy experiences. Across the world, education systems are increasingly recognizing that learning extends beyond academic achievement to encompass social, emotional, and creative development (OECD, 2019). Within this broader understanding, the arts have gained recognition as powerful enablers of student engagement, empathy, and critical thinking (Eisner, 2002; Winner & Hetland, 2008). However, despite this consensus, empirical evidence on how arts and cultural participation influence learning trajectories remains fragmented, particularly in the Global South. In Brazil, this issue intersects with persistent social inequalities and uneven access to both education and cultural opportunities. Addressing these structural disparities requires intersectoral efforts that combine educational, cultural, and social policy perspectives. Considering that, and the power of purposeful partnerships in 2024, the federal government, within the Ministry of Education (MEC), the Ministry of Culture (Minc) and Itaú Foundation (Fundação Itaú) in Brazil, signed a Technical Cooperation Agreement, establishing a purposeful partnership to generate a research agenda to develop scientific evidence on the relationship between arts, culture, and education. Also, Itaú Foundation (Fundação Itaú) developed an agreement with the Secretary of Education and the Secretary of Culture of the City of São Paulo for the same objective. These initiatives aim to inform national strategies for holistic education, an approach that seeks the full development of learners beyond academic metrics.

These efforts align with recent discussions in the Learning Sciences emphasizing the need to understand learning in its broader sociocultural ecosystems (Sawyer, 2014; Barron, 2006). It positions art experimentation and cultural participation as a site of meaningful learning, offering new perspectives for research and policymaking in diverse, inequitable contexts such as Brazil The concept of holistic or whole learning recognizes  that education should promote not only cognitive but also emotional, social, and creative growth (Bruner, 1999; Eisner, 2002). Within this paradigm, the arts are understood as essential to humandevelopment because they cultivate imagination, empathy, and cultural awareness, skills increasingly valued in the 21st century (Winner et al., 2013). Studies have shown that arts-based learning can enhance student engagement and academic motivation, but systematic, large-scale evidence remains limited (Catterall, 2009; OECD, 2019).

Analyzing Brazil in a comparative perspective, the results present that education plays a key role in fostering engagement in artistic and cultural activities, and data from PISA 2022 show that Brazilian students stand out compared to the OECD average. In Brazil, 6 3% of 15-year-olds reported participating in artistic or cultural activities at least once a week, compared to 48% in OECD countries, while only 15% said they had never participated, against 34% in the OECD average (Fundação Itaú, 2025b). Brazilian schools also provide these experiences more frequently, except in music-related activities, where access is slightly lower. However, significant inequalities persist: although students from lower socioeconomic backgrounds participate more than their peers in OECD countries, the gap between high-and lowincome students is wider in Brazil, indicating that the education system still struggles to reduce disparities in access. This inequality is reinforced by differences in the availability of activities-particularly theatre and music-across schools with different socioeconomic profiles. Since cultural consumption in Brazil is highly concentrated

among wealthier households, schools play a decisive role in ensuring access to art and culture, especia lly for 2731 students from low-income families, who make up the majority of Brazilian households but have lower participation in cultural practices (Fundação Itaú, 2025b). Following these findings, in the city of São Paulo, a first round of quasi-experimental evaluation was conducted with students from municipal public schools that are part of one of the music program initiatives (Programa Guri), which is a music initiation course focused on developing musical skills through singing and instruments. The sample was formed by students between 10 and 16 years old, who were still in or finishing the Middle School Years. Students with 1 to 3 years of participation in the program, in comparison with newly enrolled students who have never been part of the program. With these arrangements, we had a sample of 233 students in the treatment group and 2.671 students in the control group, ending with a total sample of 2.904 students (Fundação Itaú, 2025a). In the first round of results, by strands of analysis, it was identified that by gender, the participation in the program improves the scientific skills of girls by 16%, and improves the emotional control of boys also by 16%. By age, when evaluating how the students self-evaluated themselves regarding their mathematics and Portuguese skills, it's possible to see that 10-year-old students have an improvement of 35% and 28% when participating in the program. Positive effects relating the students' grade in ma thematics with their participation in the music initiation program were also discovered, but with the reduced sample, it is still not a robust finding. While still one building agenda, these findings showcase how some effects are seen in the relation of ar t and culture participation and experimentation with learning sciences, especially with what we consider through how these can engage and improve students' self-value, self-esteem and learning performance. Contributions By integrating data systems and conducting mixed-method studies, this initiative seeks to significantly expand the evidence base on the effects of arts and culture in education. It responds and initiates a movement to the persistent lack of quantitative, large-scale analyses in Latin American contexts. The resulting body of evidence can be deepened and guide policymakers in designing intersectoral frameworks that connect cultural and educational investments to child development outcomes, reinforcing the value of art experimentation and cultural participation as a dimension of learning and well-being. In doing so, the research advances the field of Learning Sciences by bridging education research and cultural policy. It reinforces a lens on learning within artistic and cultural ecosystems, showing how museums, theatres, and community spaces can serve as  non-formal learning environments that complement the school setting. This approach broadens current understandings of how learners construct meaning, motivation, and identity through artistic and cultural engagement (Bransford et al., 2006; Sawyer, 2014). The partnership between the Ministry of Education, the Ministry of Culture, and Itaú Foundation exemplifies purposeful collaboration aimed at systemic transformation. It illustrates how public –philanthropic cooperation can generate research infrastructures and evidence frameworks beyond the reach of individual institutions. More importantly, it reinforces the idea that learning and development must be understood in relation to cultural rights and equitable access to creative opportunities, not solely through traditional educational outcomes. Drawing on these axes, the study proposes a set of preliminary recommendations designed to inspire policy innovation and foster systemic collaboration. These include the creation of specific funding mechanisms for students in vulnerable situations, support  for the equitable implementation of arts curricula, the development of local collaborative networks, and the establishment of clear intersectoral governance structures.
