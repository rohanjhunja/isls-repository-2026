# Beyond the Critical-Creative Divide: Generative Game-Based Learning in the University Literature Classroom

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This design-based research study implemented three iterations of game-based learning in undergraduate literature courses to explore how constructionist game-based learning supports identity exploration and transformative learning. Using learning experience network analysis of student reflections, we found timing differences across iterations. When game elements and identity work were woven throughout the semester (iterations 1-2), students demonstrated deeper transformative learning and identity engagement aligned with theoretical principles. In contrast, condensing the game into an end-of-term unit (iteration 3) increased collaborative struggles and reduced identity exploration, despite attempts to improve scaffolding. Findings suggest that early, sustained identity work may be a critical design principle for integrating game-based learning in disciplinary higher education classrooms.

Constructionist learning designs and game-based learning work well together (Holbert et al., 2014; Kafai & Burke, 2016), but are often associated with K12, after-school or summer programs, and maker spaces. The higher education literature classroom is not a space that commonly employs constructionist and game-based learning. Indeed, some teachers of literature are even prejudiced against this sort of creative learning within the discipline of literary studies, a prejudice derived from a false distinction be tween the critical and the creative. Even when such prejudice does not exist, literary critics often have little to no experience with the creative act of making literature because English departments are often divided according to their analytical and cre ative camps (Kaguhangire-Barifaijo et al., 2022). As a result, our decision to employ constructionist principles and game-based learning (Gee, 2005) is unique within the disciplinary framework of the higher education literature classroom.

We first implemented generative role-playing game design in Spring 2023 to students in a science fiction classroom at a large research university (Cooper, 2024). We structured the first two iterations (Spring 2023 & Spring 2024) fairly similarly in that for both courses, we “weaved” elements of the role-playing game design into the reading, thereby making the “game” a semester-long experience. In the first iteration, we discovered that students exhibited a great deal of anxiety about perceived ‘entry barriers.’ For example, if they have never played a role-playing game of any kind, they are likely to exhibit distress. After the first iteration, we re-designed to include an introductory gaming session at the beginning of the semester to alleviate this stress. This was successful in the second iteration, but the data still seemed to display an ‘entry-barrier’ anxiety (Cooper, 2025). An individual student from the course helped us interpret the data and made a valuable insight: the entry-barrier also exists for those who do not see themselves as ‘creative’. Creating can be ambiguous, and ambiguity can be scary. To add to it, most students have never been expected to be creative for an English literature course. The demand contradicts their ingrained expectations regarding course delivery, shifting heavily from passive to active learning techniques. In the Fall of 2024, the nuances of course assignments gave us an opportunity to test the design in a slightly different course. Whereas the first two iterations were conducted for ENGL 334, Science Fiction Past & Present, the Fall 2024 design was implemented for ENGL 434, Advanced Studies in Science Fiction and Fantasy. We maintained an opening mini-game to alleviate any concerns about never having played a role-playing game, but instead of weaving the game elements throughout the semester, the game elements were organized into a structured unit at the end of the course. Students were allowed to work in smaller groups that allowed for less ambiguity and contradiction, and they were provided with artistic materials (markers, posterboards, etc.) by the professor. This was meant to create more comfort with creativity by harkening back to a simpler time by drawing with markers in groups on the floor. This new design focused on collaboration and creation, but overall allowed students less time to engage with their avatar identity.

## Theoretical framework

We incorporated seven theoretical frameworks for this study. Our core claim is that constructionist generative game-based learning and roleplay supports learning as becoming through identity exploration, with agency and 1487 relatedness as key drivers in authentic contexts. The theoretical foundation of the learning design was constructionist theory, emphasizing learning through making where students construct knowledge by creating personally-meaningful and authentic artifacts (Papert & Harel, 1991). To increase the theoretical depth of the knowledge construction aspects, two traditions of constructivism were used. Cognitive constructivism, rooted in Piaget’s theories (1952), focuses on the individual learner's internal knowledge construction, whereas social constructivism highlights the construction of knowledge through interaction with others in the learning environment (Powell & Kalina, 2009). Furthermore, we believed that constructionist learning is only effective when learners are deeply engaged, and therefore integrated principles from self-determination theory to help us focus on fostering intrinsic motivation through increasing learner agency, competence, and relatedness (Ryan & Deci, 2017). To expand our theoretical frame beyond knowledge construction, we also grounded our study in situated learning theory, which emphasizes the context and environment in which learning occurs (Lave & Wenger, 1991). According to this theory, learning happens as students engage with their discipline’s community through interactions with the environment and others in the community (Wenger, 2009). Because learning is not only constructive, but also a process of becoming (Donaldson & Allen-Handy, 2023), we needed theories to address the identity-related aspect of the students. The dynamic systems model of role identity (DSMRI) views identity as a continually evolving entity, with individuals in a constant state of “becoming.” In this state, various experiences can shape and alter a person's roles, perceived possibilities for action, and self-perceptions (Kaplan & Garner, 2017). Similarly, transformative learning theory aims to develop a learner’s identity by making their frame of reference more inclusive. This is achieved through discourse that challenges and expands the assumptions held by learners and others (Mezirow, 2000). These theories collectively informed not only the design of the learning experiences, but also our study design and analysis by providing a multifaceted lens through which to examine the complex interplay between generative role-play game-based learning, identity exploration, and knowledge construction in a literature classroom setting. They guided our understanding of how students engage with the course material, construct meaning and identity, and potentially transform their perspectives through the role-playing experience.

The study followed a design-based research methodology (Barab, 2022), implementing successive iterations of a Dungeons & Dragons-style tabletop role-playing game integrated into the literature curriculum. Each iteration informed design modifications for subsequent implementations which allowed us to systematically address identified challenges and leverage successful elements using learning experience network analysis (Donaldson et al., 2024).

The first iteration was conducted in Spring 2023 with 39 undergraduate students enrolled in a science fiction literature course. Students engaged in a four-step process that included collaborative creation of fictional sci-fi worlds and races, individual character development with distinct identities and reflective components exploring how situations affected character and personal identity, random party formation requiring students to navigate interactions with "others," and scenario design and gameplay. S tudent reflection papers  (n = 39) were collected at the end of the semester. Data were coded in MAXQDA qualitative analysis software using both a priori coding for theoretical principles and in vivo coding for emergent themes related to struggles and what worked well. This resulted in 98 codes covering 755 coded segments. Correlation analysis of all code pairs produced a symmetrical correlation matrix, which was transformed into a network database using UCINET software. Network mapping, Girvan-Newman clust er analysis (Girvan & Newman, 2002), and betweenness centrality calculations were conducted to identify patterns in learner experiences at the p < 0.01 significance level. Three clusters emerged (Q =.413) revealing struggles with engagement, class pacing, entry-level barriers for nongamers, personal preparedness anxieties, and world-building complexity. Strengths included creativity, autonomy, community building, and alignment with multiple theoretical principles. The second iteration was conducted in Spring 2024 with 23 undergraduate students enrolled in a science fiction literature course. Based on findings from the first iteration, several design modifications were implemented. The design began with the wiki framework created by previous students to reduce the world-building barrier. Role-playing was extended beyond gameplay by having students read and analyze course texts from their character's perspective, consciously leveraging identity exploration as a pedagogical tool. The four-phase game structure was maintained with enhanced scaffolding. Student reflection papers (n = 23) were collected at the end of the semester and the same analytical approach was employed. Data were coded using MAXQDA with in vivo coding for emergent themes and a priori coding for theoretical principles, resulting in 56 codes. Correlation analysis was conducted at the p < 0.001 significance level, and the correlation matrix was used to construct a network map in UCINET. Girvan-Newman cluster analysis identified four clusters with adjusted node sizes based on betweenness centrality values (Q =.562). Four clusters revealed that while building on prior work 1488 reduced some entry barriers, it introduced new challenges as students struggled to parse existing wiki content. Gameplay mechanics remained a concern for some students. However, one cluster showed no struggles and strong connections to transformative learning, self-determination theory, and DSMRI principles, indicating profound identity exploration and transformational experiences for many learners. The third iteration was conducted in Fall 2024 with 25 undergraduate students in a game-based learning course. Based on findings from the first two iterations, additional design modifications were implemented. The use of pre-existing wiki content was eliminated, requiring all students to start fresh to reduce information processing demands. Introductory mini-games were introduced early in the semester to familiarize students with game mechanics and alleviate anxiety before major gameplay sessions. The integ ration of role-playing with literary analysis was further refined throughout the semester. Student reflection papers (n = 23) and additional qualitative data were collected at the end of the semester. The same network analysis approach was employed, using MAXQDA for coding, correlation analysis for identifying relationships among codes, and UCINET for network mapping and Girvan-Newman cluster analysis. Four clusters emerged from the analysis (Q =.588), examining how design modifications addressed previously identified struggles and whether new patterns of learner experiences emerged.

Across all three iterations, we examined longitudinal patterns in student experiences, identifying persistent challenges, consistently successful design elements, and the cumulative effects of iterative refinements. The research question was: How can we develop an increasingly sophisticated understanding through a cross-iteration analysis of how constructionist, role-playing GBL can be leveraged to support literature learning, identity exploration, and transformative educational experiences in higher education? Across all three iterations, there are shared examples of constructionist and social constructivist theory working in practice. Students generally find the process of creating a world and characters to be profoundly transformative. Idea generation and open-ended creativity are also appealing to them because they are given the opportunity to take control of their fantasy worlds. Finally, the collaborative elements are pronounced throughout every iteration. Clustering became cleaner and more interpretable across iterations. The first run produced 3 clusters at Q =.413; the second 4 clusters at Q =.562; the third 4 clusters at Q =.588. Rising Q suggests more distinct patterns of cooccurrence in learner experiences, consistent with LENA’s guidance that highe r Q values indicate more meaningful community structure.

Some struggles persisted across all three iterations, primarily feelings of nervousness and anxiety, especially for those who do not consider themselves to be ‘creative’ or who have never played role-playing games prior to the course. Despite our efforts t o reduce ‘entry-level’ barriers, such barriers were not eliminated despite redesigning to include mini-games. Despite the barriers being impossible to eliminate, the data does show they are possible to mitigate, and that our choices to include a mini-game have helped this mitigation. For iteration 3, we reduced the cognitive load necessary to play the game by not basing it in previous iterations, as we did in iteration. Despite this, students still struggled “finding a place to start,” perhaps indicating again that such concerns can only be alleviated, not solved, by data-driven design. Creation involves risk-taking and comfort with ambiguity; such mental states cause anxiety, but one that can be mastered over time, as proven by many artists. In the third iteration, a new struggle emerged that had not been as pronounced until now: struggles with teams. While iteration three demonstrates many successes with collaboration, it also exhibits an unusual degree of struggles. Perhaps this is because students were required to work in smaller, more close-knit groups during the third iteration. This change in teamwork appears to be substantial, and may have reduced iteration 3’s ability to offer the kind of transformative identity experience that students  identified in iteration 2. In fact, the struggles related to community may explain why the third iteration contains far fewer theory nodes than iteration 2, which showed no struggles when it came to transformative learning or DSMRI. In iteration 2, the theory nodes correspond to this identity work. From this we hypothesize that when the identity work happens early in the semester, students can engage with that identity on a deeper level. This identity work is the source of the transformative experience displayed in iteration 2, an experience we deem essential to our design. The design changes taken between iteration 2 and iteration 3 served to highlight previously unexamined strengths of the original designs and to draw forth practical and theoretical issues that need to be addressed. The unexamined strengths of the original design included the careful identity exploration scaffolding (Kaplan et al., 2014) throughout the semester which enabled an entire semester of role identity work from the students (Kaplan & Garner, 2017). Future iterations will not implement the game in a single chunk and will instead weave the gaming elements through the course.  This will have a few advantages. One, students will be able to engage with their 1489 identity early on. We previously recognized that when students created an identity in the game, they suddenly displayed more “buy-in”, engaging more actively with the wikifandom but also interacting more in the course. The creation of this identity must se rve as an anchor point early in the semester to maximize the student’s engagement. When they did not create their avatars until the end of the semester, students seemed to be more “checked-out”, as indicated by the struggles with teamwork shown in iteration 3. Early identity work also allows for the possibility of role-storming and role-analyzing (Kaplan & Garner, 2017); in both cases, students think about issues from the novels we read but think about them as if they were their characters. In iteration 2, these moments seemed to contribute greatly to game engagement and reading engagement. To maintain this focus on identity while tapping into the collaborative aspects will be a primary issue that needs re-design in future iterations. In iteration 3, the struggles with teamwork seem to inhibit the possibility of deepened theoretical integration; the maps simply portray far fewer theory nodes than previous iterations. In the first two iterations, students worked in large, oversized groups to create a future Earth and an alien species. This did not lead to teamwork struggles, but it did create s ome confusion and difficulty with ambiguity or contradiction. In the current iteration (data not yet collected), students have been divided into smaller groups and given more worlds to create. Our hypothesis is that this early group engagement, when coupled with early identity work, will motivate the students to more broadly engage with each other’s perspectives and frames of reference (Mezirow, 2000) while maintaining a  profound sense of identity transformation situated within a community of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Wenger, 2009).  This identity transformation is the central piece, a  lesson that can perhaps be adapted to a wide range of classroom types, not just the higher education literature classroom. Several limitations affect this study. We relied solely on end-of-semester reflection papers, which may be subject to recency bias and do not capture in-the-moment experiences. Our network analysis approach, while revealing important patterns, may obscure individual variation in learner experiences. As with all design-based research, this study was deeply contextual (Barab, 2022). All iterations occurred at a single institution with one primary instructor, and therefore more studies in other contexts will be needed to further explore the patterns we found. While our findings emerge from this very specific context, several principles may transfer to other higher education settings where instructors want to integrate creative game-based making with analytical work. When designing role-playing or identity-based activities, it may be important to provide opportunities for students to develop and engage with their created identities early in the semester, allowing extended time for deep exploration. There may be v alue in recognizing that anxiety about creativity and ambiguity cannot be eliminated but can be reduced through low-stakes introductory activities. When integrating theory into teaching practice, the timing and structure of collaborative work may have a si gnificant impact on the depth of identity exploration students experience. Learning experience network analysis offers a productive method for examining relationships between design features and learning outcomes across multiple iterations. For literature instructors specifically, our findings suggest that analytical and creative work need not be separated. Having students analyze texts through their character's perspective appears to have created deeper engagement with both the reading and their creative identities.
