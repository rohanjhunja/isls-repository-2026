# Examining Youth Authority in Community Member Interviews during the Co- Design of an Environmental Engineering Curriculum

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines youth authority in interviews with community members during a series of environmental engineering curriculum co-design sessions. Seven high school students, together with teachers, university researchers and informal environmental educators, participated in this multi-session task. The study aimed to understand how youth articulate, realize, and negotiate authority throughout the process. The qualitative analysis of audio recordings from co-design group discussions, formal interviews, reflections, and end-of-year interviews yielded three themes. First, youth authority drew from different sources across different activities. Second, facilitated activities scaffolded youth to realize epistemic and deontic authority. Third, youth authority emerged through adapting and challenging tasks. These findings extend the previous macro-level analyses of power dynamics and suggest that a balance between scaffolding and autonomy is critical to allow youth to exercise authority. This work also contributes to broader literature on power, authority, and equitable youth engagement in curriculum design.

There has been increasing research engaging youth in participatory and co-design activities within STEM education, particularly in community-based and justice-oriented projects (e.g., Calabrese Barton et al., 2020; Gyles, 2024; Ongon et al., 2021). By participating in these projects, youth contribute their unique perspectives and ideas based on their prior experiences and knowledge (Schenkel & Calabrese Barton, 2020). Recently, researchers have also begun to pa y attention to power hierarchies in these contexts. Research has shown that recognizing and disrupting existing power hierarchies can foster a more equitable environment for youth to meaningfully engage in the co-design process (Jadallah et al., 2024). While many studies have examined power distribution within co-design activities and proposed suggestions to make c o-design processes equitable (e.g. Higgins et al., 2019; Kyza & Agesilaou, 2022), less attention has been given to the moment-to-moment ways in which youth articulate and realize their authority in co-design sessions. Analyzing the youth authority provides the analytical granularity to explain how influence is effectively realized and  negotiated during the moment-tomoment process of co-design.

To address this gap, we transition from a broad analysis of power to a focused examination of authority in co-design sessions centered on conducting community member interviews (CMI) in a design-based implementation research project. Authority, in this case, is defined as “a basic form of power” and the legitimate capacity to influence collective decisions and actions (Ekström & Stevanovic, 2023). Youth authority is suitable for co-design research since it is inherently interactional and tied to the “right to contribute” to a shared task (Stevanovic & Peräkylä, 2012). In curriculum co-design settings, where the participants are expected to contribute knowledge, make collective decisions, and take action, the authority framework is important since it allows us to distinguish between epistemic authority and deontic authority (Heritage & Raymond, 2005). Drawing on prior social theory and political philosophy, we adopt Ekström and Stevanovic’s (2023) conceptualization of authority. Specifically, they conceptualize authority through three interrelated dimensions: (1) sources, (2) realization, and

(3) negotiation. The question that guides this study is: How do youth articulate, realize, and negotiate their

authority throughout the process of CMI within a co-design context? By examining the youth authority in this concrete and collaborative task, we aim to advance analysis of power dynamics in participatory design activities and inform the facilitation of future youth-engaged co-design in community-based contexts. Framing Authority is a multifaceted construct with deep roots in social theories. Prior research has approached related questions through many lenses, such as participation framework and positioning theory (e.g., Goffman, 1981; 454 Harré & van Langenhove, 1999). While all these frameworks and theories provide valuable insights into authority, they are less directly aligned with our focus on how authority can be enacted through specific design within codesign contexts in the learning sciences. Hence, we draw on Ekström and Stevanovic’s (2023) conceptualization of authority as it provides a dynamic and three-dimensional lens for examining how authority is articulated, realized, and negotiated in situated interactions.

Sources of authority “is grounded in realities structuring distinctive relationships in institutional contexts” (Ekström & Stevanovic, 2023, p. 5). Two primary forms are: authority in virtue of specific competence, derived from expertise, experience, or unique knowledge; and authority in virtue of office, rooted in formal roles. Realization of authority  concerns how authority becomes visible in interaction. Previous literature has distinguished two forms of realization of authority: epistemic authority and deontic authority. Epistemic authority refers to the rights to know, define, and interpret aspects of the task (Heritage & Raymond, 2005), while deontic authority refers to the rights to decide and determine the course of joint action (Stevanovic & Peräkylä, 2012). Negotiation of authority  occurs in the turn-by-turn conversations. It may be “claimed, justified, approved, or resisted” (Ekström & Stevanovic, 2023, p. 5).

In this study, this framework was operationalized by anchoring the CMI in youth roles, lived experiences, and community-based knowledge and values. For instance, their sources of authority can be derived from their powered positionalities (as a formal inte rviewer) and community-based knowledge in co-design spaces. Youth realization of authority can be observed through their abilities to inform decision-making in practice, such as setting agenda for CMI. Finally, youth authority is not static, but, instead, can be negotiated through external conditions (e.g., design of task, assigned roles) or by the co-participants.

## Participants and context of study

This study is situated within a multi-year research project funded by NSF. The overarching goal of the project is to co-design and test a high school environmental engineering curriculum centered on a local river system. The co-design stage of this project adopts a participatory co-design methodology, where seven students, three teachers, two informal environmental educators, and four to five researchers acted as equal design partners to determine instructional goals, identify community-relevant challenges, and assess potential classroom activities. The student cohort consisted of five females and two males representing a diverse range of backgrounds: four Hispanic students, two Asian students, and one African American student. These participants were recruited from three local high schools (Riverside, Oakwood, and Maple Grove) and were in the 11th and 12th grade. Demographic information was collected through pre-program surveys and student enrollment forms administered at the start of the project. All names and schools used in this manuscript are pseu donyms. Participants took part in 11 co-design sessions in various formats, including but not limited to group July 2025), designed and facilitated by the university researchers and local environmental educators. Through the 11 co-design sessions, participants identified three relevant environmental engineering challenges around the river and tested potential classroom activities and instructional materials that cou ld be integrated into the curriculum. In the testing stage, environmental educators and university researchers synthesized the products and findings (e.g., the three environmental engineering challenges and CMI data analysis activities) generated during the co-design sessions into a curriculum draft. Then the three participating high school teachers implemented this curriculum in their classrooms with about 260 students to evaluate its feasibility and impact.

In this paper, we focus on the youth-led CMI conducted across several co-design sessions and students’ reflections on the CMIs in their  end-of-year individual student interviews. CMIs  were conducted to gather information about community members’ experiences and knowledge of the local river system, which helped the participants to identify the engineering challenges, and supported students’ skill development around qualitative interviewing. The CMI task was structured in three phases (pre-interview, interview, and post-interview). Phase 1 (pre-interview) consisted of two co-design sessions. In the first session, students engaged in several activities to support their understanding of how to conduct qualitative interviews with community members. In a co-design session attended in person by seven high school students, five university researchers, and two local environmental educators, participants collectively determined the interview topics, identified a range of prospective interviewees from the local community, and developed a bank of potential interview questions. Youth were also introduced to basic interview skills, such as active listening, notetaking, and follow-up questioning in this session. To practice these skills, students first evaluated example interviews demonstrated by facilitators (university researchers and environmental educators). Afterwards, students  themselves participated in mock interviews with their peers and received feedback from the facilitators. In the second co-design session, held virtually and focused on crafting 455 personalized interview protocols for the selected community members, students worked collaboratively with four university researchers, and adapted the question bank to fit their assigned interviewees’ backgrounds and expertise. As a result, students developed a unique interview protocol for each interviewee (community member). In Phase 2 (interview) university researchers contacted community members, confirmed their willingness to participate, and scheduled the interviews based on mutual availability. Each y outh conducted one or two community interviews depending on their availability and that of the interviewee. Interviews were conducted in pairs (except one) to promote collaboration and peer support, with one university researcher present as an observer to provide assistance, if needed. This structure aimed to ensure youth could lead conversations while having access to support when necessary.

In Phase 3 (post-interview) youth participated in an in-person debrief of the interview process. They reflected on what they had learned from the experience, new knowledge about the river they developed, and their improved interviewing skills. In addition, the co-design session facilitator asked them t o  identify their peers’ strength during the interview process, creating space for upliftment and peer support. Finally, some students also reflected on the CMI in their end-of-year individual interviews when prompted to provide examples of their codesign experiences. In the findings below, we focus specifically on the CMI process, rather than the full co-design and follow-up curriculum testing, to show how youth authority dynamically evolved across different CMI activities. Across the three themes, youth authority becomes visible through a progression: it first emerges and shifts through formally assigned roles, is further scaffolded through facilitated activities, and ultimately develops into off-script moments in which youth actively negotiate their authority.

Theme 1: Sources of youth authority shifted across phases of the CMI process The analysis demonstrated that youth authority was not static, but highly adaptive and drew from different sources that evolved throughout the CMI process. In the first few generative activities at Phase 1, including determining interview topics and identifying potential interviewees, their authority was based exclusively in virtue of competence. Youth drew mostly from their historical community-based knowledge, river management knowledge, and their experience with seasonal changes of the river when articulating their source of authority in the two activities. As the process moved into more technical activities, such as developing interview banks, practicing interview skills, and crafting interview protocols, their competence-based authority expanded to include many types of methodological concerns, including knowledge of interview skills, knowledge about expertise of interviewees, and interview procedure inclusivity. As youth practiced their formal positions at “interviewer” with researchers and their peers, their authority started to emerge as in virtue of office,  as codes emerged such as “politeness and professionalism during interviews”.

While a ctivities such as determining interview topics gave youth space to realize their authority aligned with set goals, there were also critical and interesting moments where youth negotiated authority in “unscripted” ways. These emergent and powerful forms of authority appeared especially when youth adapted the interview procedures based on their unique expertise and community-based awareness, and challenged the interview logistics schedules. Our analysis reveals that youth authority is not a static construct, but a dynamic, adaptive, and context-dependent practice. The findings extend previous literature that seeks to understand “power and learning as co-constituted and co-constructed through interactional, microgenetic, and structural dynamics” (Philip & Gupta, 2020, p. 211). Building upon and moving beyond a macro-level of power analysis or general empowerment, our study 460 demonstrates the sources, realizations, and negotiation of youth authority in various activities at a micro-level, and provides practical implications for future co-design projects (Ekström & Stevanovic, 2023). The adaptive nature of youth authority Youth authority is highly adaptive in its sources when high school students respond to various activities with different purposes. This aligns with classic sociological distinctions between different forms of authority. We observed the shift of authority by virtue of competence to authority by virtue of office (Parson, 1939). At the initial and generative phase of CMI, youth authority was drawn exclusively from virtue of competence. This aligns with the studies of cultivating students’ critical science agency in co-design, which emphasize recognizing the scientific knowledge that youth contribute to the collaborative effort (Vossoughi & Shea, 2019; Richards-Schuster, 2010). When activities transitioned into a more technical and structured manner, however, such as conducting the interviews, authority in virtue  of office  emerged as an important complementary source. The formal role of “interviewer” legitimized youth’s status in these activities  through forms of authority such as  managing the conversations and re-directing talks with adult experts, who are traditionally positioned as more powerful and authoritative (Jadallah et al., 2024). This dynamic shift demonstrates that youth are not limited to a single form of authority, but can leverage different sources depending on the activities’ requirements. Balancing scaffolding and autonomy to foster youth authority Our findings also reveal that co-design can foster youth authority through both scaffolding that helps them realize and negotiate their authority, and autonomously, in unstructured settings. In our study, facilitated structures legitimated youth participation to  exercise epistemic and deontic authority within activity goals (Bowler et al., 2021). These activities can be seen as a practice consistent with the principles put forward by Engle and Conant (2002), who contended that granting students authority is critical to fostering productive disciplinary engagement. Also, facilitated activities provided a “safe space” for youth to realize and negotiate basic forms of their authority (Prapaveissis et al., 2022). In our study, for example, our youth participants criti cized the adult university researchers’ behaviors during a mock interview, which developed and improved their interview skills. Without such a “safe place”, it could be  difficult to invite youth to take risks to exercise authority in this constructive approach.

The ultimate goal of scaffolding, however, is not to create compliance, but to build a foundation of trust and skill from which youth feel empowered enough to meaningfully exercise their authority (Kirshner, 2008). It is worthwhile to note here that the most authentic and powerful forms of youth authority emerged when they felt empowered enough to move beyond this scaffolding and go “off-script” through turn-by-turn interactions. These moments, where youth challenged participation rules or adapted interview procedures, provide further examples for Jadallah et al. ’s (2024) observation of youth exercising power by disrupting hierarchies to expand learning opportunities. In these instances, youth were no longer passive participants following instructions from facilitators. Instead, they began to take ownership  and hold the CMI accountable to their own norms of fairness and sense of cultural belonging (Balazs & Morello-Frosch, 2013; Engle & Conant, 2002).

## Practical implications

Based on our findings, we offer several practical implications for future co-design projects involving youth. First, we recommend researchers design activities deliberately for authority to create explicit opportunities for youth to draw upon both competency-based and office-based authority. Furthermore, designers should consider how each activity can provide a safe space for youth to realize and negotiate their authority. Second, designing for authority also requires a balance between scaffolding and autonomy. Designers must not only set up structured roles but also leave intentional room for youth to challenge, adapt, and take ownership of the  process. This balance is crucial to moving youth from passive participants to active contributors. Third, we suggest that designers always reflect on emergent authority during the co-design process and consider how the design could improve, either through a scaffolded or an autonomous approach, to  resituate power dynamics and authority. For instance, our youth participants challenged a participation rule, which led us to reflect on whether giving students control over some logistics could be beneficial for the entire CMI process. Collectively, this study advances analysis of power dynamics in participatory design activities, specifically youth authority, and informs the facilitation of future codesign projects that position youth as central design partners.
