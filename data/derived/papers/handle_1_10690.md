# Cartographic Syntonicity as a Tool for Equitable Computer Science Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We discuss the notion of cartographic syntonicity, which we define as compatibility of computer science (and specifically computational thinking and computational practices) with learner’s knowledge of place and space. We first overview Papert’s notions of syntonicity and how they relate to computing education. Then, we explore the role of place in learning. We link these together alongside theories of equitable computer science education to justify why cartographic syntonicity might be uniquely effective in supporting equitable computer science learning. We close with an outline of future research.

## Introduction

Like many learning contexts, computer science remai ns one in which women and people of color —specifically Black, Latinx, and Indigenous people —are underrepresented in the United States (NSF, 202 1). In the learning sciences, a variety of theoretical and des ign advances have sought to ameliorate this inequal ity. In designing learning, some have sought to expand and complicate Papert’s constructionism, including through the integration of new material feminisms (Keune, 2022) and Black Womanist perspectives (Shaw et al., 2023). Another branch of scholarship has focused on developing a critical  perspective on computer science, situating the dis cipline in crucial questions about what computer science is fo r, through perspectives such as ethics (Vakil, 2018), contrapuntal analysis (Philip & Sengupta, 2021), and res isting militarism (Vossoughi & Vakil, 2018). In thi s conceptual short paper, we seek to similarly build on Papert’s constructionism using critical perspect ives and perspectives of place by developing the notion of cartographic syntonicity to explore the way public map-making can shape the learning of computational thinking skills in equitable ways. Theoretical foundations of syntonicity and place Papert (1980) advocated for syntonic learning, whic h he described as learning wherein the learning act ivity is compatible with the learner in some crucial way. In Mindstorms, Papert discusses three types of syntonicity: body syntonicity (compatibility with knowledge of one’s own body), ego syntonicity (compatibility with peop le’s “sense of themselves as people with goals, desires,  likes, and dislikes,” p. 63), and cultural syntonicity (where Papert used culture to essentially mean extracurric ular). Papert introduced educational syntonicity in the context of learning computer science with the Logo programm ing language and turtle graphics. He described how the familiar procedure of walking in a circle forms a spatial bridge for novices to understand how to program the turtle to draw a circle. Keune (2021) extended the notion of syntonicity usi ng new materialist theory to develop the notion of material syntonicity, which “theorizes how the materials made themselves feelable and how the students sensed the materials’ ways to produce a material-specific character of computation together as a par t of domain learning” (p. 25). Further CS education research builds on Papert’s wo rk, including bodily movement as the syntonic context for more abstract ideas, such as p lace value (Benton et al., 2018). Syntonicity is a useful frame for computer science education in so far as students learn best when material is made concrete, and syntonicity is an operationalization of what it would mean for someth ing to become concrete.  An underlying assumption of syntonicity is that when the learning of computing concepts is compatible with the learner’s foundatio nal experiences in some way, students can more readily think and do like mathematicians and computer scientists. Constructionism’s central thesis is that people lea rn best when their learning is made concrete, usual ly through the creation and sharing of objects. However, Wilensky (1991) argued that “concreteness is not a property of an object but rather a property of  a person's relationship to an object” and so “conce pts that were hopelessly abstract at one time can become concrete for us if we get into the 'right relationship' with them” (Wilensky, 1991, as referenced in Bers, 2008). Such a perspective re sonates with research on culturally-responsive and culturallysustaining education (Madkins et al., 2020; Paris, 2012). A critique of Papert’s notion of cultural syntonicity was its relative vagueness on what culture is; while culture is certainly not the same as race o r ethnicity (Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003), a more robust perspective on culture is needed to understand why some anchoring concepts are meaningful to some students and not others. Place is a promising anchoring concept because it offers opportunities for exploring cultural and political issues but also a co ncreteness in the form of walking, exploring, knowi ng, or simply being in a place.

Using Dourish’s (2006) conceptualization,  space refers to material aspects of settings, whereas place emerges from persistent social construction and agr eement around space. Place as a conceptual mediator and a learning goal has been well-examined in the learning sciences and education res earch broadly. As Taylor and Phillips (2017) argue, there are three major thread s in research on place-conscious pedagogies: (a) how situatedness in places shapes learning, (b) how people lea rn about places, and (c) how people make and re-make places. This research dovetails with research on em bodiment and “learning on the move” (Marin et al., 2020), because people’s orientations and movements through  places become resources that are leveraged to bett er understand particular concepts, the places, or the bodies. Across Taylor and colleagues’ work, they ha ve shown that fostering “place-remaking” by supporting youth in asking questions o f place and counter-mapping regions based on their own experience of place can result i n ri gorous learning about equity, geography, and civic engagement (Taylor, 2017; Taylor, 2020). Together, these epistemologies, which include both “mobile epistemology” and “grid epistemology” (Taylor, 2020), form the basis for cartographic syntonicity. Taylor (2017) frames these as “locative literacies,” socially-mediated ways of understanding and re-making place. Significant other learning sciences work has shown how visualizing data using digital a nd computing tools can result in meaningful learning about data,  inequality, and place. For example, Radinsky (2020) studied how students understand the cartographic situatedne ss of data. He studied how a  seventh-grade social studies classroom used census data visualization tools to examine the various ways people were able to narrate this data, including through “(1) telling a story about onesel f working with data; (2) animating a data representation; (3) incorporating data into extant narratives; and (4) narrating oneself into a data-represented world” (p. 379), all of which have a spatial dimension. Another example is the work of Hostetler et al. (2018), who used agent-based models as a technology mediator for how preservice teachers could engage with racism and segregation a s complex systems in ways that promoted critical discourse. They argued that the use of agent-based models allowed for both personal engagement but  maintained enough distance from the subject of “con troversial” issues (i.e., racial red-lining) to support deeper discourse.

Thinking cartographically with an equity lens, it is crucial to recall how places become sites of criticality. Gruenewald (2003) offered the notion of critical pedagogy of place, integrating the tradition of critical pedagogy with traditions of place-based and outdoor learning. In the learning science s, thinking with the experiences of migrant youth, Gutiérrez (2014) offered the notion of learning as movement, as a metaphor useful to understand what and how learning moves across changing places,  spaces, and contexts (see also Pinkard, 2019, on freedom of movement as essential to a healthy learning ecos ystem). Learning as movement suggests a path forwar d for engaging with the cultural in computer science lear ning, through supporting sociocritical literacy. In the process of a social design experiment (Guti érrez & Jurow, 2016), these syncretic design approac hes can bring forward cultural practices that are not dominant in classro om settings. Doing so can advance a kind of a third  space in which dominant and resistant discourses can meet to generate powerful learning and sociocritical engagement. As Germinaro (2022) argued, engagement with geographic  analysis of this sort can be a healing process for communities harmed by spatial and racial injustice. This resonates with the concept of spatial justice (Soja, 2013), a framework for analyzing how space, place, and relevant social relations therein mediate inequality and injustice. Knowledge of place and space are therefore always political, and investigating data about place and space can be a way to both become aware of inequality or to counter-map it. We define cartographic syntonicity as the compatibi lity of computer science (and specifically computational thinking and computational practices)  with learner’s knowledge of place and space. Following Madkins et al’s (2020) call for more design-based research on equitable computer science education, we begin a program of design-based research to test if cartographic syntonicity can increase the success of students of color in learning computing concepts, where computing con cepts include disciplinary learning goals like codi ng alongside sociopolitical consciousness. We conjectu re that designing for cartographic syntonicity will  reliably support learners' increased understanding of computer science and, in our project, social and spatial justice. Cartographic syntonicity as an intervention into computer science education In CS education, there has been ample work on broad ening participation, especially with minoritized students, through engagement with story and narrative (Pinkar d et al, 2017), technology cultures (Vakil, 2020), and culturally-relevant and equity pedagogies (Madkins et al., 2020). Building on the way syntonicity can also support epistemological pluralism (Turkle & Papert, 1992; s ee also Rosebery et al., 2010), cartography offers an opportunity to concretize key sociocritical concepts, such as standpoint, inequality, critical theories, and history. There is significant interest in the learning sciences in how students pick up knowledge about computing and technology. However, it is also crucial for suc h learning to engage deeply with ethics (Vakil, 201 8), which cannot be treated as separate from coding. To do so, computational approaches are seen in combination with other modes of inquiry, such as wrestling with anti-Blackness in computing (Jones & Melo, 2021), algorithmic bias (Noble, 2018), and the role of technologies in maintaining oppression (Benjamin, 2019). We argue that cartographic syntonicity builds on the established approach of bodily syntonicity in CS education whil e opening opportunities for a pedagogy that attends to issues of equity and justice. Cartographic syntonicity takes many forms, from students analyzing existing digital maps, to tracking their own “daily round,” to spatial data visualizations. Such map-working offers diverse epistemological entry, as students can position their personal experiences, a round which they are an expert, of place relative t o the map (Taylor, 2020). It offers sociocritical literacy as  students historicize and ask questions of the map and data. It offers STEAM pedagogical angles (Mejias et al., 2021) in which aesthetic components become part of the map’s value. As the map’s narratives are explored further, those aesthetic components evolve into axiological ones (Bang et al., 2016), where the values represented in the map, whose stories it  tells and forecloses, and whose way of understanding it represents, become sites of interrogation and re-mediation. In our future work, we intend to engage in design-based research to understand if cartographic syntonicity is a useful design tool for equitable computer scie nce education, which to us involves engaging a dive rse study body in both the learning of computational skills as well as growing their sociopolitical consciousness, and in fact framing these as co-occurrence processes (Madkins et al., 2020). Our st udy will take place in undergraduate classrooms on Long Island. Long Island is a ~120-mile strip of land east of Manhattan, in New York in the United States. Geographically, it includes two boroughs of  New York City (Queens and Brooklyn), but we often use “Long Island” to refer culturally to the two counties on the island that are not part of New York City. Long Island has historically been, and still is, home to some o f the highest-performing public schools in the nation by conventional metrics (Brodsky, 2023). At the same time, it is still a site of vast school segregation (Meraji, 2019) and de factor housing segregation (Choi et al., 2019). These conditions make it clear that the suburbs are a crucial place for educational research that targets racism and inequality (Diamond et al., 2021). Currently, we are crafting undergraduate courses for university students on Long Island in areas outside of computer science, specifically a user experience course in Communications and a first-year experience course (required for all freshman and designed with instru ctor-chosen topics to introduce students to college-level reading, writing, and discussion). In these courses, students will examine and ultimately create their own digital maps of Long Island or surrounding areas. In design ing these courses, we will use  the principle of cartographic syntonicity to research if and how mapping can be a  tool to support equitable computer science educati on by building resonances between learners’ existing unde rstanding of place and their growing understandings  of computational concepts and social justice literacies.
