# Redesigning a Graduate Program from Instructional Design to Learning Design and Learning Sciences

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This design contribution describes the redesign of a graduate program from instructional design to learning design. The objective was to align program structures with the learning sciences while responding to student experiences. Using l earning experience network analysis, I analyzed 118 student reflections across three semesters to identify potential design changes. The redesigned program implements empirically-informed and theory-grounded changes including portfolio-based assessment replacing comprehensive exams, design thinking coursework providing collaboration scaffolding, expanded research practicum for authentic professional work, and flexible specializations. This transformation demonstrates how design-based research methods can inform curricular reform that honors both empirical evidence and theoretical coherence. The process offers a model for graduate programs navigating paradigmatic shifts from knowledge transmission to learning as becoming.

The fields of instructional design and the learning sciences represent distinct approaches to understanding and supporting learning (Hoadley  & Van Haneghan, 2011). While instructional design as a practice encompasses approaches ranging from transmission-oriented models to constructivist perspectives, the specific program under study emphasized systematic processes grounded in information transmission models (Donaldson & Allen-Handy, 2023), and the learning sciences as a field draw on contemporary theories emphasizing learning as becoming, participation in communities of practice, and the co-construction of knowledge through collaborative activity (Greeno & Nokes-Malach, 2016). This distinction matters because it shapes not only what we teach but how we conceptualize the very nature of learning and the professional identities we cultivate in graduate students. When I assumed leadership of a Master of Science program, it operated under the title Instructional Design & Learning Design (IDD), reflecting its roots in systematic instructional design models. While the program served students well in many respects, analysis of student experiences revealed opportunities to ground the program more fully in learning theories and to shift from a paradigm centered on knowledge transmission/acquisition to framing learning as becoming (Donaldson & Allen-Handy, 2023; Lave & Wenger, 1991). This reorientation represented a fundamental epistemological and ontological shift in how we prepare graduates to design powerful learning experiences. This design contribution paper (supported with empirical contributions) documents aspects of the systematic program revision process, examining both how students experienced the former IDD program and how those experiences informed design changes for the new Learning Design & Learning Sciences (LDLS) program. Revisions were based both on learner experiences and grounded in theoretical frameworks that will be described in greater in a later section.

This is the beginning of a design-based research project (Barab & Squire, 2004), and the current stage involved analyzing student experiences in the final three semesters of the IDD  program before its transformation into the LDLS program, although some revisions were already starting to be implemented. Data were collected from students enrolled in core courses during Summer 2024, Spring 2025, and Summer 2025, representing the period right before the program underwent systematic revision.  In 118 student reflection papers submitted throughout these three semesters, students were prompted to reflect on what aspects worked well for their learning, challenges they encountered, and what the experiences meant to them professionally and personally.  I employed learning experience network analysis (LENA; Donaldson et al., 2024) as the analytical methodology. The reflection papers were coded by a team of 6 student research assistants, resulting in  113 distinct codes organized into three categories: aspects that worked well, struggles, and alignment with principles from learning theories. These codes were applied to 1,481 segments of text. I then created a network map based on correlations at p <.001 describing how likely it was for any possible combination of codes to both appear within the same reflection paper. Cluster identified cluster, I analyzed the relationships among struggles, successful aspects, and theoretical principles to develop theory-grounded design moves  using the logic of leveraging aspects that worked well and experiences aligned with specific principles from theory to address struggles within each cluster. While LENA provided 1168 valuable insights into student experiences and enabled development of data-driven design moves, student reflection data alone could not  fully capture all dimensions necessary for comprehensive program design. Therefore, I developed additional theory-driven revisions grounded in learning sciences frameworks.  As the program director, I led the pro gram revision in collaboration with program students, undergraduate student researchers, and peers in other programs. At the time of this writing, the implementation of the redesigned program is only a few weeks into the first semester after launching. Empirically derived design moves LENA analysis resulted in a network map of correlations at p <.001 with five clusters at a modularity of Q =.402 (see Figure 1). The student experiences in the green up-triangles cluster (41 nodes) represented a socially-driven learning process, centered on collaboration and shared knowledge construction. Their positive experiences stemmed from active teamwork, peer discussions, open communication, and the fair distribution of work, all of which were enhanced b y scaffolding from both the instructor and their peers. There were struggles with anxiety and the vulnerability of sharing ideas, and some students  grappled with uncertainty, a preference for individual work, or the frustration of other team members being passive. These experiences reveal ed that while social constructivist learning can be powerful for developing new practices and exploring complex perspectives, its success is deeply intertwined with navigating interpersonal dynamics and individual comfort levels. Figure 1 LENA Map with 5 Clusters – Node Shapes/Colors Indicate Cluster Membership The experiences of students in this green up-triangles cluster were defined by a paradox. T hey valued collaborative knowledge construction, teacher guidance, and engaging with diverse perspectives, yet simultaneously struggled with the interpersonal friction of teamwork, such as anxiety, passiveness, and uncertainty. These strengths and struggles are deeply rooted in social constructivist principles. The LENA analysis revealed that while students experienced the benefits of collaborative learning, the old program lacked structured support for the interpersonal dimensions of that collaboration. The network showed strong correlations between collaborative struggles (anxiety, passivity, uncertainty) and the absence of explicit scaffolding for group processes. Because co-construction and co-design place the collaborative act of building shared understanding at the center of learning, grounding the program in these frameworks directly targets the gap the data revealed. The systematic instructional design course was therefore replaced with a design thinking course, which provides structured methods for human-centered collaborative problem-solving. Engaging students with cultural-historical activity theory (CHAT; Engeström, 2022) gives them an analytical framework to examine and improve their own team dynamics, directly responding to the data showing that interpersonal friction was the primary barrie r to 1169 otherwise valued collaboration. The pedagogical shift from consuming information to making things in authentic contexts leverages students' desire for meaningful, guided engagement by creating environments where robust collaboration is not just expected but is the central mechanism of learning and becoming. The large salmon circles cluster’s (52 nodes) learning experience was characterized by individual growth, reflection, and the application of knowledge in meaningful contexts. Reflections indicated development in critical areas like self-awareness, confidence, and critical thinking skills as they worked toward an authentic purpose or for a real-world audience. A key theme was the process of understanding by connecting new concepts to prior knowledge, building mental models, and seeing the “big picture.” Value was placed on learning that was hands-on, empowering, and directly relevant to their career goals or personal identity. This cluster also highlights the development of empathy, as students worked to understand different perspectives and apply that understanding to their work. There were struggles  with difficult concepts, a lack of foundational knowledge, personal time management, technical issues with tools or resources, and the difficult task of framing complex problems. This large salmon circles cluster represented a deep, transformative learning journey where students gained confidence, self-awareness, and critical thinking skills by connecting their learning to real-world applications and engaging in hands-on work. This aligned with principles from constructionist and transformative learning theories. The primary struggles, however, were barriers to this process. A lack of background knowledge, technical issues, and difficulty framing complex problems  were exacerbated by traditional assessment methods focused on knowledge acquisition. Leveraging their  success with building and applying, the most significant program revision was the complete elimination of the traditional comprehensive exam in favor of a portfoliobased system. This move directly addresses the struggle of abstract knowledge “ retention” by allowing students to demonstrate their competence through the tangible, meaningful artifacts they excelled at creating. The curriculum's new theoretical foundations in the constructionist principles of learning by making (Papert & Harel,

1991) and the dynamic systems model of role identity (DSMRI; Kaplan & Garner, 2017) explicitly seek to support

the identity development and self-awareness these students valued. New courses like game-based learning and design thinking were introduced to provide structured pathways for hands-on, reflective practice, ensuring their entire academic journey supports the kind of empowering, applied learning they found most impactful. The experiences of the smaller clusters, including the  neon green diamonds cluster (7 nodes), orange squares cluster (7 nodes), and blue down-triangles cluster’s (6 nodes) were analyzed in the same way, but will not be described in detail here. T he design moves for these small clusters were significant, but are aligned with the theory-driven design moves described in the next section so they also will not be described in detail here. Theory-driven design moves In addition to the design moves based on LENA analysis, other design moves grounded in theory were required. A principal revision involved a fundamental paradigmatic shift, reconceptualizing the program's core identity from an approach grounded in systematic instructional design practices to learning design framed through the learning sciences as a field of study. This transformation was not merely nominal but represented a deep epistemological change, moving from a philosophy centered on the effective acquisition of knowledge to one grounded in the idea that learning is an incredibly complex and situated process of becoming (Fischer et al., 2018; Sawyer, 2022). This is most evident in the redesign of the introductory course, which now examines the origins and epistemologies of the learning sciences as a distinct design science (Hoadley, 2018), rather than surveying instructional design models  as was done in the previous course. More importantly, this and all other required courses engage students in designing and testing out authentic learning experiences in their own contexts, whether it be in a corporate context, a local museum, a community organization, or even a religious space such as a church. This philosophical reorientation prepares graduates to design for holistic humandevelopment  that embraces the complexity of learning (Jacobson, & Kapur, 2012), a goal that transcends the prior focus on efficient information delivery.

To ensure sustained relevance and provide pathways for advanced study, the program underwent a significant structural revision with the introduction of a flexible specialization system. This architectural change allows the curriculum to remain agile and re sponsive to the rapidly evolving technological and theoretical landscape of the field (Schank, 2016). Rather than maintaining a static, one-size-fits-all curriculum, the program now offers specialized courses in emergent, high-impact areas that are largely absent from traditional instructional design programs. These specializations include topics such as  designing with augmented intelligence, which frames AI within 4E cognition (Embodied, Embedded, Enacted, Extended; Newen et al., 2018); the principles of game-based learning for identity exploration (Gee, 2018); and understanding learning through the lens of complex systems (Donaldson & Allen-Handy, 2023; Jacobson, & Kapur, 2012). This structure not only allows students to tailor their graduate education to specific career trajectories  but also establishes a mechanism for the program to continually integrate new frontiers of research and practice. 1170 A core component of the program's evolution was the deliberate and substantial expansion of its theoretical foundations beyond the frameworks that dominated this particular program's instructional design courses, such as behaviorism (Skinner, 1965), cognitive load theory (Sweller, 2017), and information processing theories (Mayer, 2012). The revised curriculum now integrates a diverse suite of contemporary theories to provide students with more powerful and holistic frameworks for analysis and design. Students now engage deeply with 4E cognition theory (Newen et al., 2018). They apply cultural-historical activity theory (CHAT; Engeström, 2022) to analyze learning as a complex system of human activity mediated by tools and culture. The inclusion of complex systems theory (Jacobson, & Kapur, 2012)  and the d ynamic systems model of role identity (DSMRI; Kaplan & Garner, 2017) further equips students to understand learning as a non-linear, emergent, and deeply personal process. This theoretical broadening is operationalized in courses like the advanced learning design course which has shifted from surveying various instructional design process models to the direct application of principles derived from theories, preparing students to design for complex socio-cultural and cognitive transformation.

The program revision institutionalized an explicit commitment to social justice by integrating the principles of critical pedagogy (Freire, 1970; Kincheloe, 2008; Giroux, 2013) as a core value. This ethical reorientation moves beyond designing for effectiveness and efficiency to consider the political and moral dimensions of learning design. The curriculum now engages students in examining power dynamics within learning contexts, questioning  underlying assumptions about knowledge and authority, and actively working toward disrupting epistemic injustices (Fricker, 2007). This commitment is embedded in the program’s pedagogy, which emphasizes the importance of looking to the margins of a learning community and intentionally designing for the inclusion of voices that are often overlooked. By framing design as an act with s ocial and ethical consequences, the program aims to cultivate a generation of learning scientists and designers who are not only skilled practitioners but also reflective, responsible agents of positive social change. Another major structural change was implemented to deepen students’ engagement in authentic professional contexts (Lave & Wenger, 1991) by doubling the required research practicum from three to six credit hours. This expansion transforms the practicum from a short-term preparatory exercise into a sustained, multi-semester engagement that mirrors real-world professional and research timelines. The extended duration allows for the completion of more ambitious projects, enabling students to produce tangible, high-impact outcomes such as conference submissions, journal manuscripts, or significant deployed learning designs. This revision fundamentally alters the nature of the student experience, shifting it from one of hypothetical preparation for future application to one of immediate and consequential professional practice. By leaving the program with significant real-world work already completed, graduates possess a more robust professional portfolio and are better prepared for high-level careers or advanced doctoral studies. Although I had a great deal of help from program students, undergraduate researchers, and peers in other departments, one of the greatest challenges was being the sole faculty member, teaching out the old program while designing the new one without collaborative partners working toward the same goals within the program.  This program redesign demonstrated how analysis of learner experiences can inform theory-grounded curricular reform in conjunction with theory-driven design moves. Eliminating comprehensive examinations removes barriers to constructionist learning while the portfolio defense system honors student agency and identity development. Introducing design thinking provides structured scaffolding for collaboration that students needed. Expanding the practicum creates space for consequential professional work rather than hypothetical preparation. The flexible specialization system ensures the program can evolve alongside rapidly changing technological and theoretical landscapes. What enabled this paradigm shift in practice was the combination of empirical evidence about student experiences with explicit theoretical commitments, so that each design move could be traced to both a documented struggle and a principled response. This transformation has implications beyond a single program.  Graduate education in learning design must grapple with whether programs prepare students for efficiency in knowledge transmission or for expertise and participation in communities of practice. The shift from instructional design to learning design through the perspective of the learning sciences represents not merely updated content but a fundamentally different professional identity. Programs grounded in learning sciences principles recognize learning as complex, social, and situated rather than reducible to systematic procedures. This paper explored how the LDLS program was transformed and seeks to contribute to ongoing conversations about graduate education. By attending to both learner experiences and theoretical coherence, the revision process models the iterative, evidence-based approach that learning scientists advocate. Programs that embrace this dual commitment to lived experience and theoretical rigor may better prepare graduates to design for human flourishing in increasingly complex learning contexts.
