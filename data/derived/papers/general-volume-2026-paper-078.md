Reimagining Mathematics through Children’s Play with Video Data Jamie Vescio, Vanderbilt University, jamie.l.vescio@vanderbilt.edu Abstract: This two-cycle participatory design study (PDR) develops a novel methodology for including young children in design processes for their own mathematical learning: “playing” with classroom video data. I partner with twelve K-2 students from one school, integrating student-teacher-researcher video analysis and co-design as a mechanism for reimagining participation possibilities in mathematics classrooms. I examine implications of play-based methodological spaces for children’s recognition—contributing new understandings of how young children, particularly with multiply marginalized identities, can inform theories of learning, and transform power-laden discourses of mathematical competence in schools. Power-laden discourses on what counts as mathematical participation remain deeply entrenched within K-12 mathematics, including accountability logics aimed at defining “failing” schools and students and replacing young children’s curiosities with short-term skills to optimize performance gains (Miller & Almon, 2009). Moreover, such ideologies intersect with systems of oppression to reify who is recognized as mathematically competent by stratifying racially minoritized and economically disadvantaged children into low ability groups, thereby limiting their access to opportunities to learn, including advanced mathematics coursework in future grades (Cobb & Russell, 2015). In contrast, humanizing mathematics advocates for a joint pedagogy with students—produced for the purpose of “naming” and “transforming” extant systems (Freire, 1970). Efforts to promote sociopolitical consciousness are well documented in secondary settings. Yet, methods for advancing K-2 students’ liberatory action remain undertheorized, as schools and researchers are forewarned that children’s “nonsensical,” countersocial thought patterns render them unreliable informants (Yoon & Templeton, 2019). Indeed, Wohlwend

(2018) describes children’s play as an “untidy, just-fine-as-is literacy that young children choose and use to make

sense of their worlds” (p. 301). However, such forms of sensemaking are rarely taken up as relevant to curricular priorities, and, as a result, children’s subversive provocations more often go unheard (Yoon & Templeton, 2019). The result is that children have been historically excluded from weighing in on decisions about their mathematical learning. To extend humanizing mathematics to K-2 students, the field needs methodological innovation to recognize children’s imaginative expertise and analytic modes. Objectives and research questions This PDR study develops one such novel methodology: “playing” with classroom video data alongside young children. I conjecture that by recognizing children’s playful video noticings, educators may come to recognize their students’ mathematical competencies in transformative ways. I examine the following research questions:

1. How do K-2 students and their teachers analyze video data of their mathematical learning?

2. How do K-2 students leverage video data to reimagine mathematical learning designs?

3. What are the implications of K-2 students’ reimagined learning environments for their participation

possibilities and recognition as mathematically competent in mathematics classrooms? Methodologies and data gathered Informed by critical childhood studies (Yoon & Templeton, 2019) and a situative theory of learning (Lave & Wenger, 1991), my PDR study partners with three teachers and four K-2 students from each of their classrooms (twelve students total), whom I selected based on their positioning as “less mathematically competent.” To establish a starting point for co-design, I filmed each classroom’s “business-as-usual” mathematics instruction, from which I selected “critical events” (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) of each focal child’s normative participation possibilities. Each child and teacher discussed the identified clips in multiple settings: child-researcher, teacherresearcher, child-teacher-researcher, and peer-teacher-researcher. Using their video noticings as a design “springboard,” students then engaged in peer-teacher-researcher co-design sessions to reimagine existing design features of their school’s mathematics curriculum, including tools and participation structures (Sandoval, 2014), which their teachers and I then formalized as lesson plans. Finally, each teacher enacted these lessons in their classrooms, which I again filmed. I collected additional data sources, including pre-and post-interviews. To answer Research Question 1, I will use emergent coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1990) to compare actors’ video noticings, and to consider what students and teachers can potentially learn from one another when attending t o different aspects of mathematical participation. To respond to Research Question 2, I will apply interaction and co-design sessions to trace the function of such methodological spaces for facilitating adult-child co-design of school mathematics. Lastly, to answer Research Question 3, I will apply pattern codes from RQ1 to videorecorded co-design sessions. I will identify relationships between children’s video noticings and design features (Sandoval, 2014). I will then identify salient classroom lesson episodes that illustrate these design features in context to examine the interactional conditions under which students are recognized as mathematically competent (or not) in classrooms. I will nuance findings with pre-and post-interviews for potential shifts in students’ and teachers’ conceptions of what it means to be mathematical.

## Expected findings and contributions

I used conjecture mapping (Sandoval, 2014) to inform learning designs and anticipated outcomes. At a high level, I conjecture that learning designs guided by children’s video noticings will expand what it means to be mathematical. Namely, my pilot study found that as children analyzed and shared their “favorite parts” of classroom video, they created playful mathematical plotlines (Vescio, 2025), which challenged teachers’ and researchers’ noticings of the same data (Vescio & Gresalfi, under review). The present study extends this work by amplifying new subject-subject relations in design, in turn opening new subject-object relations (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). By “seeing” mathematics with children, I posit that educators may also “see” their students and the discipline in new ways and, in so doing, redefine mathematical participation and competence. Ultimately, I expect that children’s reimagined designs will advance child-informed theories for supporting mathematical learning. Thus, this study attends to calls within the learning sciences for participatory research that directly engages with theories of learning. For instance, this dissertation could offer revised processes of conjecture mapping (Sandoval, 2014) for relating processes of partnering to designs and outcomes for learning. Relatedly, and perhaps most obviously, I aim to refine video viewing with children as a mechanism for wandering outside of the typical research gaze and for understanding how young children conceptualize their own mathematical learning (Yoon & Templeton, 2019). Therefore, this PDR study will also contribute empirical narratives of humanizing mathematics with K-2 students (Freire, 1970), while disrupting deficit framings of historically marginalized learners—instead capturing their curiosity, creativity, and playfulness.
