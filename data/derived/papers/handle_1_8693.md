# Training a Text Classification Algorithm to Predict the Presence of Dispositions and Learning Shifts in Students’ Self-Reflection

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The body of literature on how to best leverage learning analytics to measure students’ acquisition of 21st-century dispositional skills is in emergent stages. This project, based on over 800 student self-reflections, leverages natural language processing and qualitative data analysis to attempt to train a text classification algorithm to predict the presence of dispositions and learning shifts in students’ self-reflection.

Rationale For The Algorithm Society is facing an increasingly uncertain, turbul ent, and changing world. The needs of the 21st cent ury are different, and institutions, schools, and academics are building models of a 21st-century future that focuses on the soft skills and dispositions needed to thrive (Dede, 2010). This impacts educational researchers, who challenge us to rethink what we are teaching and what we are measuring so that we can support students as they confront the challenges and opportunities before them.

Stanford University’s Hasso Plattner Institute of Design (the d.school) focuses on a similar direction.  In 2016 the d.school conceived of eight design abilities  essential to enabling students to thrive, persever e, enact change and take risks in the uncertain and changing world (Carter, 2016). These eight design abilities are both a theoretical frame and central to the pedagogical practices enacted in d.school classes. Yet, measuring student learning of 21st-century skills and dispositional abilities can be very challenging, especially at scale (Dede, 2010), and metacognitive  practices such as self-reflection or thinking rout ines are a commonly cited methods (Richhart, Church, & Morriso n, 2011). Fortunately, emerging learning analytic techniques such as applied natural language processing and machine learning text classification can automate the analysis of a large corpus of student data in natural human language (Martin & Sherin, 2013) This poster describes a formative study of qualitatively coded student self-reflections from d.school classes between 2018 and 2021. These reflections were used to train an Auto ML Text Classification alg orithm to identify self-reflections that demonstrate the d.school design abilities and other shifts in learning. Creating The Algorithm In 2018 the Teaching and Learning team at the d.school instituted an effort to make their students' thinking visible. They leveraged a Making Thinking Visible thinking r outine from the Project Zero Lab at Harvard Graduat e School of Education (Richhart, Church, & Morrison 2 011). The thinking routine, I Used To Think… Now I think… is a routine for reflecting on how and why s tudents’ thinking has changed. Students are prompte d to reflect on their d.school class and write a response using each sentence prompts: “I used to think…” and “Now, I think…” Approximately 40% of the classes participated. Within those classes, compliance varied between 50-and 70%. Seven hundred fifty-two total statements were collected between 2018 and 2021.

The research team coded the statements using inductive and deductive approaches and used an inductive and grounded theory approach. First, each reflection was read to capture a sense of the response. Next, the data ICLS2022 Proceedings 2020 © ISLS was synthesized into initial categories: agency, cr eativity, purpose, failure, ambiguity, and collabor ation. The themes shifted as statements were added to the data set each year. Finally, five grounded theory-informed codes emerged: Changes in Mindsets and Worldview, Changes  in Knowledge and Tools, Changes in the World, Changes in Perspective, and Personal Change. Additi onally, 752 statements were coded using a deductive approach aligned to the eight d.school design abilities. The 752 statements were used to train a Google Cloud AutoML Custom Text Classification algorithm. Auto ML uses a machine-learning algorithm built via neural architecture search to analyze the meaning, structure, and sentiment of natural language data. It allowed for creating a supervised learning, multi-label text classification model. After importing the dataset, Google Cloud validated it and conducted a series of quality checks. The tool randomly withheld 20% of the data to utilize 10% fo r validation and 10% for testing. Once the dataset was validated and imported, the data was initiated to start training the model, and then its strength was evaluated, and it was manually trained to hone precision. An additional 82 student self-reflection statements were used to test the model against d.school educator predictions.

The AutoML Custom Text Classification algorithm was selected after experimenting with other Natural Language Processing tools. AutoML leverages Neural Architecture Search to build the custom model by automating the design of neural networks. This allo ws for greater precision than the research team's M L skills might otherwise have enabled.

Learning From The Algorithm Initially, the research goal was to understand two things: (1) can qualitative coding of student self-reflections be used to train supervised classification algorithms to predict the presence of the design abilities and learning shifts in students' self-reflections? and (2) does trainin g the classification algorithm result in alignment between what the supervised classification algorithms predict in student self-reflection and what educators predict? Preliminary results for this research indicate that the answer is “yes” to both of these research questions. Qualitative coding can be used to teach text classi fication algorithms to identify the eight design ab ilities and other learning shifts in student self-reflection. T he average precision of the model is 0.876,  althou gh there is variance in the precision for each of the individua l classifiers.  This high average precision (1 is the highest) suggests that the model largely predicts what d.school educators predicted in the training data. Additionally, the algorithm highlighted at a 95% confidence threshold that many of the 13 classifiers had very high prediction accuracy but also a reasonable rate of false negatives. Meaning that when the algorithm says a classifier is present, it likely is, but it may w ell be when it says it isn’t present. This finding suggests that our mental model of what a classifier indicates may be too varied and inconsistent. This is a work in progress and an ongoing body of w ork. Nevertheless, this small study indicates that text classification has a  role in research and sho ws promise in assessing 21st-century abilities and dispositions. The next steps in the study include (1) assessing the inter-rater reliability between the algorithm predictions and the parallel teacher evaluations by measuring Cohen ’s kappa. (2) determining if the algorithm can cont inue to reasonably predict the presence of classifiers in u nstructured student self-reflection, and (3) explor ing how researchers can refine their mental model of a qualitative code through analysis of algorithmic decisions.
