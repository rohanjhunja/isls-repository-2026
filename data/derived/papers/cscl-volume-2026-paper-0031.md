# Exploring Cognitive, Social, and Metacognitive Dimensions of Student -Student-LLM Collaboration in Problem Solving

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
If large language models (LLMs) are to be framed as collaborative tools, it is important to understand the nature of LLM collaboration and how it may differ from human collaboration. To this end, we studied problem solving episodes between undergraduate dyads and ChatGPT, observing the extent to which students’ interactions with ChatGPT mediated their cognition, social interactions, and/or metacognition. We found mixed results among dyads (including one that avoided ChatGPT altogether) and elaborate on four cases in-depth, highlighting interactions between students and juxtaposing them with ChatGPT collaboration. Dyads that collaborated with ChatGPT spent more talk turns monitoring its output whereas dyads who collaborated with each other developed more cognitive and metacognitive awareness of the task. Our findings raise implications for tradeoffs that educators should consider when deciding if and when to introduce LLMs into problem solving.

As artificial intelligence (AI) systems evolve, their growing ability to reason, hold social conversations, and offer feedback has sparked questions about whether AI can serve as a true collaborator (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2020). Large language models (LL Ms) in particular demonstrate interactive capabilities that allow them to engage in forms of reasoning and discourse once thought to be uniquely human. These advances invite us to reconsider what collaboration means when one partner in a group configuration is a LLM: what kinds of reasoning or social exchanges can such collaboration enable, and where might its limits lie? Effective collaboration requires participants to establish joint attention, monitor reasoning, and build on shared epistemic knowledge (B arron, 2003). Dialogue and explanation play central roles in supporting both cognitive and metacognitive engagement, allowing partners to co-construct knowledge rather than simply divide and conquer. We examine collaboration in this study through mathematical problem solving, a form of activity with a long and rich history of technology-mediated collaboration (Kaput, 1992). In mathematics, collaboration has long been viewed as a productive context for developing conceptual understanding and problem-solving competence.

When students explain their reasoning, question one another’s assumptions, or compare alternative strategies, they not only expand their mathematics schemas to include new ideas, but also revise and reconfigure existing ideas (Boaler, 2016; Goos et al., 2002; Slavin, 2014). These social exchanges support the kind of metacognitive monitoring that helps learners evaluate not just what they know, but how they know it. However, it is unclear the extent to which collaborating with an AI tool provides similar cognitive and metacognitive restructuring, as the social dimensions of collaborating with a human versus a pre-trained machine differ. Students must decide when and how to invite the LLM to contribute to their reasoning, interpret its contributions, and evaluate its suggestions against their own ideas. These interactions may also shift how students share cognitive responsibilities—whether they rely more on the AI’s responses, or on one another ’s situated interpretations and epistemic judgments of mathematics problems. We argue that understanding these potential modes of difference is especially relevant as students begin to take up LLMs in classrooms, and raise important questions for enacting instruction with AI. For instance, if multiple students and one LLM interact, to whom is authority conferred? How might differences in cognitive, social, and affective capacities between humans and machines shape group processes and outcomes? (Rizos et al., 2025; Bastani et al., 2025).

To address questions like this, we investigated how collaboration unfolds when undergraduate dyads solved a nonroutine function analysis task and ChatGPT was available. We examine the ways in which cognitive, social, and metacognitive processes are distrib uted across students and ChatGPT and how these processes influenced the degree to which students viewed ChatGPT as a collaborator. Through this analysis, we aim to contribute to ongoing discussions about how LLMs can be integrated into problem solving in w ays that enrich (not replace) social and cognitive dimensions of human interaction. We ask: How do students collaborate with

(a) each other and (b) LLMs during mathematical problem solving?

Collaborative problem solving Collaboration is generally defined as a social activity that involves individuals engaging in active discourse and contributing meaningfully within a group (Roschelle & Teasley, 1997; Ouyang et al., 2023). During collaboration, 204 team members share information, make decisions, and implement solutions they mutually agree upon (Sun et al., 2020). Collaborative problem solving (CPS) combines the complexities of collaboration and problem solving into a dynamic and mutually beneficial process (Swiecki et al., 2020). Because collaboration is as social as it is cognitive, successful collaboration depends on effective communication among partners and requires trust, respect, and openness to each member’s contributions and intentions. CPS allows students to bring together diverse perspectives, experiences, and areas of expertise to address a shared problem or goal. Several CPS frameworks suggest that collaboration involves a complex interplay of social, cognitive, and metacognitive skills (An et al., 2024; Andrews-Todd & Forsyth, 2020; Hesse et al., 2015). Social skills such as participation, maintaining shared understanding, perspective-taking, and attentive listening are critical for effective group coordination. Through these interactions, group members keep one another informed about progress, express ideas or concerns, and adjust their tasks and strategies accord ingly (An et al., 2024). Cognitive skills, including planning, ideating, and monitoring, enable students to reason through problems, integrate multiple perspectives, and construct shared solutions (OECD, 2017). Metacognitive skills play an equally important role, as they reflect students’ ability to monitor and regulate both their cognitive activities and social interactions during collaboration (Cini et al., 2023; Dindar et al., 2020). The interplay among these three dimensions not only influences individual performance but also shapes the overall quality and productivity of group outcomes (Goos et al., 2002). These interconnections are so critical that recent studies have shown how the diversity of individuals’ social and cognitive skills candetermine the success of collaboration. For example, Li et al. (2022) found that collaborative outcomes improved when students maintained active social interactions and made substantive cognitive contributions. Similarly, Andrews-Todd and Forsyth (2020) reported that i n triadic settings, the presence of at least one member with strong social and cognitive abilities improved CPS. As collaborative interactions become increasingly mediated by LLMs, it is likely that the cognitive, social, and metacognitive dynamics within teams will evolve in new ways. LLMs, trained on vast datasets, are likely to contribute more strongly to cognitiv e processes such as information retrieval, reasoning, and problem decomposition. In contrast, human collaborators bring contextual and situated awareness, lived experiences, and sophisticated social-emotional skills that support coordination and joint meaning-making (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2020). Understanding how these distinct capabilities interact is essential for supporting human –LLM collaboration, especially in multi-human settings where the balance of cognitive and social regulation may shift. Human-LLM collaboration Human–LLM collaborative problem solving refers to the joint effort between humans and AI systems to solve problems and produce creative outputs (Davis, 2013). As LLMs like ChatGPT gain popularity for their humanistic capabilities, human–LLM collaboration is becoming prevalent across domains, including education. Like other forms of collaboration, successful outcomes depend on good coordination between partners (An et al., 2024): sharing information, responding substantively to each other’s contributions, and jointly advancing the task (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2020). Rezwana and Ford (2025) characterize this purposeful and bidirectional exchange of information between humans and AI as AI communication, emphasizing that effective collaboration occurs when AI s ystems can interact and offer feedback in ways that resemble human teammates (Mamykina et al., 2002). In the context of LLMs, prompts serve as the primary means of communication and act as a window into individuals’ cognitive and metacognitive engagement with their AI partners (Subramonyam et al., 2024). Through prompting, humans articulate their goals, revise their input, and co-construct ideas with the AI system. The specificity of these prompts and revisions often determines the quality of the collaborat ion. Research on human– LLM collaboration in education is still emerging, and recent studies report mixed opportunities for novelty (Urmeneta & Romero, 2024), scaffold learners’ knowledge (Bastani et al., 2025), and enhance creativity in some instances (Huang et al., 2023). However, it remains unclear how best to structure these collaborations, particularly when students are still learning how to solve mathematics problems and are developing skill, confidence, and fluency in mathematics. For instance, Bastani et al. (2025) implemented a randomized control trial and found that students who used an AI system while solving problems achieved higher test scores during collaboration but performed worse once AI was no longer available, compared to peers who had never used AI. These findings suggest that while AI or LLM collaboration can improve problem solving accuracy, it may not necessarily strengthen humans’ abilities to develop independent skill in problem solving. In addition to communication, several factors, such as team members’ prior experiences, perceptions, and levels of trust in AI systems, can influence how they collaborate with AI and the overall quality of that collaboration. Research indicates that individuals’ beliefs about AI competence, reliability, and intent strongly shape their engagement behaviors and reliance patterns during collaborative tasks (Muhammad et al., 2024; Adisa & Adefisayo, 2025). These factors highlight the importance of studying not only what AI contributes to problem 205 solving but also how humans interpret, negotiate, and regulate their interactions with AI partners. Our study extends these conversations by examining how such collaborative dynamics unfold when students work with a LLM in dyads to solve open-ended mathematical problems. The purpose of our broader exploratory work was to investigate, in detail, how students think and reason through mathematics tasks when a LLM is made available to them. We conducted this work during spring-fall 2025. The overarching study included 10 individuals solving with only ChatGPT-4o, 10 individuals solving with ChatGPT and the internet, and 16 students who collaborated with each other in addition to having ChatGPT available. This paper reports on the third condition. We recruited students via physical campus advertisements. Students needed to have taken a mathematics course in the past year to reduce the likelihood that they would be unfamiliar with the tasks’ mathematics concepts, which we asked them about on a screening survey. We also asked students if they would be willing to bring a friend to the session, and eight participants agreed (16 collaborative participants total). ## Data analysis

Data analysis was iterative and partially informed by an adjacent analysis (Delaney et al., under review), where we examined collaboration patterns and AI trust among four dyads in this data corpus. However, that study did not account for pro-social collaboration moves that ChatGPT could have taken and thus, tended to frame AI as a tool rather than a humanistic collaborator. In this analysis, we do not make such a priori assumptions. We use an analytical framework from Goos et al. (2002), based on Vygotskia n sociocultural theory, that characterizes mathematics collaboration in three ways: self-disclosure (statements about one’s own understanding), othermonitoring (statements about one’s understanding of a groupmate’s understanding) or feedback request (a request for feedback on one’s own thinking). We hypothesized that the degree to which students viewed ChatGPT as their collaborator could be observed in Goos’ collaboration moves; for instance, pro-LLM collaboration could include students asking ChatGPT  for feedback on their ideas, critiquing its output against their own ideas, or using its output as the basis of new ideas or problem solving strategies. We also propose that these pro-social mathematical collaboration moves reflect broader notions of collabora tion, such as attentive listening, shared understanding, and perspective taking introduced earlier in our theoretical framework. Both authors individually reviewed the data corpus: eight autotranscribed then cleaned (by hand) transcripts of students’ collaboration on the roller coaster task, ChatGPT logs for each session, and video recordings during moments where students’ crosstalk, gesturing, and interactions were difficult to interpret from written transcripts alone. We met and discussed patterns, realizing that some students tended to treat ChatGPT more like a tool and others more like a collaborator. We then analyzed the data b y applying Goos et al.’s framework at the level of conversational turn-of-talk, attending carefully to relationships between students and ChatGPT (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). We applied one code per talk turn. If a talk turn appeared to contain multiple codes (e.g., one self-disclosure followed by a request for feedback), we split it into two talk turns and applied codes separately. Not all talk turns were coded (e.g., “Okay, let’s read the first task.”). After reaching an initial Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.86 on the first two transcripts, the first author coded the rest. The research team met two more times to discuss codes. As patterns emerged, we realized that students incorporated ChatGPT in three ways: as a collaborator, as a peripheral teacher or tutor, or as a tool. We wrote descriptive cases for each collaboration pattern (Yin, 2017). These cases form the basis of our findings. Of the eight dyads, three groups (Alex/Kam, Connor/Colin, and Itzy/Milan) treated ChatGPT like an informationseeking tool, three groups (Ken/Jazlyn, Rich/Junho, and Elsa/Gretchen) treated ChatGPT like a peripheral teacher or tutor, and two groups (Jaiden/ Natalia, Emma/Celia) treated ChatGPT like a collaborator. Groups who used ChatGPT like a tool used it only to extract information (e.g., “what is the equation of a parabola in vertex form?”). Groups who used ChatGPT like a peripheral teacher or tutor used it to extract information and for explanations about how to solve processes (e.g., “how do you find the domain and range of a function?”). Groups who used ChatGPT as a collaborator asked it questions beyond information and explanation, instead asking it for input in prosocial ways as outlined by Goos et al. (2002). We illustrate a case of each and one dyad who decided not to use ChatGPT in their problem solving process.

[selfdisclosure] Our findings reveal that students solved the task with ChatGPT in ways that differentiated their cognitive, social, and metacognitive work (An et al., 2024), and held implications for what they may have learned. Alex and Kam’s episode appeared to have simi larities to collaborative problem solving before ChatGPT, as they did not use its a solution. Jaiden and Natalia, on the other hand, spent considerable time making sense of and monitoring ChatGPT’s contributions, and transformed the goal of the task (Hughes, 2005) into investigating what types of visualizations ChatGPT could produce. Elsa and Gretchen fell somewhere in-between, drawing on ChatGPT as a more knowledgeable other during instances where they felt they could not proceed without more information. However, while they maintained social and metacognitive aspects of collaboration, they did not tend to build upon each other’s thinking in ways that suggested that they held a deep understanding of the task’s algebra concepts (Confrey, 1990). These patterns suggest that when students use LLMs during problem solving, including positioning LLMs in pro-social collaborative roles, they must negotiate whose responses will be taken up and the basis for which mathematical authority is conferred in group settings. ChatGPT also appeared to influence students’ trajectories in engaging in rich mathematical problem solving interactions. Alex and Kam, by monitoring each other’s strategies carefully, critiqued each other, jointly revised their ideas, and developed shared  meaning about what each question in the task was asking them to do. The other two focal groups tended to draw upon ChatGPT when they were stuck and took up its output before it was clear that they understood what was being asked of them. For instance, Els a and Gretchen accepted ChatGPT’s parabolic equation without verifying that it was correct (it was incorrect, as they had entered points A, B, and C incorrectly) and without confirming that they knew what it meant. This pattern aligns with concerns raised by Bastani et al. (2025), who found that students who collaborated with AI performed worse once it was removed, suggesting potential trade-offs between immediate problem-solving success and longer-term skill development. Our findings extend this concern to multi-human collaboration, where the presence of an LLM may inadvertently reduce the metacognitive monitoring that emerges through peer explanation and negotiation.

Our findings hold implications for how educators integrate LLMs into collaborative learning environments. If groups that treat ChatGPT as a collaborator spend more time monitoring its output than their own ideas, instructors must consider whether such shifts in students’ attention undermine the epistemic goals of learning how to think mathematically, collaboratively. Connor's observation that ChatGPT might constrain creative approaches to problem solving suggests that some students already recognize potent ial costs of collaborating with LLMs, even in low-stakes settings. This raises questions about when teachers should introduce (if at all) LLMs into problem solving when students are in the process of developing expertise, including learning how to build upon one another’s thinking. Our study suggests that when students do not have mathematical ideas of their own, or become stuck, they revert to LLMs’ cognitive authority and information efficiency rather than co-developing mathematical conjectures together t hrough social negotiation (Goos et al., 2002). As AI systems become integrated into education settings, teachers, designers and researchers must grapple with how to preserve cognitive, social, and metacognitive dimensions of human collaboration when possible, so that students candevelop their own epistemic agency independent of the capacities of AI systems. At the same time, as LLM-based technologies evolve, we anticipate that students’ and teachers’ notions of how to collaborate with them  will shift (e.g., Delaney et al., 2025). One unresolved question is the extent to which students in our study were ever taught differentiated ways to collaborate with LLM-based tools (Cukurova, 2025). We suspect they may not have been taught, since most used ChatGPT as an information-retrieval machine rather than a collaborator for co-constructing ideas.
