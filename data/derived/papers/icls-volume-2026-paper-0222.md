# When YouTube Meets Scientific Consensus: Laypeople’s Evaluation of Reliable and Alternative-Perspective Health Information

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigated how (1) emphasizing scientific consensus in reliable text-based information and (2) the modality of alternative-perspective health information influence laypeople’s information evaluation. A 2×2 factorial experiment was conducted with 178 undergraduates who read a reliable document about diabetes treatment with or without consensus messaging, and then either watched a video or read alternative-perspective information from YouTube. Results showed that when participants watched the YouTube video, those who read the document with the consensus messaging rated it higher for both epistemic and practical purposes and perceived it as more trustworthy. However, consensus messaging did not significantly affect their evaluation of evidence strength or possible treatments. These findings suggest that explicitly emphasizing scientific consensus enhances perceived reliability of expert information in multimodal contexts; however, it does not necessarily deepen understanding of why consensus reflects stronger evidence. Educational implications for fostering epistemic understanding of scientific consensus were discussed.

In today's digital world, people often encounter a vast array of unvetted information, which may not necessarily be misinformation or disinformation, but rather consists of scientifically potentially misleading content. The widespread presence of such info rmation in the digital environment has begun to erode public trust in accurate information and in legitimate knowledge providers (Lewandowsky et al., 2017; McIntyre, 2018). Thus, there are growing calls for education to equip individuals with the epistemic  competence required to discern accurate information and to engage critically with diverse sources (Barzilai & Chinn, 2018). This situation exists in the domain of health and medical information, which people rely on in their daily lives. Being able to navigate the vast amount of unverified health-related information in order to reason effectively for sound decision-making is now a central aim of contemporary digital and scientific literacy (Chinn et al., 2020), which aims to help laypeople make evidence-informed decisions in this area. Research in science education consistently emphasizes that individuals should base their reasoning on positions supported by broader bodies of evidence, particularly those grounding scientific consensus, rather than relying on isolated studies (Duncan et al., 2018; Osborne & Allchin, 2024). However, it is not surprising that laypeople tend to seek information disseminated through the web and social media, rather than reviews of scientific studies with systematically integrated evidence. Given this, our prior studies showed the need for experts themselves to communicate clearly that their claims are supported by extensive and convergent evidence (e.g., Chinn et al., 2024). Nonetheless, video-based social media platforms (e.g., YouTube) are popular information sources on which much of the science-related content is unvetted or misleading. Yet research on how laypeople interpret, evaluate, and respond to this information remains limited (Abed & Barzilai, 2022). Particularly, how laypeople evaluate and reason about information when exposed to both text-based reliable materials—such as expert guidelines grounded in broad evidence— and emotionally appealing content that provides alternative perspectives on video-based social media has not been sufficiently investigated. Laypeople may be prone to intuitive, affect-driven judgments when encountering such videos that convey claims that are not supported by evidence (Abed & Barzilai, 2022; Reynolds & McGrew, 2025), even if experts emphasize different information that is reliable and supported by scientific consensus. Therefore, this study addresses two research questions: (a) How do laypeople evaluate multiple pieces of information when they first read a text based on bodies of evidence and then encounter related information with alternative perspectives on YouTube? (b) To what extent does emphasizing that trustworthy information is supported by scientific consensus or extensive bodies of evidence influence the evaluation of both credible and alternative-perspective information? The study aims to provide empirical evidence for designing contemporary digital literacy education programs by clarifying how people integrate and evaluate evidence from multimodal, diverse online sources. 1592

## Method

The study took place during the spring semester of 2025 in an online introductory education course offered by a public university in Japan. Participants consisted of 178 undergraduate students who were proficient in reading Japanese, and all study materials explained below were presented in Japanese. We adopted a 2×2 factorial design to pursue the research questions. The first factor, called the Consensus Messaging factor, refers to a passage in a document (whose conclusion is in fact based on scientific consensus) which does or does not emphasize that it represents scientific consensus. The second factor, called Media Type factor, refers to additional material (not based on  scientific consensus but on popular health information) which was presented either via a YouTube video or via a written-text document. Therefore, we developed two materials that described treatments for diabetes. The pro-insulin material either did or did not indicate that the standard treatment (i.e., insulin therapy) represents scientific consensus, while the alternative dietary perspectives material differed depending on whether participants watched a YouTube video or read a summary of its content, saying that revising daily beverages was effective in managing blood sugar levels for diabetes. Table 1 shows the elements that each material (either text or video) includes. The pro-insulin material (a document of 255 or 292 words when translated into English, depending on the conditions) outlined effective diabetes treatment, emphasizing the role of insulin when lifestyle or oral medications are insufficient. Drawing on large-scale studies, the docu ment stressed that proper blood sugar management prevents complications and that insulin therapy, though often feared, is both safe and effective under medical supervision. The pro-insulin material varied depending on the conditions  and was distinguished by whether the document included consensus messaging (vander Linden et al., 2019) that indicated that insulin therapy is supported by nearly all studies as a scientific consensus and is currently defined as one of the standard treatments in the Japandiabetes Society’s guidelines. Participants in two of the four conditions read the document with the consensus  messaging, while participants in the other two conditions read the document without the consensus messaging.

The alternative dietary perspectives material (a video or a document of 261 words when translated into English) introduced five beverages (tomato juice, soy milk, coffee, tea, and lemon water), which are said to lower blood sugar levels, and highlighted tomato juice for its combined benefits on blood sugar, blood pressure, and cholesterol. The material cited a limited number of supporting studies and conveyed a popular-health tone, encouraging simple lifestyle changes to help control diabetes. The alternati ve dietary perspectives material  was originally an authentic YouTube video featuring a conversation between a male physician specializing in diabetes treatment and a female narrator; the video was used as it was for the conditions where participants watched the video for the alternative dietary perspectives material, while the other participants read the summary document without watching the video. The summary was generated by ChatGPT from the video transcript, and multiple researchers verified that it included the necessary elements explained in the video. We prepared a pre-survey assessing participants’ prior knowledge about diabetes treatments and insulin, based on Mukai et al. (2005), and their agreement with possible treatments that appeared in either of the materials. The present study examined how emphasizing scientific consensus in reliable text-based information (Consensus Messaging) and the Media Type of alternative-perspective information affect laypeople’s evaluation of information. The explicit consensus messaging in the pro-insulin material enhanced participants’ perceived goodness of the proinsulin material both for epistemic purposes and for practical purposes, particularly when they watched on YouTube for the alternative dietary perspectives material. Participants evaluated the alternative dietary perspectives material as less suitable for such practical use. Source trustworthiness for the pro-insulin material was significantly influenced by both Consensus Messaging and Media Type factors. These findings suggest that indicating scientific consensus clearly enhances laypeople’s evaluation of reliable information, particularly when alternative-perspective videobased content is present. This is consistent with prior studies of consensus messaging (e.g., vander Linden, 2019). However, it is notable that participants did not clearly recognize that the alternative dietary perspectives material was epistemically less reliable even if the other material included consensus messaging. In addition, the inclusion of consensus messaging did not significantly affect participants’ judgments of evidence strength or their evaluation of possible treatments to recommend; participants gave high ratings for the alternative perspectives such as “taking a glass of tomato juice” or “taking a cup of coffee” in all of the conditions. Taken together, these findings indicate that emphasizing scientific consensus in experts’ communications can enhance the perceived quality of information, particularly in multimodal contexts where video-based content with alternative perspectives competes for attention (cf. Chinn et al., 2024). However, emphasizing consensus alone did not influence participants’ reasoning based on the evidence strength. This implies that while consensus messaging improves perceived credibility, it does not automatically deepen laypeople’s understanding of why consensus reflects the convergence of broader bodies of evidence. Hence, educational interventions should not only stress that certain claims reflect scientific consensus, but also should explicitly teach what scientific consensus means, how it is achieved, and why it represents the integration of broader bodies of evidence (cf. Oura et al., 2024).
