# From Self-Reflection to Action: Supporting Teachers as Agentic Professionals to Improve Digital Competence Capacity

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In today's rapidly evolving digital landscape, teachers’ digital competence is a critical component of professional development. This study investigated how self-determination processes, such as self-reflection and self-efficacy, can empower teachers to enhance their digital skills. Self-determination theory suggests that individuals can achieve their personal goals if they have autonomy (feeling of control), competence (feeling capable) and are connected to others (feeling of community). When these psychological needs are fulfilled, individuals gain agency to take control of their own learning. Data were collected from three case studies with in-service teachers. Qualitative analyses showed that teachers required substantial scaffolding to progress from awareness to self-regulation and agency. Findings indicate the importance of fostering an environment where teachers can critically assess their digital competence and set meaningful goals for improvement. We conclude our discussion with recommendations for designing professional development programs that encourage self-regulated learning and reflective practices to promote digital competence.

## Introduction

Despite the importance of supporting teachers in de veloping their digital competence, t eacher professional development programs (TPDs) are argued to fall short in helping teachers adopt a continuous improvement mindset in respect to developing digital competence  (Garzón Artacho et al., 2020; Gudmundsdottir et al., 2018; Fernández-Batanero et al., 2020). Several issues on the matter are still debated, including the defi nition of the term digital competence, what emphasis the TPDs should have, and what content should be taught. Furthermore, researchers describe current efforts as incorrectly focusing on skill development rather than on the development of competence (Falloon, 2020; Janssen et al., 2013). Professional development experiences are often described as top-down directives about what should be learned and focus on a fragmented and technical understanding of the role of technology in learning and teaching, rather than on supporting teachers in developing a broader and holistic understanding of digital technologies  (Janssen et al., 2013; R økenes et al., 2016). At the same time,  teachers’ digital competence levels remain low across many countries worldwide (OECD, 2020) even though the need to be digitally agile professionals is continuously increasing. Due to the fast-evolving nature of the modern technological landscape, an agentic approach to fostering digital competence  is critical for fostering teachers’ capacity as self-directed, learning professionals who can address the challenges of modern-day teaching and learning, including rapid developments in artificial intelligence and generative AI. Such an approach to professional development can be seen as a drastic departure from traditional top-down approaches, placing the emphasis on teacher empowermen t and encouraging teachers to assume more responsibility for their learning. We argue that this ontological shift in how teacher professional development is conceptualized is essential to truly supporting teachers as lifelong learners a nd for adapting to current and future challenges of contemporary learning and teaching. In this paper, we report on a study examining the conditions that supported or inhibited in-service teachers to assume an agentic approach to developing their digital competence, by discussing three contrasting cases of professional development contexts.

## Theoretical framework and research questions

Digital competence is a crucial attribute of contemporary teachers; however, it remains elusive, due to significant variations in  its definition and operationalization within the literature (Janssen et al., 2013). We adopt the definition that digital competence refers to skills, knowledge, and attitudes of digital technologies in specific contexts. We draw on the DigCompEdu teacher digital competence framework (Redecker & Punie, 2017), which includes six main focal areas for acquiring skills, knowledge, and attitudes towards using digital technologies for: Professional Engagement  (e.g., using digital tools to facilitate  professional collaboration  and personal development); Digital Resources  (e.g., generating or  sharing digital content); Teaching and Learning  (e.g., integrating technology in learning and teaching); Assessment (e.g., using digital tools for assessment); Empowering Learners (e.g., fostering self-directed learning); and Facilitating Learners' Digital Competence (e.g., using digital tools for problem solving).  Recognizing the need to support educators in transl ating a competence framework into professional learning actions,  the European Commission recently launched the SELFIEforTEACHERS (SfT) self-reflection tool  (Economou, 2023), which allows teachers  to reflect on their digital competence and practices according to the s ix DigCompEdu areas. Teachers can use  the SfT tool individually, using a dedicated online platform. SfT includes 32 self-reflection items to which teachers are invited to reflect on and respond to. Each item describes the competence, and then presents te achers with six statements which can capture different proficiency levels (Economou, 2023). Based on their response, teachers are provided with targeted feedback to help them progress to the next competence level. According to Economou, SfT aims to engage teachers in reflecting on their digital competence in relation to the ir SfT responses and encourages them to act as agentic professionals who candesign and implement professional learning plans. Agency is the motivation and ability to control one ’s actions to produce desired change (Bandura, 2006). Reports on teachers’ agency to improve their digital competence indicate that there is still substantial need for additional research in understanding how to support teachers in these efforts (Brevik et al., 2019; Nagel et al., 2023). According to Bandura (1989), agency is emergent, interactive, and situational. We employ a socio-cultural lens (Engeström, 2014; Wenger, 1998), with the understanding that teache r learning is highly situated and develops in practice (Kelly, 2006). In this context, tools such as the SfT are hypothesized to have the potential to trigger and support self-reflection. But under which conditions can such tools also mean ingfully support agency to self-improve one’s digital competence?

In this work, we explore how tools like the SfT can support teachers be self-directed in developing their digital competence, in the context of teachers as learning profession als and lifelong learner s. Self-directed and agentic learning does not assume that teachers are alone in the proc ess of bringing about change, but rather that they assume  greater responsibility and share control of the learning si tuation in collaboration with others, supported by self-efficacy beliefs, goal setting behaviors, and through future-looking behaviors (Bandura, 1989). This context can lead to self-determination; according to the s elf-determination theory  (Ryan & Deci, 2020) individuals can achieve their personal goals if the y have autonomy (feeling of control), competence (feeling capable) and relatedness (feeling of community). When these three psychological needs are fulfilled, individuals can gain more  agency to take control of their own learning. We, therefore, ask the following main research questions: What conditions can foster teachers’ agentic development of digital competence? Conversely, what obstacles might prevent them from taking an active role in this process?

## Research context and participants

Using a comparative qualitative analysis methodolog y (Glaser, 1965), we collected data from professional development interactions with in-service teacher s in three contexts, which then formed three case studies for investigating the research questions. The first case study (CS1) focused on the use of the SfT tool in the context of a university-based TPD program for in-service teachers. The TPD program had a duration of three months, and was carefully scaffolded, supporting teachers to design and implement their action plans to develop their digital competence based on the feedback provided by the SfT. CS1 included 13 in-service teachers who completed their self-reflection using the SfT tool and participated in a n individual interview prior to the experiential wo rkshops; nine of them completed the TPD program. The second case study (CS2) investigated a bottom-up effort of an inservice secondary school Media Studies teacher to help her colleagues develop digital competence at  a private secondary education school. It also lasted three months, and support was provided by the Media Studies teacher on demand. Τhe primary participants in CS2 were the Media Studies teacher who served as the innovator and two in-service teachers (a Special Education teacher and a  Drama Teacher) who showed interest in exploring how to develop their digital competence. The third case study  (CS3) describes a University-Ministry collaboration to introduce the SfT self-reflection tool to chemistry education secondary school teachers. Invitations to participate were sent to 120 in-service chemistry teachers, five of whom also participated in a focus group  reflection after completing the SfT self-reflection; data were  also collected by the Chemistry Education Inspector. This intervention had limited duration, and no other support was provided to teachers after the group reflection.

## Data collection and analysis

Multiple types of qualitative data were collected f or each case study, using  in-depth individual interviews and group reflections; in addition, videotaped discussions of meetings, professional learning design plans and learning artifacts were collected for the first case study. This dataset was complemented with  teachers’ individual selfreflection reports produced by the SfT tool.  Interview d ata were transcribed and analyzed qualitatively using Atlas.Ti (v.23), using a detailed coding scheme. Intercoder agreement was satisfactory (Cohen’s κ=.81), and was established by the authors first independently, with disagreements then discussed and resolved. We investigated self-reflection through the teachers’ completion of the SfT self-reflection and the results shared in the SfT reports, the individual teacher interviews, and group reflections. We studied agency by examining the progression of the teachers’ learning design plans and the actions they proposed and undertook to facilitate the implementation of these plans. When no such initiatives were taken, w e examined why that was the case.  Guided by the principles of the  S elf-Determination Theory  (Ryan & Deci, 2020), we coded for teachers’ self-efficacy, agency, digital competence, digital practices, and process of impro vement, to identify statements related to  a utonomy, competence and relatedness. To analyze the data, we contrasted the three cases to examine patterns and differences across and within cases.

## Findings

Even though the SfT self-reflection helped teachers become aware of digital competence areas they should improve (Economou et al., 2024),  these three case  studies indicated that the self-reflection process was not sufficient to  help teachers  take meaningful next steps  to improv e their digital competence  on their own. Participants across the three contexts pointed to challenges in following up on the feedback provided by the SfT tool.  The analysis of each teacher’s professional learning plan in CS1 at four different points in time  indicated that all teachers faced  substantial challenges in (a) understanding how to move forward from the SfT self-reflection report, (b) independently developing an actionable plan, and (c) implementing this plan in their practice. As one teacher stated during the group reflection at the end of the CS1 workshops, “on its own the SfT tool cannot (empower teachers) [other teachers in the room voiced their affirmation  as well ]… (you need) specific and targeted actions, like what we did here, the way you had us reflect, those were successful in my opinion. I believe everyone else also supports this [four other teache rs also voice their agreement]. For me, the way you had us reflect is important…because the tool on its own does not o ffer nothing. And because I had used the tool befor e on another occasion. And for me, this reflection, the way you had us reflect helped me.” Α second finding was that the teachers overwhelmingly  gravitated towards introduc ing technology in their teaching practice, shifting discussion to their students and to the technological infrastructure they needed to support instruction, and ignoring DigCompEdu areas such as “Professional Engagement” or “Digital Resources” and “Empowering Learners”.  It was considerably more difficult to focus on themselves as learning professionals, and on the challenges they faced in enhancing their own digital competence. We observed a decline in motivation to take action to improve digital competence across the cases-with CS2 and CS3 participants being less motivated than CS 1 participants-as reflected in the quantity and quality of the teachers’ professional learning designs, and the actions they were willing to undertake in each context. Selfdetermination theory (SDT) provides a valuable framework  for examining the differences between case studies by analyzing how socio-technical affordances and limitations impacted the participants’ autonomy, competence, and relatedness. These factors, in turn, influenced the teachers’ varying levels of self-motivation to progress from self-reflection to action in improving their digital competence. We next present the main findings for each one of the three main psychological components of SDT.

Autonomy All participants in each of the three case studies  w ere intrinsically motivated and  joined the workshops and meetings because of their personal interest to unde rstand the concept of digital competence  and how to use the SfT to assess their own digital competence. Each participating teacher completed the SfT-facilitated selfreflection, examined their levels of digital compet ence and reflected on them. Nonetheless, participants differed in the actions they initiated after the SfT self-reflection, with higher autonomy and agency exhibited in CS1, less in CS2, and significantly less in CS3. This may be attributed to the autonomy support and structure (two key concepts in SDT)  provided in the first t wo case studies to the in-service teachers. More specifically, after th eir self-reflection, the participating teachers were encoura ged by the TPD facilitators (C S1) and the Media Studies teacher (CS2) to set concrete professional learning goals, whi ch were personally meaningful and aligned with their own professional interests. In CS1 considerable effort was invested by the TPD facilitators to guide participating teachers in identifying their own professional priorities in relation to the development of their digital competence, emphasizing the value of goal-setting rather than prescribing top-down goals and milestones that teachers should achieve. This was acknowledged during the teachers’ final group reflection, in which they stated that the TPD facilitators did not compromise their auton omy but, instead, they provided  scaffolding that contributed to a sense of ownership that supported their motivation to advance their professional learning (“You didn't come here as experts to show us models of (technology) integration. Instead, you built on our own capabilities”).

Competence The qualitative analysis of teachers’ interviews  and group reflections indicated that in all three case studies, the self-reflection tool supported the teachers’ critical assessment of their own digital competence as  it made them aware that digital competence is a multi-level and multi-dimensional construct and  helped them assess their personal level of  digital competence. Following their self-reflection experience with the SfT, the participati ng teachers were surprised by the low-appraised levels of digital competence, and mentioned personal (e.g., lack of knowledge, time, self-efficacy, commitment), as well as institutional and systemic challenges (e.g., lack of equipment and educational resources, school workloa d, curriculum constraints), a ffecting their ability to take action (Economou et al., 2024). However, these perceived obstacles were  notably decreased  in CS1 wh ich included a carefully scaffolded experience. In CS3, which included no scaffolding, the chemistry teachers mainly discussed barriers to taking action. On the other hand, in CS2 the support provided by the Media Studies teacher to her colleagues and the encouragement from the school leadership served as significant enablers for two of the teachers to move beyond the articulation of a general  intent to act, to focusing on developing personal and actionable learning plans. Finally, in CS1 the scaffolded TPD supported teachers to develop their capacity to move from designing to implementing more sophisticated professional learning plans, and even further to motivate other colleagues to pursue similar professional journeys.

Relatedness Participants i n all three case studies perceived relatedness (a sense of connection, belonging, and support) as significant in translating  the SfT self-reflection results to  concrete action s. In CS3, the Chemistry Education Inspector mentioned that “a community of learning can grow around this effort … because in this way they (the chemistry teachers) will develop stronger relationships and they will perceive this (the SfT) quite positively. They will have the chance to interact  (between them) ”. However, the CS3 teachers were too overwhelmed by institutional and systemic constraints to be willing to participate in a community of learner s sharing the goal of improving digital competence. In CS2 the Media Studies teacher created a small group of colleagues who were interested in further extending their digital competence after their SfT self-reflection. In this case study, the Special Education teacher and the Drama teacher, who showed interest in further exploring how to develop their digital competence, underscored the significance of belongi ng in a group, receiving support from the Media Studies teacher and participating in meaningful interactions: “having EK (the Media Studies teacher) there made it easier. That's the truth.  […] So, if maybe it wasn't for EK […] I wouldn't be aware of how to use this tool to help me progress…but then the support that I get from EK. Yes, it do es matter.” However, it was only in CS1, that the teachers self-identified as members of a  vibrant community of practice supported by the TPD facilitators. CS1 teachers explained that they learned a lot from their peers and that they felt  that their personal contribution s mattered and were valued. As one of the teachers shared: “…creating an environment of trust and security, so that we could share our  […] it's not always easy to create this environmen t […] First of all, it was the environment you [the researchers and TPD facilitators] created for us and then it was us… You were the reason that we were having these sessions, and we were sharing our thoughts, otherwise we woul dn’t feel safe to do so.” The emergence of this collegial environment was crucial in motivating teachers’ agency to proceed from self-reflection to the design and implementation of concrete professional learning plans for developing their digital competence.

## Discussion

Self-reflection as a mechanism to drive self-improvement and transformation is not a new idea (Mezirow, 2018; Schön, 2017). Nonetheless, reports indicate that reflective discourse in teacher education does not often translate to actions (Beauchamp, 2015; Nocetti et al., 2020).  The findings of this multi-case study indicate that strengthening teachers’ digital competence capacity through self-reflection and self-regulation is not a straightforward task. While self-reflection, supported by tools such as SfT, can help teachers become self-aware and assess their digital competence, assuming an ag entic approach to self-improvement requires mentorship and establishing a professional learning culture that values and acknowledges the teachers’ volition, while at the same time it scaffolds them to become agents of change. To become self-determined learners, teachers should be given opportunities to participate in scaffolded collaborative, professional learning exp eriences that support the development of autonomous motivation, through provi ding sufficient and gradually fading structure, and by helping them find solutions to overcom e various personal, institutional and systemic challe nges that may compromise their agency.
