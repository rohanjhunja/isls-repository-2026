# Understanding the Impact of Physical, Online, and Hybrid Modalities on Self and Co-Regulation during Collaborative Problem Solving

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite substantial research on self-regulated learning (SRL) and co-regulated learning, the interplay of these behaviors across different modes of presence is understudied. Using data from six groups with meetings across three modalities (physical, online, and hybrid), we used Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) to model regulation behaviors and how they vary based on modalities. Preliminary findings reveal significant differences in self and co-regulation patterns across the modalities, emphasizing the need for modality-specific support strategies and tools in computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) environments to address the unique challenges of each modality.

## Introduction and theoretical background

The literature on computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL)  has highlighted the importance of self and co-regulation for the success of collaborative learning (Roschelle, 1992; Chan, 2012). However, the dynamics of CSCL environments can cause difficulties for studen ts to self-regulate and co-regulate the learning of others in the group (Chan, 2012); that is, be able to plan, set goals and strategies, monitor and evaluate progress, and manage their emotions and behavior (Zimmerman, 2000; Winne, 2011), particularly, during small group collaboration work (Damsa, 2014). Empirical evidence shows that self-regulated learning (SRL) is ‘situated’ and influenced by the specific learning environment and the context in which it occurs (Hadwin & Oshige, 2011; Broadbent, 2017). Thus, within CSCL environments, SRL processes could be influenced by the mode of presence (e.g., whether the collaboration occurs in physical, online, or hybrid  settings) (Broadbent, 2017). For instance, physica l learning environments offer opportunities for spontaneous in teraction, which can enhance group regulation by fo stering real-time feedback and mutual understanding (Raes et al., 2020). Conversely, online settings often require explicit communication and structured tools to support regulatory processes (Chan, 2012). Hybrid environments combine these features but present unique challenges, such as ensuring equitable participation among remote and in-person students (McKellar & Wang, 2023). In this regard, students working in these environments may need pedagogical and technological support to navigate the challenges they face. While our understanding of SRL during collaboration has advanced, more empirical evidence is needed to expla in how SRL and co-regulation behaviors manifest during small group collaboration across di fferent modalities. With this background, the work in progress reported in this poster explores whether students’ mode of presence (e.g., online, physical or hybrid) duri ng collaborative tasks affects their self and co-regulation behaviours. Guided by established SRL frameworks (Winne, 2022; Hadwin & Oshige, 2011; Zimmerman, 2000), we coded and analysed students’ group meetings with one primary objective: To explore how regulation processes manifest among individuals and groups across differ ent learning modalities.  The study’s findings will deepen understanding of how students regulate their learni ng in CSCL across modalities and how teachers can s upport and design for these processes. Moreover, the findi ngs can guide the design of CSCL tools (e.g., colla boration reflection dashboards) that help students reflect and become aware of their regulatory behaviors.

## Methods

The study utilised data from a hybrid master’s-level legal technology course, where students engag ed in collaborative problem-solving tasks. Six groups participated, each holding physical, online, and hybrid meetings. Data included twenty hours of video recordings anal ysed using a coding scheme informed by SRL framewor ks (Zimmerman, 2000; Winne, 2011). The coding scheme distinguished between SRL behaviors, such as individual goal setting (PLAN) and monitoring (SELFMON), and co-regulated behaviors, such as collaborative planning (COPLAN) and group monitoring (COMON). Epistemic network analysis (Shaffer et al., 2016) was employed to model and visualise the co-occurrence of regulatory behaviors across modalities, enabling a nuanced understanding of regulatory dynamics at individual, group, and modality-specific levels.

## Preliminary findings, significance, and future work

Based on the analysis of three meetings representin g each modality, preliminary findings showed signif icant differences in SRL and co-regulation behaviors across modalities. For example,  physical Meetings  were characterized by dense connections with co-regulated monitoring (COMON), suggesting that physical proximity facilitates group interactions. Online meetings  have stronger connections between individual planni ng (SELFPLAN) and monitoring (SELFMON), indicating that online participants rely more heavily on autonomous regulatory processes, likely due to limited non-verbal cues and structured communication. Hybrid Meetings showed fragmented links between shared and individu al regulation behaviors, reflecting challenges in balancing participation and integrating remote and in-person contributions effectively. These findings sh ow how physical, online, and hybrid modalities shape SRL and coregulation processes during small-group collaboration in CSCL environments (figure 1). While these are preliminary findings based on a s mall dataset, they highlight the importance of designing modality-specific tools and pedagogies to support small-group collaboration and regulation. Future analysis of all recorded meetings will likely offer a more comprehensive understanding of how SRL and coregulation manifest across physical, online, and hybrid settin gs and identify patterns that may have been overlooked in the preliminary examination.
