# A Community-Engaged Curriculum Design Model for Culturally-Responsive Tech Consulting

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Minority small businesses operating in resource-constrained areas are actively building their digital capacity while facing significant barriers that existing digital services fail to address. We designed and implemented the Community Tech Consultants (CTC s) program to address this gap by offering accessible and effective digital support grounded in a deep understanding of these businesses’ realities, challenges, and needs. To deepen community partnerships and meaningfully engage university students, this study aims to develop a community-engaged curriculum that prepares students for culturally responsive tech consulting with these businesses, and that can be adapted across diverse cultural and community contexts. Through design-based research and co-design with community partners, we propose a community-engaged curriculum design model with five interrelated elements: co-design and co-instruction, authentic learning, adaptive scaffolding, iteration, and adaptability. We extend the discourse on cultural responsiveness in community-engaged learning and underscore the value of co-design between the community and the university.

Minority small businesses operating in resource-constrained areas are actively building their digital capacity while facing significant barriers that existing digital services fail to address, including limited access to affordable digital services and tools, scheduling difficulties, and other structural constraints (Hui et al., 2023). To help bridge this growing socio-technical gap, we implemented the Community Tech Consultants (CTCs) program on Detroit’s Eastside. Most businesses in the neighborhoods served are Black-women-owned, primarily in restaurants, retail, and e-commerce. Since 2021, the CTCs program has recruited and trained two cohorts of local residents to serve as Community Tech Consultants (CTCs) and is now expanding to include university students. Findings from the pilot program illustrate the efforts, strengths, and challenges of these businesses in adopting and using technologies, as well as the reciprocal capacity building between CTCs and business owners (Hui et al., 2024), further underscoring the importance of cultural responsiveness training for effective and sustainable community engagement. This study aims to develop a community-engaged curriculum to prepare university students for culturally responsive community engagement.

Considering the ongoing challenges in community-engaged learning (CEL) —including episodic partnerships and misalignment between academic standards and community needs (Machimana et al., 2018; Preece, 2016; Reilly et al., 2023) —our study seeks to reimagine this type of learning experience. We aim to contribute to this discourse by offering new perspectives on community-engaged curriculum design. Guided by the overarching goal of strengthening long-term community –university partnerships and enhancing digital capacity among business owners, we examined what a curriculum design model might look like. Specifically, this study addresses the following research question: What are the key design elements of a community-engaged curriculum that prepares university students for culturally responsive tech consulting? Theoretical foundations This study draws on and extends scholarship in asset-based pedagogies and community-engaged learning (CEL). Contrary to deficit-based perspectives, asset-based pedagogies—such as culturally relevant pedagogy, culturally responsive teaching, and culturally sustaining pedagogy (Gay, 2018; Ladson-Billings, 1995; Paris & Alim, 2017)—emphasize teaching and learning that acknowledge, leverage, and sustain students’ and communities’ cultures. As asset-based pedagogies have been extended into different fields, this  study further expands their application by integrating them into community-engaged tech learning in two ways. First, we adopt culturally responsive teaching as a guiding framework to align both the curriculum design process and the curriculum content with students’ prior knowledge and experiences, as well as the cultures and practices of local communities. Second, we frame the development of students’ capacity for culturally responsive community engagement as an overarching goal and align the curriculum’s structure, content, and pedagogy to support it. 1797 Community-based learning (CBL), as a broader “umbrella” term, refers to any pedagogical approach in which the community serves as an active partner in the learning process (Mooney & Edwards, 2001). As a more specific and rigorously defined subset of CBL, community-engaged learning foregrounds reciprocity and mutual benefit. It intentionally integrates community-identified needs with academic learning objectives, generating reciprocal value for community partners, campus partners, and students (Jacoby, 2015;  Furco, 1996). In our context, we adopt the term community-engaged learning to align with the university’s broader community engagement initiatives and to underscore the principle of mutual contribution and shared benefit between local communities and students (University of Michigan, Edward Ginsberg Center, n.d.).

## Research design

The study is being conducted within our long-term community-engaged tech consulting program, which provides free one-on-one tech consulting to help resource-constrained minority small business owners integrate technology into their operations. Building on the existing infrastructure, our study adopts a design-based research (DBR) approach (McKenney & Reeves, 2019) to iteratively analyze, design, and refine the curriculum to foster culturally responsive engagement. As a dynamic and use-inspired process, DBR emphasizes iteration throughout the research cycle and facilitates the collaborative, situated, and adaptive redesign of learning activities and content (de Oliveira Lozada, 2022).

## Participants

The primary participants in this study included three Detroit resident CTCs, local small business owners, training instructors, and other community partners. Additionally, undergraduates from the School of Education participated in the learner assessment to help inform the curriculum design.

## Phase 1: data collection and analysis

We used the in-person training sessions for resident CTCs as a starting point, systematically recording, analyzing, evaluating, and refining them to inform the design of the curriculum for university students. We conducted observations of training sessions, interviews with resident CTCs and instructors, discussions with community partners—including small business owners and representatives from community development organizations — and a learner assessment. All authors participated in at least part of the training sessions and interviews, and crosschecked emerging themes with the wider program team. Interviews and observations of resident CTCs: We conducted three rounds of semi-structured interviews with resident CTCs to capture their training and community tech consulting experiences. We recorded, transcribed, and coded interview data for analysis, generating emerging insights and real-life scen arios that informed curriculum design. The first round of interviews was conducted individually after the training. CTCs appreciated the activities for self-discovery and engaging with local business owners. One CTC noted: “The business people came and talked with us— we saw what daily life is like for a tech consultant, how they approach their work and build different partnerships.”  This feedback highlighted relationship building and engagement with community partners as authentic learning experiences. The second round of interviews, conducted individually three months after the training, focused on the effectiveness of the training sessions and CTCs’ reflections on their work. One CTC remarked, “I’m so happy that you paired us together because his expertise in tech and his willingness to share resources and discuss upcoming events have helped me stay up to date with the field.” This feedback revealed the critical importance of peer support and collaboration. The third interview, conducted as a group session six months after the training, had CTCs share a typical day and reflect on their experiences, strategies, and challenges. It demonstrated how the training prepared them for real-world consulting, how they developed strategies to build trust with clients, and highlighted the importance of ongoing reflection in navigating contexts that can sometimes challenge their best intentions. Interviews with instructors:  Acting as a bridge between the university and the community, the three instructors included detailed introductions to the program, community, and partnership in the training. In interviews, they reflected on the program’s infrastructure, highlighting the importance of consistent professionalism among CTCs while also acknowledging individual growth and supporting each CTC’s professional development. This approach further highlighted the balance between learning and service centra l to community-engaged learning (Furco, 1996). community development organizations to learn about their routines, stories, and community identities. One community partner shared their lived experiences of mapping the neighborhood, which went beyond the labels and icons on Google Maps. Instead, local landmarks are co-defined by the stories of generations of community 1798 partners. This insight further underscored the value of community knowledge and highlighted the importance of structuring our curriculum and tech consulting services for capacity building to sustain the local community. Learner assessment: We conducted a needs assessment by examining students’ backgrounds and their major program curricula to better understand their prior knowledge and experiences, ensure the curriculum’s cultural responsiveness, and complement their program. The assessment highlighted the need for training in cultural awareness and professional communication, as most students are international and have limited experience engaging with Detroit communities.

We synthesized the following insights to inform the curriculum co-design and refinement in Phase 2: ● Insight 1: Engage the community throughout the curriculum co-design and co-instruction process. ● Insight 2: Leverage community knowledge and resources to create authentic learning experiences and build meaningful relationships.

● Insight 3:  Support learners both individually and collectively while fostering peer collaboration in training and community tech consulting.

Phase 2: curriculum co-design and iterative refinement Consistent with the collaborative and context-sensitive nature of design-based research, we employed a co-design strategy that engaged resident CTCs, instructors, community partners, and university students, staff, and faculty as co-designers and critical contributors throughout the curriculum development process. Each participant brought unique perspectives, experi ences, and strengths to the research and design of the curriculum. We iteratively integrated the research and design processes and conducted ongoing evaluations to gather feedback and insights that informed design decisions. For instance, drawing on discussions with resident CTCs and community partners, we refined the learning objectives to be more specific and measurable, supporting students’ growth within the community context. Panels of subject-matter experts in cultural intelligence provided critical feedback on guiding students step by step in developing both awareness and practice. The proposed curriculum design model Grounded in asset-based pedagogies, community-engaged learning, and insights from data analysis, we propose a curriculum design model comprising five interrelated elements (Figure 1): (1) co-design and co-instruction, (2) authentic learning, (3) adaptive scaffolding, (4) iteration, and (5) adaptability. Figure 1 The Community-Engaged Curriculum Design Model Co-design and co-instruction: Consistent with the principles of community-engaged learning, the curriculum is co-designed by university and community partners. Their voices, expertise, and insights are fully integrated to ensure responsiveness to community cultures and practices. The curriculum is intended to be coinstructed by multiple partners to honor their expertise and enrich students’ learning, including three experienced instructors, resident CTCs, and community and faculty members with complementary expertise spanning business, communication, community engagement, and technology. Authentic learning:  Pilot training with resident CTCs underscores the value of authentic learning experiences. Embedded in both the learning objectives and content (Table 1), authentic learning begins with selfdiscovery and learning about and with the community. Adopting an asset-based perspective, the curriculum recognizes the knowledge and cultural resources of local communities and further engages students in these rich contexts through activities such as cultural asset scavenger hunt and canvassing. The case studies, task scenarios, and improvisation exercises are grounded in real-world contexts, helping students understand local communities while developing cultural awareness and the capacity for culturally responsive engagement. Adaptive scaffolding:  Scaffolding from instructors and peers has proven critical in resident CTCs’ training and work routines. To better support students in navigating community engagement, scaffolding is 1799 integrated throughout the learning process and designed to be adaptive and responsive to learners’ prior knowledge, cultural backgrounds, and evolving needs. We structure and tailor scaffolding across individual, peer, and whole-class learning, including real-time personalized coaching, reflective prompts to foster self-awareness, and regular check-ins to address challenges. The level of scaffolding is gradually reduced as students gain confidence and autonomy.

Iteration: As a dynamic design process, we position iteration as a core element of the curriculum model by aligning its content with the needs and aspirations of both the community and students, and by adopting a flexible, modular design. The curriculum is organized into five modules that progressively prepare students for culturally responsive community tech consulting. Formative assessment and curriculum evaluation are integrated throughout the process, engaging students, instructors, and community partners in providing feedback to guide ongoing curriculum iterations and ensure its long-term relevance and sustainability across contexts. Adaptability: The curriculum integrates conceptual learning, local case studies, and structured reflections, and is designed to be flexible and adaptable across contexts. Conceptual learning provides a foundation for knowledge transfer and application; local case studies can be customized by learning designers or instructors to fit their specific contexts; and structured reflections consolidate learning. Together, these situated and reflective experiences aim to help students empathize with local business o wners and recognize problems, patterns, and strategies that can be applied to similar challenges across contexts. In-person and online versions of the curriculum are developed to accommodate different learning contexts.

The study presents a community-engaged curriculum co-design process and a curriculum design model, offering implications for both practice and theory. Practically, the proposed curriculum design model highlights co-design and co-instruction as mechanisms f or equitable participation, authentic learning and adaptive scaffolding as pathways for meaningful engagement, and iteration and adaptability as mechanisms for curricular sustainability. It underscores the value of university–community collaboration and reciprocal capacity building (Hui et al., 2024) within a co-design process, offering a promising framework for community-engaged curriculum design in higher education. Theoretically, the study extends the discourse on cultural responsiveness in community-engaged learning by proposing two interconnected layers of cultural responsiveness: (1) designing the curriculum itself to be responsive to students’ and communities’ cultures, and (2) cultivating students’ capabilities to engage in culturally responsive community engagement.

During the co-design process, several tensions arose around time limits, scheduling constraints, differing expectations, and the goal of reciprocal capacity building. These experiences prompted us to reflect regularly on how our perspectives, institutional  positions, and decisions could shape both the process and outcomes. We negotiated between the community’s and university’s ways of knowing and working, aligned community practices with university students’ modes of inquiry and learning objectives, and fostered open, honest feedback. The curriculum is currently being implemented with seven School of Education undergraduate students, co-instructed by the same three instructors. To evaluate students’ development and the curriculum’s impact, our ongoing and future work includes conducting observations, interviews, and surveys with students and instructors; gathering feedback from resident CTCs and community partners; and cross-analyzing these data sources. These evaluative insights will inform the iterative refinement of the curriculum. We also acknowledge that students require time to internalize cultural awareness and culturally responsive practices into their routines, with these developments manifesting as longer-term impact of the curriculum.
