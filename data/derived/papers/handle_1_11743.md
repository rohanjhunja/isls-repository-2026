# Patterns of Cognitive Offloading and Extended Mind in Digital Academic Reading with AI-generated Annotations

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates patterns of distributed intelligence in digital academic reading. Data was collected from 47 higher education students in South Korea engaging with two types of AI-generated annotations: Korean translations (cognitive offloading) and deep-reasoning questions (extended mind). Process mining of trace data and interview data showed that translation annotations enabled more linear, purposeful reading processes, whereas question annotations led to more complex integration of information. The findings contribute to our understanding of actions that represent cognitive offloading and extended mind, informing the design of distributed intelligence in digital academic reading.

## Introduction

Digital academic reading is a complex, difficult ac tivity, especially for learners who are reading in a foreign language (Reiber-Kuijpers et al., 2021); yet digital environments can be  conducive to technological support, forming a system comprised of interactions between the learner, tools, digital environment, and text. For instance, generative AI tools are increasingly used to support reading, a phenomenon with the potential to introduce various changes in the activity of digital academic reading. This calls for an investigation into the distribution of the learning activity within this system, and how this changes learners’ learning experiences. In the context of digital academic reading, using an AI tool to translate the text into one’s mother tongue for faster, easier reading is likely to induce different interactions from interactions that occur while using AI tools to generate  deep-reasoning questions (Anson, 2024; Graesser & Person, 1994).  Using the lens of distributed intelligence theory, which  posits that intelligence emerges from collaborative  activities amongst actors and artifacts (Hutchins, 1995; Kim & Reeves, 2007; Pea, 1993), we examine the varied interactions in digital academic reading that occur due to different uses of generative AI, focusing on two main forms of distribution: ‘cognitive offloading’ and ‘extended mind’. This distinction is loosely based on the typology by Lodge et al. (2023), which distinguishes individual interactions with generative AI. However, in this paper ‘extended mind’ interactions are viewed as those that reorganize the learning activi ty, allowing the system to go beyond the sum of its  parts (Hernández-Orallo & Vold, 201 9; Pea, 1985). Conversely, cognitive offloading inte ractions involve the tool freeing up a learner’s cognitive resources by performing part of the task (Kim & Reeves, 2007; Lodge et al., 2023). In this study, we looked at translations and questi ons given to readers in the form of annotations, to heighten the spatial integration of the system and thus better observe distribution patterns. We then analyzed the data based on the following two research questions: 1) what different patterns of distribution (cognitive offloading and extended mind) are induced by learners’ interactions with differing annotation tools? and 2) how do learners’ learning experiences change based on the different distribution patterns?

## Methods

50 undergraduate and graduate EFL (English as a for eign language) students in South Korean higher education institutions were recruited for this within-experiment study. Three participants were excluded based on screening procedures, leaving a total of 47 participants (29 female, 18 male, average age = 23.34 years).  Participants went through two reading tasks; for each task, they were asked to interact with the introduction section of an English research article presented on a computer screen. While reading the text, participants selected meaningful segments of 1-2 sentences to view one of the two types of AI-generated annotations (pre-generated using prompts given to Claude.ai) and add comments to the annotations. Participants were informed that all annotations wer e generated by AI. They were also able to minimize or delete annotations and edit their comments. Trace data was collected from learners’ interactions with the text, annotations, and comments. To extract patterns from the data, this study utilized the TraMineR library in R for sequence analysis, and simple Markov models to calculate and visualize transition probab ilities in a stochastic process mining procedure depicted by Helske et al. (2024). After the two tasks, 30-minute individual semi-structured interviews were conducted to probe how learning experiences changed based on annotatio n type. Interviews were transcribed, then segmented and qualitatively coded by the researchers following thematic analysis procedures (Braun & Clarke, 2006).

## Findings

Patterns of distribution Sequence analysis revealed similar patterns of learners connecting varied actions to repeated views of the text in both conditions. However, the simple Markov models we built based on these seq uences showed differences in transition probabilities, as depicted in  the subtraction plot (translation – question) in  Figure 1. When using question annotations, learners showed stronger tendencies to review the  text, especially after maximizing (transition probability: question 40%, translation 10%) or minimizing the  annotation (question 24%, translation 8%), or after beginning to write (question 7%, tran slation <5%) or edit a comment (question 33%, trans lation <5%). While interacting with translation annotations, lea rners more easily transition ed from starting to write a comment to publishing it (question <5%, translation  40%), from viewing a text to selecting an annotati on (question 22%, translation 29%), or from editing a comment to minimizing it (question 33%, translation  100%). This suggests a smooth, purposeful form of reading  reminiscent of cognitive offloading, whereas transitions in the question annotation condition indicate distributions that extend the mind, with complex reviewing processes and patterns of combining information from annotations and the text. Red edges: translation transitions; blue edges: question transitions Changes in learners’ learning experiences Thematic analysis revealed that different annotations changed learners’ experiences in complex ways. Two themes arose regarding translation annotations: fast, efficient reading  and reliance on translations. Translation annotations were deemed “efficient”, allowing learners to read through the text uninterrupted while easily building comprehension. However, some learners mentioned realizing that the y were relying  too much on  the AI translations, which prevented additional critical thought; for instance, one participant explained: “I wondered, ‘am I depending on [these translations] too much and not fully using my own reading abilities?’” Learners reported less ease when using the question  annotations, with some participants claiming that viewing the questions “disrupted the reading”. Two conflicting themes showed that learners sometim es felt that questions extended the mind, while at times the questions were also thought to  limit the mind. Most often, novel questions were seen to expand thinking, gearing the learners towards the creation of n ew hypothetical questions. However, some learners felt that “there was definitely a possibility that  receiving questions first could prevent [them] from coming up with [their] own questions. ” These conflicting effects led many learners to use question annotations more selectively, only utilizing questions that provided new insights for creative or analytical thought. This appeared to indicate a shifting of the activity from comprehension towards question asking.

## Discussion

Distinct patterns of interaction with translation a nd question annotations revealed interactions that potentially represent cognitive offloading and extended mind, which were also reflected in  learners’ varied experiences. Translations often lessened learners’ cognitive load due to the AI-generated annotations performing translation in their stead. Conversely, strategic use of questions appeared to reorganize learning to induce critica l engagement with the text and lead to question asking (Pea, 1985). These findings can inform the design of AI tools that support digital academic reading  based on an understanding of distribution (White & Pea, 2011), perhaps suggesting a two-step integration in which translations first offload cognition to lessen EFL learners’ struggles, then questions help them reorganize the activity towards question asking. Further qualitative analysis on diverging patterns within interactions with different annotation types  will provide additional insights into how the socio-technical system evolves (Hutchins, 1995) towards comprehension and question-asking, allowing for more intricate design.
