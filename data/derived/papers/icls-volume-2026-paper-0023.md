# Innovation, Iteration, and the Interaction Order: When Is It Okay to Copy Code?

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Computer science education contexts are microcosms for studying broader tech culture. As students learn programming, they are shaping —and shaped by—the normative expectations for creating, contributing, and innovating. This paper explores norms and obligations in an elementary computer science context and analyzes a tension between how children learn how, when, and whether to copy code. Through interaction analysis of 20 hours of video-recorded lessons in a second-grade classroom, we examined students’ sense-making about geometric patterns in two different mathematics-coding tasks. We found that sense-making about iteration, unitizing, and variables involved interpreting a mixed message about when it is okay to copy code. We discuss this mixed message in terms of a relationship between social and computational ordering of interactions. We then draw implications for teaching and learning computer science in an era of rapid technological innovation and shifting sociocomputational norms.

Computer science education contexts are microcosms for studying broader tech culture. As students learn programming, they are shaping—and shaped by—the normative expectations for creating, for contributing, and for innovating. This paper explores social ordering of norms and obligations in an elementary computer science context and analyzes contradictions as children learn how, when, and whether to copy code. The integrated mathematics-coding tasks we analyze below expose the tensions between what constitutes good solutions (as novice coders) and what constitutes proper conduct (as students, group members, classmates). While establishing “social-computational norms” (Silvis et al., 2025; Fong et al., 2023), students and teachers communicated a mixed message. On the one hand, students were expected to produce novel solutions that did not copy each other’s ideas. On the other hand, they were learning to iterate units of code. In other words, copying chunks of code was a good strategy, but copying each other’s solutions was not accepted. Exploring this contradiction in novelty norms contributes to situated and critical perspectives on students’ socio-computational practices in an era of rapid technological innovation and within a broader tech culture that incentivizes novel solution-finding (Kafai & Proctor, 2022).

Because of the interactive nature of early childhood tangible programming, novel solutions in and of themselves were insufficient for understanding children’s sense-making. In the collaborative activities we designed and analyzed for this paper, children’s  programs were not individual cognitive achievements. Rather, they were built and edited by groups of children. Furthermore, the pedagogical strategies of the classroom teacher (i.e., whole group discussions, collecting groups’ solutions on the whiteboard), contributed to shared sense-making but also sometimes complicated the implicit prohibition on copying solutions. This led us to ask a question that arose as children were learning how to code: when is it okay to copy in early computer science classrooms?

In this paper, we address this question through interaction analysis of a series of lessons in which children used a tangible coding robot in small groups (e.g., Wang et al., 2019). Through close qualitative analysis of children’s talk-in-interaction during the Shapes unit, we argue that “copying” was important for the social and computational ordering of interactions. We then draw on these findings to interrogate how innovation norms are organizing broader tech culture.

## Framing perspectives

We frame our study through Goffman’s (1983) notion of the interaction order, the idea that person-to-person interactions organize how people make (and make sense of) social situations. Just as the economic order orders economic activity, and social order o rders social activity, the interaction order orders interactions. The idea that interactions are ordered does not imply they are orderly, an important point to keep in mind when analyzing the interactions of young children. Rather, the regular patterns and regulating function of interactions serve to establish interactional order, and this order then feeds back into social situations, structuring what is expected 151 (Rawls, 1987). Thus, norms of conduct and communication are established and maintained, and the interaction order becomes useful for understanding the norming of social structures, such as schooling or “tech culture.” Goffman’s (1983) account of the “interactional ground rules” by which people constitute themselves as social actors and establish meaning together has influenced the practice of interaction analysis, the method we employed in this study (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). Goffman was clear that the social order cannot be reduced to situated accounts of person-to-person interactions, nor is the micro (interactional) scale simply a “symptom” or “effect” of the macro (structural) scale (p. 2). Rather, the features of the interaction order (e.g., who has the right to speak; proper ways to present a new idea or change topics; rules for correcting conduct of others) are “expressions in regard” to social arrangements and “what they come to be taken as a ‘reflection’ of is necessarily an open question” (p. 3). It is this open question that motivates our theoretical approach to the problem of social norming of coding.

In this paper, we use Goffman’s ideas as a lens through which to deepen disciplinary perspectives on sociomathematical norming (Herschkowitz & Schwarz, 1999; Sfard, 2007; Yakel & Cobb, 1999—and more recently, sociocomputational norming (Fong et al., 2023; Silvis et al., 2025) —in accounts of learning and teaching. In drawing on Goffman, we are particularly interested in how social ordering of learning in emerging computational contexts reflects broader social norms in today’s tech culture. For example, obligations governing when, how, and whether students are allowed to copy code reflect normative expectations in tech culture more broadly, where the drive to innovate pressures coders to create new things (Vinsel & Russell, 2019). At the same time, core programming concepts like refactoring, loops, functions, and variables dictate when, how, and whether novice programmers should copy code when programming. The contradictions between copying (each other’s) code and copying (units of) code is the subject of this paper, where we attempt to follow Suchman’s (2008) "aim to characterize innovation’s enactment as a member’s category, as an identification produced through multiple, particular, and intersecting performances” [emphasis added] (p. 2-3). For Suchman, innovation and creativity are not sudden, magical acts of individual genius, but are actually collaborative, social practices that are gradual and emergent. Suchman (2011) described innovation as “artful integration” and “laborious reconfigurations” (p. 15) whereby artifacts or technologies are reworked, only later to be deemed inventions. She explained “originals and copies are not different in kind so much as in time and place, and that just as translation invariably produces difference, nove lty requires imitation or likenesses to familiar forms.” It is important to note, that while Suchman’s most familiar work centered on copy machines (a copying technology, par excellence!), her critique of innovation expanded to the unexamined category of the new that is normalized in tech hubs like Silicon Valley (see Author, 2022). In the following analysis, Goffman’s interaction order serves as a lens for how novelty norms are enacted, and Suchman’s perspective, as a lens for understanding what sociotechnical norms mean within the broader culture. This study was part of a larger design-based research project on development of young children’s Computational Thinking (CT) integrated with mathematics and builds on our previous study on sociocomputational elements of CT (Silvis et al., 2025), and on a larger body of research on teaching young children basic programming (Papert, 1980; Wang et al., 2019). The current analysis involves a partnership with a second-grade teacher Dr. J and twenty-five students in a campus-based school in the  western United States. Every Friday Dr. J scheduled a minimum of 45 minutes for students to work through five units and 19 weekly lessons focused on integrated mathCT activities using tangible coding toys like Botley 2.0, a robot controlled through a remote with directional arrow command buttons (L-rotate left; F-move forward). During these sessions students were organized into groups of three working together on coding tasks and occasionally coming together for whole-class discussions–– referred to as demos. Lessons typically began as a whole-class session, after which the teacher divided students into their groups. Throughout a lesson, Dr. J occasionally reconvened the class to share and demo group strategies or solutions, before closing each lesson through a whole class discussion. Within their groups, students were assigned specific roles: a code recorder who transcribed and annotated programs with a marker on the floor whiteboard surface; a tracker who traced Botley’s movement on the whiteboard and a programmer who pitched coding ideas to the group, controlled the Botley’s remote (i.e., entered the codes). Within a group, students were encouraged to collaborate and to periodically switch roles, which most groups did voluntarily and organically. The current study drew primarily on video recordings of two groups, each consisting of three second-grade students (ages 7-8) who worked together on tasks in 19 lessons that were organized into units. For example, the Shapes unit consisted of five lessons (lesson 6-10) for children to program Botley to move in differently shaped paths or to analyze programs for shapes, such as squares and rectangles. Through methods of interaction children established shared understandings during math-coding tasks. Our initial analysis attuned us to the ways 152 in which children and their teacher prioritized “novelty” as a goal of coding (Silvis et al., 2025). The research team met regularly to review videos, discuss emerging themes, and develop these themes through analytic memos. As we iteratively analyzed 20 hours of video data, our focus shifted to the tension between the slower rhythm of lessons ov er the school year and the efficient strategies children developed for devising novel solutions to computational problems.

We refined emerging themes and eventually began open coding based on recurring patterns in efficiency strategies and categories of novelty. We noticed that copying patterns of code was a primary way children created novel programs; in the Shapes and Loops units, we observed that every group utilized strategies for copying code. This led us to examine relationships between the social obligations and the math-coding objectives influencing how children copied units of code. The second author transcribed each v ideo using Sacks et al.’s (1974) transcription method, which was then collaboratively reviewed with the first author to refine themes. Both the Loops and Shapes unit s provided multiple representative examples of the focal phenomenon. We narrowed our focus to Shapes because it occurred before Loops in the curriculum progression, providing insight into the early establishment of norms for copying code. In the current analysis, we focused on classroom activities in the Shapes unit to better understand how students made sense of copying norms when creating novel coding solutions.

## Empirical findings: Children establishing copying norms

We focused analysis on two separate coding tasks in the Shapes unit  (lessons 6 and 7), both of which involved coding the robot to move in the shape of quadrilaterals. In the first task, children were asked to program the robot to travel in a square by sequencing a series of forward codes followed by a 90-degree rotation and then iterating this chunk of forwards+rotation four times (for the four sides and angles of the square). In the Program a Square (PaS) task in Lesson 6, children were encouraged to use their emerging pattern recognition skills to substitute the variable for side length by making squares of different sizes. In the second task, children were asked to program the robot to travel in a rectangle shape. In the  Program a Rectangle (PaR) task  in Lesson 7, children were encouraged to recognize the repeating patterns of sides’ lengths to iterate code for a quadrilateral (i.e., short sides 1&3 were the same as long sides 2&4; pattern ABAB).

In what follows, we closely analyze the talk-in-interaction that occurred during two key instances in the PaS and PaR tasks. We found that three forms of copying were involved in children’s pattern recognition— copying repeating patterns in quadrilaterals; copying repeating patterns of quadrilaterals; and copying a program for a quadrilateral and then changing the variable (i.e., the quantifier for side length). In what follows, we illustrate how children socially constructed the computational norms in these  two tasks by building on existing patterns to create new solutions. Through microanalysis of two episodes, we address a question that children implicitly (and sometimes explicitly asked): When is it okay to copy or repeat previous codes? Episode 1: Copying units in the PaR task Molly’s trick One day during the Shapes unit, Lexi and Molly worked as a pair on the task of programming Botley to move in a rectangle (Finn was absent). Lexi began by writing the program 5FL 2FL  (five forward, rotate 90 degrees left, two forward, rotate 90 degrees left) on their floor whiteboard to represent the first two sides of the rectangle. As Dr. J moved around the class checking in with groups, he asked Lexi and Molly what program they were trying to create; they responded that they were aiming to make a tall rectangle. When Dr. J pressed further and asked what program they thought would achieve this, Molly then quickly suggested to Lexi that they should add another 5FL 2FL program for the remaining two sides of the rectangle. Surprised by how quickly Molly arrived at this solution, Dr. J asked, “how do you know that so quick?” Molly pointed to their existing two code chunks 5FL 2FL, explaining, “because it’s right there.” Dr. J asked if Molly meant that they had essentially taken the first two chunks of code and repeated them for the last two sides of the rectangle, which Molly confirmed (Figure 1). Dr. J asked the class if they could explain how Molly knew the third and fourth chunks so quickly.

The students began offering their ideas. Some suggested that Molly had copied another group’s program from a previous demo, while others thought she had simply “remembered” what she had already written for the first two sides and repeated it. But Dr. J clarified that “that’s not what she told him.” Eventually, Evan articulated Molly’s approach. He explained, “so, since the first one and the second one are different, but the third one and the first one are the same, so she just remembered the first one and she did it on the third one” (Figure 2, lines 2-4). Dr. J emphasized that the “trick” lay in Molly’s recognition of a pattern within the rectangle program —that the code chunks for the first two sides could simply be reused for the other two sides. By noticing the repeating ABAB pattern in the structure of rectangles, Molly was able to quickly produce a new solution to the PaR task. All of the students gathered around as he pointed to the programs that different groups had written for their rectangles, which they had been recording on the board; as the PaR task continued over multiple school days, the list of solutions had grown (Figure 3). As he pointed to each rectangle program, Dr. J asked if they thought “the first chunk is the exact same as the third chunk?” and if “the second chunk the same as the fourth chunk?” Students agreed that, in each program for a rectangle, long sides are the same and short sides are the same, in terms of chunks of codes.

Dr. J reiterated that across all of the rectangle solutions, the same structure appeared: sides 1 and 3 of the rectangle matched, and sides 2 and 4 matched —an ABAB pattern. By showing the class these repeated chunks across multiple groups’ work, Dr. J highlighted every rectangle had a similar pattern and suggested to the class that these patterns in code could help them program efficiently. Shortly after the groups began working, Dr. J gathered them for a whole-class demo of Evan’s group’s program, which they wrote as FFL FFL FFL FFL (Figure 4, Left). After testing and discussing the program, he drew boxes around the four chunks of FFL to show the class the repeated pattern in the square program: the pattern of [quantifier, forward][rotation] repeated four times in the program (Figure 4).

Figure 4 Repeating two chunks representing each side of squares, and changing the quantifier Immediately following the demo, Finn, Lexi, and Molly returned to their project floorboard to work on their own square code. Finn, who had been leading the group’s programming efforts, held Botley’s remote control and told the others, “That pattern was exactly my idea.” Lexi then asked if they wanted to try “the other group’s coding idea [referring to Evan’s square program of FFL]”, to which Finn insisted that Evan’s code was “exactly his idea.” At that point, Molly suggested that their group could change the lengths of the square sides from 2 Forwards to 1 Forward (Figure 5, right). Finn agreed enthusiastically with Molly’s suggestion and replied, “We could kind of copy them but not copy-copy” (Figure 5, Line 7). Finn’s distinction between “copy-copying”, which they defined as "doing their idea” “exactly” vs. “copy-copying” a program (i.e., simply changing the quantifier to make a novel program) represents a norm children were establishing between ways they should (and should not) copy code.

And for theirs it’s three [forwards]. So, they are basically all related” (Figure 6, left). One student suggested that “you could do a few forwards then the rotation.” When Dr. J asked if anyone could clarify, Evan explained that “a few means they could do three, or four, or two.” Dr. J pressed further, asking if they meant that any number of forwards could be used, and the students confirmed this. At that point, Finn exclaimed, “ten!” (Figure 6, right). Expressing surprise at Finn’s idea, Dr. J asked the class if they thought adding ten forwards into each of the four chunks of the square would result in a larger one. The children enthusiastically agreed. Reinforcing Isaac’s rationale about the “relatedness” of square programs, Finn had applied his own earlier understanding of “copy-copying” to suggest a novel solution to the PaS task. These examples illustrate how children in one group collaboratively coded shapes, along with their whole class and supported by teaching practices that emphasized “the emergent mathematical practices of the group as they solve problems together” (Ma, 2016, p. 293). In the first example, learning how to copy code involved understanding repeating patterns within rectangles. In the second example, learning how to copy code involved understanding repeating patterns of  rectangles. In the third and fourth examples, learning how to copy code involved understanding how to change the variable for side-length, while leveraging a repeating pattern within the program for squares. Across the PaS and PaR tasks, we saw how coding shapes provided opportunities for children to engage with core integrated math-CT concepts of pattern recognition, unitizing, and iterating. We discuss each of these core concepts with respect the empirical examples. As children learn to recognize patterns in their code, they can use chunks of code for efficiency and are able to more easily solve problems. During the PaS task, Dr. J’s discussion with the class about how programs for squares were “related” emphasized th e pattern in the formula for programming a square (length-angle, lengthangle, length-angle, length-angle). Like the PaS task, programming rectangles involved learning patterns of lengths and angles. However, compared to squares, rectangles have a more com plex pattern of sides and angles. When Molly identified a “trick” for coding the rectangle “super-fast” by copying the code for the short and long sides, she demonstrated emerging knowledge of transitivity and mathematical congruence (Kamii & Clark, 1997), consolidating her knowledge of geometric patterns (Clements & Sarama, 2020). Not only do the two sides (AB) repeat within a rectangle, children also recognized a pattern across all rectangles: the ABAB pattern held for all programs on their list of different rectangles. The rectangles list itself represented how a geometric pattern applied across different programs. Once students recognized how to repeat the pattern of four sides, they could copy that formula to create many different size rectangles.

As the form of patterns children recognized shifted, so too did the grain size of the unit or “chunk” that repeated. For squares, it might seem that the salient pattern was four equal, repeating sides, each side being a unit. However, Isacc’s comment that the demo program FFFL FFFL FFFL FFFL was “related” to other programs of variable side length, suggests that even within the square pattern, students were decomposing into (1) units of code that went forward a specified number of moves (a variable code) and (2) units of code that rotated (a constant code). When children adopted the program writing convention of drawing boxes around units they perceived as chunks, it helped us understand how they were unitizing. For example, in the rectangles lesson, while two units of code (length-rotation +length-rotation) repeated to make a single rectangle, four units of code (i.e., the entire 156 rectangle program) repeated as a formula for rectangles in general. Thus, children’s different ideas about size and nature of units served as another means of learning how and when to copy code. Finally, the shapes lessons allowed children to grapple with complex concept of iteration that is a foundational concept of elementary mathematics (Lehrer, 2003; Steffe, 1994) and coding (Welch et al., 2022). Iterating does not mean imitating, as Finn made clear with this dictum that they could “kind of copy, but not copycopy" Evan's program. On the one hand, iterating required identifying and changing the value of variables in the program (i.e., changing the number of forward codes to create squares of different sizes). On the other hand, iteration involved children understanding what values or variables stayed the same across different programs. This interplay between modifying surface details while maintaining underlying structure mirrors Suchman’s (2011) concept of innovation as “artful integration.” Children’s iterations on familiar patterns reframes inventiveness not as a radical break with the past but as reusing units to create a shape as Molly had in the PaR task or iterating on the related pattern of squares as Isaac and Finn did. These “laborious reconfigurations... to familiar arrangements” (p. 15) required children recognizing familiar patterns and reconfiguring them through the work of iterating. Ironically, innovation is achieved through iteration. The students learned that the path to being recognized as an innovator is to master the community’s shared patterns and then artfully iterate upon them. Furthermore, iterating or copying code called up (for kids) questions about whether and how to copy each others’ programs. We interpret this last form of copying through a moral register. Finn’s rationale that their program should “copy” prior code but not “copy-copy” suggests this moral valence of coding: “copy-copying” someone else’s program code-for-code—without iterating  on their formula—is not allowed. Therefore, interacting with Dr. J and other groups in the whole class demo conveyed a mixed message about copying: copying is dis couraged (i.e., programs should be novel); and yet, copying is encouraged (i.e., iterating un its of codes leads to novel solutions). In these tasks, students worked through the contradictions to collectively achieve a social norm where iteration became the means of innovation.

## Implications: Broader “expressions” of copying in tech culture

The interaction order is rife with mixed messages. In everyday conversation, our words might convey one message, while our body language tells another story. In a classroom setting, the explicit rules can sometimes contradict implicit norms (DeLiema et al., 2019; Sfard, 2007). In the empirical examples we presented in this paper, children were learning to code in a sociotechnical context where cheating and copying were at odds with novelty and innovation. In other words, as they were explicitly asked to create new programs using “tricks” for copying code, children were implicitly discouraged from simply reproducing prior programs (i.e. from “copy-copying” others’ programs). In the PaR task, we observed how Molly discovered a trick for quickly creating a rectangle after coding only the first two sides and so was able to program the rest of the rectangle “super-fast.” This valuing of speed contributed to an innovation norm whereby students raced to be the first to identify new programs before other groups (Silvis et al., 2025).

As children learn to code, they are preparing to contribute to a broader culture which values innovation and speed. This reflects the dominant ideology of tech hubs like Silicon Valley, which as Suchman (2011) critically observed, imagine themselves as places where the future arrives “faster.” Tech companies encourage programmers to “move fast and break things” (Taplin, 2017). The logic of innovation fosters an environment in which newness is incentivized  but also closely guarded by copyright laws and patents. Just as in the classroom we studied, producing a novel solution quickly using tricks-of-the-trade and shortcuts is valued, but stealing ideas or cheating is forbidden. To borrow Finn’s words, copying is okay, but copy-copying is not. Furthermore, what is similar vs. what is different are both open to interpretation and mutually achieved designations. As Suchman

(2008) put it “acts of invention... involve folding histories into future-making" (p. 9).

## References & Back Matter

Finally, while Goffman (1983) instructed us not to reduce broad social structures to moment-to-moment interactions, there is a sense in which a tech ethic of innovation finds “expression” in everyday computer science classroom learning. For not only are students learning valued math and computer science skills like recognizing patterns, unitizing, decomposing, and iterating, they are also embodying the skills as normative forms of being good computer scientists or members of tech culture. This interplay between students’ coding, conduct, and copying is important to watch as teachers and designers consider how to prepare students to contribute to emerging social arrangements in tech culture.
