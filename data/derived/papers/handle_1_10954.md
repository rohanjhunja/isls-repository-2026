# Capturing Interdisciplinary Dialogic Argumentation among Science Teachers: An Analysis of Epistemic Practices in a Dialogue

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research in the learning sciences has recognized dialogic argumentation as a key approach to enhance learning. Productive features of such dialogues include openness to alternative perspectives, critical examination, and collaborative knowledge construction. Such deliberation is difficult to achieve within the compartmentalized structure of traditional science teaching, which hinders the exploration of different perspectives on a scientific problem. We propose interdisciplinarity as a means to promote deliberativeness in the discourse of science teachers from different disciplinary backgrounds. We introduce a quantitative coding instrument to examine both the dialogic and epistemic aspects of teachers' discourse and to account for deliberation through the integration of different disciplinary epistemic practices.

## Introduction

There is a growing consensus regarding the features  of academically productive educational dialogues, which include plurality of ideas, respectful and critical  examination, building on each other’s contribution s, and more (deliberative argumentation, Felton et al., 2022). Accordingly, a growing variety of quantitative coding tools have emerged to capture these kinds of dialogic interact ions in classroom discourse and examine how they ar e shaped by different preconditions or may influence different learning outcomes. Already Dewey (1916, p.139) pointed to the contrast  between learning within the boundaries of one discipline and the ability to raise doubt or engage in creative  inquiry that is related to real-life problems. We conjecture that true deliberation in science education contexts requires interdisciplinarity, combining perspective and reasoning styles from the different scientific disciplines to resolve complex problems. This idyllic aim meets an educational realm that impede such interactions. Specifically, the compartmentali zation of the scientific disciplines in school causes each discipline to encourage a univocal discourse-representing one style of reasoning and a narrow set of epistemic practices-practices related to the construction, justificatio n and evaluation of scientific knowledge (Kelly, 2016).

Let us ta ke as an example  from the school disciplines of physics and biology. Physics learning is concerned with abstract simplified systems, which can be modeled based on theoretical laws and therefore invites abstract, quantitative reasoning. In contrast, the systems investigated in school biology are more real-life and complex, inviting empirical justifications and qualitative, mechanistic reasoning (Redish & Cooke, 2013).

## Research context and questions

We relied on design principles articulated in the l iterature (Kiochu et al., 2022) for promoting deliberative argumentation through interdisciplinarity to elaborate  a professional development program for physics teachers, named Gateway to physics, which involved both in-field (IF) and out-of-field (OOF) teachers with a background in biology. Based on our design, w e expected learners to apply a wide range of disciplinary epis temic practices, integrating empirical with abstract reasoning (Perl-Nussbaum et al., 2023). Our research questions are:

1. What is the relation between group composition (i.e., homogeneous or heterogeneous groups) and the

type of discourse that evolves among teachers?

2. What is the relation between group composition and epistemic practices enacted in the discourse?

3. What is the interplay between the dialogic and the epistemic aspects of the discourse?

We hypothesize that the epistemic diversity of lear ners, together with an educational design that fost ers deliberative argumentation, would result in scientific argumentation that is both dialogic and epistemically rich.

## Methods

We recorded and transcribed the discourse 29 triads  of teachers (15 groups in the 1st argumentative activity and 14 in the second) who participated in the Gateway to physics  professional learning communities during 2021-2023. 18 groups were heterogeneous (IF and OOF teachers together) and 11 were homogeneous (only IF or OOF).

## Data analysis-coding dialogic moves and epistemic practices

To characterize the discourse among teachers (RQ1),  we relied on Felton and colleagues' (2022) coding scheme, which aims to operationalize and capture deliberati ve argumentation among learners. We reduced the number of codes to enable reliability, clarity and coherence with other existing coding tools. We ended up with 16 moves in 6 categories: a) Declarative initiations  – claims (including counter-claims); b) Questions-asking for clarification?, justification? Or other? questions; c) Elaborative responses-justification or clarification of one's claim, challenging or adding to other's claims; d) Non-elaborative responses-agreement, disagreement, or reiteration; e) Meta-dialogic statements-either framing the discussion or addressing meta aspects; and f) Aside-talking about activity management, pedagogy and professional identity, or completely off-topic talk. The 'aside'

category was added to reflect the characteristics of teachers' talk. The analysis was at the discussion-turn level, thus several codes could be assigned to a single turn.  The coding scheme was applied to characterize the t ype of discourse in the different groups. Deliberative argumentation is characterized by a higher proportion of elaborative moves: leaners are engaged in longer exchanges around each claim, and mutually construct knowledge. To account for the epistemic dimension of the dialogue (RQ2), participants' utterances were coded based on the epistemic practices they applied in their di scourse. Thus, our study joins efforts in science education research to investigate the epistemic practices  of learners in situ, in their actual learning interactions and during the course of their practical work  (Kelly, 2016).
