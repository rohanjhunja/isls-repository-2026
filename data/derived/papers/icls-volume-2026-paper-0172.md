# Evidence from NGSS-Aligned Assessments of First Graders’ Reasoning About Celestial Motion and Daylight

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how first-graders reason about celestial motion and daylight through NGSS-aligned formative assessments designed with an Evidence-Centered Design (ECD) inspired framework. We created five tasks aligned to two Earth and Space Science performance expectations to probe students’ abilities to observe, describe, and predict patterns in celestial objects and seasonal daylight. S tudent responses (N = 134) were thematically analyzed to surface common strengths and challenges. Findings indicate that many students showed strong observational abilities and emerging recognition of recurring celestial patterns, particularly when supported with scaffolds and visual representations. Students were able to make predictions, though they frequently struggled to connect celestial positions with specific times of day or seasonal changes. These results suggest that first graders are capable of more sophisticated astronomical reasoning than often assumed. The study underscores the importance of thoughtful task design for fostering and accurately capturing early science understanding.

Astronomy learning supports the development of scientific reasoning, pattern recognition, and spatial-temporal thinking from an early age  (Ampartzaki & Kalogiannakis, 2016; Plummer et al., 2016) as well as fostering an appreciation of the universe’s natural beauty (N ational Research Council [NRC], 2001). Accordingly, t he Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS) include Earth and Space Science performance expectations (PEs) for the early grades, including first grade (NGSS Lead States, 2013). These standards emphasize understanding celestial patterns and daylight variation through the integration of observa tion, prediction, and conceptual reasoning. Extensive research has documented young children’s apparent conceptual challenges regarding these astronomy concepts. For example, early elementary school students often describe the Sun’s motion in terms of sunrise and sunset or depict it as going straight up and down rather than along an  arc, and they frequently misunderstand seasonal changes and daylight (Plummer, 2009; Plummer et al., 2010; Samarapungavan et al., 1996). Much of this work, grounded in the  conceptual change and learning progression frameworks, interprets such student reasoning as evidence that young children’s  conceptions are non-scientific (Plummer & Maynard, 2014; Vosniadou & Skopeliti, 2017).

More recent research (e.g., Nobes et al., 2023; Prain & Tyler, 2024) has challenged this view, arguing that many of these studies used assessments with ambiguous or decontextualized questions that may have exaggerated young children’s conceptual challenges. Nobes et al. (2023) found that both children and professional astronomers struggled with ambiguously worded astronomy items  used in previous studies, suggesting that responses may reflect question design rather than stable conceptual challenges or misconceptions. These findings underscore the importance of assessment design. This is especially critical for early elementary students, whose stage of language, literacy, and socio-emotional skills development can influence how they express their understanding of scientific phenomena (Billman et al., 2021). Building on these insights, we developed formative assessment tasks targeting two NGSS first-grade PEs in Earth and Space Sciences that emphasize students’ ability to observe, describe, and reason about celestial motion and seasonal variations in daylight. To guide our design process, we applied the Evidence-Centered Design (ECD) framework (Mislevy & Haertel, 2006; Riconscente et al., 2015) and accounted for the development of language and literacy abilities as well as social-emotional development in first graders (Alozie et al., 2025). ECD is predicated on the idea that assessment design should begin with a clear understanding of the disciplinary knowledge and practices to be assessed and the kinds of evidence that would demonstrate student understanding. In the context of early elementary science education, this involves unpacking the NGSS PEs to identify core ideas, science and engineering practices, and crosscutting concepts, along with the language, literacy, and social emotional demands embedded in each. Following ECD principles, our earth and space science assessment tasks 1342 were (1) situated in familiar and engaging contexts that reflect students lived experiences and encourage connections to nature and the real world, (2) used everyday language and visuals to support comprehension and engagement, (3) provided multiple modes of response and scaffolding opportunities that allow young learners to demonstrate their ability to apply disciplinary ideas and practices in context through developmentally suitable formats.

## Methodology & Study Context

In this study we investigated (1) how first-grade students observe and reason about patterns in the movement of the Sun, Moon, stars, and seasonal daylight, and (2) to what extent they can use these observed patterns to make accurate and consistent predictions about celestial positions and daylight duration over time. By examining students’ reasoning through developmentally responsive, ECD-informed tasks, we test whether previously documented conceptual challenges reflect stable misconceptions about astronomy concepts or are sensitive to assessment design. This framing allows us to interpret student performance not only in terms of correctness, but also how task features shape the visibility of scientific reasoning. This study is part of a larger project focused on developing NGSS-aligned formative assessments for first-grade students, with particular attention to language and literacy development. The current study focuses specifically on two Earth and Space Science PEs: 1-ESS1-1: Use observations of the sun, moon, and stars to describe patterns that can be predicted, and 1-ESS1-2: Make observations at different times of year to relate the amount of daylight to the time of year. Following the ECD framework, we unpacked each PE into learning performances (LPs), specified associated focal knowledge, skills, and abilities, as well as mapped them to observable evidence in task design. For 1-ESS1-1, the LPs were: LP1. Use observations of the movement of the sun, moon, or stars to describe patterns of these movements, and LP2. Make predictions about the movement of the sun, moon, or stars based on these patterns. For 1-ESS1-2, the LPs were: LP1. Use observations to describe patterns of sunrise and sunset and/or the amount of daylight in relation to the time of year, and LP2. Use these observable patterns to predict the amount of daylight based on the time of year.

Figure 1 Information About the Five Tasks Developed and Used in This Study We designed five formative assessment tasks aligned to these PEs and LPs, using two formats adapted from Llosa et al. (2021): small-group and individual accountability checks. Descriptions of each task and their alignment to the LPs are presented in Figure  1. Tasks were refined  through iterative expert review, cognitive interview with students, and rubric calibration to strengthen alignment among LPs, student responses, and evaluation criteria. A description of the development process appears in Alozie et al. (2025) and Rachmatullah et al. (2025).

Across all five tasks, we collected a total of 134 student responses from two teachers. This study was conducted as a pilot focused on testing assessment design and administration procedures. The two participating teachers – who taught in two different school districts in two different U.S. states – administered the tasks within their regular science instruction at times they deemed appropriate. Because the implementation was flexible and not tied to a standardized curriculum sequence, findings should be interpreted as exploratory and design oriented. As shown in Figure  1, the number of students completing each task varied. All tasks  were administered by classroom teachers with detailed administration guides following professional development. Small-group tasks were implemented with one or two groups of three to four students per teacher  and individual check tasks were implemented with the whole class.

We collected student work samples, observer and teacher notes capturing students’ verbal reasoning and thinking during small-group tasks. Due to IRB constraints, recordings were not permitted during this pilot phase. Some sessions were observed by researchers; others relied on structured teacher notes. While supporting classroom implementation, this approach limited the granularity of discourse and implementation data. For open1343 ended responses involving writing, drawing, or teacher notes, two researchers independently scored the data using pre-developed rubrics. Inter-rater agreement ranged from 78% to 100%, and discrepancies led to rubric refinements and re-coding by consensus. The coded responses were then thematically analyzed to identify patterns of student strengths and challenges in relation to the three-dimensional learning components of each PE and LP. Students’ observations and reasoning about celestial patterns and daylight Across tasks first-grade students demonstrated strong observational skills and emerging abilities in pattern recognition. These abilities are aligned with the expectations of the PEs and are developmentally appropriate for this age group.  In contrast to earlier conceptual change accounts emphasizing persistent misconceptions, these ambiguity and provide visual scaffolds, such as images, videos, and figures. Observational Skills. For 1-ESS1-1, most students accurately observed the positions of the Sun and stars, particularly when supported by static or dynamic visual representations. Students consistently recognized that celestial objects change position over time. For example, in the Sun Motion task, after viewing a video of the Sun moving across the sky during a single day, 38 out of 52 students (73%) correctly identified the direction of the Sun’s movement. Similarly, in the Seeing Stars  task, after watching a video about constellations and arranging seasonal sky images from winter to fall, 3 out of 4 students correctly identified the direction of star movement. In the Mara Observation task, 24 out of 36 students (67%) were able to identify the changing position of the Sun at different times of day based on visual cues. For 1-ESS1-2, students also demonstrated strong observational skills in identifying sunrise and sunset times and determining how long the Sun is visible across seasons. In the Seasons task, after viewing a video of the Sun’s path from morning to night, nearly all students (89%) accurately identified when the Sun rises and sets.

Pattern Recognition. In both PEs, students showed emerging ability to recognize recurring patterns in celestial phenomena. Many were able to distinguish between the Sun’s morning, afternoon, and evening positions. In the Sun Motion task, after watching two videos showing the Sun’s path, students were asked if the Sun moved in the same direction across both videos. Forty out of 52 students (77%) answered “yes,” indicating their ability to identify a consistent directional pattern. When subsequently asked to identify the Sun’s position in the morning, afternoon, and evening, 46 out of 52 students (88%) correctly sequenced the Sun’s movement over the course of a day. In 1-ESS1-2 tasks, most students (at least 73%) recognized that summer has more daylight than w inter. Notably, no student selected the option “daylight stays the same,” suggesting a basic understanding of seasonal variation. In the Seasons  task, where students viewed a video and static daylight models, all students (100%) correctly identified the daylight pattern. However, in the Sun Moon Power task—where students were shown only static figures comparing summer and winter daylight —recognition dropped to between 38% and 73%, highlighting the role of representational supports in pattern recognition skills. Conceptual Challenges. Although many students recognized that the Sun moves across the sky, only a small portion (14 out of 52; 27%) understood that this motion is not straight up and down. Instead, it follows an arc, involving both side-to-side (horizontal) and up-and-down (ver tical) movement. This suggests that even though students can track the Sun’s position at different times, understanding the geometric pattern of its movement may remain a conceptual challenge for some students at this age, that can be reinforced in instruction. Students’ use of patterns for predicting celestial motion and daylight Across tasks aligned with LP2 of the two PEs—focusing on students’ ability to use recognized patterns to predict about the movement of the Sun, Moon, and stars, as well as daylight duration based on the time of year— many first-grade students demonstrated emerging skills in prediction. Students also faced challenges in linking specific times of day to the positions of celestial objects and in making accurate comparisons of daylight across seasons. Prediction Skills and Model Interpretation.  Students began applying their observational data to make predictions, such as where the Sun would at different times of day or which constellations might appear in certain months. In the Mara Observation  task, for instance, after reviewing a record of the Sun’s position at different times during the day, students were asked to predict where the Sun would appear the next day. Most students (22 out of 36; 61%) made reasonable predictions, recognizing that the Sun moves in a consistent direction across days. Similarly, in the Sun Moon Power task, students interpreted static figures comparing early and mid-summer days with early and mid-winter days. When asked to predict what happens to the amount of daylight from summer to winter, 46 out of 52 students (88%) correctly selected “it decreases. ” This indicates that most students could interpret visual representations to make appropriate predictions about seasonal changes in daylight. Moreover, 1344 this finding supports critiques that prior reports of misunderstanding may partly reflect how questions were posed instead of the absence of underlying pattern-based reasoning. Conceptual Challenges. Despite these strengths, many students struggled to connect celestial positions to specific times of day, which limited the precision and consistency of their predictions. In the Mara Observation task, some students relied more on prior beliefs or personal experiences rather than on patterns in the data. For instance, when asked to draw the Sun’s position throughout the day, a number of students depicted straight upand-down movement (Figure 2C), while others omitted the Sun’s position in the evening altogether (Figure 2D), even though the observation record showed a sunset. In the Sun Moon Power task, while most students identified general daylight trends, a subset (12 out of 52; 23%) showed difficulty comparing the number of daylight hours between different times of year. Additionally, about 10% of students selected “it increases,” suggesting they may have correctly identified daylight patterns but interpreted them in reverse--reasoning from winter to summer rather than summer to winter. These findings indicate that although students are beginning to use patterns for prediction, their understanding of directional changes and comparative reasoning across time is still developing. It is also possible that students might not understand what the question asks which demands further exploration. Figure 2 (A) Observational Data by Fictional Student— Mara, (B) Correct Student’s Prediction, (C) Straight Up-and-Down Movement of Sun, and (D) Omitted Sun in the Evening Our findings contribute to the literature on science teaching and learning in the early elementary grades. Given the small pilot sample and flexible implementation context, these findings are intended to inform assessment design rather than support broad generalizations about fir st grade astronomy learning.  Although children’s reasoning about celestial motion and seasons has been widely studied—often emphasizing persistent misconceptions—our results suggest that first graders’ reasoning is highly responsive to task design and representational support. Ou r study suggests that the  use of the ECD framework combined with careful consideration of developmental perspectives enabled the creation of assessment tasks that revealed important insights. Although some gaps in student understanding were identified in this work—for instance  reasoning about the movement and directionality of the Sun—these findings suggest that teachers can leverage students’ emerging strengths, such as their ability to recognize patterns, to address conceptual challenges in classroom. Doing so may help foster more scientifically accurate understanding of phenomena that were previously considered too complex for students at this age. A second contribution of this work  lies in the design features of the tasks themselves. Through the development and implementation process, we observed that certain features, such as specific visual representations, were effective in supporting student understanding, while others occasionally posed obstacles. Together, these findings extend prior work by suggesting that some documented challengers in early astronomy learning may be partly assessment-mediated. These insights underscore that assessment design does not merely measure understanding but shapes what becomes visible as evidence of understanding in early science learning and provide guidance for future task and curriculum design. 1345 Alozie, N., Rachmatullah, A., Rutstein, D., & Fried, R.
