# Reflections on Developing a Cooperative Virtual Reality Experience for a Character Education Course

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In creating an undergraduate-level virtue and character development course that implements virtual reality (VR) as a learning tool, multiple and diverse stakeholders must come together to collaborate. This paper outlines the initial project actions that we re taken to build teamwork and understanding both across disciplines as well as with the desired theme of the course. This project involves a multidisciplinary team in education, humandevelopment, instructional design, computer science, scriptwriting, and visual arts. It effectively brought experts in these disparate fields together to create a complex and thoughtful VR experience. Set in the design cycle framework, the project demonstrates how each step can be utilized for progress. Reflections on the process and how multidisciplinary teams can function effectively are shared. Purpose At Montclair State University (MSU), a new core curriculum, known as SEEDS, seeks to align courses to a character focused mission statement and set of values. A multidisciplinary team with expertise in character development, instructional design, game deve lopment, screenwriting/directing, animation, virtual reality (VR), computer science, and evaluation is developing a character education course that infuses VR experiences and meets the character-focused learning objectives set forth in the new general education curriculum. To date, there has been minimal research on the use of VR in the context of character education, yet “VR has the potential to contribute positively and significantly to the current character education movement” (Harrison, 2024, p. 235). Relying upon the Cognitive Affective Model of Immersive Learning (Makransky & Petersen, 2020) and the efficacy in which gamified interactions can promote learning and motivation (Gee, 2005), the VR experiences we are creating aim to give students meaningful and transformative character-building experiences. The particular VR experience this paper focuses on targets the potential biases young adults may have towards aging and attempts to illuminate the emotional complexities encountered in the latter stages of life. The research question explored in this paper is: what strategies and project actions can be employed to enhance the collaborative work done by a multidisciplinary team? ## Theoretical framework

This paper analyzes the multidisciplinary team and their work within the context of Paletz and Schunn’s (2010) social-cognitive framework of multidisciplinary team innovation. This framework combines an understanding of cognitive processes, such as analogi es, information search, and appropriate evaluation, with social constructs, such as formal roles and content knowledge diversity within the team. By analyzing the project’s process and activities within a social-cognitive framework, one candetermine where  and why progress was made as well as where difficulties were encountered.

Within the project, a Relational Developmental Systems meta-theoretical framework is applied to character development, emphasizing the role of individual agency in the development of character (Lerner, 2006). This project launched in September 2024 with a five month launch period to build foundational cross-disciplinary knowledge, develop a detailed theory of change for the character development course (pathway modeling), validate the theory of change against the extant literature (evidence mapping), and revise the theory of change as well as selected scenarios for the VR experiences. Project actions Throughout the project, we participated in different workshops and meetings designed to help participants understand the language, knowledge  base, and perspectives of our partners from different disciplines. These 2614 project actions enabled us to build team rapport, brainstorm ideas, and conduct research prior to the development phase of the VR experiences. • Journal club-this was a weekly meeting where the core project team (project leads, development team

leads, visual arts team leads, scriptwriter, and instructional designers) met and shared readings, presentations, and artifacts to help orient the team to their disciplinary perspective.

• Pathway modeling and evidence m apping-to develop the character education course, the project

employed Relational Systems Evaluation (Urban, Archibald, et al., 2021). Our pathway model articulates the connections between the specific activities students in our character education course will complete (e.g., reflection activities, critical analysis, VR modules) and the specific changes related to character we believe participants in the course will experience (e.g., increased empathy, critical reasoning, hu mility).

• Human flourishing workshops-these workshops were led by MSU’s Office for Faculty Excellence to

encourage personal growth of both faculty and students. This series utilized sharing of experiences and focusing on how to bring the element of human flourishing into the classroom.

• Innovation lab-this was a one-time, half-day meeting where multiple stakeholders met to brainstorm and

discuss ideas for the VR experiences. The focus of the lab encompassed the three main themes of the VR experiences: neurodiversity, technology in society, and aging. Multi-disciplinary work is not easy! During the Journal Club meetings, each expert would share their field of expertise and the group would discuss how it played a role in the project. As theorized in social cognitive frameworks, this knowledge diversity in a team could have mixed results when innovating new ideas. Communication was another identified area where mixed backgrounds and different vocabulary came into conflict. The Journal Club allowed the team to understand each of the disparate fields and their nuanced vocabulary.

Timeframes are another area of friction when it comes to working multi-disciplinarily. Thoughtful course planning and instructional design require a great amount of research and brainstorming prior to the final product. This became an area of difficulty for the creatives on the team since they were naturally ready to create and “do” something. Bringing in the artists and developers later on in the project could solve this issue in the future. Simply getting a satisfactory project out is often better than working for a long time on a perfect one. A middle ground was often found between the grandiose ideas of theorists and visionaries and the practical-minded creators who would be building the idea. However, there were also times where the creative teams would strive to create beautiful and fully functional pieces throughout the VR experience. One method used to combat this was to consistently have prototype testing during the development phase, so that artists and developers could receive feedback on what is absolutely necessary.

The process for creating this VR experience has been a long, thoughtful journey that has been a learning experience for all involved. Due to its multidisciplinary nature, we often find ourselves being exposed to a field in which we are novices.
