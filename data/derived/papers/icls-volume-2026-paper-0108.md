# Design as Relational Pedagogy: Culturally Sustaining Computing with Tibetan Girls in Diaspora

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines how design and pedagogy jointly shaped learning, belonging, and identity in a six-week virtual computing program for Tibetandiaspora youth in the United States. Guided by sociocultural and relational perspectives, we analyze two cases—Lhamo and Tenzin, Tibetan girls who designed games and websites that expressed community stories, language, and values. Using abductive thematic analysis of session recordings, artifacts, and interviews, we identify four mechanisms of design as relational pedagogy: pedagogical mediation of tools, collaborative care, storytelling as reflection, and community-oriented visioning. Design elements such as demo codes and AI chatbot became meaningful only when relationally activated through modeling, dialogue, humor, and recognition. These interactions transformed computing into a practice of cultural care —a medium for remembering, reimagining, and embracing Tibetan identity. The study advances a relational framework for culturally sustaining computing, highlighting how pedagogical and design interactions animate learning as an ethical, affective, and community-rooted practice.

Over the past decade, computing education has expanded through widespread equity initiatives. Yet research warns that broadening access alone does not guarantee justice or belonging (Ryoo, 2019; Vakil, 2018). Many youths from marginalized or diasporic communities, especially girls, still encounter computing as a technical and decontextualized subject, disconnected from their languages, cultures, and community aspirations (Kapor Center, 2021). As a result, participation gaps persist not only in enrollment but also in the deeper sense of identity, authorship, and continuity that shapes learners’ sustained engagement. This paper examines how computing can become culturally sustaining, affirming and extending the cultural and linguistic practices of communities historically excluded from technology design. Culturally sustaining pedagogy (Paris & Alim, 2017) reframes education as an act of maintaining and evolving the cultural lifeways of marginalized groups. Within computing, this vision calls for pedagogies that center identity, heritage, and community voice alongside technical skills (Scott, Sheridan, & Clark, 2015; Ryoo, 2019). However, less is known about how these commitments take form in refugee or diasporic youth contexts, and how such contexts extend and rearticulate culturally sustaining pedagogies in relation to the communities and traditions from which they emerged.

Our ongoing collaboration with Tibetandiaspora youth in the United States through community-based computer-science programs has sought to understand how disciplinary learning can sustain cultural identity and collective memory. Prior work showed that culturally sustaining computing can foster pride, agency, and resilience among Tibetan participants (Paljor et al., 2025). Building on that foundation, this paper investigates how such outcomes emerge in practice, through the interactions between pedagogy and design that shape learning for two Tibetan girls, Lhamo and Tenzin (pseudonyms), whose creative and reflective trajectories illuminate how gender, culture, and pedagogy intertwine in a virtual learning ecology. From culturally sustaining pedagogy to relational design We situate this work at the intersection of culturally sustaining computing and design-based learning within the learning sciences. Prior work in justice-centered computing education has argued that computing learning environments must not only teach technical concepts but also cultivate agency, cri tique, and belonging (Ryoo, 2019; Vakil & Ayers, 2019). The Culturally Responsive–Sustaining CS Framework (Kapor Center, 2021) further articulates three interwoven pedagogical aims: (1) affirming learners’ identities, (2) connecting learning to community and sociopolitical realities, and (3) supporting learners to imagine new, just futures. Our study builds on this tradition by examining how these aims become realized, through design as relational practice. Design-based learning perspectives have long positioned design as a generative context for conceptual understanding and creative agency (Oleson et al., 2020; Sandoval, 2014). However, when applied in culturally 963 sustaining contexts, design is not simply a method or task, it becomes a mediational structure through which relational and cultural meaning are co-constructed. We therefore conceptualize design as relational pedagogy: the idea that tools, tasks, and artifacts only become pedagogically and culturally significant when they are animated through caring, dialogic, and community-rooted relations. An empirical case: Tibetandiaspora youth and cultural continuity The Tibetandiaspora represents a global community negotiating the ongoing effects of displacement, language loss, and cultural marginalization. For Tibetan youth born or raised outside Tibet, maintaining linguistic and cultural continuity involves navigat ing between inherited traditions and digital, transnational modernities. Computing offers a powerful yet underexplored medium for this negotiation, one that can enable youth to author and circulate Tibetan stories and values in contemporary forms. Within this context, our program sought to create a learning ecology that joined technological skill-building with cultural affirmation. Through storytelling, peer collaboration, and iterative coding, youth engaged in designing games such as “Voices of Tibet,”  where characters navigated challenges rooted in Tibetan history and community narratives. The virtual environment, with its mix of live sessions, collaborative slides, and AI-mediated support, required participants and facilitators to continuously negotiate meaning across distance, language, and expertise. These negotiations became the primary site where design as relational pedagogy emerged. We ask two research questions:

1. How did pedagogy and design interact to support culturally sustaining participation among Tibetan

diaspora youth?

2. What mechanisms made design a site of relational and identity-affirming learning?

Through narrative and interactional analyses of two focal cases, we show how pedagogical mediation, modeling, dialogue, humor, recognition, and cultural framing, transformed technical structures into relational and cultural ones. By analyzing moments of co-design, problem-solving, and reflection, we illustrate how design as relational pedagogy enabled computing to become a site of belonging, creativity, and cultural care. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The next section develops the conceptual framework, situating design as relational pedagogy within traditions of culturally sustaining computing, relational pedagogy, and cultural care. We then describe the methodology, outlining the design of the virtual program, participants, data sources, and analytic approach. The findings illustrate four mechanisms through which design and pedagogy interdependently supported belonging and cultural engagement among the two focal participants. The discussion extends these insights to elaborate how relational activation operates within learning ecologies and how care functions as a pedagogical practice. Finally, we conclude by articulating the implications of this work for justiceoriented and culturally sustaining computing education.

## Conceptual framework: Design as relational pedagogy and cultural care

Justice-centered and culturally sustaining computing Equity-oriented computing education research argues that expanding participation alone cannot close opportunity gaps if computing remains disconnected from learners’ lived cultures and languages (Ryoo, 2019; Vakil & Ayers, 2019). The Culturally Responsive-Sustaining Computer Science Framework (Kapor Center, 2021) defines culturally sustaining computing (CSC) as teaching that affirms learners’ identities, engages sociopolitical realities, and enables communities to imagine just technological futures. CSC thus reframes computing from a technical subject into a cultural practice: learning to code becomes learning to narrate, critique, and sustain one’s community through computing.

Most empirical CSC studies have been grounded in classroom-based and urban U.S. contexts, particularly within Black and Latinx communities where culturally sustaining pedagogies have been extensively developed. Less is known about how culturally sustaining approaches take shape in diasporic or refugee communities, where youth navigate belonging, linguistic continuity, and displacement. Our work builds in relation to these traditions by examining how culturally sustaining commitments are taken up and reimagi ned within Tibetandiasporic contexts, viewing computing not only as a context for cultural expression but as a relational medium through which culture is cared for, reimagined, and sustained.  Such efforts are closely tied to the preservation of language, intergenerational knowledge, and collective identity, which remain central to community life shaped by histories of exile and displacement (Samphel, 2009; Central Tibetan Administration, 2001).

Relational pedagogy and the ethic of cultural care 964 Relational pedagogy places the teacher –student relationship at the moral and epistemic center of education. It draws on philosophical traditions of care (Gilligan, 1982; Noddings, 1984), ethics of the Other (Levinas, 1969), and contemporary elaborations that see knowing as emerging in-relation (Biesta, 2004; Hinsdale, 2023). Rather than viewing pedagogy as the delivery of content, relational theorists conceive learning as “coming into presence” through ethical encounter, moments of attentiveness, trust, and mutual transformation. Hinsdale (2023) describe s relational pedagogy as an educational theory of responsiveness that foregrounds caring, listening, and hesitation before action. Teachers and learners co-constitute understanding within what Biesta (2004) calls the gap, the unpredictable space between them where new meaning arises. This orientation demands attentiveness to difference and to the asymmetries of power that shape educational encounters (Margonis, 2011).

We extend this tradition through the construct of cultural care. Building on Noddings’ ethic of care yet responding to critiques of its Eurocentric and individualist assumptions (Tronto, 1993; Valenzuela, 1999), cultural care refers to relational practices that sustain the collective lifeways, languages, and values of communities through education. It links affective responsibility with cultural continuity. In contexts of exile or diaspora, caring for learners also entails caring for the survival of cultural memory and the right to self-representation. Thus, cultural care is not sentimental but political: it resists assimilationist norms and affirms the epistemic legitimacy of marginalized cultures.

Within our study, cultural care operates when instructors and youth use computing as a medium to honor Tibetan stories, language, and artefacts; when dialogue enacts respect for difference s/questions; and when pedagogical relations cultivate belonging and dignity. It bridges relational pedagogy’s ethical responsiveness with culturally sustaining computing’s sociopolitical commitments. Design as relational pedagogy Design has long been central to the learning sciences as both method and context for learning (Kolodner, 2002; Sandoval, 2014; Oleson et al., 2020). Design-based environments engage learners in iterative cycles of making, testing, and reflecting that foste r conceptual understanding and agency. However, design is often treated as a cognitive or methodological construct rather than a relational one. We argue that in justice-oriented learning, design itself becomes pedagogical through relation.

Design as relational pedagogy conceptualizes the structures, tools, and artifacts of learning, such as tasks, demo codes, or technological supports, as mediational structures that gain meaning only when animated through human relationship. A scaffolded code sample becomes a site of care when a facilitator contextualizes it through dialogue; a collaborative digital slide becomes a social space of belonging when peers use humor and affirmation to co-construct knowledge. In this view, design and pedagogy are c o-constitutive: design affords relation, and relation animates design.

This perspective extends relational pedagogy’s ethical responsiveness into the domain of learning design. It also resonates with sociocultural notions of mediation (Vygotsky, 1978; Rogoff, 1994): tools and symbols mediate participation, but the mediation is always social and culturally situated. Where traditional learning-sciences models emphasize cognitive scaffolding, relational design emphasizes relational scaffolding, the affective and ethical processes that make cognitive work possible. An integrative lens for culturally sustaining computing By integrating these traditions, we propose an interpretive framework for analyzing learning in the Tibetandiaspora computing program.

1. Culturally Sustaining Computing provides the justice-oriented goal: designing computing education that

sustains community identities and languages.

2. Relational Pedagogy provides the ethical and epistemic stance: learning occurs through responsive,

caring, and power-aware relationships.

3. Design-Based Learning provides the methodological substrate: tools and artifacts structure participation

and reflection.

Together, they form the construct of design as relational pedagogy, sustained through cultural care. This framework positions pedagogy and design not as separate layers but as interdependent forces that co-produce learning. It anticipates that culturally sustaining participation will be visible in the relational moments where design and pedagogy meet  when tools become conversational partners, when design decisions embody cultural meaning, and when learners’ identities are recognized through collaborative creation. The conceptual framework guides our analysis in two ways. First, it directs attention to pedagogy–design interactions as the unit of analysis, moments where the use of design elements (AI chatbot, demo code, visual 965 assets) intersects with relational gestures (modeling, affirmation, humor, storytelling). Second, it frames our interpretation through the lens of cultural care, asking how these moments sustain or disrupt belonging, identity, and community connection.

In doing so, we move from studying what students learned toward understanding how learning became culturally and relationally meaningful. This shift aligns with recent calls in the learning sciences to examine the affective, ethical, and sociopolitical dim ensions of learning (Nasir et al., 2020; Bang & Medin, 2010). It also repositions design research as a form of care work, an ongoing relational practice that makes justice not only a goal of education but a quality of interaction.

To further specify how design as relational pedagogy operates in practice, we introduce the concept of relational activation. Relational activation refers to the process by which designed tools or activities become pedagogically meaningful through human relationships, including care, dialogue, and shared meaning-making. From this perspective, tools such as AI chatbots, demo codes, or collaborative tasks do not inherently support learning; rather, their pedagogical significance emerges through how they are relationally mediated. In this way, relational activation provides a mechanism through which design becomes pedagogical, extending sociocultural accounts of mediation by foregrounding the ethical and affective dimensions of interaction, and highlighting how learning environments cultivate belonging, trust, and cultural me aning.

## Research design

We draw on design research traditions in the learning sciences to investigate how culturally sustaining computing emerges through design as relational pedagogy (Barab & Squire, 2004; Sandoval, 2014). Rather than iterating materials, we treat design as a mediational structure that organizes participation and meaning. Framed as a relational case study (Hinsdale, 2023; Gutiérrez & Vossoughi, 2010), our abductive analysis moved between theory and data to interpret how moments of care, design, and pedagogy became interdependent (Timmermans & Tavory, 2012).

## Context and participants

The study took place in a six-week virtual computing program aligned with the goals of Tibetan community centers in Queens (NY), Richmond (CA), and St. Paul (MN), which support youth engagement in language, culture, and community life through weekend schools and related programs  in the United States. These centers facilitated participant recruitment and coordination, reflecting shared priorities around cultural preservation and youth engagement. Twenty-two youth (ages 12– 16) participated in live Zoom sessions, meeting for three-hour sessions on alternating days in the first two weeks, followed by alternating two-hour and one-hour sessions each weekday for the remaining four weeks.

Instruction combined demonstrations with hands-on design. Students created and published websites using GitHub Pages to share stories of Tibetan identity and ancestry, and then worked in five teams to develop games using p5.js. The program also incorporated collaborative slides, a generative AI workshop, a culturally sustaining AI chatbot, and interactions with Tibetan community members, alongside activities on identity, collaboration, and engagement.

We focus on two focal participants, Lhamo (12) and Tenzin (14), whose trajectories provide analytically rich and contrasting instances of how design as relational pedagogy unfolded in practice. Data sources We analyzed ~6 hours of session recordings  featuring two participants, student artifacts (slides, code, games), pre/post interviews, and researcher fieldnotes/memos, capturing pedagogical, affective, and cultural dynamics across the design process.

Analytic approach.

1. Pedagogy–design episodes: Transcripts were segmented into episodes defined by shifts in activity or

interaction where engagement with a design element (e.g., code, tool, asset) intersected with relational interaction (e.g., dialogue, modeling, recognition).

2. Reflexive Thematic coding: Using an abductive approach, we iteratively moved between data and theory

to cluster episodes into mechanisms (e.g., animating design through pedagogy; building relational infrastructure; embedding reflection/storying identity). 966

3. Narrative cases: W e constructed  interpretive cases for Lhamo  and Tenzin, integrating discourse,

artifacts, and interviews to show how design and relational care shaped participation. Primary coding was conducted by the first author and refined through collaborative discussions with a doctoral research group and co-authors. This approach is consistent with sociocultural, design-based ethnographic traditions examining meaning-making in practice (Gutiérrez & Vossoughi, 2010). Our analysis examined how design and pedagogy interacted to support learning, participation, and belonging for two girls, identifying four recurring mechanisms through which design as relational pedagogy operated: Animating design through pedagogical mediation Design features such as scaffolded demo codes, the AI chatbot, and milestone tasks provided structure but only became meaningful when paired with pedagogical support. Across cases, students described that these scaffolds “worked” when instructors modeled or contextualized them rather than when used in isolation. During one session, Lhamo’s team worked on implementing game transitions between scenes. When the AI chatbot suggested creating a variable to track levels, the instructor drew on an example from a demo code and explained, “Think of it like a box that holds things, your character’s image is inside that box.” Lhamo replied, “Oh, so it can keep track of each level, now it makes sense!”. This dialogue shows how conceptual understanding emerged through relational scaffolding; the instructor’s explanation translated abstract syntax into accessible, embodied meaning.

In interviews, students emphasized that the same design features only supported learning when mediated in this way. Lhamo said she was able to recall demo codes and “remember the errors [she] faced” with help from the instructor and the AI chatbot. Similarly, Tenzin  explained how she found the useful ways to get the help the AI chatbot, “When I asked the chatbot, it didn’t just give me the answer. It shows the code but also explains it, which made it easier to understand.” Together, these accounts show that it was not the tools themselves that supported learning but how they were framed, modeled, and revisited through pedagogical interaction. Instructor mediation turned design elements, AI scaffolds, demo codes, and project milestones, into relational learning supports that encouraged understanding and agency rather than simple replication. Building relational infrastructure through collaboration Collaboration functioned as a designed structure of belonging. While all teams worked collaboratively, it played especially transformative roles in fostering social persistence and relational co nfidence, particularly for Tenzin. Tenzin described herself as shy and “not really good at socializing but working in a team helped me. I felt good talking about CS with others since I don’t have someone to talk to about it normally.” Early sessions showed her relying mainly on the chat, but over time she began unmuting, sharing her screen, and joking with teammates. A pivotal moment came when the instructor mistook one of the team’s drawings for an internet image: Peer: “It was drawn by Tenzin!” Instructor: “Really? Embedding reflection and storytelling into the design cycle Structured reflection and storytelling embedded cultural meaning into technical design, positioning identity work as part of learning rather than as an external add-on.

Reflection activities, such as the identity-pie chart and weekly forms, prompted students to connect their computing work with who they were becoming as learners and community members. Lhamo  explained that her team’s story centered on a Tibetan boy who leaves Tibet but creates a website so “Tibet would never be forgotten.” She described how these reflections helped her realize that “language and culture are important, even in games ”, their game emphasized remembering Tibet and sustaining Tibetan language and culture across distance and wants to build more interesting games with her brother.

Similarly, reflection in Tenzin ’s team focused on fun  ways of engaging community expression. Describing the Escape Game, she said, “We made an escape game where you go through funny puzzles and doors in the mountains, it’s like escaping to freedom.” She added, “Games can show who we are too, they don’t have to be serious to mean something.”  Figure 1b while evidently shows the visual graphics developed by Tenzin  as discussed in the previous theme, this is also an important representation of sociocultural identity her team associated while sharing the escape of Tibetans through the Himalayas into Freedom in India and beyond. These reflections demonstrate how storytelling functioned as cultural mediation, linking design choices to collective memory and identity. For both participants, coding became a medium for  remembering and expressing Tibetan culture through digital narrative.

In addition to individual reflection and storytelling, the program’s milestone structure intentionally connected students’ creative work to their broader community contexts. Each group prepared short presentations articulating the Tibetan values, aspirations, and sociocultural or political realities their projects addressed, such as language preservation, collective memory, or everyday resilience. These presentations invited youth to consider what knowledge about their community their games could convey and to whom. The program culminated in a virtual demo day attended by parents and community members, where students presented their games and reflections in both English and Tibetan. Through these moments, computing was situated within a living space of community dialogue, allowing participants to reimagine what it could mean to engage in computing as a collective, culturally grounded practice.

Scaffolding future-oriented trajectories linking computing and community By the program’s conclusion, both participants articulated future trajectories connecting computing to cultural continuity and community service. Tenzin envisioned “writing stories in Tibetan and making websites for children since there aren’t many Tibetan books.” Her reflection marks a shift from learning technical skills to seeing computing as cultural care, a means to preserve and circulate heritage knowledge.  Lhamo described wanting “to make more games with my friends, Tibetan-style games with funny stories.” Her focus on collaborative creation frames computing as a shared, expressive community practice. Projects were later shared with families in an online presentation, giving participants an opportunity to explain their work in their own words. These reflections illustrate how relational design and cultural belonging intertwined to sustain participation and identity beyond the program. computing Together, these four mechanisms show that culturally sustaining computing was not achieved by curriculum or tool design alone, but by the relational activation of design, how instructors and peers transformed structures into spaces of care, recognition, and cultural continuity.

Our findings deepen the concept of design as relational pedagogy by showing how designed structures (tools, prompts, collaborative tasks) became pedagogical only when animated through interaction. Across both teams, instructional modeling, dialogue, and mo ments of humor translated abstract computing structures into 968 personally and culturally meaningful practice. For Lhamo, instructor mediation, such as using metaphors (“an array is like a box that holds items like friends”), made invisible code logic tangible, linking computational reasoning with familiar relational forms. For Tenzin, instructor affirmation and peer interactions, including humor and recognition, transformed design collaboration into a context for confidence and belonging. In this sense, design was not a fixed curriculum but a mediational ecology, its pedagogical value emerged through relationships. This extends sociocultural learning theory (Vygotsky, 1978; Rogoff, 2003) by emphasizing ethical and affective mediation.  Where sociocultural theory conceptualizes tools as mediators of cognitive development, our data suggest that tools also mediate relational development, they become meaningful through how care, humor, and recognition are enacted around them. This perspective also nuances the “design as research” tradition in the learning sciences (Sandoval, 2014; Barab & Squire, 2004). Rather than focusing on iterative refinement of materials, we frame design as a relational enactment that unfolds moment to moment. Each design element, AI chatbot, demo code, collaborative slide, only functioned pedagogically when activated through human relation. Thus, the study advances a model in which design and pedagogy are co-constitutive: pedagogy gives design its moral and cultural purpose, while design provides the material scaffolds through which pedagogy is realized. Cultural care as a pedagogical practice The instructors’ responsiveness, peers’ humor and social space, and collaborative recognition (“It was drawn by Tenzin!”) were not incidental affective gestures; they functioned as relational infrastructures that sustained participation and identity.

Drawing on relational pedagogy (Hinsdale, 2023; Noddings, 1984; Biesta, 2004), care here can be understood as attentiveness to learners’ subjectivities and cultural worlds.  Yet, our analysis extends this framework through what we call cultural care, pedagogical actions that sustain community identity and belonging, not only individual well-being. Cultural care was visible in two interdependent forms:

• Conceptual care, where the instructor’s modeling and patient explanation helped students connect

technical learning with meaning (e.g., Lhamo’s debugging and reasoning through level transitions).

• Social care, where peer engagement/recognition and humor made participation safe and joyful (e.g.,

Tenzin’s growing comfort and creative contribution within her team). Both forms coexisted within an ethics of responsiveness. In a diasporic context, where youth navigate multiple cultural worlds, such relational gestures allowed computing to become a site of recognition and continuity. For Tibetan youth, this meant engaging computing as a way to sustain language, storytelling, and shared identity in alignment with broader community efforts toward cultural preservation. These findings refine culturally sustaining pedagogy (Paris & Alim, 2017; Ryoo, 2019) by showing that sustaining culture involves not only integrating heritage content but also designing relationships that embody care for that culture and its people. In this sense, computing functioned not only as a site of learning but as a medium for the youth to engage language, storytelling, and identity in ways aligned with community goals. By creating artifacts rooted in heritage narratives, participants positioned computing as a means of sustaining and reimagining cultural practices in diaspora, supporting identity preservation and intergenerational connection. Justice-oriented computing This work directly answers calls within Computing Education Research to document how equity-and justiceoriented pedagogies are enacted in situ (Ryoo, 2019; Vakil & Ayers, 2019). Ryoo’s ethnography of the Exploring Computer Science program demonstrated that equitable computing depends not only on access but on pedagogical mediation that connects content to student s’ lives and communities.  Our study extends this insight by making visible how design and pedagogy intertwine to enact such mediation in virtual and culturally sustaining contexts. The findings show that justice in computing education cannot be reduced to  mere representation of cultural artefacts but requires deeper engagements with community practices. Equity emerged through relational processes, through how tools were framed and how learners were recognized, and how learning structures made belonging possible.

For example, when the instructor and peers celebrated Tenzin’s artistic contribution, this act of recognition redefined competence and belonging in computing: creativity and humor became as valued as technical skill. Similarly, when Lhamo’s instructor grounded code in relational metaphors, the lesson connected computing to social meaning rather than abstract notions. By tracing such moments, this study situates culturally sustaining computing within a relational theory of justice: equity is enacted not only through redistributing opportunity but through reconfiguring relationships, 969 who is heard, affirmed, and cared for in the learning process. This relational turn complements structural critiques of computing inequity by illustrating how justice is lived and felt within micro-interactions of pedagogy and design.

Relational activation within the learning ecology Sociocultural theories emphasize that learning occurs through mediation by tools and participation in activity systems (Wertsch, 1991; Rogoff, 2003). Building on this perspective, our findings show  how these interactions activated learning within the  ecology. Across cases, moments of instructor modeling, peer interactions such as humor, and dialogic support transformed students’ engagements with tools such as AI chatbots, demo codes, and collaborative tasks into opportunities for sense-making, participation, and cultural expression. These interactions highlight how relational scaffolding, the ethical and affective work of instructors  and peers, functioned as a key dimension of design, shaping not only conceptual understanding but also learners’ sense of belonging and cultural connection.

This challenges the field’s tendency to equate “well-designed” with “technically scaffolded,” suggesting instead that the effectiveness of design depends on how it is relationally enacted in practice.

## Pedagogical and design implications

The findings translate into practical guidance for educators, designers, and researchers seeking to build culturally sustaining and justice-oriented computing programs.

1. Animate design through pedagogy: Treat tools and scaffolds as resources  that will be mediated through

pedagogy. AI tools, code examples, or project milestones acquire value only when modeled and contextualized through dialogue and care. Design should anticipate opportunities for instructors to model reasoning, connect content to lived experience, and make abstract logic humanly comprehensible.

2. Build relational infrastructure: Design collaboration not simply as teamwork but as a structure of

belonging. Include social check-ins, peer recognition, and moments of humor that humanize technical work. In virtual contexts, deliberate relational design (e.g., encouraging mutual validation or peer-led tasks) sustains motivation and confidence.

3. Embed reflection and storytelling: Integrate reflective prompts and narrative framing throughout design

cycles. When learners articulate the cultural and personal meaning of their projects, computing becomes a medium for identity expression rather than mere production.

4. Scaffold future-oriented trajectories: Create opportunities for learners to imagine how computing can

serve their communities. Encouraging future visioning transforms technical skill development into civic and cultural commitment.

These principles show that culturally sustaining computing design must account for relational affordances, the ways structures invite dialogue, care, and community connection.

## Limitations and future research

As a qualitative, small-scale case study, we prioritized depth over generalization. Findings derive from two focal participants and approximately six hours of session data, illustrating mechanisms rather than universal patterns. Because the first author served as both facilitator and researcher, relational proximity offered insight into classroom nuance while introducing potential interpretive bias. To address this, we drew on multiple data sources and collaborative analysis to support triangulation and reflexive interpretation. Future research could examine how design as relational pedagogy operates across diverse diasporic contexts, in-person and hybrid settings, and longitudinal programs. Further work could also explore AI as a relational mediator, including how machine-generated scaffolds shape cultural care. This study shows that culturally sustaining computing emerges not from content alone but from relational design practice, the interplay of pedagogy, care, and community.  For Lhamo, instructor mediation turned code into narrative reasoning; for Tenzin, collaboration turned participation into belonging.  Through these relational processes, computing became a medium of cultural care, an avenue for remembering, representing, and imagining Tibetan identity in digital form.

Design as relational pedagogy redefines what it means to teach and learn computing: to design is to relate; to relate is to sustain culture. When pedagogy and design intertwine through care, computing becomes not only a site of skill development but a practice of belonging and cultural continuity, an act of learning that remembers and reimagines community futures.
