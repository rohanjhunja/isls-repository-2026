# Competing to See

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how an experienced mathematics teacher organized and disciplined a student’s perception during a lesson that incorporated data science practices. As data science gains national attention, this research addresses the complexities teachers face in integrating data practices into mathematics curricula. Drawing from a multiyear design-based study, the analysis focuses on classroom interactions during a “Barbie Bungee” lesson, highlighting competing disciplinary goals of mathematics and data science. Using an interactionist perspective and the construct of disciplined perception, this paper illuminates the breakdowns and interactional work involved in aligning student understanding with mathematical concepts like slope while grappling with noisy, real-world data. This case underscores the need to address competing disciplinary practices in classroom contexts, offering insights into fostering interdisciplinary engagement and reshaping data science integration in mathematics education.

## Introduction

Data has become an integral part of our society and culture – from the constant creation of data on mobile devices to the everyday use of data in news coverage. The newfound ubiquity of data opens up opportunities for relevant, meaningful, and empowering c ontextual mathematics engagement using data and dat a analysis practices (Rosenberg et al., 2022) and has prompted calls to modernize mathematics curricula (Boaler & Levitt, 2 019). Furthermore, the dynamic and ever changing nature o f data necessitates more formalized development of data practices in mathematics education. By data practic es, I mean the necessary skills and abilities to ac cess, make sense of, interpret, critique, represent, and ethically use data. While there is enthusiasm for integrating data science practices into math education (e.g. Bargagliotti et  al., 2021; NCTM, 2024; The California Mathematics Framework, 2023), there is little guidance for teachers to integrate data practices in school mathematics contexts. In light of these complexities, this study responds  to calls to integrate data practices into mathemat ics curricula while shedding light on the complexities mathematics teachers face as they learn to incorpor ate more data practices into their instruction.  Policymakers and curriculum writers can push for ne w content in the mathematics curriculum, but ultimately, teachers’ understandings of mathematics, students, learning, and teaching will shape how that content comes to life in the classroom (Horn, 2005). For this reason, this study is relevant and timely, as data science education is garnering national attention (NCTM, 2024) and federal funding efforts (Data Science and Literacy Act of 2023), yet teachers’ sensemaking around data science education has not been centered in these discussions. This exploratory analysis inv estigates how an experienced mathematics teacher or ganized and disciplined a student’s perception during a mat hematics lesson that incorporated data science prac tices. Drawing from a larger d esign based study exploring the ways experienced se condary mathematics teachers’ designed and implemented data science lessons in th eir Algebra 1 and Algebra 2 classes, this paper pre sents an exploratory case of disciplined perception in the c ontext of a m athematics class, illuminating the complex interactional work of integrating data into mathema tics teaching and learning as well as interrogating underlying competing disciplinary goals.

## Theoretical perspectives

This analysis follows an interactionist perspective, emphasizing socially organized activities as coor dinated, dynamic systems (Greeno, 1998) and conceptualizing activity as fundamentally social and situated in pa rticular social and material contexts (Jord an & Henderson, 1995). Furthermore, there are patte rns of situated activity or practices in a community, and learning, then, is a change in practice or participation in community practices (Lave, 1996; Lave & Wenger, 1991). This study is particularly concerned with the organization of activity in a classroom context, in which people engage in or coordinate so cial and technical practices together (Hall & Stevens, 2015). As the larger study aims to explore how experienced  teachers integrate data practices in their teachin g, an interactionist perspective allows for inquiry into what is the work of doing and learning mathematics as it is mediated through data practices and in social and material interaction. This study is also concerned with the interplay bet ween disciplinary practices of mathematics and data science and technoscientific tools and practices. T herefore, I also take up the construct of disciplin ed perception (Stevens & Hall, 1998). Disciplined perception is c oncerned with visual interaction, or seeing, and seeing in particular and coordinated ways. According to Steve ns and Hall (1998), seeing is socially accomplished  through interactions between people, material displays, and  contexts. Disciplined perception is a deliberate p ractice of seeing in ways that are organized and relevant to a partic ular discipline. That is to say, disciplines carry specific visual and interpretative practices, and disciplined perception is a “performance” of “specific forms of embodied action” (Stevens & Hall, 1998, p. 108). Considering a classroom context, then, thi s analysis explores the interactional work of a teacher to (re)organize the way a student is visually orienting to data display. In this way, disciplined perception is inherently asymmetric in which one interactant works to reorganize another ineractan’s ways of seeing the world. Stevens and Hall (1998) a rgue that perception is socially constructed and co nstituted, and as interactants work to  organize their seeing and sensemaking, they can be in alignment or they m ight face contention in which breakdowns or disruptions occur. Breakdowns happen when routine practices are  disrupted, and these ruptures can occur between tasks, tools, and people. Hall and Stevens found that these breakdowns were significant moments for teaching and learning.

To situate these moments of breakdown and negotiati on, I also draw on previous research on integrated STEM education. Integrated STEM refers to an effort  to intentionally combine concepts and practices fr om multiple STEM disciplines (Roehrig et al., 2021)  – making conceptual connections, solving problems, and engaging in inquiry (Kelley & Knowles, 2016). Now, integrating STEM disciplines involves more t han simply co-locating practices; it requires coordination and co herence across concepts, tools, and practices (Nathan et al., 2013). At the same time, integrative work is epistemolog ically fraught. Despite apparent overlap (e.g. a mathematical perspective of slope versus a data scientific perspective of slope), different disciplines have distinct ways of knowing, reasoning, and justif ying claims (Lehrer & Schauble, 2021). When disciplinary practices are uncritically brought into contact, they “may tug in different directions” (Lehrer & Schauble, 2021, p. 24), creating epistemological tensions that must be negotiated. This is especially precarious for school contexts, where teachers not only must navigate epistemological tensions as well as institutional pressures and structures (Coburn, 2001). This study is concerned with integrating disciplina ry practices of data science and mathematics. While both disciplines make use of similar representation s and concepts (e.g., scatter plots, linear functio ns, slope), it stands to reason that mathematical and data scienti fic orientations to potentially shared practices di ffer. Mathematics tends to emphasize formalized procedures, abstract generalizations, and deterministic relationships, privileging clean structures and deductive reasonin g (Boston & Smith,  2009; Louie, 2017). Data science, by contrast, embraces variability, uncertainty, and co ntext as inherent features of real-world data (Lee et al., 2022; Wild & Pfannkuch, 1999). Reasoning in data science often requires navigating ambiguity, drawing on per sonal or contextual knowledge, and interpreting complex or “messy” datasets. Importantly, these disciplinary contrasts suggest that activity that seeks to integrate these  disciplines will likely encounter breakdowns (Stevens & Hall, 1998). Lee and Wilkerson (2018) found that teachers often experience tension when teaching data science because its open-ended, uncertain nature contrasts sharply with the more procedural, deterministic norms of school mathematics. Similarly, Vieyra and Himmelsbach (202 2) highlight how teachers navigating novel discipli nary terrain often encounter breakdowns not just in student understanding but in their own pedagogical framing. These studies suggest that disciplinary integration can c reate a kind of “epistemic friction,” where f amiliar teaching routines and pedagogical orientations can be destab ilized. In this way, breakdowns are not only moment s where student perception could be reorganized, but also where teachers must actively negotiate disciplinary boundaries.

## Study context and data sources

This analysis is a part of a larger, multiyear desi gn based research study (Philippakos et al., 2021),  collaborating with experienced mathematics teachers who are part of a well-regarded professional development organization (PDO) with an explicit commitme nt to ambitious and equitable mathematics teaching.  The PDO supports teachers’ professional growth through teacher fellowship programs which include stipends, monthly professional development, and conference travel support. Based o n teachers’ interest in and reported need for profe ssional development and classroom resources related to data  science, I worked with four teachers to co-design and implement Algebra 1 and 2 lessons that incorporate data science practices, content, and tools. To furt her support teachers in this work, I facilitated a monthly PD working group. The primary data for the larger study come from (1)  audio recordings of lesson planning meetings and conversations (2) fieldnotes and video-recorded classroom observations of teachers enactin g data lessons, (3) fieldnotes, transcripts, and video-recordings of monthly working group conversations. This analys is focuses on one lesson, with one teacher whom I call Bridgette Campbell (1). The data sources for this analysis are a one hour working group meeting where we workshopped the less on for one of the teachers (about a month before Ms. Campbell taught the lesson), two planning meetings where Ms. Campbell and I met to design the lesson (one over zoom and one in her room during her planning period), multi-perspective classroom video of the classroom lesson (POV and a student camera), and an audio-recording of a debriefing conversation Ms. Campbell  and I had after she taught day 2 of the lesson in a different class period (summarized in Figure 1 below). Illustrative case This analysis explores one lesson, taught by Bridgette Campbell in her Algebra 1 course. Ms. Campbell had been teaching for over ten years. She taught at Katherin e Johnson High School, a public high school in a la rge urbandistrict in the Western United St ates. Johnson High school was located in a communit y that experienced a significant amount of violence. As a way to support  the surrounding community and combat localized vio lence, Johnson was designated as a community school that p rovided wrap-around services and supported and fostered partnerships between the school, local community, and local agencies. The school was committed to social justice and restorative practices and community and collabo ration. For Ms. Campbell, she contributed to the sc hool mission by holding “high standards and giving them the support, whatever they need to meet their needs to get to that expectation.” She wanted to integrate data sci ence to move beyond “preparing [students] for the m ath,” but “to give them skills for the future.” Ms. Campbell wanted her students to have access to rich mathematical learning and social mobility: “my root of it all is I want them to be in a better space wherever they are in their lives.”  This case was selected as an exploratory case to explore  int eractional dimensions in a mathematics lesson that integrated data practices (of which there are few e mpirical examples). I selected this particular less on and classroom episode for two reasons. First, this episode was a longer interaction (around four minutes). Second, this lesson felt particularly fraught in terms of the content and balancing the data reasoning work with the mathematical content. This was felt during data collection, as I  worked with Ms. Campbell to design and refine the lesson multiple times, and was  noticeable in the classroom data. Ms. Campbell was notably intentional in trying to get students to a particular place, or to see something particular compared to her other lessons or other teachers. This episode offers a fruitful case to ex plore processes of disciplining perception in class room interactions and to explore the coordination and interplay of different disciplines of data science and mathematics. Focal lesson: Barbie Bungee The focal lesson for this analysis occurred in early October of 2023. This lesson was an introduction to slope and y-intercept and was the first data lesson that Ms. Ca mpbell and I designed together. We expanded a well-known lesson called “Barbie Bungee” (e.g. Barbie Bungee, 2012). The lesson occurred over two class session s, which were 75 to 90 minutes long. During the first class,  students collected data, working in groups. They a ttached rubber bands to Barbie’s feet, dropped Barbie while  holding the end of rubber band(s), and measured th e lowest point that Barbie reached. Students would then add another rubber band and complete another drop. They continued until they had measured Barbie’s drops for five to eight rubber bands. Students recorded their data in a Google Spreadsheet. In the second lesson, students used Jupyter Notebooks, an open source, web-based application that supports R programming language. Students ran a variety of code cells to read their data from the Google Spreadsheet, create a scatter plo t, graph prediction points, and plot a linear model. The second lesson focused on two primary learning goals: predicting how far Barbie will drop with a given number of rubber bands and finding and interpreting the slope of a linear model. Articulating a vision of the lesson Initially, I proposed adapting the Barbie Bungee lesson to provide students with “an experiential way for them to think about [slope]” (Planning Meeting 1). I had al so suggested combining student groups’ data as a wa y to introduce noise into the data set, making it larger  and messier (as opposed to each group simply looki ng at only the data they recorded from their drops) and allowi ng for conversation around variation and error in t he process of collecting data (see Figure 2 below).

## Findings: disciplining Link’s prediction

This four minute classroom episode centers an inter action between Ms. Campbell and one student, Link. Their conversation occurred as Ms. Campbell facilitated whole class discussion. She randomly called on Link and asked him to share his prediction for how far Barbie woul d drop with ten rubber bands (extrapolating beyond the data students collected). Now, consider what was available to Link to make his prediction. First, he had the scatter plot displayed in the Jupyter notebook. Now, the visualization was actually rather complex, especially when compared to a single group’s data (or experience collecting the data; refer to Figure 2A). There is noise that somewhat obscures the relatively linear relationship between  the distance Barbie dropped and the number of  rubber bands stemming aggregating all groups’ data points, creat ing stacks of points and of different densities. Ac ross the scatter plot, there is variation within the stacks – that groups unsurprisingly did not measure the same distance for their Barbie given the same number of rubber bands. This could b e for a variety of reasons – inaccuracy in their measurements, dropping Barbies of different sizes o r weights, or dropping with different levels of for ce. Across the stacks, there is increasing variation – the stacks get progressively taller and more spread out. This might make the data look less linear to students. There is not additional coding of the points either. For example, the data could have been color coded by group, which might have made the linearity of the data more visible. At the same time, Link had his experience of dropping Barbies as a resource as well, where he  and his group created longer and longer chains of rubber bands, and Barbie dropped f urther and further, even requiring them to stand on  chairs to have enough room to drop Barbie without her hitting the floor. In response to Ms. Campbell’s question, Link shared, “well, since we saw the last chart and it went lower, I think it would go around like a hundred twenty.” Perhaps surprisingly, Link described a decrease in distance. Looking at the scatter plot, there are indeed two points at eight rubber bands that are lower than some of the points at five, six, and seven rubber bands. Ms. Campbell did not press about Link’s proposition that “it wen t lower,” and demonstrated how to edit the code in the Jupyter notebook to plot Link’s prediction, projecting the notebook on the front white board of the classroom (Figure 4 A). As their conversation unfolded, Ms. Campbell wo rked to discipline Link’s prediction, and he eventually revised his prediction (Figure 4B). ## Discussion

This analysis reveals how competing disciplinary go als shaped the interaction between Link and Ms. Cam pbell during the Barbie Bungee lesson. I described a brea kdown between Link and Ms. Campbell, but the breakd own was not just between the two interactants – there was also a breakdown between the underlying d isciplinary practices of mathematics and data science. While Li nk’s reasoning drew on the noise and variability of  the data set, aligning with the disciplinary work of data sc ience, his understanding and  interpretation of the scatter plot conflicted with the conceptualization of slope as M s. Campbell intended. Consequently, Ms. Campbell en gaged in significant interactional work to discipline his  perception toward her intended, mathematical under standing of slope. In doing so, she emphasized a linear relatio nship between the number of rubber bands and the di stance Barbie dropped, and undermined the nonlinear behavior of the data that Link observed, thus making his reasoning irrelevant. This moment exemplifi es what Stevens and Hall (1998) described as a brea kdown in perception – a disruption that surfaces the tacit assumptions about what counts as appropriate disciplinary seeing. The breakdown revealed deeper epistemic dissonance between discip lines: Link’s perceptual orientation was entirely r easonable from a data science perspective, but was misaligned with Ms. Campbell’s mathematical orientation. As Link and Ms. Campbell navigated these fraught disciplinary boundaries, Ms. Campbell used a variety of discursive moves of varying strength. She displa yed his predicted point and asked him to evaluate h is prediction, then she invited him to elaborate, and finally, she created a discursive contrast between his proposition (that the distance will begin to decrease after eig ht rubber bands) and the experiment from the previo us lesson. Link resisted these presses, committing to his prop osition. Ultimately, Ms. Campbell explicitly declar ed his proposition to be untrue, using the experiment as evidence, and directed him to revise his prediction to be higher. Through these moves, Ms. Campbell disciplined Link’ s perception (and the rest of the students who were watching their interaction unfold) about what it means to make predictions based on data. Initially, Ms. Campbell positioned the task in an open, even subjective way  – Link could guess or he might have an opinion or cho ice. However, it became increasingly clear that there were certain necessary conditions or evidence for the prediction. Data-based predictions must be close to the pattern, and  in this particular case, aligned with the experien ce of dropping Barbie farther and farther with each addit ional rubber band. Now, Link’s purported decrease w as close to a pattern – following the extreme values. The data had a strong  linear relationship (with an R 2 value of 0.8), however the noise made the covariation and slope ha rder to see, and made it possible for Link  to propose a nonlinear relationship. However, Ms. Campbell did not address the noise, she relied on the experiential covariation from the experiment. In doing so, she privileged physical intuition over representational complexity – a move that disciplined not only Link’s perception of the graph but also what counted as a legitimate way to reason with data in this setting.

Considering the differing disciplinary practices at play here, there was a disconnect for Link between the scatter plot and the experience of dropping Barbie.  Ms. Campbell responded to this disconnect by essen tially abandoning the graph, and directing Link’s attention to the experiment. Importantly, data science, as a discipline entails a process of asking questions; collecting, organizing, and considering data; analyzing, modeli ng, synthesizing, and interpreting; and communicating (LaMar & Boaler, 2021). D ata science work requires communication, storytelling, and collaboration, and  data scientists value creativity, curiosity, skept icism, and efficiency (Lee et al., 2022). Data science require s different and more interpretative and sophisticat ed data practices to make sense of complex data (Jiang & Kahn, 202 0; Wild & Pfannkuch, 1999). Bringing data science into classroom contexts presents an opportunity to expand beyond narrow and traditional mathematical disciplinary practices and ways of knowing that dom inate school mathematics (Louie, 2017). However, in  this interaction, the openness and possibility available  in data scientific work (Metts, 2024) became inter actionally irrelevant and untenable, displaced by the mathemat ical task at hand. This case, then, ra ises important questions about what is required to make data science visible and viable in classroom contexts: what must be reorganized – not just in students’ perceptions, but in teachers’  pedagogical approaches and instructional resources ? In this interaction, the space for epistemic ambiguity collapsed as Ms. Campbell worked to center a particular interpretation of slope. This echoes concerns raise d by Lee and Wilkerson (2018), who found that teach ers often revert to familiar disciplinary norms when confronted with the complexity and ambiguity inherent in data science. While Ms. Campbell’s moves were grounded in her mat hematical commitments and goals, they came at the c ost of suppressing potentially productive and rich data scientific engagement. It is possible to reimagine the activity in a way that might foster more interdisciplinary engagement and ultimately reshape disciplined perception in a mathematics classroom context. To foster deeper engagement with data science practices, visual scaffol ds like color-coding by group could help students better see and interpret patterns in noisy data, especially with explicit attention to locating covariation in the graph. Encouraging students to explore multiple lines and compare steepness mig ht support r easoning about variability while still building toward slope. Such scaffolds would not eliminate am biguity, but could help students navigate it more productively, preserving more exploratory data scie nce engagement while still supporting mathematical generalization. Ultimately, this case underscores the ne ed to consider how competing disciplinary practices  are coordinated – or contested – in classroom contexts and illustrates the complexities of integrating data science into mathematics instruction. It also  foregrounds the critical role of teachers in naviga ting those complexities – how they manage epistemic tensions, interpret student t hinking, and decide which disciplinary norms to uph old. This case contributes to a growing body of work that foregrounds the deeply epistemic work of teaching, showing how integrating data science into mathematics instruction not only demands new tools and tasks, but alsonew ways of seeing, valuing, and responding to student reasoning.
