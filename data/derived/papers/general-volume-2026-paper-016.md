# Supporting Classroom Orchestration and Collaborative Programming with Jupyter Analytics

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Jupyter notebooks have been widely adopted in STEM courses that have programming or data science integrated. Jupyter Analytics, a learning analytics system, has been built to provide teachers with visibility into student activity. In combination with its existing functions as an awareness and reflection tool, this demo illustrates how the new code-sharing feature can address the real needs of both instructors and learners within Jupyter notebooks. Specifically, we discuss its potential value in two use cases: (1) supporting teachers in orchestrating live-coding lectures, and (2) helping students merge and synchronize work in collaborative programming. This covers two common pedagogical contexts in higher education: teacher-led lectures and student-centered active learning environments with group projects or collaborative sessions.

The integration of learning analytics (LA) into higher education promises actionable insight for instructors and timely support for learners. According to the Society for Learning Analytics Research (SoLAR, 2025), LA is “the collection, analysis, interpretation and communication of data about learners and their learning that provides theoretically relevant and actionable insights to enhance learning and teaching” (p. 1). The first step of data collection makes the learning process observable in ways that promote reflection and informed action. Taking awareness tools as an example, they can support learners in creating group awareness in collaborative tasks to regulate their work (Janssen et al., 2011); instructors can use them to monitor engagement, spot bottlenecks, and intervene at multiple levels (i.e., individual, group, class) (Holstein, et al., 2018; Alavi et al., 2009), leading to effective classroom orchestration (Dillenbourg, 2013).

In parallel, data science and programming have become more accessible and widely integrated into STEM curricula. Among the platforms and tools in use, computational notebooks like Jupyter notebooks stand out for their web-based versatility and ease of use as interactive textbooks, virtual demonstrations, and more. This convergence has motivated efforts to embed LA directly into Jupyter ecosystems. For example, Jupyter Analytics (Cai et al., 2025) provides an end-to-end toolkit that manages the entire data pipeline—from learner activity to teacher insights—covering data collection, storage, preprocessing, and visualization. JELAI (Valle Torre et al.,

2025) extends the idea by coupling fine-grained LA with LLM-based tutoring inside Jupyter, enabling context-aware support and research on student-AI interactions.

Despite these advances, live teaching with notebooks still exposes two gaps. First, live code sharing during a session is inefficient. In practice, instructors push updates via Git or learning-platform links and students pull them—workable for asynchronous distribution but ill-suited to live lecturing where the class needs the latest cell immediately. Second, real-time co-editing in standard Jupyter requires the jupyter-collaboration extension and typically grants collaborators broad access to the same server environment; while commercial platforms (e.g., Deepnote, Databricks) offer polished co-authoring, adopting them would require migration effort and costs that many existing courses cannot accommodate.

To address these needs, we extend Jupyter Analytics with a lightweight code-sharing channel that leverages its existing architecture. Instructors can broadcast code updates instantly to all online students; students can share updates within their assigned groups—without granting full workspace access or requiring a platform switch. This design preserves current Jupyter-based workflows, keeps orchestration within the instructor’s dashboard, and adds group-level sharing aligned with collaborative learning scenarios. Tool Overview: Jupyter Analytics As shown in Figure 1, Jupyter Analytics comprises of a containerized Flask backend and two JupyterLab extensions, Telemetry on the student side, and Dashboard on the teacher side. Two use cases on classroom orchestration and collaborative learning have been reported, justifying its values in ecological learning environments. Previously, its prototype version has also been demonstrated in the 14th Learning Analytics and K nowledge (LAK) Conference, showcasing the key features that can be used to monitor both synchronous and asynchronous learning activities (Cai et al., 2024).

The Student Location dashboard integrated into the notebook’s table of contents displays each student’s current location in real time, allowing the teacher to decide when to push updates on the spot. By right-clicking the code cell in Part 2, the teacher sees the “Push the Selected Cell” option at the bottom of the menu and can use it to send the update to all online students. In addition, this feature can be enhanced by enabling a plugin adapted from RISE—a JupyterLab extension that renders a notebook as a slideshow—allowing the teacher to share updates while in presentation mode as well. Pedagogical scenarios In STEM contexts, while students do write code in certain sections, the primary goal is not programming proficiency but rather immediate, tangible feedback on abstract ideas. Our tool is designed accordingly and supports a range of classroom orchestration patterns. Below, we describe how instructors can leverage the codesharing and awareness features to facilitate different teaching activities with Jupyter notebooks. Adapting to Pedagogical Goals. Instructors can adapt their code-sharing behavior depending on the activity at hand. When the goal is for students to observe and understand a concept, the instructor pushes complete code cells sequentially; students execute each cell and immediately see the output, allowing them to connect the result to the concept being discussed. When the goal is for students to engage more actively, the instructor pushes incomplete code with guiding hints, inviting students to complete portions of the code, modify parameters, or test hypotheses before running the cell. Although this may involve writing code, the focus remains on understanding system behavior rather than programming syntax.

Transitioning Between Activities. A typical orchestration cycle might proceed as follows. The instructor begins by pushing cells as students observe results that reinforce the lecture material. At a suitable breakpoint, the instructor pauses and prompts student participation, for example, by giving students five minutes to complete a function or vary inputs. During this phase, students experiment with the concepts by manipulating code, adjusting parameters, or both. To reconvene, the instructor pushes a reference solution and leads a discussion on the results and their conceptual implications.

Monitoring, Intervention, and Synchronization. The awareness dashboard allows instructors to monitor student progress without interruption. If the dashboard indicates that students are struggling, the instructor can push a corrective snippet to resolve technical issues quickly. This is particularly valuable in STEM settings, where debugging candistract from the lesson. At the end of each section, instructors can push the complete code to e nsure all students have identical, working notebooks. This synchronization ensures consistent outputs across the class, allowing students to reflect on the material rather than troubleshooting code discrepancies. Interactive demo and technological setup As part of this demo, we will present the core features of Jupyter Analytics, and its distinct interaction flows for teachers and students. Moving beyond the functional examples shown in Figures 2 and 3, participants will engage with a pedagogically grounded Jupyter notebook featuring a realistic classroom scenario—one that situates the need for classroom awareness and orchestration.

Hands-on Experience: Attendees will experience the system firsthand by taking on the role of either “student” or “teacher”, working through the notebook and interacting with other participants in real time. To facilitate this, participants will use their own laptops to access our university-hosted JupyterHub via temporary test accounts.

Research & Collaborative Design: We will share preliminary results from real classroom settings to demonstrate the research and practical significance of Jupyter Analytics, particularly regarding its code-sharing capabilities. We will also present outcomes from our participatory design process. Finally, we will invite participants to critique the tool’s design and collaboratively explore new ideas for its application in both research and practice.

## Implications and future work

While Jupyter Analytics has been implemented in several university courses for exercise sessions, the new release reported in this paper expands its application to live lectures. Instructors can now synchronize their code with the entire class while demonstrating a workflow. This new capability is complemented by the awareness features (e.g., students’ locations or their error distribution) from the original design, which can help instructors gauge student progress and inform their decision-making (e.g., when to synchronize). Moreover, this combination of features also benefits students’ collaborative learning by enabling code sharing within the same platform. Finally, in both scenarios, instructors can benefit from the data exporting feature, which enables them to reflect on their teaching practices and engage in the teacher inquiry loop. Future studies will focus on collecting in situ feedback from both teachers and students to iterate on the tool's design. Additionally, we plan to conduct comparative analyses across multiple sessions for both the lecture and collaborative settings, exploring how the code-sharing feature impacts interaction dynamics within each context.
