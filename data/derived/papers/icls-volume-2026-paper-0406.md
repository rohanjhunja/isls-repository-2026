# Digital Technologies and Philosophy Teaching: Indications of the Need for an Epistemological Turn

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines the epistemic role of digital technologies in Philosophy teacher education beyond instrumental integration. Using Design-Based Research, we conducted an exploratory cycle involving interviews, institutional document analysis, and cartog raphic mapping. Findings reveal a dominant textual orientation that constrains digital experimentation and reinforces traditional academic legitimation. The study identifies tensions between philosophizing and teaching practice, informing the design of interventions aimed at fostering non-instrumental uses of digital technologies.

The expansion of digital technologies has reshaped daily life and consolidated cyberculture as a transformation that is not only behavioral but also cognitive, marked by non-linear, collaborative, and virtualized modes of thought (Lévy, 1996, 1998, 2000).

In the 2020s, the initial enthusiasm gave way to concerns: studies point to cognitive impacts of excessive screen exposure (Desmurget, 2021), analyses criticize the performative logic of social networks (Han, 2022), and the diffusion of generative AI requires a critical understanding of its modes of standardization and data processing (Vicari et al., 2023). In this context, reflection on technologies must move from the instrumental to the conceptual plane. Philosophy offers decisive resources: ethics guides  action, and the creation of concepts (a function proper to philosophy (Deleuze & Guattari, 2010)) sustains the production of meaning. This study investigates the integration of digital technologies in the training of Philosophy teachers at a Brazilian university. We ask how to structure a training process that enables the creation of original pedagogical proposals using digital technologies, overcoming instrumental use and promoting critical practice. Although technology integration has been widely discussed in the CA literature (e.g., TPACK, transformative use of technology, authentic learning design), these approaches tend to focus on pedagogical effectiveness rather than on epistemological transformations of disciplinary thinking. We base our work on Nóvoa's (1992) threefold vision (life, profession, and school) and treat technologies as interfaces of thought (Lévy, 1995), whose mobilization can imply an epistemological shift in teacher training.

The research adopts Design-Based Research (DBR) (Hoadley & Campos, 2022), structured in rationale, conjectures, iteration, and reflection. The study will produce two articulated results: a formative proposal for undergraduate students in Philosophy and design principles derived from the process (Fahd et al., 2021), organized into three cycles.

(1) Exploratory cycle: We mapped the formative context through interviews with a trainer, two students,

and two graduates, in addition to the analysis of the policy guidelines. The production and analysis of the data followed a cartographic approach (Passos & Barros, 2015; Deleuze & Guattari, 2011). The interviews were transcribed and reorganized into narrative maps that articulate recurring statements about digital technologies, emerging tensions, and possible lines of flight, understood as displacements in teacher subjectivation. The analytical focus fell on relationships and inflections in the discourse, making visible the political, ethical, and aesthetic dimensions of the phenomenon.

(2)–(3) Implementation cycles: Based on the tensions identified, a training workshop will be developed using conjecture maps, which connect epistemological assumptions to provisional didactic decisions. Two successive implementations will allow for iterative refinement. The data will include field diaries, participant productions, questionnaires, and interviews. The analysis will maintain the cartographic approach, seeking evidence of shifts in the ways in which the undergraduates problematize. In the end, we will synthesize the results, refine the proposal, and explain the design principles and limitations of the study.

Research status 2647 We are at the beginning of the second iteration. So far, we have cartographically analyzed the CPP and co-creator interviews, tracing forces that shape subjectivation processes at the site. Tensions were identified when institutional statements produced hierarchical or divergent formative orientations; lines of flight were recognized when practices or discourses introduced shifts in this dominant arrangement. Philosophizing versus teaching philosophy The CPP presents philosophy as a profound engagement with the human condition and as epistemological support across fields. It frames teaching along two dimensions: knowing philosophy and thinking philosophically, affirming that teaching should involve reflection on knowledge itself. However, other passages subordinate teaching to academic knowledge production. Emphasis on methodological preparation and didactic transposition suggests that mastering curricular content and transferring it pedagogically suffices for teaching. Although practice may generate knowledge, academic philosophy appears as its ultimate validator, creating a tension between philosophizing and teaching philosophy. Philosophical technologies The CPP privileges text as the primary intellectual technology. Assessment formats and learning practices remain text-centered, while digital technologies appear mainly as support tools or complements to in-person instruction. The recurrent coupling of textual practices with academic legitimation operates as a dominant formative vector, defining what counts as valid philosophical work.

Interviews reinforce this pattern. Participants struggle to imagine alternatives to traditional, text-centered teaching, though some explicitly call for rupture and greater technological integration. Together, these elements suggest that the program cultivates subjectivities aligned with textual logics, distancing philosophy teaching from everyday digital experience.

Lines of flight The analysis identified three lines of flight that challenge this arrangement. First, individual teacher educators assume responsibility for integrating alternative technological forms. Second, potential integration between distance and on-campus programs may reconfigure curricular dynamics. Third, experiential knowledge developed in initiatives such as PIBID and the Pedagogical Residency introduces problems from school contexts that exceed purely textual mediation.

These movements open possibilities for reconfiguring teacher education beyond the program’s dominant logic, even if they remain partial and context-bound.

Preliminary results indicate the need for an epistemological shift in the training of philosophy teachers in the context studied. The mapped tensions suggest redefining what it means to teach philosophy and reviewing the intellectual technologies mobilized  by the practice, considering that technologies shape thought and expand abstraction (Ong, 1998; Lévy, 1995). It is not about rejecting tradition, but about opening space for new philosophical experiences, including those made possible by devices such as hypertext. These issues will guide the next codesign cycles. The training proposal will be built from the knowledge produced, paying attention to the resistances identified and the absence of consolidated institutional initiatives for technological integration. The challenge is to design a process aligned with local conditions and capable of promoting a non-instrumental use of technologies, repositioning the teaching of philosophy in the face of digital transformations.

Although situated, the analysis allows for cautious generalization. If philosophy is defined by the creation of concepts and if all fields operate conceptually through their instruments, the findings can inform other training areas.
