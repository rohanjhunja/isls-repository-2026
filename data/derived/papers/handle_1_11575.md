# Equity in the Making: Opportunities and Barriers for Productive Engagement in a Primary School Makerspace

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
School makerspaces are claimed to represent accessible learning environments. Concurrently, concerns are being raised about whether makerspaces serve all students on equal grounds. This study uses nexus analysis to examine social practices that define productive engagement in a school’s makerspace from an equity perspective. Over a year, video interactions of 94 fourth to sixth graders were documented in a makerspace in a primary school in Finland. Preliminary findings highlight three key social practices that were pivotal for productive engagement in the makerspace, thus affecting students’ equal engagement in making: Students’ agency in making, becoming co-directed, and engaging in active knowledge creation. We illustrate these through two case students with attention to situated examples about the opportunities and challenges for productive engagement in the makerspace. The results advocate for tailored pedagogical design strategies to ensure equity in the making.

## Study purposes and theoretical perspectives

In school makerspaces, students work on personal pr ojects, often combining digital and analog material s, and working across arts, crafts, science, and technolog y disciplines. These learning environments hold potential to enhance students’ developing so-called 21st century skills, deemed essential for ac ting in today’s society (Fields et al., 2018; Peppler et al., 2023). Further, earlier research indicates that learning-by-making can promote equitable learning opportunities for students regardless of g ender (Buchholz et al., 2014; Keune et al., 2019), ethnic background (Peppler et al., 2020), or disabilities (Peppler & Warschauer, 2011). Despite the widely acknowledged benefits of makerspace s, concerns have been raised about whether school makerspaces serve all students on equal grounds (Leskinen et al., 2021, 2022; Saarinen, 2020). Moreover, the pedagogical design of makerspaces in mediating equity has been vastly overlooked in previous resea rch (Vossoughi et al., 2016). To address this gap in cu rrent knowledge, this study investigates how the pe dagogical design of a primary school’s makerspace can create opportunities and/or barriers for students’ product ive engagement in making. By pedagogical design we refer to assemblages of people, social interacti on, and digital and analog artifacts (see also Bjørn et al., 2022) that shape students’ making activities. We define equity as students’ equitable access to making (Fields et al., 2018), and examine it as a socially, culturally, and materially mediated process shaped by interaction and associated social practices (Benjamin, 2003; Vygotsky & Cole, 1978). We ask:

1. Which social practices define productive engagement in making in the school makerspace?

2. How do socially and materially mediated social practices in the makerspace support and/or challenge

students’ access to productive engagement in making

## Study design, data sources, and methods

Our paper presents an ethnographic case study in a school makerspace known as the FUSE Studio. The FUS E Studio is a makerspace model that combines digital tools (such as computers and 3D printers) with anal og materials (including foam rubber, electronics, and textiles) for the students to use. A key design  principle of the FUSE Studio is the central role of students in desi gning and executing design projects, and teachers f acilitating the students’ work and promoting peer-to-peer learning. The primary data consists of 111 hours of video recordi ngs of the activities of 94 students and their teachers, collected over one academic year. Purposely selected portions of the video data were analyzed u sing Atlas.ti. Our analysis focuse d on video data of two students with contrasting leve ls of engagement and success. Consequently, these cases offer valuable insights into the social practices conducive to productive engagement in the making from an equity perspective. The first ca se highli ghts social and material conditions that created opportunities for the students’ productive engageme nt, whereas the second case demonstrates challenges  in productive engagement.

Following the principles  of nexus analysis, we traced interactions between the participants and  the makerspace learning environment, assessing (1) the social practices that emerged in the FUSE Studio and (2) the students’, teachers’, and the environment’s role in supporting and/or challenging students’ engagement in making. By the term social practices, we refer to interactional processes ongoing in nature, regardless of spatial or temporal gaps (Green & Bridges, 2018). Further, we viewed the social practices as parts of nexuse s of practice-that is, clusters of routine practices signaling valued ways of participating in the makerspace (Sco llon, 2001), posing opportunities and barriers for productive engagemen t in making activities (see also Sparks & Butterwic k, 2004). Inspired by Wohlwend & Kargin (2013), our analysis was layered in four distinct but interconnected lay ers of practice within  (1) The relational space, i.e. interaction between part icipants, (2) The physical space, i.e. classroom/hallways, tangible materials, (3) The digital space, i.e. the FUSE Studio and associa ted digital platforms, (4) The discursive space, i.e. the ideological space that justifies valued practices and available modes.

## Results

In response to our first research question, our preliminary results reveal three distinct social practices that mediated productive engagement in the FUSE Studio: (1) stude nts’ agentic involvement in design projects, (2) su pporting the students’ co-direction of maker activities, and (3) fostering active knowledge creation across digital and social funds of knowledge. These practices were primarily formed across relational and digital layers of the learning environment, with the physical space also playing a  mediating role. These practices established and hi ghlighted available modes valued ways of working within the FUSE Studio, and thus had consequences for students’ (in)equitable opportunities to engage in making.

Students’ agency in making, was founded on one of the core design principles of the FUSE Studio: student choice. This social practice encouraged the  students to take a proactive role in designing and  executing design projects. It also motivated hem to use peer resources and digital and analog materi als to plan projects and related learning processes. The second social practice, becoming co-directed, involved peer-to-peer learning, collaboration, and networking while working on proj ects. The third pedagogical practice, engaging in active knowledge creation, focused on  acquiring essential knowledge across the digital and physical realms of the makerspace while participating in making activities.

Our first case student, Anni, is a fifth-grade student. She seemed to thrive in the makerspa ce learning environment, as working independently, gathering in formation from various sources, and connecting with teachers and peers came naturally to her while work ing on a computational textile project. Moreover, she  shared her expertise, supported and collaborated with her peers. The other case student, Samuel, is a sixth-grade student who speaks Finnish as a second language. He encountered several difficulties in the makerspace environment. He struggled to take an agentic role in making, had trouble finding interesting topics for projects, and faced challenges in completing any design projects. Additionally, he had difficulties engaging in social practices conducive to codirected learning, often struggling to meet the exp ectations of his teachers and peers, and frequently  becoming involved in disputes. His struggles with basic lite racy skills further hindered his ability to access relevant information.

## Discussion and conclusions

The social practices found in our study seemed to assume that the students had specific skills—such as basic and digital literacy skills, resource management skills, and the ability to take charge of their projects —which in turn mediated the students’ opportunities for productive  engagement in the FUSE Studio on equal grounds.  Further, our in-depth analysis of the two student cases  revealed that the students’ opportunities and strug gles to meet the expectations of the FUSE Studio makerspace had cons equences for their engagement, including learning opportunities and motivation to pursue design proje cts. Our study makes visible how the interaction between the students and their teachers (the relational space),  situated within the FUSE Studio learning environme nt (the physical and digital spaces), collectively mediated  productive engagement in the makerspace. These rel ational and material spaces mediated equity in the making activities, creating both opportunities and barriers for Anni’s and Samuel’s engagement.

Our study addresses the call to enhance research understanding of how opportunities to learn by making unfold in makerspaces (Ryoo & Calabrese Barton, 201 8). In this respect, the sociocultural theoretical approach adopted for our analysis highlights the interplay between individuals and th eir socio-cultural environment in constructing (in)equity in makerspaces (see also Es pinoza & Vossoughi, 2014). The results underscore s ocially and culturally valued ways of creating, communicati ng, and negotiating content, enabling (or challengi ng) the students’ opportunities to thrive in the makerspace (Wohlwend  & Kargin, 2013). Our results call for further investigation, and perhaps a reconsideration of ped agogical design of makerspace learning environments  to promote student equity.
