# Analyzing Human–AI Interaction Patterns in Generative-AI-Supported Inquiry-Based Learning

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study contributes to computer-supported collaborative learning research by examining human– artificial intelligence (AI) dialogue patterns across teams with different experience levels with generative AI (GAI) -supported inquiry-based learning. Epistemi c network analysis of dialogues from 26 teams reveals that teams using GAI support for the second time exhibit projective actions toward their goals through active questioning and concept clarification. In contrast, inexperienced teams rely on GA I scaffolding with passive responses. Notably, goal-oriented questions emerge as critical mediators connecting different question types among first-time user teams.

Inquiry-based learning is a prominent theme in the learning sciences and computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL). Edelson et al. (1999) identified key learning opportunities, including developing inquiry skills and deepening scientific understanding. Established CSCL environments  such as the Knowledge Forum (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2022) and WISE (Linn et al., 2003) have engaged students in scientific inquiry. Subsequent work has addressed assessment (Gobert et al., 2013) and questioning skills (Hakkarainen & Sintonen, 2002). However, these studies have predominantly focused on inquiry in science education. Keifert and Stevens

(2018) broadened this scope, using ethnographic analysis to show that inquiries in daily life consist of three

elements: orienting to inquiry, progressing through meaning-making resources, and orienting to closure. Japanese national education standards (MEXT, 2017a, 2017b, 2018) emphasize collaborative inquirybased learning from elementary school through high school, structuring the process into four phases: problem identification, information gathering, organization and analysis, and summary and expression. This framework integrates both Keifert and Stevens’s (2018) model of question emergence in inquiries in daily life and Edelson et al.’s (1999) model of general inquiry skill development. At the high school level, problem identification relates to student identities and life goals (MEXT, 2018), requiring support to formulate meaningful questions, which Scardamalia and Bereiter (1992) classified as knowledge-based questions rooted in deep personal interests. However, supporting the formulation of such questions remains a significant challenge in practice for high school teachers. As of 2025, 230 selected “super science high schools (SSHs)” had implemented inquiry-based research courses (JST, 2025), requiring teachers to manage approximately 10 teams with varying themes. This instructional challenge demands adaptive support, suggesting the potential of generative artificial intelligence (GAI) -based interactive assistance (Borge et al., 2024; Kasneci et al., 2023). GAI-supported learning is advancing rapidly in both practice and research, with growing attention being devoted to human –AI collaboration. Ojeda-Ramirez et al. (2025) suggested that student reflection prompts a critical awareness of AI’s role in collaborative creative tasks. Ohsaki and Matsukawa (2025) proposed quantitative and visual analyses of AI–student dialogues in inquiry-based learning, demonstrating that commercial GAI often provides excessive support. However, limited attention has been paid to the  relationship between learner agency in inquiry and autonomous AI use, despite CSCL research on knowledge creation highlighting shared epistemic agency within teams (Damşa et al., 2010; Scardamalia, 2002). Accordingly, this study examines how human– AI interaction patterns vary across teams with different experience levels with GAI-supported inquiry to inform CSCL design in the AI era. Specifically, we address the following question: How do human– AI interaction patterns differ between first-time and second-time GAI user teams?

The GAI tool used in this study was developed through iterative co-design with experienced teachers and guided by five design principles: operational simplicity, automatic logging, explicit mode switching, institutional data storage (Google Workspace), and step-by-step questioning. Structurally, the tool is designed for one computer per team to facilitate group discussion and consists of two modes. The inquiry support mode engages students in adaptive dialogue using the 5W1H framework, drawing on active listening principles (Rogers, 1957) to facilitate 788 student-driven inquiry, rather than providing answers directly. The upper-grade research exploration mode enables students to identify upper-grade presentations for their inquiries. Both modes run on either Claude 3.5 Sonnet (Anthropic, 2024) or GPT-4.1 (OpenAI, 2025), and teachers selected the model for their class. Data were collected during a single lesson in a high school research project course (SSH designation), where 268 students across 87 teams explored self-generated questions. Among the 87 teams, 69 used an AI support tool, with 17 having prior experience. A survey conducted a week later yielded 199 valid responses from 221 students across 85 teams. Each team’s responses were summarized using median scores from three five-point Likert items: two measures of GAI support tool effectiveness (research theme specif ication and motivational impact) and a measure of overall satisfaction. Based on the responses, teams with two or more respondents and a median score of at least four were selected (n = 39) to ensure meaningful engagement with the GAI-assisted tool, reflecting high perceived effectiveness and satisfaction. Among the 26 teams with usable log data, five were second-time users (Group A), and 21 were first-time users (Group B). We employed epistemic network analysis (ENA; Shaffer, 2017) to compare interaction patterns between Groups A and B, coding utterances using a 21-code scheme. Eighteen codes were adapted from Graesser and Person (1994). Codes 1 to 10 correspond to basic questions: Verification (yes/no), Disjunctive (either/or options), Concept Completion (CC; who/what), Feature Specification (FS; characteristics), Quantification (quantities), Definition, Comparison, Example, Interpretation, and Judgmental (value-based). Codes 11 to 16 correspond to deeper questions: Antecedent (causes), Consequence (hypot hetical outcomes), Goal Orientation (GO; motives), Enablement (means), Instrumental/Procedural (IP; alternative methods), and Expectational (why expected events did not occur). Codes 17 and 18 contained non-question utterances: Assertion (expressing lack of knowledge) and Request/Directive (RD: requesting actions). Codes 19 to 21 were developed to classify student responses: Choice (selecting from given options), Single-Word Response (SWR), and Full Sentence Answer (FSA). Speaker identity was encoded by appending “_GAI” and “_S” suffixes to each code. These coded units were then entered into ENA, configured with Group and Team as the analysis units, a moving stanza window of four, and nodes arranged on a unit circle.

The ENA results reveal distinct human –AI interaction patterns between first-time and second-time GAI user teams (Figure 1). Stronger connections indicate more frequent co-occurrences of coded lines. The results suggest that collaboration patterns with GAI may vary according to team experience with GAI-supported inquiry and that GAI has the potential to compensate for such differences dynamically. Group A (blue) demonstrated active engagement, as students posed questions and made requests to the GAI (Verific ation_S, CC_S, RD_S) and provided full-sentence responses (FSA_S), indicating clearer goal recognition and knowledge-based questioning (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 1992). In contrast, Group B (red) exhibited more passive patterns, providing SWRs (SWR_S) or selecting GAI-provided options (Choice_S). Furthermore, the results reveal that GAI’s verbalization of student motivation  (GO_GAI), corresponding to the “why” question in the 5W1H framework, serves an important bridging function when inquiry goals are unclear, consistent with Goal Orientation as a deep question (Graesser & Person, 1994). Additionally, although not a planned feature, the GAI may have adjusted scaffolding intensity in response to student inputs, suggesting its potential for tailored support in o pen-ended inquiry.
