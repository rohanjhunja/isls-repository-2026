## Introduction

Educators, learners, and designers of learning experiences must make critical decisions not only about the what, why, to what ends and for whom of learning (Philip et al., 2018), but also when, where and at what pace. Space and time demand consideration not only as objective  dimensions of learning and design unfolding in the  real world, but also as subjective perceptions and culturally constructed understandings of our experience that pattern activity. Indeed, different assumptions about and perceptions of learning rate, space and time influence our very understanding of what counts as good, effective, diverse, equitable, inclusive and just learning and design. Many vexing and familiar tensions and failures in designing for equity, for instance, can be traced to differences in the speed with which equity is pursued, or to lack of attention to when and where learners are put into interaction for more equitable outcomes. As a field, the learning sciences must continue to develop new ways of seeing, analyzing and responding to the implications of space and time in learning both in and through design. This symposium is organized around the general conj ecture that by more explicitly and systematically considering the time-space dimensions of learning and design processes, we can more reliably surface differences in the way we understand learning as well as urgent  inequities that result from how it is conceptualiz ed and designed for, thereby helping us make progress towards more just educational futures.

## Overview of Structure and Content

Collectively, this symposium shows progress that one group of learning scientists, assembled in the International Society of the Learning Sciences (ISLS) 2021 Early Career Workshop, has made in bringing careful atten tion, useful theory and systematic approaches to understa nding equity issues related to space and time, whil e also working out ways to attend to these issues in our temporally and spatially distributed design processes. In the first two papers, design processes themselves are examined in terms of the “narratives” that pattern innovation and the “trajectories” of experience and identity development that play out over the course of design. These p ieces deal with how the dynamic and distributed arrangements o f educational design unfold in ways that prioritize  some “protagonists” over others, for instance, and act immediately and explicitly on some goals and measures of equity while tacitly deferring others. The third and fourt h papers focus on spatial-temporal dynamics within specific designed learning environments. These papers deal with how individual learners move across and link virtual and real spaces in different configurations at different moments. The studies bring attention to how learners assemble and disassemble in spatial-temporal contexts and em phasize the importance of attending to these dynami cs in designing for inclusive and equitable experiences.

The symposium organizers will engage ISLS participa nts in a discussion of paradigmatic, methodological and practical perspectives that help us grapple with critical questions like the following: (1) Where ICLS2022 Proceedings 1755 © ISLS and when is equity being foregrounded and prioritiz ed through learning and design? (2) How does design  for equity at one rate, scale or place reinforce, disrupt, or stand in tension with its pursuit at others? (3) How do we make abstract notions of space and time more technical and tractable for the design of contemporary learning in, for instance, virtual and online spaces? (4) And, h ow do we work across different, culturally-and con textuallyspecific understandings and experiences of space-time for more equitable and just outcomes? Time, Space and Equity in EdTech Crisis-Innovation Narratives Adam Papendieck, University of Texas at Austin While technology in education is often taken up in the work of designing for more equitable and just f utures, scholarship on educational innovation has shown how  public narratives and broadly shared assumptions a bout school and schooling tend to pattern technological integration in ultimate service of status quo goals, beneficiaries and ways of doing things (Cuban, 2013; Sims, 2017; Tyack & Tobin, 1994). Designing for substantive technology innovation in education, in other words, requires values innovation. In this study, the case of the EdTech Network, an urban innovation hub, is examined to show how the very spatial and temporal configurations of the stories that these innovators tell about educational change reflect and pattern how they organize and design for more equitable futures.

This study draws upon the concept of chronotope—Bakhtin’s (1981) way of theorizing how space-time works in novels—to examine narratives of crisis and  innovation told by ed-tech innovators. Space and t ime are treated not (just) as separate, objective dimension s of an independent real world, but more importantl y, as interdependent sociocultural constructions that pat tern human experience (Ritella et al., 2016), narra tive sensemaking (Boje, 2011; Bruner, 1986; Weick, 1995) and the organization of work and learning (Orr, 1996). For the purposes of this study, technology is treated as a social-material process of developm ent suspended among different possibilities (Feenberg, 2004). Technolog y innovation is an abductive (Cross, 1999) process of organizing for specific possible futures, and critical innovation is a contradiction-driven material-discursive process (Foot, 2002; Miettinen, 1999) that both critiques and works to repattern a persistent, unjust, inequitable orthodoxy.

## Method

This analysis was undertaken as part of a larger mu ltimodal narrative case study of the pseudonymous E dTech Network, an open, urban innovation network that mee ts online and in a downtown coworking space called TechAssembly. Overall, the approach involved collec ting and comparing different stories of innovation work from teachers, entrepreneurs, educators, administrators, tech professionals in the EdTech Network. A s nowball sample and high-level structural representation of the EdTech Network and its various subnetworks was developed using a name-generator instrument, system atic interviewing and standard community detection algorithms for network analysis. Employing a minima l definition of narrative as a passage from one sta te of equilibrium to another (Czarniawska, 1998), crisis-innovation narratives  were identified in the sample of interviews, abstracted and compared. A crisis-innovation narrative was defined as a genre of innovation story that combines (1) some disruptive event or transition th at results in a state of educational crisis (i.e., narrative disequilibrium) with (2) some kind of innovative or abductive move or plan to resolve the crisis (i.e., achieve an equilibrium). These narratives were then examined w ith particular attention to how their chronotopes p atterned and reflected the logic, pace, practice, protagonists and ends of equity-and justice-oriented changemaking.

## Findings

Two general chronotopes organized many of the crisis-innovation narratives reviewed in this study. First, crisisinnovation narratives patterned by an institutional chronotope played out relatively gradually, within and across separate but linked and interdependent organizational spaces. Innovation is both supported and constrained by a complex network of persistent, hierarchical, bureaucratic actors and stakeholders. One inter-institutional group of computer science education champions, for instance,  relayed stories of how their work addressed a cris is of “coding skills” (interview transcript) by gradually collaborating across schools, educational institutions, and state and federal funding agencies to develop new curricula, teacher preparation programs, and support networks. The innovational end—that is, the resolution of the crisis and establis hment of a new, more equitable equilibrium where coding skills are taught and learned system-w ide—was seen and acted upon as a decades-long story  of concerted, coordinating planning and action in rela tively compartmentalized domains by often isolated actors, including individual teachers in classrooms and educational researchers in universities. The abductive  logic of these narratives promised impact through collective, collaborative, and coordinated action of a relatively complex set of stakeholders.

ICLS2022 Proceedings 1756 © ISLS Other crisis-innovation narratives were patterned by a very different understanding of and distributio n across space and time, a market chronotope. Within this second chronotope, innovation stories  play out more quickly as innovators from different environments “rub shoulders” (interview transcript) in a densely populated, frenetic market, working opportunistically around the TechAssembly innovation hub, their social-material “center of gravity” (artifact text). One entrepreneur, for instance, “pitched” (interview transcript) a story to other innovators at TechAssembly about how virtual reality tools could be deployed to immediately address cr ises of equitable access to quality curriculum, rapidly sca ling virtual project-based learning experiences to a broad network (and market) of schools. The urgency and speed of innovation that characterized stories in this chronotope seems to be related at least in part to the way tha t innovators experience and value space and time it self in the urban market. Unlike the institutional chronotope, the market chronotope is a shifting, hectic, precar ious, and commodified space-time, within which innovators mus t organize quickly and efficiently to demonstrate b oth valuable progress on equity goals as well as market  value. The abductive logic of an effective crisis-innovation narrative in the market can often be crystallized in the form of a curt “value-proposition” or a promising “return on investment.” The ends of these crisis narratives are often described in terms of “multiple bottom lines,” one of which is new market value and another is a more equitable equilibrium (fieldnotes). At the same time, the urban and social infrastructure that constitutes the cent er of gravity around which these stories are develo ped (i.e., TechAssembly) is not particularly accessible to innovators who cannot pay to play by traveling regular ly to the urban center or afford to spend time and materials on projects that may not last or immediately pay of f. The general chronotopic conditions of openness and prec arity favor those who are well-positioned and resou rced to act as first movers.

## Significance

Without inappropriately confusing what innovators say they do with what they actually do, it seems rea sonable that these chronotropic configurations likely also help us understand how and why innovation plays out in practice. For instance, we can see how space-time influences the abductive rationale behind design and innovatio n by establishing different meaningful ends of innovation as well as what amounts to compelling evidence of pace and progress towards those ends. Entrepreneurs developing innovations in a frenetic, urban chronotope characterized by precarity and the urgent need to make equity tec hnical (e.g., as an issue of scaled access to curri culum) and demonstrate immediate progress (e.g., through sales  or user metrics) may not align with equity innovat ion patterned per a longer term, cyclical, institutiona l chronotope, where change is a complex, halting, n on-linear, collective journey that may not have an immediate end in sight. Furthermore, we see how chronotope as a materialdiscursive construction can feed into the maintenance of exclusive innovation practices and infrastructures, for instance, by making innovation something that happens in urban centers by those who can afford to pay for heavily commodified space and time. Going forward, careful chronotopic analysis of innovation stories and prac tices could generate new useful typologies of innovators, innovative practices, and abductive goals of innovation, and contribute to a more sophisticated and inclusive un derstanding of what counts as innovative and who co unts as innovative.

Accounting for Spatial-Temporal Identity Trajectories in Student Co-Design Gavin Tierney, California State University, Fullerton, gtierney@fullerton.edu Teacher preparation traditionally follows a model i n which teacher candidates complete prerequisites p rior to entering into a credential program, then, once in the credential program, participate in a series of siloed courses taught by different instructors, while simultaneous ly completing field work with a master teacher. Thi s siloed model of learning is similar to other contexts in formal and informal education. Despite efforts to create coherence within teacher education programs and a recognition of teacher education as a moment of identity trans ition for beginning teachers, the connections between prerequisite coursework, coursework within the credential programs, fieldwork, and future teaching are often unclear (N olen et al., 2011). One significant detriment of th is model is that it does not purposefully or equitably support teacher candidates’ developmental trajectories as they navigate multiple courses and new experiences, while also making sense of their developing and new identities as teachers. Courses and experiences tend to lack connections or  systematic supports for candidates’ developmental trajectories. Within this model of teacher education, success is often defined as completion of course assignments, external measures of teaching, and teaching observa tions, without a systematic view of development as a professional. For this work, I instead look at teac her candidates’ development as professionals who le arn and, ideally, enact equitable teaching practices. Nasir and Hand (2008) introduced the concept of practice-linked identities, in which, with the support of different  identity resources, learners will be more engaged and develop identities that are linked to specific disciplinary  practices. By supporting teacher candidates’ ident ities in and ICLS2022 Proceedings 1757 © ISLS across learning contexts, I seek to support their s patial-temporal linkages to specific teaching practices, specifically practices connected to just, equitable, and inclusive education. Researchers have argued that without explicitly and purposefully supporting learners’ id entity development, learning environments run the r isk of perpetuating inequities, focusing too narrowly on m easures and content over learners’ experiences and development (Banks et al., 2007; Nasir et al., 2006). Focusing on teacher education specifically, without naming and supporting all novice teachers’ development perpetuates inequities around whose identities are supported and how identities are supported. As such, a premise of  this work is that the onus of identity development  falls not just on individual learners, in this case teacher c andidates, but that designers of learning environme nts need to equitably support learners’ identities and developmental trajectories over time and space.

## Method

This study draws upon perspectives in the Learning Sciences that focus on designing to equitably support learners’ identity (Nasir, 2012; Tierney, 2020), student co-design of learning environments (Pitt et al., 2019; Tierney et al., 2021), and the design of digital badge systems to represent learning and development in and across contexts and timescales (Bakhtin, 1981; Davis & Singh, 2015; Horstman et al., 2020; Pinkard et al., 2008) by identifying and supporting teacher candidates’ developmental trajectories into and through the Single-Subject Credential Program. Candidates’ developmental trajectories were mapped through students co-design of digital badge systems  to identify learning and supports for identity development within and across courses and experiences in the program. As opposed to content-centric perspectives of courses, such as curriculum maps or backwards planning, the digital badge systems focus on candidates’ experiences and development in specific courses into and through th e Credential Program. Built upon the ideas of badges from Boy Scouts and Girl Scouts, digital badge systems are used as the underlying design framework to map out valued learning accomplishments and identity pathways for learners, creating a trajectory for professional development in the program. In essence, the badges serve as a map for what it means to demonstrate professional ident ity development into and throughout the teacher education program. However, through co-design, the badge syst ems represent candidates’ perspectives and experien ces, highlighting how candidates interacted with course content and developed over time.

## Findings

Results from the study show what identity trajectories for individual students look like in a classroom co-design context, and how they can be mapped out to inform m ore equitable co-design processes and outcomes. Specifically, when looking at designing for equity across different learning contexts, it is valuable to orient to not only where learning and development occurred, but also when. By centering on learners’ experiences through codesign, the designers negotiated what should be included in the badge systems by also situating the co ntent and learning within their developmental trajectories. This was particularly salient when looking at the discourse of codesigners who were at different points in the credential program.

## Significance

Designing for equity across space and time requires an element of narrative. In doing so, it is important to consider who or what becomes the protagonist in that narrati ve (Iversen et al., 2017). Put another way, what is  the organizational framework for thinking about space and time? This study presents one model, in which le arners’ experience is centered to map out, and then design for, learning and development in and across learning contexts over time.

Charting Learner Experiences of Immersion Across Digital Space and Time Rebecca M. Quintana, University of Michigan, rebeccaq@umich.edu Extended reality (XR) technologies hold great promi se for engaging and immersive learning experiences.  Yet, issues of equity and access related to the use of such advanced technologies for learning are of significant concern. In XR environments, students can experience challen ging interactions with content, peers, and instruct ors. For instance, students may encounter Internet connectivity problems or even have adverse physical reactions such as nausea, hampering their ability to fully participate. Additionally, because XR technologies allow for the creation of highly realistic and immersive scenarios and contexts, there is the potential for students to experience difficult emotional reactions such as fear and anxiety, which  could also be a detriment to their learning. Desig ners of learning environments that include XR technologies have an opportunity to positively shape learners’ experiences by carefully attending to a range of design elements that promote equitable access. As XR technologies become increasingly prevalent in educational contexts, it will be especially important to understand how arrangements of ICLS2022 Proceedings 1758 © ISLS space (e.g., levels of immersion in a learning cont ext) and time (e.g., course structure and sequence)  can play a role in advancing equity-focused design goals.

## Method

To make progress on these design goals, we investig ated the extent to which an online, graduate level XR for Learning course fostered an equitable and immersive  experience for students. The course aimed to inter rogate claims that are made about the educational value of  XR technologies in support of learning. To explore  these themes experientially, students borrowed virtual reality (VR) headsets from the university library. Over the course of each class session, students experienced both low-immersion spaces (e.g., a web-based, interactive classroom) and high-immersion spaces (e.g., the university cam pus recreated in VR). Each class session was carefu lly structured, creating intentional opportunities for students to “dwell in and step out” (Ackerman, 2004) of these spaces, according to the learning goals for the cla ss and students' comfort with technology and course  topics. Instructors offered alternatives for high immersion  activities (e.g., watching a video screencast or u sing a VR application). Instructors’ design choices were motivated by efforts to ensure that all students would be supported pragmatically in their use of XR technologies and upheld emotionally in their explorations of sometimes difficult topics and subject matter.

We created visual timelines (Quintana & Slotta, 2017) to portray all pedagogical design elements from each class session. These timelines emphasized two important dimensions of engagement: space (i.e., le vel of immersion, ranging from viewing non-immersive digit al artifacts to interaction in a 3D world through a  VR headset) and time (i.e., lesson structure and seque nce). We used the Simulation Experience Design Fram ework (Raybourn, 2007)—a framework to facilitate the desi gn of connected, immersive, and equitable  learning experiences— to analyze and reflect on the visual timelines.

## Findings

Our analysis and findings are structured according to the four dimensions of the Simulation experience  Design Framework: interaction, narrative, place, and emerging culture. ● Interaction: The course design allowed for co-present action and co-present reflection (Raybourn, 2007). Various XR technologies allowed students to be co-present, engaging in collaborative problem-solving using shared artifacts. In one class session, students met together in the university’s central meeting place in virtual reality. For some students, this was their first time “on campus” due to pandemic restrictions. In this setting, students joined a live networking event as part of an XR Summit, meeting with conference attendees including researchers, academics, and industry professionals. Each class session also included opportunities for co-present reflection, with structured and facilitated discussion opportunities in small groups, occurring in both low-or high-immersion spaces. ● Narrative: The course design achieved co-created immersion with and through interactivity  in an immersive and information-rich problem space (Raybo urn, 2007). In a low-immersion space (GatherTown), students engaged with various knowledge artifacts connected to the session’s topic, such as short lecture videos, digital posters, and inter active whiteboards, together with their peers. Stud ents participated in “fireside chats” around a digital fireplace, which underscored the communal nature of the experience. In high-immersion spaces, students participated in a variety of educational narratives, such as sitting alongside peers in a futuristic lecture hall and co-viewing a movie clip in an “art house” environment. While these experiences all connected to learning goals for the course, they were experienced in a narrative context, with students “taking on the role” of a student or critic. ● Place: The course design allowed for active immersion through contextual cues (Raybourn, 2007). In a high-immersion space (e.g., virtual chemotherapy sc enario), students interacted with textual overlays, audio cues, and wayfinding symbols. Students were a lso provided low-immersion alternatives to experience this medical setting through narrated wa lkthrough videos of the treatment scenario. This allowed students to select the level of immersion that made them most comfortable, while still exploring the contextual cues provided through representations visible in the digital pace. ● Emerging culture: One criticism of XR environments is that they promote single points of view (Raybourn, 2019). An opportunity exists to support multiple points of view and perceptions across multiple spaces. Using this approach allows for a socially-constructed culture of learning  to emerge (Raybourn, 2007). The visual timelines revealed that the course structure allowed for both homogenous experiences (e.g., students could come together in the same virtual space) and multiple divergent opportunities, such as choice of spatial modality (low or high immersion) and even choice of XR application. To explore the topic of empathy, students chose from applications that highlighted the lived ICLS2022 Proceedings 1759 © ISLS experience of individuals from a range of social identities. Students had multiple opportunities to share their reflections and reactions through digital workbook entries and small group discussions, contributing to a shared, emerging culture of learning.

## Significance

The analysis revealed how arrangements of space (i.e., levels of immersion) and time (i.e., course str ucture and sequence) contributed to an equitable and immersive  experience. Intentional pairing of high-immersion experiences with low-immersion alternatives created options for students who experienced barriers to engagement. Interleaving low-and high-immersion experiences in  one class session helped mitigate the “intensity” of XR experiences, while creating a connected experience for students. This study shines a light on arrangem ents of space and time that contribute to equitable and immersive XR experiences. While findings are promising, we are just beginning to understand how instructors can advance equity-focus design goals in XR. Integrating Academic and Professional Space-Time with Technology Nikki James, Northeastern University, ni.james@northeastern.edu Experiential learning is “the process whereby knowl edge is created through the transformation of exper ience” (Kolb, 1984, pp. 38). In higher education, experiential learning pedagogy is used to assist learners with the process of transferring knowledge from the classroom into a  real-world professional setting (James et al., 202 0). In a capstone project, students are physically immersed in the classroom with the instructor integrating a real-world context through the introduction of an industry project. In an internship or co-op, students are physically immersed in a professional setting with the internship coordinator integrating a learning context through reflective writing and discussion. Despite which physical context is c entered, these pedagogical practices introduce an i ndustry professional into the learning environment, increasing the complexity of delivery and can impact the willingness of instructors to make this pedagogical choice (Jona & James, 2021). Distance and online learning is often attributed with the role of democratizing and opening up access to education through its ability to mediate the transfer of learning remotely and asynchronously. Despite its benefits, it is not without its shadow sides when it comes to  engagement and learning retention. If distance and  online learning make access to education equitable and accessible, and experiential learning help learners transfer their learning to real-world contexts, then in order to t o broaden participation in experiential learning di stance and online options are required. However, combining exp eriential learning with online learning also combin es the complexities they bring. In this new space-time con figuration, the learner, instructor, and industry p rofessional are in their own physical space and time making facilitation of the learning experience infinitely more complex.

## Framing & Context

Bakhtin’s notion of chronotopes (1981) is used in learning science as an analytical tool to examine the changes in space-time relations, particularly as learning adapts as a result of technology innovation and educational reform (Ritella et al., 2016). This study leveraged chronotopes to reflexively examine virtual internships, a technologymediated learning intervention (hereafter referred to as virtual internship) developed specifically to  broaden participation in experiential learning for students  who are unable to access place-based and time-boun d opportunities like internships that align with more traditional learning environments. The virtual internship emerged over a three-year de sign-based research project. Over several design iterations the intervention designers focused on re ducing the complexity of online experiential learni ng so that participation in a virtual internship was feasible for the learner, the instructor, and the industry professional.

## Findings

In discussion with colleagues while preparing this symposium it became apparent that our research team  was using instructional technology to reconfigure the s pace-time relationships between the learner, instru ctor, and industry professional. Moreover, the reconfiguration as opposed to the technology itself enabled a more accessible and scalable model of experiential learning that held its pedagogical integrity for individual students. The virtual internship intervention used technology to pass the work the learner was producing and the feedback the industry professional was providing back and forth between t he learner and the industry professional while givi ng the instructor a birds eye view of the interactions and  the ability to intervene in the exchange if requir ed. Using the technology in this way folded the space-time distance between the classroom and professional space.

## Significance

ICLS2022 Proceedings 1760 © ISLS A reflexive examination of the ‘new’ space-time relations between the learner, teacher, and industry partner that the model illuminates two roles the technology was playing:

1. To uphold the operating rhythm and momentum of the virtual internship

2. To clarify and reinforce the agreed roles and responsibilities of the learner, teacher and industry partner.

The technology playing these two roles allowed the learner, teacher, and industry partner to ‘sync’ with the virtual internship itself. In a time-bound, place based experience the human actors would ‘sync’ with each other in a team meeting to clarify tasks, align expectations and check project status. Intentionally designing the role of the student, teacher and industry partner upfront and structuring the technology to capture and pass information back and forth enabled them to stay in sync and aligned despite the physical space-time difference. During the initial phases of the research project t he research team identified multiple different configurations of roles and responsibilities used a t different research sites. Moreover, multiple diff erent perceptions of the roles and responsibilities betwe en the learner, teacher and industry partner in one  virtual internship. Despite these perceptions of the roles and responsibilities having a significant impact on the expected actions of the learner, teacher and industry partne r these expectations were implicit, leaving it up t o chance whether the student, teacher and industry partner were engaging in the virtual internship with expectations aligned. By using the technology to layout and uphold the ro les and responsibilities of the learner, teacher and industry partner the roles and responsibilities within the v irtual internship were made explicit upfront and co nstantly realigned throughout the learning experience. Makin g the roles and responsibilities explicit up front and leveraging the technology to enforce them enabled t he learner, teacher, and industry partner to align and consistently re-align their expectations of each other and stay in sync despite the physical space-time difference. The key outcome of this reflective examination of the virtual internship through the lens of space-time highlights the need for intentionally considering the role of the technology when designing remote and distance models of experiential learning that are accessible to all learners.
