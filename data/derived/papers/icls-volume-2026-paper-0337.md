# Learning through Partnership: Integrating Disciplinary and Experiential Knowledge in Teacher Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This practice-oriented paper presents a Research-Practice Partnership between (organisation 1), a formal pre-service teacher-education institute (TEI), and (organisation 2), an experiential learning organisation. While the core design of the collaboration was informed by Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory, this paper demonstrates how the integration of Gandhi’s principles of 3H (Head, Hand and Heart) facilitates non-linear, transformational reflections within a structured design. By analysing a 5-day-long urban expedition in Mumbai, we gained valuable insights about structuring immersive experiences, balancing guidance with autonomy, supporting diverse learners, and maintaining authentic engagement. Based on which we argue that the ‘Heart’ (affective immersion) acts as a catalyst that integrates the ‘Head’ (disciplinary knowledge-Knowledge that) with the ‘Hand’ (procedural knowledge-knowledge how). The paper also offers design principles for RPPs enabling transition of a "Legitimate Peripheral Participant " (LPP) from ‘periphery’ to the ‘core’ of the learning processes.

Pre-service teacher education programs, by design, are programs of praxis (Freire, 2020), which are mandated to bridge the gap between theory and practice (Ministry of Education, Government of India, 2020). However, institutions often resort to episodic field trips rather than establishing an ongoing cycle. To address this, we, The Tata Institute of Social Sciences, Mumbai (India), partnered with Disha India, a non-profit organisation specialising in experiential learning, to co-design and implement a 5-day immersive workshop for 26 student-teachers. The partnership aimed to guide student-teachers to experience authentic learning through immersive, practice-based learning cycles that connect disciplinary knowledge with the local context. Moving beyond a descriptive account, we synthesised Kolb’s ELT with Gandhi’s 3H as a cognitive tool which foregrounds that learning occurs in a cyclical process of concrete experience in a non-linear fashion. Thereby, highlighting the transformative potential of integrating organisational expertise in ELT and  the 3H principles (Gandhi, 1951) towards creating a community of practice that enables each LPP (Lave & Wenger, 1991) to move to the core. Partnership for praxis: Design and Implementation This paper employs a Narrative Inquiry approach within a Design-Based Research (DBR) paradigm to reveal the insights from the design and procedural aspects based on a narrative account of a participant.  The partnership commenced through synchronous and asynchronous planning sessions to align pedagogical expectations with the offerings of the field sites. Two sites for the field expedition were finalised through deliberations, ensuring that these sites were pedagogically potential, logistically viable, and financially prudent. The students were divided into two groups based on their broader pedagogical interests: “How a Historian Works (HHW)” and “Nature our Teacher (NoT)”.  While HHW focused on urban heritage at colonial-era sites and a museum, the NoT was anchored by an ecological inquiry at a Flamingo Sanctuary and urban wetlands. The workshop followed Kolb's ELT cycle across five days: three days of field immersion, followed by on-campus guided reflective sessions (including Reader’s, Writer’s and illustration workshops) to synthesise reflections into tangible learning outcomes (museum leaflets and bird identification cards) of use to the broader community. Throughout the cycle, facilitators provided scaffolding through checklists, observation worksheets, and emphasising sensory engagement and learner agency towards ensuring the transition of an LPP to the core community of practice (CoP).

The HHW group moved from reading textbooks (the Head) to embodied exploration of heritage (the Hand);  beginning with a "wonder walk" through colonial-era sites, including the General Post Office, Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Terminus (CSMT), and the Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation building. This transition was facilitated by the Heart; non-linear affective nudging propelling realisations of how power shapes urban aesthetics. For instance, during the heritage walk, a student noticed Makrana marble on building facades and connected it to the Taj-Mehal, sparking an immediate deep-dive into material sourcing for imperial architecture. Similarly, students analysing Emperor Akbar's shield discovered zodiac signs and Persian floral motifs connecting them to Akbar's political alliances and religious syncretism, wi linking it to the concept of "Zill-e-Ilahi" (shadow of God). The NoT group focused on getting deeper into behavioural patterns and ecological relationships, otherwise invisible in single visits. In these moments, the Hand (Observing/Mapping) clarified the Head (conceptual understanding), while Heart (shared realisation of connections) provided the scaffolding for praxis. Challenges and adaptations Time constraints emerged as a recurring tension. Additionally, ‘Skill anxiety’ surfaced particularly during illustration tasks, with many participants expressing discomfort with drawing. The conceptual challenge of "wondering like a child" proved difficult for student-teachers used to demonstrating mastery rather than embracing not-knowing. We responded by leveraging distributed expertise, where artistically confident students assisted their peers, strengthening collaborative dynamics aligned to design-based research principles, emphasising iterative refinement based on learner needs (Design-Based Research Collective, 2003). We learnt that these challenges reflect broader tensions in learning sciences around balancing structure and emergence in authentic learning environments (Greeno, 2006).

## What we learned: Multiple perspectives on impact

Our analysis offers three core design principles for an integrated ELT and 3H immersive learning: 1) Productive Labour as Cognition:  the five-day expedition enabled us to revoke the Gandhian principle of productive labour (crafting leaflets, identifying birds, etc.) as a form of cognition. Thus, the ‘Hand-work’ emerged as evidence of understanding of the’Head-work’. 2) The ‘Heart’ acts as a catalyst: The ‘Heart’ ushers in meaningful praxis of ‘Hand’ and ‘Head as it helps navigate emotional constraints like anxiety, resulting in LPP’s transition into an active member of a CoP. One student-teacher from a rural, non-English speaking background, who used to hesitate in speaking up in the classroom discussions, started actively participating in post-visit reflection sessions. The palpable camaraderie among peers encouraged her to express her thoughts and ideas. This exemplifies what Lev and Wenger call the CoP, participating in which translates into learning (Lev & Wenger, 1991). 3) Research-Practice Mutualism: Teacher-educator and ELT field experts noted that there was definite reciprocal learning. Student-teachers brought tons of questions, comments, thoughts, and ideas to their 2177 subsequent classroom discussions, which, they shared, had their origin in the workshop. Further, the framework broke moulds and dissolved boundaries in more than one way. First, the city became a scaffolded learning resource, and the 3H framework revealed non-linearity as an inherent character of experiential learning. Second, it renewed mutual respect and alignment between academic theorisation and experiential expeditions. This has far-reaching implications in the context where academic theorisation is traditionally associated with privileged caste groups, while experiential by default is ‘left’ to the marginalised sections of society (Guru, 2002). Overall, the integrated structures of  ELT and 3H, with reflection embedded into it, helped participants transform raw experiences into conceptual and practical knowledge. The partnership also highlighted how centring local knowledge and community engagement transforms learning, makes it authentic, and fosters situated pedagogy.  Overall, this organisational integration of expertise paves the way towards dissolving the boundaries between what modern analytical philosopher Gilbert Ryle (1949) calls ‘knowledge that’(the disciplinary knowledge) and ‘knowledge how’ (the experiential, practical knowledge). Lessons for educators and institutions Transferable and contextualised design principles Drawing on learning sciences frameworks for designing effective learning environments (Bransford, Brown, & Cocking, 2000), our experience yielded several principles. First, authentic tasks with real audiences generate meaningful learning. Second, multiple feedback loops (peer, facilitator, expert) supported iterative improvement. Third, structured reflection time proved non-negotiable for meaning-making. Fourth, balancing scaffolding with autonomy mattered: worksheets guided observation but allowed emergent curiosities. Finally, if the design is informed by frameworks available in one’s context, i.e. Gandhian 3H, the entire field engagement proved far more effective.

For implementing field-based learning Start with accessible local sites. Urban heritage buildings and natural sanctuaries offer rich learning without extensive travel. Co-plan with community partners; facilitators' prior site visits identified pedagogically rich features and logistical requirements. Build time buffers; immersive learning cannot be rushed. Embrace student discomfort with ambiguity as growth signals. Create space for multiple modes of expression (verbal, visual, written).

For building research-practice partnerships Clear role division prevents conflicts. Practitioners designed pedagogy while the institution handled logistics, preventing mission drift. Joint planning sessions proved essential for alignment. We learned practitioners need academic frameworks to articulate tacit knowledge, while academics need field expertise to translate theory into practice. Mixed-ability grouping benefited all learners. Budget for facilitator site visits; reconnaissance identified challenges that shaped design. Our model aligns with Penuel, Allen, Coburn, and Farrell's (2015) framework for RPPs, emphasising mutualism and joint work. Clear role division and continuous communication address common collaborative challenges (Coburn, Penuel, & Geil, 2013). Resource considerations and adaptations Five days minimum for meaningful immersion: Day 1 orientation, Days 2-3 sustained engagement, Day 4 synthesis, Day 5 reflection. Thirteen students per facilitator allowed individualised attention. Expert sessions enhanced conceptual depth. Digital tools enabled professional-quality products. This model adapts across contexts. Rural settings can leverage local heritage sites. Shorter timeframes might focus on single disciplinary groups. Larger cohorts require additional facilitators. Different grade levels necessitate adjusting product complexity. The partnership model proves highly transferable. Any collaboration between practice-focused organisations and educational institutions can leverage complementary strengths through genuine co-design rather than hierarchical service.
