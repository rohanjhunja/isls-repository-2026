# Recognition Without Continuity: Selective Attenuation of Mathematics Identity Across the High School-to-STEM Transition

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Recognition is widely understood as central to mathematics participation, yet whether recognition-based identity persists across educational transitions remains untested at scale. Using nationally representative longitudinal data from 23,000 students over six years, this study examines how distinct dimensions of mathematics identity predict proximal motivation and distal STEM major selection. Results show that recognition-based identity (feeling seen as a “math person”) strongly predicts proximal engagement but loses 99% of its predictive power for STEM major selection across the high school–college transition. In contrast, internally held identity (centrality) remains robust, directly predicting STEM major choice (OR = 1.78, p <.001). Typicality fully attenuates for distal outcomes (OR = 1.01, p =.97), while female students had 61% lower odds of pursuing STEM regardless of identity strength. Findings suggest identity portability depends on source: recognition may initiate participation, but only internaliz ed identity transfers across institutional transitions and shapes long-term educational trajectories.

## Context and problem

Despite decades of reform, disparities in STEM degree attainment remain persistent, particularly for women, Black, Hispanic, and Indigenous students  (Kennedy et al., 2021; NSB, 2024). Women comprised 58% of bachelor's recipients in 2021, but earned only 24% of engineering and 22% of computer science degrees. These inequities are sustained by identity processes that shape who feels seen, valued, and capable within mathematics (Cass et al., 2011; Nasir & Hand, 2008). Yet whether recognition-based identity persists across the institutional transitions where STEM attrition occurs remains untested at scale. This study addresses this gap by tracing two dimensions of mathematics identity —centrality (personal importance) and typicality (recognition as a "math person")—from high school through postsecondary STEM major selection and demonstrating that mathematics identity is not uniformly portable across transitions. If recognition-based identity attenuates while internally-held identity persists, this would explain wh y many well-intentioned interventions enhance student engagement without narrowing demographic gaps in STEM attainment. Beyond testing predictive differences, this study also informs future design hypotheses about how recognition continuity might be sustained across institutional transitions.

Theoretical and empirical gap This study positions mathematics identity as a critical lens for understanding how inequities in STEM participation take shape long before college enrollment. We use nationally representative data from the High School Longitudinal Study of 2009 (HSLS:09) t o distinguish two dimensions of mathematics identity —centrality and typicality—and trace each dimension's predictive reach from high school through postsecondary STEM major selection. These constructs, distinguished through confirmatory factor analysis, capture how students internalize mathematics as part of who they are (centrality) and how they perceive themselves as fitting into social images of 'math people' (typicality). By linking these constructs to both proximal course-taking and distal STEM major selection, this study provides one of the first national-level examinations of how different facets of mathematics identity persist or attenuate across the high school-to-postsecondary transition. We draw on Carlone and Johnson's (2007) tripartite framework of competence, performance, and recognition, which conceptualizes mathematics identity as integrating knowing, doing, and being seen as capable. This study also extends consequential transitions theory (Beach, 2003) by testing whether mathematics identity dimensions differ in portability as students move across institutional boundaries.  Recognition (i.e., being seen as a legitimate participant) is underexplored in quantitative research yet central to inclusion. Peer recognition functions as a developmental mirror during adolescence, signaling whether students fit the cultural prototype of a "math person" (Riegle-Crumb et al., 2012; Starr, 2018). This raises a critical question: do recognition-based forms 820 of identity travel with learners across institutional contexts, or do they depend on the continuity of the recognition structures that sustain them?

T his framing  distinguishes between two theoretically complementary facets of mathematics identity. Centrality reflects the extent to which mathematics is integral to one’s self-concept—an internalized sense of importance and meaning (Carlone & Johnson, 2007; Hazari et al., 2010). Typicality, in contrast, reflects perceived social fit: whether one sees oneself as resembling those recognized as “math people” (Starr, 2018; Starr & Leaper, 2024). Centrality captures identity’s internal structure; typicality captures its social permeability —how recognition and belonging shape the negotiation of identity in interaction with peers and contexts. Together, these constructs position mathematics identity as personally meaningful and socially situated, a perspective consistent with Learning Sciences theories of participation, recognition, and community membership (Nasir & Cooks, 2009; Wortham, 2006). This distinction suggests that different components of identity may differ not only in form but also in portability across transitions.

Guided by Bronfenbrenner's bioecological theory (1979; 2006) and Craig and Guzmán's (2018) Social Theory of Numeracy, this study conceptualizes mathematics identity as nested within ecological systems and distributed across recognition processes. Bronfenbr enner's model situates identity development within interdependent layers of influence, from individual beliefs to institutional and societal systems. The Social Theory of Numeracy frames mathematical identity as relational, cultural, and historically contingent, shaped by who receives recognition and how that recognition is distributed across social positions. Despite rich qualitative evidence linking identity to persistence, quantitative analyses testing whether different dimensions of mathematics identity persist across institutional transitions remain scarce. Using large-scale longitudinal data, we test a specific prediction derived from these frameworks: recognition-dependent identity should attenuate more across transitions than internally held identity.

Study overview and contribution Using HSLS:09 data, this study examines how mathematics identity measured during 9th and 10th grades predicts STEM outcomes across a formative period when course-taking patterns crystallize, and postsecondary pathways diverge (Bozick & Ingels, 2008; Gottfried, 2015; Riegle-Crumb et al., 2012). This work complements qualitative and design-based traditions in the Learning Sciences by providing population-generalizable estimates of identityoutcome pathways that cannot be obtained from site-specific designs. This study advances a central claim that mathematics identity is not uniformly portable across transitions through three contributions.  First, we distinguish two dimensions of mathematics identity, centrality and recognition-based typicality, and demonstrate through chi-square difference testing that they are empirically separable despite substantial correlation (φ =.90), thereby establishing the conceptual basis for differential portability. Although highly correlated, their divergent structural pathways provide stronger evidence of functional distinction than the  magnitude of their correlation alone.  Second, we provide the first formal test of selective identity attenuation across educational transitions, showing that typicality's predictive power for STEM major selection attenuates completely (from OR = 1.64 to OR = 1.01). At the same time, centrality persists (from OR = 1.87 to OR = 1.78), with this divergence confirmed by cross-equation testing (χ² = 46.57, p <.0001), demonstrating selective attenuatio n across transitions. Third, we show that centrality's effect on STEM major selection is direct rather than mediated by motivational beliefs, suggesting that identity-driven commitment to STEM operates through mechanisms distinct from interest, self-efficacy, or perceived utility. Through these contributions, this study argues that mathematics identity should not be understood as a unitary construct that either persists or does not. Rather, identity comprises components with different portability across institutional contexts. Recognition-based identity, grounded in local social validation, is expected to attenuate when recognition structures change, whereas internally held identity is expected to persist. This study tests this claim using longitudinal, nationally representative data.  To test whether identity components differ in their portability across transitions, we address the following questions: RQ1: Can mathematics identity be distinguished empirically as two correlated dimensions, centrality (personal importance) and typicality (recognition-based belonging), using HSLS:09 survey indicators? RQ2: Does recognition-dependent identity (typicality) attenuate more than internally-held identity (centrality) across the six-year transition from high school course-taking to STEM major selection? RQ3:  Is centrality's effect on STEM major selection mediated by motivational beliefs (interest, self-efficacy, utility), or does it operate through a direct pathway?

Using survey-weighted regression models with HSLS:09 data, we formally test whether the predictive reach of recognition-based identity differs from that of internally held identity as students traverse the high school-to821 STEM transition. The following framework elaborates why identity components should differ in their ability to transfer across institutional transitions.

## Theoretical framework

Understanding whether mathematics identity persists across educational transitions requires distinguishing between identity facets that may transfer across contexts and those that remain context-bound. This study integrates perspectives from identity development theory (Carlone & Johnson, 2007), figured worlds (Holland et al., 1998), and the Social Theory of Numeracy (Craig & Guzmán, 2018) to generate a testable hypothesis: recognition-dependent identity should attenuate more across institutional transitions than internally held identity. The following subsections elaborate on the theoretical basis for distinguishing centrality from typicality and for predicting their differential persistence across the high school-to-STEM transition. Identity and recognition in learning In the Learning Sciences, identity is understood as a dynamic process through which learners come to see themselves (and to be recognized by others) as participants in disciplinary practices (Nasir & Hand, 2008; Sfard & Prusak, 2005). Identity thus bridges the psychological and sociocultural, simultaneously self-constructed and socially mediated through recognition, participation, and belonging (Carlone & Johnson, 2007; Wortham, 2006). Recognition, in particular, plays a critical role in shaping who is perceived as competent and legitimate wit hin mathematics, reflecting deep-seated sociocultural hierarchies about who “belongs” in STEM (Carlone, Scott, & Lowder, 2015).

Recognition—how others perceive and affirm one's participation—is central to mathematics identity yet underexplored in large-scale quantitative research (Carlone & Johnson, 2007). Peer recognition, in particular, functions as a developmental mirror during adolescence, signaling to students whether they fit within the cultural prototype of a “math person” (Riegle-Crumb et al., 2012; Starr, 2018). Thus, identity in mathematics is not only a reflection of skill or interest  but also a relational and political process negotiated within peer and institutional contexts. This study builds on this foundation by quantitatively modeling recognition as an essential component of mathematics identity and testing whether recognition-based identity maintains its influence when the peer and institutional contexts that confer recognition change across educational transitions. Centrality and typicality as dimensions of math identity Within this broader recognition framework, mathematics identity comprises two interrelated dimensions:

• Centrality captures the degree to which mathematics is integral to one’s self-concept, how deeply

mathematics is woven into a student’s sense of who they are (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002; Hazari et al., 2010). Centrality reflects internalization and self-meaning: mathematics is not merely something one does, but part of who one is.  This conception also aligns with identity commitment traditions emphasizing durable self-structure across contexts (Stryker & Burke, 2000).

• Typicality, in contrast, reflects how students perceive themselves as fitting the social image or stereotype

of a “math person” (Starr, 2018; Starr & Leaper, 2024). It represents recognition-based belonging, or the degree to which students feel seen and accepted within mathematics communities. These dimensions have different theoretical implications for portability across transitions.  Centrality, as an internally held commitment, should be context-independent. Once mathematics becomes central to self-concept, this centrality is expected to transfer across institutional settings because it is authored by the person rather than conferred by a specific community. Typicality, in contrast, is inherently relational and context-dependent. Being recognized as a "math person" depends on who is doing the r ecognizing, what prototypes they hold, and what signals count as evidence of mathematical competence. When students transition across institutional contexts, recognition structures shift through new peers, norms, and evaluative communities.  Figured worlds theory (Holland et al., 1998) predicts that participation-dependent identity resources do not automatically transfer when learners move across figured worlds. We test this prediction by comparing each dimension's influence on proximal outcomes (within the high school figured world where identity was formed) and distal outcomes (in the postsecondary figured world entered years later).

Recognition, continuity, and institutional transitions Bronfenbrenner's (2006) bioecological theory and Craig and Guzmán's (2018) Social Theory of Numeracy predict that developmental influences shift across institutional contexts. Recognition structures that validate mathematical identity in high school may not persist in postsecondary settings.  In contrast, centrality, reflecting internalized personal importance, may be less dependent on external validation and therefore more portable. 822 Together, these frameworks generate a specific prediction: across the high school-to-STEM transition, recognition-dependent identity dimensions should attenuate more than internally held identity dimensions. This study provides the first large-scale test of this prediction, comparing how centrality and typicality measured in 9th grade predict both proximal high school outcomes and distal postsecondary major selection.

## Data and methods

Dataset and sample This study uses the High School Longitudinal Study of 2009 (HSLS:09), a nationally representative survey following 23,000 students from 944 U.S. high schools (Ingels et al., 2015), with identity measured in 9th grade

(2009) and outcomes through 2016. Analytic samples include N = 7,101 (STEM major selection) and N = 1,190

(advanced course-taking). Robustness checks include within-student analysis (N = 708 with both outcomes) and STEM-aspiring subsample (N = 1,731).

## Measures

Mathematics identity was modeled as a two-factor latent construct reflecting both centrality, the degree to which mathematics is integral to one’s self-concept, and typicality, or the sense of being recognized by others as a “math person.” Items were drawn from the HSLS:09 base-year and first follow-up student questionnaires, capturing identity development across the early high school years. Centrality captured intrinsic value and self-identification with mathematics, while typicality operationalized recognition-based belonging as a population-scale quantitative extension of recognition identity theory (Carlone & Johnson, 2007). Confirmatory factor analysis supported a two-factor model (RMSEA =.036; CFI =.982; TLI =.975; Δχ² = 311.56 vs. one-factor, p <.0001, composite reliability >.75).

The proximal outcome was the highest mathematics course completed during high school (transcript variable S4HIMATH, nine ordered categories). STEM major selection at the 2016 follow-up was coded as a binary indicator. Sensitivity analyses used math-intensive STEM (CIP codes 11, 14, 15, 27, 40) and NSF STEM definitions. Controls included gender, race/ethnicity, SES, and 8th-grade mathematics achievement. Analytic strategy The analysis proceeds in three steps. Step 1 validates the measurement structure through confirmatory factor survey-weighted regressions for both outcomes using identical covariate specifications. Advanced course-taking is modeled using ordered logistic regression; STEM major selection is modeled using binary logistic regression. Step 3 conducts a formal cross-equation comparison using simultane ous equation estimation to test whether the coefficients for each identity dimension differ significantly across the proximal and distal outcomes. The binary outcome variable, STEM major selection, was modeled using a logit link function to estimate the probability that student i selected a STEM field by the 2016 follow-up. Formal test confirms selective attenuation To test whether the divergence between dimensions is statistically significant rather than artifactual, we conducted a formal cross-equation comparison using simultaneous equation estimation. This approach tests whether each identity dimension's coefficient differs significantly across the proximal (course-taking) and distal (STEM major) outcomes. For typicality, the course-taking coefficient (β = 0.495) was significantly larger than the STEM major coefficient (β = 0.006), with χ²(1) = 46.57, p <.0001. In contrast, centrality's course-taking coefficient (β = 0.626) did not differ significantly from its STEM major coefficient (β = 0.579), χ²(1) = 0.64, p =.42. These results confirm that typicality's influence attenuates across the transition while centrality's persists. Expressed as proportional attenuation, typicality loses 99% of its predictive strength (odds ratio drops from 1.64 to 1.01), while centrality loses only 5% (odds ratio drops from 1.87 to 1.78). This selective attenuation pattern constitutes the central empirical finding of this study (Figure 1). Divergent mechanisms: Motivation versus direct pathways The two dimensions differ not only in their temporal reach but also in the proximal mechanisms through which they operate. Typicality significantly predicted mathematics interest (β = 1.56, p =.008) and self-efficacy (β = 1.24, p =.015) in the original survey-weighted structural equation model. Students who felt recognized as "math people" in 9th grade reported stronger motivation in early high school. However, these motivational beliefs did not independently predict STEM major selection (all p >.34), and the indirect effect of typicality on STEM major selection through motivation was nonsignificant. Typicality shapes affective engagement, but this does not translate into degree outcomes.

Centrality showed a different pattern. It did not significantly predict mathematics interest or self-efficacy (all p >.50). When motivation composites (interest, self-efficacy, perceived utility) were added to the STEM major logistic regression, centrality's odds ratio decreased only marginally from 1.78 to 1.74, a reduction of 2.7%. None of the motivational composites independently predicted major selection (all p >.34). This indicates centrality's effect on STEM major selection operates through a direct pathway rather than through interest, confidence, or utility beliefs. Students for whom mathematics is central to their identity select STEM majors regardless of their reported motivational beliefs at the time of  identity measurement. Together, these patterns constitute a double dissociation. Typicality predicts how students feel about mathematics but not what majors they choose. Centrality predicts what majors they choose, but not how they feel. Each dimension influences STEM participation through a distinct channel, providing the strongest evidence that they capture separable facets of mathematics identity despite their high latent correlation. The selective attenuation of recognition-based identity This study reveals why mathematics identity interventions may fail to close equity gaps. Recognition-based identity (typicality) loses 99% of its predictive power across the high school-to-college transition, while self-authored identity (centrality) loses only 5%—a 20-fold difference in attenuation. Most critically, female students had 61% lower odds of selecting a STEM major (OR = 0.39, p <.001), regardless of identity strength. Neither typicality (interaction OR = 1.19, p =.57) nor centrality (interaction OR = 0.99, p =.98) moderated this gap. Strengthening mathematics identity —through re cognition or internalization —cannot overcome structural barriers. This selective attenuation pattern (Figure 1) provides the first large-scale evidence for Beach's (2003) concept of consequential transitions and has three critical implications for equity-focused design. Consistent with bioecological, consequential transition, and social numeracy frameworks, these findings suggest that identity portability depends on whether identity resources are internally consolidated or remain dependent on context-specific recognition systems.

First, it provides the first large-scale quantitative evidence that recognition-dependent identity is indeed context-bound, as figured worlds theory predicts (Holland et al., 1998). Typicality depends on being recognized within a specific community of practice, with specific peers and specific norms about who counts as a "math person." As students move across figured worlds, disrupted recognition continuity may weaken recognitiondependent identity by altering norms of legitimacy. Our finding that typicality loses all predictive power suggests it may not reconstitute when these recognition structures change, though we cannot directly observe this process. Second, the persistence of centrality suggests that internally authored identity operates through a different mechanism. Students for whom mathematics is deeply woven into self-concept carry that commitment across institutional boundaries. Centrality's effect is not mediated by interest, self-efficacy, or utility beliefs, suggesting it operates through what identity theorists call commitment or self-verification processes (Burke & Stets, 2009). When mathematics is central to who you are, major selection may reflect identity maintenance rather than rational calculation of interest or expected success. This interpretation is consistent with centrality predicting major selection even when it does not predict concurrent motivation. The double dissociation: Affect versus commitment The two dimensions also differ in the proximal mechanisms through which they operate (Figure 2). Typicality significantly predicted mathematics interest and self-efficacy, but these motivational beliefs did not carry forward to predict major selection. Centrality did not predict motivation but directly predicted major selection, with minimal attenuation when motivational variables were statistically controlled. This constitutes a double dissociation. Typicality influences how students feel about mathematics in the near term but not what educational pathways they choose years later. Centrality influences pathway choice directly without necessarily shaping concurrent affective experiences.

The finding also suggests why motivation-focused interventions may show short-term gains without long-term persistence. Interventions that increase interest or self-efficacy without also making mathematics central to identity may produce the typicality pattern: enhanced proximal engagement that does not transfer. Conversely, interventions that strengthen identity centrality without necessarily increasing enjoyment may produce the centrality pattern: sustained commitment even in the absence of high motivation. Future research should test whether designed recognition reflection at institutional transitions can sustain typicality's influence for populations—especially women —facing structural barriers that identity alone cannot overcome. This gender gap was not moderated by identity (typicality × gender OR = 1.19, p =.57; centrality × gender OR =  0.99, p =.98), meaning that strengthening mathematics identity alone cannot close gender gaps. Equitable STEM participation requires both identitysustaining environments and institutional reforms that dismantle gendered barriers.

## Design implications: The recognition reflection principle

The selective attenuation of typicality points to a specific design opportunity. If identity attenuation reflects disrupted recognition continuity, then transition design should focus on sustaining recognition across institutional boundaries. We propose the Recognition Reflection Principle as a preliminary design conjecture (see Figure 3; Sandoval, 2014): embedding structured opportunities for learners to surface, examine, and contest the recognition dynamics that shape who counts as a "math person" can make recognition portable rather than context-bound. This principle has three components. First, make recognition explicit. Structured reflection activities (e.g., discussing "who gets recognized as good at math and why") make recognition dynamics visible and contestable. Second, support learner-authored recognition through peer nomination, portfolio presentations, or mentored identity work rather than passive reception. Third, embed recognition reflection at transition points such as first-year STEM seminars or bridge programs, where students can collectively examine prototypes across figured worlds and construct more inclusive images of mathematical competence. Prior work found that STEM mentoring combined with math club participation strongly predicted early mathematics identity (β =.37, p =.004; Havard & Quirós -Arauz, 2025). This suggests structured recognition environments may already function as sites where recognition is made negotiable, lending plausibility to the conjecture that designed recognition reflection could sustain typicality across transitions. However, the principle remains untested and is offered as a hypothesis awaiting design-based validation. ## Limitations, future research directions, and conclusions

Key limitations include single-timepoint identity measurement and the  lack of postsecondary recognition measures to test whether recognition discontinuity directly explains typicality's attenuation. The outcome captures broad STEM rather than exclusively math-intensive fields, though sensitivity analyses produced substantively identical results. The interpretation is theoretically grounded but empirically untested. This study reframes STEM equity as a problem of recognition continuity rather than  identity strength. Recognition-based identity shapes proximal behavior but loses all distal influence, while self-authored identity persists across the six-year transition. Across transitions, what appears to weaken may be less identity itself than the continuity of the recognition systems sustaining it. This provides the first large-scale evidence that identity resources differ fundamentally in portability across figured worlds (Holland et al., 1998). The policy implication is stark: interventions that strengthen students'  feelings about mathematics—without also making mathematics central to who they are—will not close equity gaps. This is especially critical for addressing gender disparities: female students had 61% lower STEM odds regardless of identity. For Learning Sciences, these findings suggest that designing identity-supportive environments may require sustaining recognition continuity across consequential transitions, not simply strengthening identity within isolated contexts.
