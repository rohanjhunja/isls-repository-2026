# Speaking of Engagement: From Student Voice to a Practical Frame for Motivation in Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This grounded qualitative study analyzes nine interviews from a fourth-grade arts-integrated in-class learning experience to explore what makes learning engaging for students. Using thematic coding, 27 student-identified motivators were synthesized into four categories—Community, Action, Intriguing, and Upliftment. These findings highlight the potential of a student-informed framework to support motivation in K–12 learning and offer practical guidance for pedagogical and instructional action, while pointing to further research with larger, diverse datasets.

## Introduction

As an educator committed to dynamic pedagogy, I’ve repeatedly heard the same plea from teachers across diverse K–12 settings: “How can we make learning more engagin g?” Despite decades of research showing that studen t classroom motivation and engagement sharply decline  from third through ninth grade across demographics (Skinner et al., 2008) —educators still lack practical, student-centered tools to address this issue. While some scholarship has begun to incorporate student voices  (Baroutsis, McGregor, & Mill s, 2015), much of the field remains focused on top-down solutions. This study responds to that gap by examining motivation at the level of individual learning tasks, spotlighting how teacher  behaviors and pedagogical choices —shaped by student perspectives—can be leveraged to create more inspiring learning experiences. In the Learning Sciences, motivation is most often stu died as what drives learners to engage in learning experiences, while engagement research tends to foc us on the observable indicators of students partici pating in those experiences (Renninger, Ren, & Kern, 2018). M uch of the research conducted  in the domain uses as a springboard two theories: Fredricks, Blumenfeld, & Paris’s (2004) multi-dimensional engagement theory, which categorizes engagement into behavioral, emotional, and cognitive dimensions and Deci and Ryan’s (2000)  selfdetermination theory, which anchors motivation around the psychological n eeds of relatedness, autonomy, and competence. Most studies on motivation and teaching and learning focus on is olated interventions in postsecondary contexts. Learning experiences that are arts-based offer guidance in motivating student engagement (Halverson, 2018). Although there are frameworks that have been developed to help teachers increase motivation and engagement in the classroom (Oyserman & Destin,  2010) most remain domain-specific and limited in the motivators engaged. There is a growing need for integrated, student-informed models that unify insights to better reflect the complexity of motivation and engagement  in real classrooms (Fredricks et al., 2019b).  In this study I ask the following questions: 1) What aspects of an in-class arts integration program do 4th-grade students recall as impactful and engaging as they describe the learning experience? 2) How might the analysis of patterns across these student descriptors inform the development of  a theoretical framework for designing more engagin g K-12 learning experiences?

## Methods

This grounded qualitative study is rooted in a prag matic paradigm and guided by social-constructivist ways of honoring the student’s onto-epistemological being as rich and generative, prioritizing student voice as intertwined with actionable insights for real-world classroom contexts. Using existing interview data from a fourth-grade artsbased learning program, the study treats student reflections as dialogic texts, embracing the idea that student selfreport is among the most  valid and reliable measures o f engagement (Furlong & Christenson, 2008). As a researcher, I approach this work from an asset-based stance, maximally preserving students’ original language and honoring their agency in shaping a nuanced understanding of what makes learning meaningful and motivating. This study draws on qualitative data from the UW  Madison Community Arts Collaboratory’s 2021 partnership with a fourth-grade class participating in Whoopensocker, an arts-integrated program blending dramatic arts with narrative writing instruction. Nine semi-structured student interviews that were part of that data were reanalyzed in this study through a grounded research approach. In the first round of thematic coding—guided by multi-dimensional engagement theory—student expressed motivators (that accompanied engagement indices) were coded (see Figure 1 below) using In Vivo and Thematic Aggregation Coding (Saldana, 2021) and compared to self-determination theory constructs, for triangulation. Codes were then synthesized into overarching categories based on shared characteristics (Saldana, 2021).

## Discussion

The high-level categories represent cross-cutting aspects of learning experiences students fo und motivating and contribute to a developing theoretical framework ro oted in student voice for designing more engaging K –12 learning e xperiences. While some codes could fit into more than one categ ory, the four motivator categories generally represent distinct aspects of learning experience design. Community emphasizes connection and cultural relevance; Action involves physical, hands-on participation; Intriguing taps into novelty, challenge, and curiosity; and Upliftment centers on affirmation through recognition and encouragement. This study attests to self-determination theory’s emphasis on autonomy, competence, and relatedness as key motivators, but also confirms additional driver s highlighted by other researchers, like novelty, d iscovery, creativity, engaging student background knowledge, feeling esteemed, and fostering curiosity and explo ration (Kesebir & Pyszczynski, 2012; Sedikides & Alicke, 2 012; Silvia, 2012). Notably, students named multipl e motivators for the same learning experience, sugges ting that engagement may be strongest when several motivators are strategically c ombined rather than isolated. This points to the ne ed for a broader pedagogical framework that integrates multiple theoretical insights into practical educational strategies to enhance real-world learning environments (Martin, 2007).

Future research would build on these findings by analyzing larger and more diverse datasets, examining how the identified codes align with or challenge ex isting motivation theories, and testing the practic al use, in pedagogy and curriculum, of the Community, Action, Intriguing, and Upliftment framework in real-time classroom settings.
