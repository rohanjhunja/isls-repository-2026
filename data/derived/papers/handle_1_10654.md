# Pre-Service Teacher Identity Crucibles and “Good” Teaching

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this research, we draw connections between Tyack and Tobin’s grammars of schooling and contextualized pre-service teachers conceptualizations of their teacher identities to make sense of the ways that deficit oriented binary thinking patterns prevail in classrooms even in the face of coursework designed to challenge the status quo. Video data was collected during final reflections of pre-service STEM teachers during a Project Based Instruction course that included a field-teach element. Through vignette analysis, we bring to light the ways in which binary perspectives in PST reflective practices exhibit how grammars of schooling are integrated into teacher identity. Two binaries are discussed: “learning and reinforcing time” and “fast and slow students”. Each binary is analyzed to reveal perceptions of knowledge and learning held by the pre-service teachers as well as how PST are actively engaging in negotiation of what it means to “be a teacher”.

## Introduction

Understanding the experiences and identities that u nderpin the ways that pre-service teachers define “good teaching” and their role as a teacher is important for creating teacher education programs that effectively support individuals before they enter the  classroom. The concept of the “grammar of schooling ”, coined by Tyack and Tobin in 1994, encompasses the traditionally unques tioned and challenged traits, traditions, and polic ies that govern the U.S. Education Institution. These traits  such as the role o f the teacher, grades, student classification, division of knowledge into school “subjects” and perceptions of time and space in educational settings have stayed strong for decades, despite numerous attempts to disrupt them. They have pervaded U.S. culture to the extent that Tyack and Tobin found congruencies between cultural  beliefs and grammar traits, leading to public supp ort for these traits, going so far as to defend their existence by defining schools following the “correct” grammar as “real school” (p. 456).

In educator preparation programs, such grammars are  often challenged during course work through assignments that advocate for and demonstrate resea rch-based pedagogical strategies that promote asset oriented pedagogies and student agency. The teacher educatio n course that is the focus of this study emphasizes  project based learning (Krajcik & Blumenfeld, 2005), multicultural pedagogies, and innovation in assessment of  student knowledge. This often contrasts deeply with the gra mmar-of-schooling aligned coaching provided by mentor teachers (Horn et al, 2008).

In this research, we draw connections between Tyack  and Tobin’s grammars of schooling and the tensions that  pre-service teachers must negotiate in order to develop their identities as teachers (Horn et al., 2008). The tensions experienced by pre-service teachers can be seen in the ways that binary thinkin g patterns prevail, even in the face of coursework designed to challeng e the status quo. Through vignette analysis, we bri ng to light the ways in which binary perspectives that reflect the grammar of schoo ling play an integral part in how pre-service teachers take up definitions of “good teach ing” and thus construct their own teacher identitie s in part through the perpetuation of these grammars in their own classrooms. We make sense of the way that PSTs grapple with these conflicting contexts with regards to the ir own identities as teachers through the conceptua lization of figured worlds (Holland et al, 1998; Horn et al, 20 08).  In this paper we show two instances of binari es in PST reflective practices that  exhibit how grammars of schooling are integrated in to teacher identity while different perspectives of schooling experienced during a teac her preparation program are simultaneously negotiat ed into what it means to “be a teacher”.

## Theoretical framework

We draw broadly on various studies that specify the contextual nature of knowledge and identity. We take Gholson and Martin’s (2014) notion of identity as prismatic  to define identity as being made up of complex lay ers of personal positioning and individual relationships a nd are thus deeply influenced by the sociocultural context and participation level of the individual. In line with  Horn et al. (2008), we see the different contexts of teacher education programs as different figured worlds, and  thus important and relevant contexts in which to u nderstand the work of preservice teachers that maintains spec ial significance and attached identities and power structures (Holland et al, 1998; Urrieta, 2007).

The tensions and challenges exhibited by the use of  binaries are analyzed through the lens that PSTs participate in two different figured worlds-socially constructed roles with meaning systems and  symbols for the cultural contexts they encompass: that of the field  and of the certification program (Horn et al, 2008). Horn et al

(2008) connects identity to ontologies of “good tea ching” through identifying that PSTs inhabit two fi gured

worlds. Figured World A: courses where strong bound aries and guidelines di rect PSTs toward specific views of knowing, learning and teaching.  Figured World B: t eaching placements within classrooms where content from their courses must be enacted, but within a context governed by grammars of schooling. PSTs identities as teachers are constructed within and between these two worlds  through integration-taking new ideologies and integrating them into existing identities-and negotiation-changing existing identities to be in line with new  ideologies (Urietta, 2007; Horn et al, 2008). The binaries use d in the PST presentations are understood here as d ialectical representations of the negotiation and integration of what it means to “be a teacher” that PSTs are engaging with. They represent the elements from the figured world B that PSTs are integrating and negotiating into their identities as teachers through texting them in figured world A. We introduce a third figured world, Figured World C: the hybrid or “crucible” space represented by the PST presentations. We posit that in this unique fig ured world, PSTs actively bring experiences, percep tions and knowledge from both Figured World A and B  through the act of reflecting on and recounting the ir teaching experiences (B) within the context of Figured World  A (the teacher education classroom). We use this t hird Figured world to investigate how teachers are making sense of their own teaching and learning practices.

## Methods

A qualitative case study (Yin, 2014) was conducted with data collected through video recording and col lecting documents from student’s reflective practices. Analysis was conducted using techniques from interaction analysis including content logging, collaborative note takin g, and analytic memoing. This study is part of an o ngoing, larger research project that seeks to gather inform ation on the efficacy of a large teacher certificat ion program housed in a large, public university in the Souther n U.S. The goal of the larger research study is to inform the iterative redesign of the program to better prepare  teachers for the multicultural, socio-politically complex environment that is the U.S. public school system.

Data were collected in an “advanced” teaching metho ds class that is the capstone course in the certification sequence, taken directly before stude nt teaching. The course covers the theory and practice behind executing project based curriculum (Krajcik & Blumenfeld, 2005) in 4-12th grade, curricular STEM courses. Data collection occurred during the presentation of fina l reflections on “successes and failures” pre-service teachers experienced while delivering their project based cu rriculum designed during the  class in local, urban, STEM middle and high school classrooms. We drew our cases from over 8 hours of recorded reflective PST presentations which were content logged and transcribed. Cases for analysis were chosen as representative instances of binaries that were verbalized across many cases.

While identity is complex and dynamic, we have chos en to focus on PST identity as teachers. This reflection took the form of a short presentation wh ere teachers discussed what aspects of their field-teach they considered to be successes and failures. We understand these presentations as the crucible in which PSTs engage in the process of figuring out what it means to be a “good teacher” by  negotiating and integrating id eologies of grammar of schooling and the teacher preparation program.

## Data analysis

Select passages were chosen from three student presentations of their successes and failures while teaching a three day unit in high school math and science courses.

Binary 1: Learning/Reinforcing Time AN (High School Computer Science): “Basically how t he class was set up was that the first semester, uh, the mentor teacher like already taught them all the lik e information more or less on like how to code. And  then this entire second semester just them doing projects to solidify their learning…” BR and KU (High School Calculus): “Like this class is like a…study period. Like…a discussion period. And they don't learn, they just kind of build off their learning...” The Grammars of Schooling and other elements from f igured world B are reflected in the ways that the PSTs define certain activities and contexts as bein g part of learning or working-implicitly and explicitly suggesting that learning is not happening  at other  times. The PSTs attribute the class structure to “ how the class was set up” and thus this binary can be attributed to the mentor teacher and the underlying structures  of the US education system.

The use of binaries like AN’s  assessment that the students had already been taught, and were now just “solidifying” their learning and BR and KU’s separation of student class periods as a period where they learn and a period that is “like a study [or] discussion peri od” shows how the PSTs feel that student learning o nly happens at specific times, as determined by the teacher and  the format of the class period. Both of these bina ries reveal PST perceptions that an element of “good teaching” consists of periods of learning and periods of not-learning. Learning versus reinforcing time reflects the way t hat teachers define their role in the classroom, so cioculturally constructing their identity based on how  they interact with students and how they author th eir relationship to their students. The PSTs eng aged in negotiation of their teacher identities thr ough testing this concept in the hybridized figure world C, where it conflicts with the pedagogical methods taught in th e course. The course that comprises figured world A in this case calls for the teacher to step back from a role as “holder of knowledge”, authoring their relationship as one of guide or facilitator of learning, thus losing the “teaching/learning” vs. “reinforcing” differential (Krajcik & Blumenfeld, 2005). This is in opposition to the more traditional teacher role that was modeled by mentor  teachers where the teacher is the “instructor” and  authority figure in the classroom-source of knowledge. This is cyclically reinforced by the grammar of schooling through traditional lecture formats, homework and the division of activities between com prehension versus application and the “right knowledge” and thus success at teaching is defined by the standards. Binary 2: Teacher Descriptions of Students as “Fast” or “Slow” M and Y (High School Chemistry): “And then (it is important to remember that), they're not all gonna be like, as fast as learners (as) some of the ir other students and (it is important to make sure we are)... preparing our lessons to be able to  adapt (for the slow learners)…And then our mentor teacher…did warn us that these students take  a little bit longer to like, understand the concepts and to like grasp everything…(other period s) score like in the high eighties and nineties and our, our class scored in the sixties and seventies, so that's kind of why the teachers have told us like, oh, like these students are as fast learners or stuff like that.” The concept of grading and succeeding/failing to move to the next grade level is a cornerstone of the U.S. public school system and prominent in figured world B. Fro m this, the concept of students being “slow” or “fa st” emerged, reflecting that some studen ts took longer to progress in coursework or grades than others (Tyack & Tobin, 2005). While the likelihood of students bein g held back to repeat a grade has decreased over ti me, the terms associated with it and the emphasis on end of  term exams and passing/failing letter grades and assessment in general has remained. This school grading system is reflected in how teachers talk about their students, showing the way that “good teaching” and one’s identity as a teacher has become associated with student succes s as measured by how quickly students “get it”.  The use of this binary expresses the way the PSTs view learning as a skill or attribute that some students possess to a higher level than others. Its uptake as part of a t eacher’s identity creates a context where teachers are not held entirely responsible fo r the success of their students, as opposed to figured world A which emphasizes pedagogical choice s and asset mindsets that focus on equal learning opportunities and knowledge availability for all students. M and Y engage in the practice of negotiation in  their identities as teachers when reflecting on the binary of “not as fast” learners who may need the lesson a dapted, and the “fast learners” whoneed no additio nal assistance from the other class period. M and Y seem to hesitate to use the binary of fast and slow students as seen in the data when they initially refer to students a s learning “fast”, then shift to attributing the la nguage of “fast” students to their mentor teacher, and proceed to ex plain why the student descriptor was used. We theor ize this hesitation as evidence of PST negotiation and integ ration of grammars of schooling into their own iden tities as teachers and conceptualizations of “good teaching”.

The teacher preparation program in this study prese nts students with an alternative to these grammars that emphasize group homogeneity in learning: Project Based Learning. Labeling learners as “fast and slow” loses meaning when students are given the intellectual and academic freedom to focus on a question they find relevant to themselves and present the knowledge gained in a  way that values all types-not just facts and procedures-of knowledge equally. This shift from ownership of the  binary to attributin g it to a mentor teacher demonstrates negotiation as the PSTs internally challenge the gr ammars of schooling that have become part of how te achers view the students they work with, thus defining their goals as teachers.

## Discussion

While our data does not explicitly show evidence of  pre-service teachers changing their perceptions of what  it means to be a teacher, we suggest that the tensions  and challenges seen in our data exhibited by PSTs as they navigate different educational contexts represents the culmination of the identity work done as PSTs have moved through an educational system bounded by grammars of schooling over their careers as k-12 students, as education students and now as PSTs about to enter classrooms of their own. The data collected over two semesters is rife with examples of binary thinking and real-time identity negotiating in challenging these binaries. For example, another PST took on a contrasting perspective to traditional grammars of schooling when he described his design of an activity that honored different approaches and student work paces as essential to student success. His pre sentation revealed the tensions experienced in Figu red World C. He contradicted this view by falling back on gra mmars of schooling. He re-created the grammar-based binary of fast-slow by identifying the disparities in the time it took students to complete assigned work as one of t he failures of his lesson. This revealed his struggle to reconcile the two figured worlds he occupies as a PST. While Figured world C was originally theorized to b e a hybrid space, the proliferation of binary think ing across PSTs, grade levels and class content demonstrated the overwhelming role that Figured world B plays in how PSTs build their definition of good teaching and what it  means to be a teacher. This is in line with Horn e t al’s (2008) findings that when preservice teachers  are caught between these two contexts, they often align themse lves with their mentor teachers and the status quo.

Further binaries found in the PST presentation data  include: good/bad students, presence/absence of engagement and participation, student understanding  of topics, useful/not useful assessments, and work  being graded/not graded. Each of these binaries includes instances of tension between the traditional grammars they are grounded in and the new ideologies taught in the te acher preparation program meant to disrupt them. In  a future manuscript more extensive analysis will be done ill uminating the ways that these binaries reflect the tensions PSTs wrestle with during their time straddling the multiple worlds that comprise university-based teacher education programs.

Understanding ways in which grammars are perpetuate d by PSTs and in-service teachers as part of identifying as a teacher is crucial to course construction for teacher preparation programs as they seek to redefine teaching to be based on asset-oriented pedagogy, principled improvisation, studen t agency and culturally sustaining pedagogy. Furthermore, understanding the  ways that traditional perceptions of teaching and learning are passed down through the format of teacher prepa ration programs can help teacher-educators to begin dismantling systemic inequities and begin to heal t he damage done to marginalized communities by the p ublic education system in the United States.
