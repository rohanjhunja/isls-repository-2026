# Unpacking Elementary Teacher Interactions in an Asynchronous Online Computational Thinking Professional Development Course

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To support elementary teachers in developing computational thinking (CT) knowledge and pedagogy, professional development (PD) programs must engage them in meaningful and collaborative learning environments. This study examined how elementary teachers interacted in a n asynchronous online CT PD designed to build foundational coding skills and pedagogical strategies situated in a game-design context before integrating it into their classrooms. Using an explanatory mixed-methods approach, we first employed social network analysis to characterize teachers’ interaction patterns across three cohorts and then conducted qualitative analyses of discussion posts to interpret their roles in context. Fi ndings revealed that teachers who actively engaged in programming practice, lesson design, and reflection developed shared understandings of students’ struggles and pedagogical approaches to support student CT learning in classrooms. These results highlight the potential of asynchronous online CT PDs to foster peer-supported reflection and pedagogical reasoning among teachers new to teaching CT.

Integrating computational thinking (CT) into K –12 education is essential for preparing students to thrive in a technology-driven, AI-mediated world (CSTA & AI4K12, 2025; Heintz, 2022). One way of a chieving this goal relies on teachers who understand core CT concepts and integrate them into instruction to expand students’ early CT learning opportunities (e.g., Mouza et al., 2020). Yet many elementary and secondary teachers without computer science (CS) backgrounds have limited exposure to programming or computing (e.g., Yadav et al., 2018), making it challenging to develop both CT knowledge and effective pedagogy (Qian et al., 2018). Teacher professional development (PD) thus plays a central role in supporting CT integration and must engage teachers in active, meaningful learning (Ni et al., 2021). While in-person PD offers immersive experiences, it is resourceintensive and difficult to scale, whereas online and hybrid PDs provide more flexible and accessible opportunities for teacher learning (Webb et al., 2017; Colwell et al., 2024). Teacher learning in online learning environments is inherently social and relies on opportunities for collaboration and reflection (e.g., Rosato et al., 2017). Peer-supported learning, in particular, plays a vital role in supporting teachers’ engagement, reflection, and sense of community within PD contexts (Kellogg et al., 2014). Therefore, designing online PDs, particularly asynchronous ones, that intentionally foster peer-supported learning and social presence is thus crucial, especially for teachers who are new to CS and CT concepts  (Mouza et al., 2021). Despite growing attention to online PD design, limited research has examined how elementary teachers, who are either  new to computing or novice CS teachers, interact with peers and take on different social or pedagogical roles within CT PD environments. Prior work using qualitative case studies and surveys has documented teachers’ perceptions of asynchronous online PD experiences  (e.g., Ma et al., 2025; Mouza et al., 2022), but relatively few studies have investigated the interaction structures and roles that emerge among teachers in online CT courses. Social network analysis (SNA) provides a methodological approach for capturing the structure of learner  interactions, complementing qualitative analysis that can interpret the meanings of these interactions in context (Gašević et al., 2019). Understanding how teachers participate and position themselves in asynchronous online CT PDs can offer insight into how social and cognitive presence develop in these learning environments.

The present study seeks to address this gap by examining the interaction patterns of elementary teachers participating in a n asynchronous online CT PD course. Specifically, this study combines SNA and qualitative pedagogical reflections about CT. Through this mixed-methods approach, we aim to better understand how peersupported learning and professional communities emerge among teachers new to computing through the following research questions:  RQ1: What structural patterns characterize teacher interactions across cohorts in the asynchronous online CT PD?  And RQ2: How do teachers’ interaction roles reflect their engagement with CT concepts and pedagogical reflection in the asynchronous online PD context? 1417 Asynchronous online CT PD game activities for upper elementary teachers This study is situated within a larger research and evaluation project that aims to prepare upper elementary teachers in rural regions with the knowledge of CT, programming skills, and pedagogies for implementing CT in the classroom. The PD consists of two six-unit asynchronous online courses: a beginner course (3D Game Design for Beginners) in the fall semester  and a follow-up advanced course (Advanced 3D Simulation Creation)  in the spring semester. Each course was designed around a game-design context and included conceptual introductions to CS and CT, pedagogical strategies for CT integration, and hands-on activities that enabled teachers to design their own games or simulation projects in a block-based programming environment. Each cohort first completed the beginner course, implemented CT-integrated lessons, and then enrolled in the advanced course. The fully asynchronous PD allowed teachers about eight weeks to complete activities at their own pace, with end-of-unit forums requiring one teacher-initiated response to the discussion prompt and two peer responses. There were one or two facilitators who constantly monitored discussions as needed. Data source and analysis This study examined teacher interactions in the end-of-unit discussion forums of the 3D Game Design for Beginners course (“3D Game Design”), across three cohorts of teachers. Cohort 1 included 19 teachers facilitated by 2 teacher educators  (300 posts), Cohort 2 included 15 teachers with 2 facilitators (21 8 posts), and Cohort 3 included 14 teachers with 1 facilitator (116 posts). All participants were elementary teachers from rural-serving schools in the Midwestern United States. Pre-survey data showed that over 90% were female and White, with an average of 11 years of teaching experience. Most had little or no programming background. O nly 20% had attended prior coding PD, and about 15% had integrated coding activities previously. No significant cohort differences were found on pre-survey measures. We analyzed all discussion posts from Units 2–6 of the 3D Game Design course across three cohorts, excluding facilitator posts, which primarily served as reminders. Discussion data were organized to record who initiated, responded to, and followed up on each post, producing three directed, weighted networks that represented teacher interactions. Using an explanatory mixed-methods design (Creswell & Clark, 2017), we first conducted SNA  to identify interaction patterns and role distributions, then used a qualitative case study to interpret teachers’ engagement through their posts. For RQ1, we used the igraph package in RStudio (Csardi & Nepusz, 2006) to construct and analyze networks, calculating metrics such as density, degree, and reciprocity to describe participation patterns. Drawing on prior relevant research and our review of the teachers’ posts (Kellogg et al., 2014; Gašević et al., 2019), we classified teachers ’ network metrics into five interaction-based roles (Figure 1). For RQ2, we purposefully selected one teacher from each role per cohort for qualitative analysis, examining their posts for evidence of CT understanding, pedagogical reasoning, and peer interaction (Hatch, 2023). Analytic memos and cross-cohort comparisons were documented to capture  how teachers’ discourse reflected evolving perspectives on CT and classroom practice.

## Key findings

RQ1: Overall teacher interactions and roles across the three cohorts 1418 Figure 2 summarizes the overall network characteristics for the three cohorts of teachers who participated in the 3D Game Design PD, as well as the distribution of interaction roles across the three cohorts.  The distribution of teacher roles in 3D Game Design PD shows different interaction patterns through various roles observed by each cohort. Cohort 1, with the largest and most active network (19 teachers, 101 directed edges; density =  0.30), indicates a moderately connected community where multiple teachers exchanged posts and replies. O ver half of the participants were identified as networks (~53%), indicating a preference for sharing ideas broadly without engaging in mutual exchanges.  Cohort 2 had the highest network density (0.36), comprising 15 teachers and 76 edges, indicating balanced and cohesive engagement. Most teachers (~80%) actively exchanged ideas with peers reciprocally. Cohort 3 was the smallest and least connected network  (14 teachers, 30 directed edges; density = 0.16), exhibiting more limited communication and fewer reciprocal exchanges among participants. This cohort included a mixture of reciprocators (~36%), contributors (~14%), and networkers (14%), and had a higher proportion of isolators (~29%) compared to the other cohorts. Overall, even with the same PD structure, the three networks varied in size, density, and interaction patterns, reflecting differences in how teachers engaged with others during online discussions.

RQ2: Vignettes of teacher roles in asynchronous online CT PD on 3D game design The vignettes below are representative cases from each role across cohorts that illustrate how teachers expressed their understanding of CT, pedagogical reasoning, and peer collaboration through their online posts. Reciprocators: Across all cohorts, reciprocators formed multiple mutual ties with other teachers, engaging in discussions that involved various CT practices and pedagogies. In Cohort 1, Teacher K formed mutual ties with four other teachers in the same cohort. In one of her mutual posts with another teacher who discussed the need for students to do more real-world problems, Teacher K noted that her students often get overwhelmed by even just simple problems, and hoped coding would offer a more meaningful learning experienc e, to which a peer teacher responded that coding can serve as both an educational tool and a reward that students enjoy. In Cohort 2, Teacher R built mutual ties with four teachers, and their interactions centered on agreement and extending viewpoints on how to teach and assess CT in classrooms. In one of the follow-up posts of Teacher R, she responded to a teacher that “I like that you thought of using the game as a teaching tool as well. As students go through the process of making the game you could talk about what could happen or is not happening at certain points and talk about why or the results of it. ” The teacher, she responded, also replied that “It looks like the CTPA tool will be useful to see an overview of all the students' projects and quickly note which students seem to be missing out on some of the important Computational Thinking concepts.” In Cohort 3, Teacher RH formed ties with two teachers with an agreement that CT is more than problem-solving alone, extending into perseverance, critical thinking, and confidence that can prepare them to succeed in the STEM field. Teacher RH also often react ed to and/or shared practical strategies with the other teachers about how to teach CT, especially on the technical part of coding, such as peer code checking, practicing IF/THEN commands, which are new to students. Such posts revealed that reciprocators’ exchanges of discussions often reflected shared reflection on CT concepts and mutual support for pedagogical strategies.

Networkers: Across the three cohorts, networker teachers actively responded to others’ posts and received comments from other teachers, without forming any mutual ties at all. Teacher RHL in Cohort 1 interacted with nine  different teachers to discuss the importance of CT and how to teach it in the classrooms. From the posts she sent out, the contents were about reflecting her view of CT as a cross-disciplinary skill-building practice and also discussed personal struggles with coding to reflect her reflections and growt h while learning coding as a new skillset. Moreover, she has widely endorsed strategies to teach CT, ranging from project-based learning to integrating CT 1419 into other subject areas. She also received  responses from peer teachers who  shared their own struggles with coding and debugging, and ways to teach CT in authentic classrooms. Teacher LP in Cohort 2 interacted with eight different teachers. She actively responded to others’ posts on the importance of CT and the  transferability of CT across subjects. Her posts often agreed with and/or extended others’ viewpoints on teaching strategies, such as game-based learning, formative assessment, and the importance  of maintaining student engagement. Her interactions reflected that she was more interested in student learning and student access to computing beyond classroom settings. Teacher W in Cohort 3 interacted with six  teachers in her cohort. Based on her interactions with other teachers, she endorsed the gaming approach to promote students’  problem-solving, perseverance and creativity. Her posts also advocated using various strategies to teach CT through project-based learning and guided inquiry. She also acknowledged the potential struggle of student learning about coding by sharing her experience: “I can see how students would feel this way at first. I also felt this way about coding at first, but now I see that it can be done and it is a manageable thing to learn. I hope that students will practice this and find the same thing to be true. ”  These teachers’ participation as networkers showed active outreach and a reflective stance on CT pedagogy.

Contributors: Contributors consistently initiated or responded to peers’ posts but received no replies. Teacher SM in Cohort 1 responded to nine  other teachers’ posts without receiving anything from others. Her posts covered a variety of topics on the importance of CT, as well as emphasizing the importance of trail-and-error learning connecting with her own struggles in programming and her student  experiences and framed learning  as a co-experience between teachers and students: “I think it is wonderful when we can learn from our students and all become more competent together!”. Her teaching strategies entered active learning to allow students to engage in game-based learning while adding scaffolding and peer-to-peer support. Teacher MS in Cohort 2 interacted with five other teachers in the cohort. Her responses to others’ posts revealed her interests on the implementation of CT and game design in classroom settings, along with her pedagogical reflections  (“I love how you used independent thinkers. Many students can’t problem solve on their own so guided inquiry will be very helpful to help them learn how to computationally think.”) Similarly, Teacher SL in Cohort 3 interacted with five  other teachers in the cohort  on topics centering pedagogical ideas and reflections (“I agree that using my own Frogger game will help the students walk through the process in a guided way. I also made mistakes and it will help them to see how to work through those problems. ”). These teachers’ posts often focused on topics around the pedagogical content knowledge, which combined their own learning experience on programming, as well as how to teach CT and programming in the classroom context, and engage students in learning. Receivers and Isolators: Receivers (in Cohorts 2 and 3) and Isolators (in Cohort 3) participated only in required prompts without further interaction. Their posts typically framed CT as a general problem-solving process connected to other subjects but offered little elaboration or peer exchange.

This study examined elementary teachers’ interactions in an asynchronous 3D Game Design CT PD, where programming and reflection activities fostered engagement with both CT content and pedagogical knowledge (Qian et al., 2018).  Engaging teachers in student-facing CT activities allowed them to experience the potential challenges and problem-solving processes their students might face  during computing (McElhaney et al., 2025). This experiential perspective appeared to prompt teachers to reflect on how to make programming and CT more accessible and meaningful for their own students  through their self-reflection, the articulation of pedagogical ideas, and a desire for peer feedback and affirmation in their discussion posts. F indings resulting from an explanatory mixed-methods approach illustrate how asynchronous online PD can serve as a productive space for teachers to develop CT understanding while building a sense of professional community. Even though patterns of participation varied across cohorts, teachers’ engagement reflected evolving conceptions of CT as a combination of problem-solving, creativity, and instructional design. By highlighting how teachers interact, reflect, and support one another in virtual spaces, this study contributes to understanding how asynchronous online PDs can foster both conceptual growth and pedagogical agency among elementary teachers new to computing. Future research could further explore how these social learning patterns influence teachers’ implementation of CT in classroom practice and student learning outcomes.
