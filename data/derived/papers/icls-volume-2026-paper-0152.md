# Simulations to Facilitate AI Literacy for Teachers and School Leaders

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We developed a novel web-based simulation targeting the concept of feed-forward neural networks for an adult educator audience comprising two distinct cohorts of teacher s (N = 24) and school leaders (N = 23). Both cohorts showed gains in their conceptual knowledge from pretest to posttest, with the school leaders cohort improving relatively more (Cohen’s d = 0.40). Comparative analyses suggested that using our simulation led to lower perceptions of usability and learning effectiveness in school leaders relative to teachers, but higher actual a s well as normalized learning gains on the administered performance assessments. These results highlight a perception-performance discrepancy when learners grapple with less-than-perfectly-intuitive interfaces, which despite resulting in low fluency, lead to better learning.

Providing inquiry-focused learning experiences that allow educators to work with and probe  the black box of AI algorithms is paramount to developing their AI literacy (Bearman  & Ajjawi, 2023; Du et al., 2024; Stenberg et al., 2025). The current landscape of AI education, however, falls short of addressing the foundational learning needs of adult educators, specifically teachers and school leaders, so that they can c onfidently talk about AI (Sperling et al., 2024). We conjecture that if educators have the opportunity to experiment, observe, and explore the boundary conditions of when AI algorithms succeed and fail, their grasp on abstract AI concepts will improve, in turn better preparing them to orchestrate AI li teracy in their classrooms (Sinha, 2025a; Sinha, 2025b). That is precisely the present goal, where we introduce a simulation-focused approach to help educators with little  / no technical backgrounds understand and visualize how neural networks process information and learn from data. Our simulation is designed to provide immediate feedback, personalize inquiry, and scale cost-effectively. Theoretical and empirical background Web-based simulations ha ve a longstanding history in the learning sciences (De Jong & Van Joolingen, 1998). These interactive digital environments allow for active knowledge construction and experiential learning, while affording scaffolded inquiry with otherwise unobservable phenomena, hypothesis testing, and cognitive load management through progressive complexity (Quintana et al., 2004). For instance, PhET (Wieman et al., 2008) is one highly successful class of web-based simulations for K-12 STEM education. When it comes to teaching AI, however, although there exist  a few online and offline (e.g., role-playing) simulations designed to cater to students’ understanding of AI algorithms (offered as part of curricula like AI4K12, DAILY, e.g., Lee & Ali, 2021) as well as professional development programs to facilitate teachers’ AI literacy (e.g., Nazaretsky et al., 2022; Younis, 2024), we lack simulations grounded in  locally-relevant contexts that directly target educators  in developing technological AI foundations. Within teacher education, empirical evidence suggests that simulations hold high potential to develop complex skills like problem-solving (e.g., performing diagnoses), classroom management, and communication (Hedge’s g = 0.84, 95% CI [0.28; 1.69], see Chernikova et al., 2020). Here, we tap on th is demonstrated learning potential of simulations to develop a novel web-based interface introducing teachers and school leaders toneural networks. Existing work like Smilkov & Carter (n.d.) that focuses on neural network playgrounds for image recognition problems and/or abstract datasets  missing real-world relevance, we posit, lacks appropriate contextualization that educators need to engage in sensemaking with the  AI algorithm. Here, we allow educators to reflect on the relevance of AI decisions within an authentic school problem context.

## Participants

We engaged two cohort s of participants as part of regular coursework at a teacher training institute – (i) N = 24 teachers enrolled in a graduate certificate in AI for education, and (ii) N = 23 school leaders enrolled in a leadership programme on AI for education for middle managers. The majority of participants had completed a postgraduate 1242 diploma in education, with teaching experiences spanning primary, secondary, and postsecondary education. The study was approved by the ethics commission of Nanyang Technological University (IRB-2025-006). Development of the web-based simulation Our newly designed simulation focused on the foundational AI algorithm of (feed-forward) neural networks. This introductory topic was chosen by a team comprising a domain expert, a software developer, and three learning sciences researchers. Following best practices for developing scalable simulations (Adams et al., 2008a; 2008b), we began by creating specific learning goals drawing on existing literature (e.g., Laupichler et al., 2022, Wollowski 2025) and our own experiences of teaching AI. After an initial layout was sketched for expected functionalities, most critically the ability for participants to self-construct the network representation, we chose a topic that teachers and school leaders frequently grapple with – whoneeds help at school? A curated mock dataset of n = 50 students mimicking a locally  relevant context was used in the backend. After iterating  on the user-interface and operational feasibility of the simulation, we developed an initial prototype (see Figure 1), which afforded the following possibilities – (i) choosing pre-defined nodes of the input layer, (ii) creating new nodes in hidden layers that were weighted combinations of these input nodes, (iii) full flexibility in adjusting all weights and biases across the network, and (iv) viewing simulation results via a confusion matrix flagging (in)accurate predictions, with the ability to click on individual student reports, examine neurons, and perform error analysis. ## Study design

We started by first introducing the prerequisite vocabulary of nodes, layers (input, output, hidden), activation functions (e.g., those relevant for classification such as sigmoid), and bias, which was needed to interact with our web-based simulation. This  foundational knowledge briefing also used analogies (e.g., teaching a child how to throw a ball in basket) to explain how neural networks are trained to make predictions via feeding input data, calculating errors, and updating weights. Everyone subsequently completed a 15-item pretest designed to capture their baseline understanding of the learning topic. In the next phase, we introduced our simulation and posed the challenge question where everyone was asked to construct their own neural network to make predictions about whoneeds help at school. We laid out the expectation that their goal was to try and explore different features of the simulation freely, understand the nature of (in)correct predictions, and attempt  to improve predictions from the neural network by iterating on the network architecture at least a few times. Once participants completed this phase, they answered an identical 15-item posttest, followed by a survey capturing their perceptions of usability and learning effectiveness on a five-point Likert scale. Our usability survey comprised six  closed-ended items (e.g., “It was easy to find my way around and navigate through different parts of the simulation”, “The visual 1243 design of the simulation (e.g., colors, layout, readability) was appealing”) and three open-ended items focusing on (i) what was difficult or confusing, (ii) what was helpful or intuitive in the simulation’s interface, and (iii) what would be their suggested changes to the simulation’s user experience. Internal consistency of these closed-ended items was acceptable for both our samples of teachers (Cronbach 𝛼𝛼 = 0.81) as well as school leaders (Cronbach 𝛼𝛼 = 0.61). Our learning effectiveness survey comprised seven closed-ended items (e.g., “ The simulation helped me understand the fundamental concept of how a neural network learns from data”, “The non-technical approach of the simulation (e.g., visual elements, simplified language) was very effective for my learning ”) and two openended items focusing on (i) what were the insights gained about how neural networks work, and (ii) how might this understanding influence their approach to teaching topics related to data and AI in classrooms. Internal consistency was good across both samples – teachers (Cronbach 𝛼𝛼 = 0.89), school leaders (Cronbach 𝛼𝛼 = 0.82).

## Research questions

RQ1: How does the use of web-based simulations affect participants’ learning from pretest to posttest? RQ2: How do learning gains compare to perceptions of usability and learning effectiveness of the simulation? Two paired samples t-tests comparing differences from pretest to posttest were used to answer RQ1 for our teacher and school leader cohorts separately. Those scoring the maximum at pretest were excluded from this analysis (n = 4 out of 47 participants). For RQ2, independent samples t-tests were used to perform comparative analyses of learning gains, perceptions of usability, and perceptions of learning effectiveness across our two cohort samples. We computed both actual learning gains (posttest minus pretest) as well as a normalized version (posttest minus pretest divided by the difference between the maximum possible score and pretest). Changes from pretest to posttest influenced by the simulation (RQ1) Our cohort of teachers showed an improvement of Cohen’s d = 0.24 from pretest (M = 13.04, SE = 0.25) to posttest (M = 13.55, SE  = 0.28), t (21) = 1.14, p = 0.27. Despite starting at a lower baseline, the school leaders cohort showed a larger improvement of Cohen’s d = 0.40 from pretest (M = 12.14, SE = 0.34) to posttest (M = 12.81, SE = 0.40), t(20) = 1.85, p = 0.08. One key challenge  stemmed from inaccessibility of certain interface functions on devices with smaller screen s (e.g., scrolling, activating tooltips). However, participants found several features helpful and intuitive, including the visual representation of strengths and areas for improvement on the confusion matrix page, clear depiction of the layered network structure, and the straightforward output that indicated efficacy. They also appreciated the step-by-step tutorial presented at the start, and the intuitive selection of nodes and data inputs, which contributed to the simulation’s user-friendly interface. Learning effectiveness was also perceived as lower in the school leader cohort (M = 3.07, SE  = 0.11) relative to the teachers cohort (M = 3.46, SE  = 0.14), t(45) = - 2.17, p = 0.03, Cohen’s d = -0.63. Challenges stemmed from the necessity of selecting correct input features to achieve more accurate outputs, potentially insufficient data sources and network layers, and a lack of time in f ully grasping the simulation’s potential. 1244 However, some perceptions of improved understanding of neural networks were driven by insights into how predictions are made through adjusting weights and biases, the power of school-relevant data like result slips, and the importance of authentic locally relevant examples and patterns for generalization in machine learning. Despite the lower perception of usability and learning effectiveness,  normalized learning gains were in fact higher in the school leader s cohort (M = 0.18, SE = 0.14) compared to the teachers cohort (M = -0.13, SE = 0.28), t(41) = 0.95, p = 0.35, Cohen’s d = 0.29. Figure 3 Inferential Statistics (M, SE)  for Perceptions of Usability and Learning Effectiveness, along with Learning Gains Two contributions of our exploratory study are worth noting. First, as learning design contribution, we showcased how intuitive web-based simulations grounded in locally-relevant data contexts can make black box AI algorithms like neural networks accessible and authentic for educators without a technical background. Second, as empirical contributions (RQ1, RQ2), our results highlighted a discrepancy between perception and performance for the two distinct cohorts of teachers and school leaders. Learning sciences research on desirable difficulties (Bjork & Bjork,

2011) and productive failure (Sinha & Kapur, 2021) show that fluency can often be mistaken for a fleeting feeling

of understanding, and that messier, more effortful learning experiences result in greater conceptual knowledge gains (Deslauriers et al., 2019). For the teachers cohort, our simulation presumably presented a low extraneous load and was a comfortable, easy-to-use experience, leading to high usability ratings. This comfort, however, could have minimized germane load (Kalyuga & Singh, 2016), resulting in comparatively less substantial learning gains. For school leaders, we posit that the simulation may have forced them to engage in a deeper connection building to understand critical features of neural networks, ultimately leading to more aversive perceptions of mental effort (David et al., 2024) but greater learning gains. Intrinsic differences in our learner profiles could offer an alternative explanation for our results – while school leaders may have been approaching the simulation from a strategic or managerial perspective (forcing them to engage with its concrete mechanics in a new way), teachers’ perceptions may have  been driven narrowly by alignment with their teaching frameworks and relevance for classroom application (resulting in a more confirmatory rather than a transformative learning experience). Limitations of our work stem from small sample size, which we will address by continuing to administer these simulations across our teacher training initiatives in AI and data literacy.
