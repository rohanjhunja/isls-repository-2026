# Expansive Learning for Enacting Utopias: Theoretical and Practical Advances

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This symposium brings together leading scholars from four continents to explore the potential of expansive learning in enacting utopias, understood as practical alternatives to the logic of profit and exploitation. By drawing on the theoretical framework of cultural-historical activity theory (CHAT), the symposium highlights the role of expansive learning in addressing societal challenges and fostering social change. The contributions presented in this symposium showcase a diverse range of case studies, including community-based initiatives, formative interventions, and social movements. These studies demonstrate how expansive learning can be used to challenge the status quo, enact alternative futures, and create more just and equitable societies.

## Introduction

In line with the theme of the conference, this symposium presents cutting-edge research on expansive learning for enacting utopias. The theory of expansive learning (Engeström, 2015; Engeström & Sannino, 2010) is a unifying conceptual framework of the contributions. This theory proposes that learning collectively ‘what is not yet there’ can play a critical role in just societal transform ations. Expansive learning is triggered by contradi ctions that require qualitative change in the involved activity  systems. The process begins with actions of questi oning the existing state of the activity and proceeds through  the actions of analysis, modeling, examining the m odel, implementation, and consolidation. In an expansive learning cycle, the object of the activity is expanded along socio-spatial, temporal and ethical-political dimensions, resulting in a new concept of the activity. Utopias are traditionally defined as images of a  perfect society in which  people work well with each other and are happy. In contrast, in line with Eric Olin Wright (2020), we understand utopias as ways to build and sustain forms of activity that serve the common good and offer alternatives to the logic of profit and exploitation. Such utopias are not isolated islands or perfect wo rlds of happiness. They are inherently contradictor y as they struggle to exist and flourish in the midst of a wo rld dominated by global capitalism. The verb ‘to en act’ means to put something into practice or to put something into action. It is not sufficient to talk about env isioning or dreaming about alternatives. Our symposium focuses on learning processes that involve practical action s for material transformations (Sannino, 2020).

The four papers of the symposium represent importan t theoretical and practical advances in research on expansive learning. By examining a broad range of activities, they highlight the potentials of the fourth generation of cultural-historical activity theory which focuses on expansive learning in heterogenous coalitions facing critical societal challenges. A twin symposium will focus on methodological challenges such advances bring about. In the first paper of the symposium, Annalisa Sanni no argues for the significance of the concepts of enactment and utopia for the learning sciences, usi ng three examples, namely the organization ‘Emergen cy’ that provides medical care for civilian victims of wars and natural disasters, the Finnish Housing First policy aimed at the eradication of homelessness, and the MST Landless Workers Movement in Brazil. In the second paper, Aydin Bal analyzes the design and implementation of a new  culturally responsive behavioral support system wi th the help of a formative intervention enacted at a rural High School serving Anishinaabe youth from a tribal reservation in Wisconsin. In the third paper, Mutizwa Mukute pr esents a longitudinal study conducted in sustainabl e agriculture workplace contexts in southern Africa, fo cusing on how contradictions were used as sources o f expansive learning and change. In the fourth paper,  Jesica de la Torre analyzes the cycles of expansiv e learning accomplished by communities in the Valles region of  Jalisco, Mexico, in which residents have been orga nizing since 2015 to defend the water of their hydrological basin. The presenters come from four continents and include both senior and junior scholars. The discussants, Kris Gutiérrez and Marco Querol, bring to the sympos ium their extensive experience and insights from both the global north and the global south. Activity-centred enactment for a just renewal of the learning sciences: A perspective and examples of utopias in the making Annalisa Sannino Developments in the past decade of nascent fourth-generation cultural-historical activity theory (CHAT-4G) bring together the Vygotskian-Leont'evian and Davydovian scholarly traditions, which have led to the conceptual framework of expansive learning, with cognitive, philosophical and sociological approaches to enactment and utopias. These developments give evidence that what CHAT refers to as enacted utopias (Sannino, 2020, 2024) face struggles that often go unnoticed and remain unsupported. This paper presents arguments substantiated by empirical examples explaining why these developments matter for the learning sciences to sustain just solutions to global crises. More specifically this paper argues why and how CHAT-4G studies of expansive learning can contribute to renewing the learning sciences toward greater academic and societal impact. The paper will proceed in two steps.

As the first step, the paper delves into what enactment means from a CHAT-4G activity-centered view and what this perspective adds to conceptualizations of enactment in the learning sciences (Poizat et al, 2024). In CHAT-4G, perspectives from embodied and distributed cognition (Sengupta & Koh,  2022), constructivist sociology of science and philosophy of technology, and the study of real utopias (Wright, 2020) are brought into dialogue with expansive learning with a focus on the concept of enactment. The above-mentioned perspectives highlight the importance of deepening our understanding of the relationship between processes of agenc y for change and hidden, unrecognized and even suppressed  forms of power (Sannino, 2023). This paper proposes an enriched conceptualization that includes transforma tive agency by double stimulation and generation of  objectoriented power as inherent qualities of expansive learning. As the second step, this conceptualization is tested  by exploring how h eterogenous coalitions come together to productively contribute to the making o f a just and sustainable world.  Three examples of enacted utopias are examined by means of identifying expansive learning cycles that have shaped their enactment. The three examples are: 1) the organization Emergency, offering free medical care by top practitioners with cutting-edge facilities for civilian victims of war s and natural disasters, 2) the Finnish Housing First policy aimed at the eradication of homelessness, and 3) the MST Landless Workers’ Movement in Brazil. The expansive cycles in each case are examined with the help of historical studies and documents, including participants’ personal accounts.

The paper shows how the conceptually enriched CHAT-4G perspective on expansive learning helps to identify enacted utopias, how it enables grasping learning processes that shape them, how it affords understanding the dynamics of becoming of such utopias as well as developing ways to support them. The paper concludes with an outline of next steps for strengthening both res earch and practical engagement in expansive learning in the learning sciences in support of enacted utopias for social-justice world-making. Learning as kin-making: Enacted utopias on a damaged planet Aydin Bal Schools have been sites of Indigenous suffering whe rein the forced notions of settler-colonial ontology, epistemology, and axiology operate in a system of p unishment and exclusion (Brayboy, 2005). In the Uni ted States, American Indian students experience h arsher and more frequent exclusionary discipline and are disproportionately placed in special education as e motionally disturbed (Bal et al., 2019; Whitford et  al., 2019).

To address racial disproportionality in school and heal the historical trauma together, the Indigenous  Learning Lab (ILL), a four-year-long formative intervention, was enacted between 20 19 and 2024 at Northwoods High School (NHS), a rural high school in Wisconsin serv ing Anishinaabe youth from a nearby tribal reservat ion in Wisconsin.

NHS is in a lakeside town that was a battleground o ver Anishinaabe traditional spearfishing, publicly well-known as the “Walleye War” of the 1980s and early 1 990s (Nesper, 2002). White settlers expressed the inherent motivation for the extinction of Indige nous people with ecofascist chants such as “Kill an  Indian save a Walleye,” claiming. Indigenous people destroy natur e as Indigenous families engage in cultural practic es (e.g., spearfishing and wild rice harvesting). Traumatic s cars of racism and violence surrounding the Walleye War left deep mistrust between Indigenous and non-Indigenous people. ILL functioned as a community-led, problem-solving process through which local stakeholders an alyze systemic challenges in the existing school system a nd design a new culturally responsive behavioral su pport system. ILL sought a cross-pollination among Cul tural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT), decolonizing methodologies (Brayboy, 2013; Smith, 2012), and Anishinaabe and other Indigenous cosmologies (Kopenewa & Albert, 2013) to create systemic changes driven by leveraging settler colonial local histories, experi ential knowledge(s), and Indigenous futurity of school stakeholders. As each research site involves a unique intersection of historical, geospatial, sociopolitical, and cult ural factors, ILL’s collaborative theoretical stanc e centers on Indigenous culture and ways of being, affirming the  local tribe’s le gal right to self-determination and equal participation within public schools serving reservation youth.

## Methods and data

ILL members included Indigenous youth, parents, edu cators, leaders, and White-settler teachers and administrators across four academic years. ILL involved three phases: Design, implementation, and sustainability. We generated data through video recordings of month ly ILL sessions, follow-up interviews, participant observations, and document analyses. The data supported identifying expansive learning actions, collective agency, and emergent shifts in educational practices and power dynamics.

## Findings

Centering questions of hegemony, settler colonialism, and capitalist relations to each other and nature, ILL responded to the histories of inequality and oppres sion, hunted landscapes, and shaped multispecies re lations. In the design phase, members engaged in seven expansiv e actions: Forming ILL, questioning, analyzing, mod eling the new system, examining, planning for implementation, and reflecting on the ILL process and outcomes. There were three phases: Designing the new system, partia l implementation, and The ILL members engaged in ei ght expansive actions in the implementation phase: Form ing the implementation team, setting the goals of implementation (finalizing the new system and incre asing buy-in), creating the schoolwide values and expectations, presenting the new system to staff fo r their feedback, finalizing the system, redefining  the goals of the implementation (visibility, phased implementati on, and clarifying the new system), and presenting the improved draft to the staff. The school started implementing the system in the 2022-2023 academic year. Members designed a sympoietic behavioral support system as a situated, inclusive, dynamic, responsive, and his torical collective activity system. The systemic innovations have already generated positive results in school climate, discipline, and academic achievements (Bal & Bird Bear, 2023; Ko, Bal, Mawene, Orie, Yoo, & Schrader, 2025). The ILL facilitator was elected as the principal. T he school’s leadership has modeled after ILL and ut ilized distributive leadership to make every decision with the school's teacher, student, and community representatives. ILL’s explicit decolonial acts to support tribal so vereignty included basing team interactions in Indigenous members’ cultural practices, evidenced using sm udging, pipe ceremony, and interactional norms based on The Seven Grandfather Teachings: Wisdom, L ove, Respect, Bravery, Honesty, Humility, and Truth (Benton-Banai, 1988). In Anishinaabe cosmology, nature is their first teacher. Therefore, the new system included new academic and behavioral support programs focusing on collaboration-instead of competition, asset-instead of deficit, and learning with Nature informed by Indigenous practices, knowledge, and epistemologies.

## Discussion: Learning as Kin-Making

This work contributes to the learning sciences by t heorizing learning as kin-making. As the last two timescapes, Anthropocene and Capitaloce, brought irreversible d amage to the planet and the landscapes of humans and nonhuman species (Tsing et al., 2017), any attempts to become with, relay, create enacted utopias on a damaged planet require activity-producing activities that create expansive objects and sympathetic systems inclusively build around the shared expansive objects in the ru ins of human exceptionalism and ut ilitarian individualism (Engeström, 2024; Sannino, 2023). I call this proce ss learning kin-making, that is, ethical and political activities of building alliances of multiple species, histories, and futures, caring for the planet, and healing, living, and living collectively in a complex, entangled world (Harraway, 2016). The Earth is in the throes of a sixth mass extincti on, propelled by the forces of capitalism. In respo nse, we must turn to kin-making as a mode of learning —becoming with —through situated pedagogies, play, and cosmological enactments, or a kind of Duende: an alliance of human and more-than-human beings, moving through shadow, scar, and struggle, summoned only i n the presence of death (Garcia Lorca, 2023; Harawa y, 2016). The ILL exemplifies how expansive learning p rocesses can be reoriented through decolon ial and Indigenous frameworks to foster kin-making as a pedagogical and political project.

By centering Indigenous epistemologies and honoring  local histories, the intervention offered not only  systemic redesign but also a radical reimagining of what lea rning can become in service of justice, healing, and planetary care. ILL processes promoted organic growth imbued with t he spirit of socially just human interactions cente red around a shared object, the creation, and implementation of the new school system, which includes transformative possibilities for all youth to heal with hózhǫ́ (Clayton Long, 2022; Reed, 2021) - survive and thrive collaboratively with joy in ever-evolving contradictions and contaminations on a damaged planet. Expansive learning for the defense of water and life Jesica de la Torre Currently, in Mexico, we are facing complex issues such as poverty, insecurity, and inequality, which limit the quality of life for those of us who inhabit this land, compounded by global problems like climate change and the consequences of the capitalist system. Within this complexity, there are some cracks where it is possible to create community proposals to address certain elements that make up this web of problems; in our case, processes to defend water, territory, and life.

Cultural-historical activity theory as a framework has helped foster processes in which the communities, I work with can exercise their agency by organizing themselves to achieve common goals, as occurs in three cases in which I am currently collaborating. I will focus the analysis on the longest-running case, which was researched with a case study method (de la Torre, 2022) guided  by the question How has collective learning been b uilt by territory defenders in the Mexican Valles region? D uring the research  it was found that collective learning is a type of learning that, due to the way it is constru cted, has gone unnoticed in the world of educationa l research, since its processes are long-term, and its manifestations are often ethereal (En geström, 2015). However, its existence has allowed groups, communities and organ izations to face the problems of their context and move towards their utopias (Sannino, 2020). As shown bel ow, from the proposals of the cultural-historical activity theory, whose postulates contributed to the understanding of this type of learning. In 10 communities in the Valles region of Jalisco, Mexico, various groups of residents have been organizing since 2015 to defend the water of their hydrological basin. What began with a group of fish ermen addressing a specific socio-environmental crisis du e to the pollution of a dam has evolved into a netw ork of community water monitors. They have been trained and follow up with standardized tools and procedures on water quality in their regions to document results and present them as complaints and legal p rocesses that allow them to reorganize water and territory management, both through state channels and social organization. In this case, expansive learning can be observed in the three dimensions proposed by Engeström (2016): a socio-spatial expansion, visible with the original group of fishermen broadening the scope of their efforts, both with neighboring communities and other individuals,  groups, and institutions, now involving participat ion from 4 municipalities. The temporal dimension is evident  in two elements: the use of the past as an aspirat ion that serves as a motivation for acting in the present and projecting into the future, as well as the sustained organized struggle over time —10 years now. Finally, in the ethical-political dimension, expansion has been observed through changes in the way participants have deepen ed their understanding of the problems they face and the implications that it has for the region's life, whether they act on it or not. The utopia these communities pursue is to regain clean water and restore territories, mana ging them by themselves. However, they face pressur e from companies and governments due to the demand for wat er from neighboring cities, which makes it difficul t to achieve their goals, which seem increasingly complex and distant. Understanding how collective and expansive learning  is constructed opens up a space to understand social transformations from the socio-cognitive learning processes on which they are base d, offering some guidelines that will allow different groups to iden tify whether the way in which they are carrying out  their processes can be sustained or needs to be modified based on some learning action that complements thei r work. This becomes important in social contexts such as t he Mexican one, in which different sec tors, social organizations and educational institutions seek ways to respond in an organized way to the demands for security, justice and a dignified life.
