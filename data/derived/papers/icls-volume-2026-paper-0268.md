# How Educational Practitioners Envision Employment-Focused Transition Services for Students with Disabilities: Insights from a Game-Based Activity

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores how educational practitioners conceptualize employment‑focused transition services for students with disabilities through a collaborative, game‑based planning activity. Drawing on a fictional case study of a high school student, practitioners worked in cross‑role teams to design a four‑year plan to document how they can support the student’s transition from high school into adulthood. Analysis of 217 practitioner contributions, coded using Lee and Carter’s (2012) framework of high‑qualit y employment transition services, revealed that career development experiences and customized transition supports were most frequently emphasized, while family support, job‑related supports, and self‑determination appeared less consistently and often only in response to roadblocks. These findings align with established predictors of post‑school success but also highlight gaps in proactive family engagement, and employer and interagency collaboration. The study demonstrates how designed, game‑based environme nts can surface practitioner reasoning, mediate professional learning, and inform the design of more inclusive, employment‑focused transition services.

Employment is closely tied to well-being and sense of self-worth, meaning that limited access to quality jobs can negatively affect mental health, life satisfaction, and overall quality of life (Konrad et al., 2013; Schmitt et al., 2014). Students with disabilities, however, face significant barriers as they transition from secondary school into the workforce (Goodall et al., 2022), as evident from recent labor statistics: In 2024, unemployment rates for disabled youth aged 16-19 and 20-24 were 23.9% and 13.3%, compared to 12.3% and 7.1% for their non-disabled peers (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024).

Schools and the transition services they provide play a critical role in preparing students with disabilities for employment (Goodall et al., 2022). High-quality, consistent support within educational institutions has been shown to improve post-school employment outcomes and facilitate a smoother transition to adulthood (Mazzotti et al., 2021). As a result, educational practitioners are central to helping students navigate this transition and access meaningful employment opportunities.

For this paper, we engaged educational practitioners in a collaborative, game‑based planning activity designed to elicit their perspectives on effective transition supports that can strengthen employment outcomes for students with disabilities. This work c ontributes to the learning sciences by examining how educational practitioners conceptualize their role in shaping students’ employment pathways and highlighting how their insights can inform the design of more responsive and effective transition services. According to the Individuals with Disabilities Education Improvement Act (IDEIA, 2004), transition services are coordinated, results-oriented activities that support movement from school to post-school life. These services must be individualized to students’ strengths, preferences, and interests, and require collaboration among schools, families, and community agencies to promote meaningful adult outcomes (IDEIA, 2004). A substantial body of research identifies several key predictors of post-school employment success for students with disabilities, including early work experiences, self-determination, family engagement, interagency collaboration, and employment-related skill development (Carter et al., 2012; Hirano et al., 2018; Mazzotti et al., 2021; Shogren & Plotner, 2012; Test et al., 2009; Wehmeyer & Palmer, 2003). This robust evidence base provides strong empirical grounding for the framework advanced by Lee and Carter (2012), who outline seven components of high-quality employment transition services: individualized planning, early work experiences, interagency collaboration, family supports, self-determination, social and employment-related skill instruction, and job-related supports. Together, these components emphasize that effective transition services require intentional, evidence-based strategies that integrate individualized planning, family and community engagement, and sustained 1827 workplace support. For clarity and consistency, we refer to the seven parts of Lee and Carter’s (2012) framework as “components,” and use this term throughout the paper to describe the categories guiding our coding and Given this complexity, game‑based design activities function as a method that structures how participants engage with and think about complex problems of practice. In the learning sciences, such activities help organize interaction, surface assumptions, and support new forms of reasoning (Horn & Little, 2017; Penuel et al., 2011). Through rules, roles, and representational tools, games both constrain and expand participants’ sensemaking, enabling participants to externalize thinking, negotiate perspectives, and test ideas in low‑stakes but consequential ways (Abdul Jabbar & Felicia, 2015; DeLiema et al., 2019). This makes game‑based activities a productive method for examining how practitioners deliberate about transition supports. Guided by the substantive framework of employment transition services (Lee & Carter, 2012) and the methodological orientation toward game‑based design activities, this study asks, How do educational practitioners describe and prioritize employment transition services when considering employment outcomes for students with disabilities through a game-based activity?

## Partnership context

This study is part of a multi-year design-based implementation research (DBIR; Fishman et al., 2013) partnership between a university and a school district in the mountain West co-designing tools to strengthen collaborative transition service delivery for students with disabilities. The broader initiative aims to unify and strengthen crossrole collaboration among district educational practitioners involved in transition planning. As part of this work, we hold ongoing co-design meetings with special educato rs, general educators, school counselors, and district administrators to develop shared tools and practices that support transition services and improve post-school outcomes for students with disabilities.

Data for this paper came from a co-design meeting held at the end of the partnership’s second year. Prior sessions surfaced educational practitioners’ perspectives on barriers to providing transition services, existing resources, and potential solutions. D rawing on these insights, we designed an interactive, game-based activity to elicit educational practitioners’ perspectives on planning and implementing effective transition services as a method to synthesize our two year partnership and wrap up our co-design work. The game centered on a case study developed by National Technical Assistance Center on Transition: The Collaborative (NTACT:C, n.d.), featuring Jamal, a fictional high school student with disability. The case provided shared information about Jamal’s strengths, needs, interests, preferences, and post school goals, enabling educational practitioners to engage in concrete, role-specific planning conversations. Educational practitioners were divided into four cross-role teams (special educator, general educator, and school counselor) representing the five participating high schools. Each team developed a four-year transition plan using cards representing transiti on services (direction cards), potential barriers (road block cards), and opportunities for collaboration (outside perspective cards). Teams placed cards on a visual roadmap (see Figure

1) to showcase how each role would contribute to supporting his employment goals. Completed cards earned

points, and the session concluded with a gallery walk and whole-group reflection. During the session we collected participant-created artifacts (completed game cards), audio and video recordings, and observation notes. The paper focuses on the participant-created artifacts because they provide a direct record of how educational practitioners conceptualized supports for strengthening employment outcomes. Each team received 12 service cards, 8 roadblock cards, and 4 collaboration cards (96 total cards), and educational practitioners’ completed 89 cards across all teams.

We conducted a qualitative content analysis of the participant-created artifacts. Our codebook was developed using Lee and Carter’s (2012) framework of high-quality employment transition services (see Table 1). Physical artifacts were digitized and transcribed into a spreadsheet, with each practitioner contribution entered as a separate line item. This produced a structured dataset in which each row represented a unit of analysis eligible for one or more components, reflecting the interconnected nature of t ransition practices. To ensure reliability, the first and second authors independently coded 25% of the dataset, achieving 95% agreement and a Cohen’s kappa of 0.84 1828 (Cohen, 1988). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and the first author coded the remaining data using the finalized codebook.

Using Lee and Carter’s (2012) framework, we applied 298 codes across the seven components of high‑quality transition services. Across the dataset, career development experiences and customized transition support components appeared most frequently. Practitioners consistently highlighted supports such as job exploration, résumé development, work‑based learning, and individualized planning conversations. These components appeared predominantly on transition service cards, reflecting practitioners’ orientation toward concrete, employment‑focused supports.

The Interagency collaboration component also appeared regularly, though most often in response to barriers. Practitioners invoked collaboration when the case student struggled to secure internships or needed additional support, identifying partners such as CTE coordinators, WBL coordinators, VR counselors, and community organizations. This pattern suggests that collaboration was viewed as a resource mobilized when challenges arose rather than as a routine feature of planning. The Social and employment‑related skill development component was similarly tied to transition services, with practitioners emphasizing communication skills, self‑advocacy, and structured practice opportunities. Together, these patterns illustrate which components of high‑quality transition services practitioners readily drew upon and which were less salient in their reasoning. This study examined how educational practitioners conceptualize employment‑focused transition services when collaboratively planning for a single case student in a game‑based activity. Using Lee and Carter’s (2012) framework, practitioners most frequently emphasized the career development experiences and customized transition support components, with less consistent attention to the family support, self‑determination, and job‑related support components.

A key contribution of this analysis lies in how different components surfaced across different types of game cards. Components tied to concrete, school‑based planning, such as job exploration, résumé development, and individualized conversations, appeared predominantly on transition service cards, suggesting these components are central to practitioners’ routine planning. In contrast, components requiring broader coordination or sustained instructional focus, such as interagency collaboration, self‑advocacy, and employer accommodations, were more likely to appear on roadblock cards, indicating that practitioners tend to activate these supports reactively when challenges arise. Family support appeared most often on collaboration cards, reflecting recognition of its importance but limited integration into everyday planning. These patterns echo themes from our broader co‑design work: practitioners are confident in the supports they candeliver directly, but less certain about how to embed family engagement and interagency collaboration into routine practice. The game‑based environment made these distinctions visible by structuring contributions across planning, problem‑solving,  and collaboration prompts. In this way, the activity functioned both as a research instrument and as a mediating artifact, revealing how practitioners prioritize and coordinate transition components.

Taken together, the findings suggest that while practitioners readily identify high‑leverage components aligned with established predictors of post‑school success, they may underemphasize components that require systemic coordination or proactive instructional attention. Strengthening employment outcomes for students with disabilities will require not only tools that support planning for career development and individualized services, but also intentional efforts to weave family engagement, and interagency and employer collaboration into the everyday fabric of transition planning.
