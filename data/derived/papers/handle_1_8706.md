# Argumentative Characteristics of Equal and Active Participation in Project-Based Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Equal and active participation is a desirable team state in the process of collaborative learning. In this study, we analyzed an argumentative aspect of equal and active participation in project-based learning: an 8-day online innovation workshop. Epistemic network analysis revealed that learners engage in elaborative discourse when they participate equally and actively. Furthermore, such time can be clustered into two types which have a distinct mechanism for facilitating participation.

## Introduction

In project-based learning, learners collaborate in teams to work on projects in real-world contexts. Although the quality of collaboration severely influences the le arning outcomes, learners are not always able to co llaborate effectively, which raises needs to support their collaboration. One of the important aspects to analyze quality of collaboration is participation (Weinberger and Fisc her, 2006). Over a course of project, the level of learners’ participation fluctuates depending on their motivational, emotional, and cognitive states. At the team level, equal and active participation is desirable. Although existing research investigates and the process leading to equal or active participation and how it is sustained in other learning situations (Sinha et al, 2015), deeper understanding is needed particularly in project-based learning, where learners explore questions, generate ideas, and critique and improve their ideas through discussion. To this end, we investigate argumentative patterns in periods when teams participate equally and actively in an authentic case of project-based learning.

## Methods

This study used video record of an 8-day workshop o n innovative solutions to the challenges of a super-aging society, held at an off-campus educational program in Japan. There were 34 participants, including students and working adults, in six teams of five to six people. The entire workshop was conducted online using Zoom, Slack, and Apisnote. Apisnote is a digital sticky note system for information structuring (https://www.apisnote.com/). We selected the second day and the seventh day for our analysis and processed 27 hours of video record to computationally identify the start times and end times of each speaking turn, and manually transcri bed them. Next, the entire period was divided into 3-minute segments, and the amount of speaking time and the number of turn-taking of each team member in each segment wer e calculated. We define an equally-participating segment (ES) as a segment where coefficient of variation of  both the amount of speaking time and the number of  turntaking among team members are below median of those  among all segments. Similarly, actively-participat ing segment (AS) are defined as a segment where sum of both the amount of speaking time and number of turn-taking among team members are above median of those among all segments. There were 46 segments that are both ES and AS (equally-and-actively-participating segments, EAS), which accounts for 8.3% of the whole segments. Then, we chose 30% of all segments which include al l of the EAS and coded the utterances in these segments for the epistemic network analysis (Shaffe r et al., 2016). We employed coding scheme of Kuhn et al

(2020) with modification to adjust to our data, which includes Agree, Counter, Claim, Clarify, Elaborate, Elicit,

Socio-emotional interaction and Residuals. We compa red EAS and other segments to clarify differences i n argumentative characteristics between them. Besides, we clustered and compared EAS based on coding res ults using k-means algorithm with silhouette approach for identifying the ideal number of clusters.

## Result and Discussion

As shown in Figure 1 left, there was a difference b etween the EAS and other segments on the X-axis, wh ich is statistically significant (U = 4267, p = 0.00, r = -0.5). Links between Elaboration and other codes were particularly strong in EAS. This means that members listen to each other and make new claims based on the other’s claim in EAS. This type of utterances might help to maintain the attention of more members towards ongoing discussion topics by rewarding and integrating their contribut ion, which facilitates equal and active participati on of team members as a result.

The difference between two clusters is shown in Fig.1 right, which is statistically significant on the X-axis (U = 409, p = 0.00, r = -0.56). In cluster 1, links betw een Agree, Elaboration, and Elicitation were strong, while in cluster 2, links between Counter, Inform, and Socio-emotional interaction dominate. In cluster 1, some members tried to build a common understanding of ongoing to pics by confirming their perceptions or tried to ma ke consensus through questions eliciting others to sha re their opinion (Elicit and Agree). These were fol lowed by new claims (Elaboration) based on the common unders tandings or the consensus that they had just made, advancing discussion rapidly as a result. On the other hand, in cluster 2, teams typically make critical arguments (Counter) while utilizing supporting facts (Inform)  or laughing (Socio-emotional interaction) to form friendly moods. In this way, team members could express thei r critical views which provoke others’ explanations while avoiding interpersonal conflict. Stories of both clusters seem to be related to the equal and active participation of teams. Attentional and coordinative factors seem to be at play in both clusters; an advance in a topic which induces new consideration and development of common understandings or consensus which ensure common grounds in cluster 1, and a critical view which requires expla nation and supporting evidence and a friendly mood which maintain an interpersonal relationship in cluster 2.

Figure 1 The comparison plot between EAS and other segments (left) and between clusters of EAS (right) Our initial result suggests a mechanism that equal and active participation occurs and sustains in projectbased learning. As well as increasing cases for val idating our findings, future works will compare tea ms that experience more or less of certain types of equal and active participation and reveal underlying factors explaining their differences, which leads to a concrete way for supporting collaboration in project-based learning. Also, the effects of experiencing different types of equal and active participation for learning outcomes are of interest. In our case, teams that experienced the cluster 2 type of EAS more received higher evaluations for their final products from participants in the workshop, which suggests different effects among types of equal and active participation.
