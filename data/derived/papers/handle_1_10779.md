# Paper or Virtual Reality First? The Impact of Material Sequence on Science Learning Outcomes

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Although past research has examined the efficacy of sequencing learning materials, most studies on the use of virtual reality (VR) in educational settings have focused on comparing its effectiveness with traditional learning media. However, insufficient investigation exists exploring how utilizing VR sequencing alongside existing learning materials affects learning outcomes. This study aims to address this knowledge gap by examining learning outcomes when student subjects engaged with scientific concepts via VR simulation and paper-based materials, assessing how the sequence of exposure—VR or paper first—influenced their learning. Results show that students who studied paper materials before learning through VR performed significantly better than those who did the reverse. Furthermore, we found students who first learned the material using VR showed a slight decrease in their ability to verbally explain the concepts they had learned after studying with paper materials.

## Introduction

The effectiveness of sequencing learning materials can vary depending on various factors, including th e technologies used (Kant et al., 2017; Kokkonen & Schalk, 2021;  Stefaniak & Turkelson, 2014). For instance, Stefaniak and Turkelson (2014) noted students who engaged with simulations before attending lectures achieved better outcomes than peers to whom the media was presented in reverse order. Similarly, Kant et al. (2017) found improved scientific reasoning skills in students wh o viewed video modeling before engaging in inquiry tasks, in contrast to those who experienced the opposite sequ ence. While t hese findings highlight the importance of material sequencing in learning scenario design, the novel affordances and frictions of new technologies can impact the optimal sequencing of learning activities (McEneaney, 2016). As an emerging technology, virtual reality (VR) has the potential to enhance learning outcomes, offering students a new  sense of agency in immersive experiences (Makransky et al., 2018) while also supporting more physical learning via movement and gesture  interactions tied to the content (Kim et al., 2023). However, the majority of existing research on the use of VR in educational environmen ts tends to compare its effectiveness against that of existing learning media (e.g., Parong & Mayer, 2018; Wu et a l., 2020), with limited exploration into how the sequencing of VR alongside existing learning materials influences student learning outcom es. Vogt et al. (2021) conducted research on whether it is m ore effective for VR or auditory text-based learning materials to be presented first, but that study focused primarily on the effectiveness of pictures preceding text, or vice versa. In response, this study aims to investigate the effective sequencing of incorporati ng VR simulations and paper study materials into biology learning environments.

## Participants and design

In Spring 2023, a total of 35 students from a biology class at a local M idwestern high school participated in this quasi-experimental study. All students participated in the research individua lly in one-on-one sessions, where each student was matched with a researcher. The only difference between the two groups in the s tudy procedure was the sequencing of the learning materials, as outlined in Table 1. The pre-questionnaire, adapted from Parong and Mayer (2018), measured students’ biology self-efficacy for this study. The pre-and post-test consisted of the same six multiple-choice questions on cell division  (e.g., “What organelle provides energy  for the cell?  A. cell membrane, B. chromatids, C. mitochondria, D. spindle fiber”), but the order of the questions was changed between subjects. An information panel present within the simulation provided students with information about the phase  they were currently learning about, the ir mission (describing the necessary interactions at the current stage), and related science concepts. We designed the diagrams to ensure the illustrations used were as similar as possible to the virtual objects in the VR simulation. The research team allowed students to study each learning material at their own pace. Coding and scoring the verbal explanation of cell division Students were asked to provide verbal explanations of the cell division process three times, as detailed in Table 1. The prompt was consistent at all stages: “Based on what you know, can you explain the cell division process from scratch?” We developed five key elements (one for each phase) in collaboration with local biology teachers (Table 2). During the verbal explanation of cell division,  if a student included a specific element, they rec eived a score of 1; if not, they scored 0, allowing for a maximum of 5 points. When scoring verbal explanations, we awarded 1 point for demonstrat ing understanding of a concept, even if the student  did not use perfect terminology. For instance, if a student could not recall the word “mitochondria” but described the phase as “bringing nutrients to a yellow object to create energy, ” we considered this representative of understanding of the concept. Twenty-five percent of the verbal explanation data from the students were independently coded by both the author and another coder, resulting in 85.8% agreement and a Cohen’s K value of 0.72. Then, the author coded all the remaining data. ## Results

Table 3 summarizes the mean values for each set of measurement data. Given that the sample size of eac h group does not exceed 30, it is important to verify whether the data for each group follow a normal distribution (Lachin, 1981). The Shapiro-Wilk test results indicate all measurement values except in the paper-first group’s post-verbal explanation 2 follow a normal distribution. Therefore, we used a nonparametric method to analyze the paper-first group’s post-verbal explanation 2, and for all other measurements, we applied parametric methods. A t-test examining  the verbal explanation results showed no significan t difference between the two groups in the pre-explanation phase (p = 0.61). However, when comparing post-verbal explanation 2 using the Mann-Whitney U test, learning outcomes for the paper-first group were significantly higher than for the VR-first group (p < 0.001). For the paper-first group, learning outcomes improved in the first post-explanation, as compared to the pre-explanation (p < 0.001), and they improved even more in the second post-explanation (p < 0.001). In contrast, for the VR-first group, there was an improvement from pre-explanation to the first post-explanation (p < 0.001), but no significant difference was observed between the first and second post-explanations (p = 0.31), with a decrease in mean score from 2.66 to  2.13. Additionally, in the paper-first group, only one student had a lower score in the second post-explanation than in the first post-explanation. In contrast, in the VRfirst group, seven students had lower scores in the second post-explanation than in the first post-explanation. We transcribed and analyzed student verbal explanat ions to understand why seven (out of 15) VR-first group students' verbal explanation scores decreased after paper material study. To introduce a sample datum from one student’s explanation of the cell division proc ess in the first post-verbal explanation: “Um... the thing in the middle (mitochondria) I forgot what to call, but yo u have to give it protein so that it can generate e nergy, then hold the DNA to make it as a chromosome... And then you separate the X shape (chromosome) into one side and another side. And then it creates new cells. ” After studying with paper materials, in the second post-verbal explanation, the same student described it as: “Cell makes sure all the things are ready to divide. There were more descriptions of what's happening, but I don't remem ber.” After learning with VR, even if students couldn't r ecall exact terms, they could describe the cell division process using the shapes, colors, and location of virtual objects with relative detail. In contrast, after studying w ith paper materials, they tended to give shorter su mmaries and skipped terms when they could not recall them. This trend was similarly observed in the other six students. We observed a similar trend in the written test. The t-test showed no significant difference between the two groups in the pre-test (p = 0.13), but, in the post-test, the paper-first group scored significantly higher than the VR-first group (p = 0.01). Thus, in both the verbal explanation  test (focused on conceptual knowledge)  and the written test (focused on factual knowledge), the paper-first group demonstrated significantly higher learning outcomes than the VR-first group. From the pre-questionnaire, we assessed students’ self-reported biology selfefficacy scores. Although there were no significant  differences, the paper-first group  tended to have slightly higher biology self-efficacy even before the study began (p = 0.08). Therefore, we conducted an ANCOVA on the post-test results, using each group ’s self-efficacy as a covariate. This analysis still indica ted higher learning outcomes for the group that started with paper material (p = 0.03). ## Discussion

Providing students with multiple forms of  learning materials is important in biology education (Hansen & Righland, 2020), but presenting too much material c an also cause cognitive overload (Mayer et al., 2001). As such, it is critical to consider the order in which learning materials are presented to students. This study explored the effectiveness of VR and paper materials in different sequences. The results show students who first studied the cel l division process through paper materials and then played the VR simulation demonstrated relatively higher outcomes than those who did the reverse. This trend was consistent in both the students ’ written test results and their verbal explanation scoring. One reason for these results could be that students first learned scientific concepts through the more familiar paper materials, enhancing their understanding when applying these concepts in the VR simulation. In contrast, when content was first learned through VR, the experience did not seem to significantly improve the subsequent effectiveness of paper materials. The analysis of the transcription data of the expla nations from the seven students in the VR-first group who scored lower in the second post-explanation compared to the first post-explanation showed that, after studying the VR materials, students could explain the cell division process and the interactions between organisms using the shapes and colors of objects, even if they could not recall the exact terms. However, after studying their paper materials, if the students could not remember  the correct terms, they tended to skip that part o f the explanation instead of connecting it with what they had learned in the simulation. This might be because students prioritized terminology use with text-heavy material. Additionally, it seems that VR simu lation experiences did not translate into prior knowledge for the paper ma terial study. Conversely, the students who started with the paper materials were able to explain VR simulation content using their learning experiences from paper materials as prior knowledge. Therefore, paper materials migh t be ideal for introducing initial concepts, with V R simulations subsequent ly reinforcing and solidifying this knowledge. Give n that the written test results also showed higher performance by the paper-first group, a learning scenario in science education where students learn basic concepts through traditional paper materials before exploring them further in VR might be effective. This study was limited by the relatively small number of students in each group, and further research is needed to determine whether the same results could be obtained with a larger sample. Future research should also consider how use of the VR simulation, analyzed via extracted log data (controller position, interacted  objects, gaze, etc.), shows differences between the two groups, and how this influences students’ verbal explanations.
