# Mixed-Reality Training in a Cleanroom to Bridge Intentional Learning and Actual Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores two major disconnections in cleanroom learning: limited hands-on engagement due to advanced automation and the disconnect between theoretical learning goals and students' focus on procedural tasks. Implementing a mixed reality (MR)-enhanced training approach shows thematic analysis evidence that it bridges these educational gaps and connects conceptual knowledge with practical procedures, also motivating students.

## Introduction

The gap between intentional and actual learning is recognized in the educational community (Nunan, 1995) and applies to cleanroom practical training, which  faces two major challenges. First, automat ed equipment ha s disconnected hands-on learning, limiting students' opportunities to interact directly with experimental processes that are replaced by computer interfaces with reduced physical aspects of experiments. In many instances, resource constraints mean that teachers perform while students merely observe, further diminishing hands-on learning opportunities. Second, there exists a disconnect between theoretical learning objectives and students' practical engagement. Instead, students followed instructions procedurally to perform the correct  tool usage but failed to acquire conceptual knowledge (Hofstein & Kind, 2012; Hofstein & Lunetta, 2004). This study aims to implement mixed reality (MR) in microfabrication training for undergraduate students to mitigate the identified disconnections, aligning with the framework addressed by (Abrahams & Millar, 2008), which emphasizes the importance of connecting practical work to conceptual understanding.

The study’s theoretical foundation is grounded in two complementary learning theories. Embodied learning theory posits that physical actions are intrinsically linked to cognitive processes  and enhance learning (Johnson-Glenberg et al., 2016, 2023). MR technology provides an innovative platform for embodied learning by enabling students to interact with visual representations, in this case,  in environments with limited physical manipulation. Second, applying MR to address the challenges is built upon  the Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML) (R. E. Mayer, 2005). CTML states multimedia learning is most effective when learners actively select, organize, and integrate information from different modalities. MR encourages active processing by providing interactive features that allow students to explore processes at their own pace and make connections between theory and practice, view visual cues with auditory information.

## Methods

The study involved 28 third-year Bachelor microfabrication students, divided into an MR-enhanced group (N =

12) and a teacher-led group (N = 16). The MR-enhanced group used MR goggles during practical sessions (Figure

1-a), while the teacher-led group followed the conventional instructions-observation method (Figure 1-b). Figure 1

(a) Students Learn Theory and Practical Tips (MR approach); (b) Teacher Prepares Tool while Students

Observe the Process (teacher-led approach); (c) First-Person Perspective Seen via the MR Goggles. The MR intervention incorporated interactive instructions, recorded videos, 3D animations, and real-time feedback. A 30-minute pre-training session was conducted to familiarize students with the MR system and reduce the novelty effect.  During the intervention, students are provided immersive visualizations  (Figure 1-c) linking cleanroom procedures to theoretical knowledge. The intervention lasted approximately 20 minutes per session, excluding the set-up time. This approach allowed students to engage with content in a self-paced, interactive manner, deepening their understanding and preparing them for future learning.

## Findings and conclusion

We conducted a thematic analysis  (Braun & Clarke, 2012)  to understand how MR influenced student learning qualitatively. A modified version of the Technology-Enabled Active Learning inventory (TEAL) (Shroff et al.,

2019) was used to evaluate differences between the MR-enhanced group and the teacher-led group quantitatively.

As the Cognitive Affective Model of Immersive Learning (CAMIL) model (Merensky & Petersen, 2021) explains the active cognitive method in immersive learning, we posited the MR into the immersive learning framework, as a result, the thematic analysis identified five themes, grouped under two high-level categories: Bridging theory and practical understanding This high-level category describes how MR helps students to link theoretical concepts to practical tasks.

• Link to  Theory: MR helped students make connections between theoretical content and hands-on

experiences by providing opportunities for interactive learning.  For example, a student states "If you're actually physically doing it, you don't know, or maybe you don't think of the theory in your mind while you're doing it. Yeah. So having the goggles sort of creates your thought bubbles to remind you of the more theoretical chemical processes that happen."

• Immersive Visualization:  The immersive visualizations provided by MR helped students understand

complex concepts that were difficult to grasp. “In the cleanroom, you listen to the TA[teaching assistant] […] It [MR goggles] kind of unlocks another way to visualize things." and "I like having visual cues and remembering stuff visually when I learn. So having them visualize really helps."  These examples highlight how MR enabled students to see beyond the procedural aspects of experiments and truly visualize the underlying scientific phenomena, thus bridging the gap between theory and practice.

• Practical Self-Paced Feedback: MR allowed students to receive real-time feedback and explore practical

processes at their own pace. This personalized feedback was particularly helpful in reinforcing theoretical learning during practical activities. Statement "It's really helpful to have questions about the course and watch what you're gonna  do just before the use of the equipment,"  emphasizes the value of MR in preparing them for hands-on tasks. "It was much more self-paced and into the specifics, rather than really just observing everything, like we did in practical." Promote active motivation and sustained engagement The study uncovered improvements in student engagement and motivation. The second high-level category focuses on how MR influenced student motivation and sustained engagement throughout the learning process.

• Sustained Engagement: MR technology fostered sustained engagement by maintaining students' interest

during cleanroom training. "I just keep that excitement throughout the experiments, throughout the day." "But after as we didn't do anything really just watch, the interest can kind of faded. " indicating that less interactive activities led to decreased engagement, but using MR keeps it interactive and active even with limited hands-on tasks.

• Motivation: MR promoted motivation by providing students with a more active role in the learning

process. "I'm much more motivated to see it in the specifics, more practically, by doing it myself”, “I stay relatively encouraged."  These quotes illustrate how MR made learning more enjoyable and motivating, particularly by giving students more autonomy and control over their learning experience.

In conclusion, the study suggests that MR effectively addresses two critical dis connections. First, MR directly confronts the challenge of automated equipment disconnecting hands-on learning, enabling students to engage with experimental processes through dynamic visual interfaces that restore physical interaction. Second, by leveraging CTML, MR bridges the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical engagement, allowing students to integrate conceptual understanding with procedural tasks through intera ctive visualizations, real-time feedback, and self-paced exploration that transforms passive observation into active learning experiences.
