# Bridging Research and Practice: Expanding Access to Transformative Computer Science Teacher Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster discusses the collaborative efforts of a CS education community to develop an open-access, online educational resource. Throughout our design, revision, and publication processes, we show how our community intentionally sought to bridge gaps between research and practice to sustain CS teachers on their equity learning journeys. Motivation A key challenge in educational research is its inaccessibility for the classroom teachers it is intended to help. This gap between research and practice exists because of multiple structural and systemic barriers. Research is often siloed within — rather than integrated across —fields in ways that support the complexity of classroom teaching. Research findings are presented using academic norms (e.g., abstract theoretical arguments, statistical analyses, technical jargon) that are disconnected from the everyday realities of classroom life. Teacher voices are often excluded from research, sidelining their expertise in addressing education challenges (Penuel, 2019). Access to research is generally restricted behind paywalls, requiring institutional connections not generally available to K-12 teachers (Cook et al., 2024). It is no wonder that teachers already pressed for time and resources perceive a gap between their work and educational researchers. This research-practice gap has particular relevance for computer science education (CS Ed) as an emerging field. Aware of how patterns of inequity have shaped computing and technology, many CS educators are striving to thoughtfully grow the field to make equity-oriented CS education a reality for all students (e.g., Computer Science Teacher Association, n.d.). We conceptualize equity as access to CS learning opportunities and as transforming the field by centering marginalized people, knowledge systems, and technologies (Grapin et al., 2023; Vogel et al., 2017). Realizing this vision requires CS teachers who engage in equity work as an ongoing effort demanding careful identity work, continued learning, and collective reflection and dreaming (Benjamin, 2022, 2024). This poster discusses the collaborative efforts of a CS Ed community to develop an open-access online book entitled Advancing Educational Equity in Computer Science: A Guide for Educators and Their Allies. We show how we intentionally sought to bridge the gap between research and practice in developing our guide to create a resource that supports CS teachers on their equity learning journeys. Project background Our book was published under the collective authorship of the Computer Science Educational Justice Collective (CS-EJC). The CS-EJC is comprised of three teams with different areas of expertise related to equity in CS Ed: DiversFYI, a consultancy focused on racial literacy; the Creative Technology Research Lab at the University of Florida focused on leveraging Universal Design for Learning to disrupt ableism in CS Ed; and the Participating in Literacies and Computer Science team focused on supporting bi/mult ilingual learners in CS Ed. We collaborated from 2020-2025 to iteratively develop summer workshops and school-year professional development sessions as part of a CS-focused professional development (PD) initiative for New York City Public School teachers. Recognizing the local impact of our holistic, equity-centered PD, we decided to take what we had learned from our collaboration and create a new resource that could be available to CS educators across the United States. We began meeting in 2021 and wrote most of the book between 2022 and 2024. We led multiple rounds of revision, feedback, and beta testing during the 2024-2025 school year and officially launched the book with our original New York City partner teachers in May 2025. Strategies to bridge research and practice Our primary goal was to create a guide that would be accessible and useful for CS educators with different roles and commitments. To achieve these goals, we were intentional in the design elements we incorporated into the book, how we engaged in our feedback and revision process, and our publishing decisions. 2692 Design elements We designed the book to address barriers that may make research inaccessible for teachers. We intentionally integrated our team’s expertise to create a holistic resource that addresses many challenges that teachers face in classrooms. The first four chapters provide a foundation of diversity, equity, and inequity in CS Ed. The remainder of the book expands on issues related to race, language, and ableism, offering strategies for equity-oriented CS Ed. This connected approach emphasizes the interrelatedness of inequity and the intersectional approach needed for transformative CS Ed. We also implemented rhetorical and design features to support teacher engagement. Although our work was grounded in equit y-oriented CS and Learning Sciences theory, we intentionally adopted a more conversational, less academic tone throughout the book, avoiding extensive use of academic jargon and including a glossary and definitions for key terms. Each chapter also contains reflection questions and implications for practice to directly link theory and research with practice. Chapter 5 offers an example of how we concretely illustrated the discussion of abstract ideas. It introduces the theoretical constructs of intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989, 1991) and multi-layered societal oppression (ideological, institutional, interpersonal, internalized; Bell, 2013; Chinook Fund, n.d.). These theories help teachers interpret what is happening in their classrooms but are often presented in dense and abstract ways that may feel ina ccessible or irrelevant. To address this, Chapter 5 is framed around myths, or common narratives that teachers may hear in connection with CS Ed. Each myth is then debunked by applying theoretical ideas to current CS Ed scenarios. These elements lower barr iers to accessing the book’s content and provide immediate ways for educators to apply their learning to their classroom contexts. Feedback and revision process In our feedback and revision process, we worked to intentionally center rather than sideline teacher voices and expertise. After the book was drafted, we reached out to the New York City Public Schools teachers we had worked with earlier. 25 of them agreed to provide written feedback and participate in focus groups on the book’s content. Teachers’ insights helped us consider different intended audiences and tailor the book’s structure to meet these anticipated needs. For instance, we broke each original chapter into shorter chapters to make the content easier to reference on a need-to-know basis. One of the teachers’ most significant contributions were the stories they provided from their own classrooms that illustrated concepts discussed in the book. With permission and attribution, we incorporated these examples as anchors in each chapter, weaving in teachers’ own words throughout the book. This key addition showed how real teachers applied research to their own practice, building bridges for readers to follow. After revising the book based on teacher feedback, we beta-tested it in three CS teacher education courses, two at New York University and one at SUNY-Buffalo. The book was assigned as a required course reading, and 12 students provided written feedback on their experience using the book. Our beta-testers consistently identified the teacher stories as their favorite and most impactful element of the book. The beta-testers themselves also helped us further refine the book, offering incisive comments that enabled us to nuance our arguments. For instance, one student provided insights into current sociopolitical dynamics related to named languages like Chinese that helped us clarify our terminology (see Chapters 11 and 12). Publication approach We sought to avoid gatekeeping mechanisms that restrict access to knowledge in academia and in CS. We committed to making the book free and open-access instead of behind an academic paywall largely inaccessible to K-12 educators. To that end, we published the book via the SUNY-Buffalo Pressbooks platform as an Open Educational Resource. Pressbooks offered a number of affordances (e.g., accessibility features, multiple formats to increase availability) that allowed us to maintain our equity focus. A second key decision was our choice to publish the book under a Creative Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0) license. Because equity work is an ongoing process that changes over time, the CC license allows the book to become a communal resource where the field can update details like language and elaborate on other issues relevant to equity in CS Ed (e.g., gender) that we did not address due to our team’s resource constraints. Additionally, we look forward to how readers will remix and adapt the book to tailor it to meet local community contexts and needs. Significance The CS-EJC represents a group of scholars committed to transforming CS Ed to achieve its equitable potential. Through intentional efforts to design, revise, and publish our educational resource in ways that bridge between research and practice, we hope our work will sustain teachers in their CS equity efforts. Similarly, we hope that our design approach offers insights into centering equity issues in content creation and publication that can be generative for the broader Learning Sciences community, contributing to ongoing conversations with researchers and practitioners about creating learning spaces where every student can belong and thrive. 2693 References Bell, J.
