# Reevaluating the Effectiveness of Inquiry-Based Learning through Decades of Long-term Assessment in Japanese STEM Programs

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper aims to offer methodological insights that demonstrate the long-term advantages of inquiry-based learning (hereafter IBL) by reviewing case reports using a Hypothesis-Experiment-Instruction (hereafter HEI) along with a small-scale retrospective survey. We prepared two measures: retaining the learning process of inquiry and using model-based reasoning skills. Based on previous research showing evidence for Measure 1 with HEI, we conducted a survey focusing on Measure 2, which revealed a tendency to utilize explanatory models (hereafter EMs). However, it was found that the explanatory models used by participants were not always the same as those employed in their past classes. The results imply necessity for an innovative approach to achieving a more continuous view of long-term learning processes, visualizing interactive processes of enriching domain knowledge and skill development, and devising metrics that capture these complex processes.

## Introduction

While many learning scientists have been strong proponents of IBL for decades, the debate between IBL and direct instruction has resurfaced in recent years. Many journals publish papers that assume that direct instruction is superior to IBL (Zhang et al., 2021), often based on a mis conception of what IBL entails. Cognitive science and learning science are now tasked with conducting research that clearly demonstrates the outcomes of IBL and highlights its advantages. For example, Chinn et al. (2023), suggest that assessments should be designed to focus on three key aspects of IBL environments: epistemic agency, community interacti on, and the complexity of IBL environments. Specifically, they suggest assessing whether students can express and develop their ideas through arguments and counterarguments that emerge organically  in social interaction. Such outcomes are often overlooked by immediate paper tests. One approach for this  assessment is Miyake & Pea ’s new perspective of learning goals, which focuses on assessing the long-term outcomes of IBL (Miyake & Pea, 2007).  This paper seeks to offer  methodological insights that demonstrate the long-term advantages of IBL by a small-scale retrospective survey of HEI students. Developed in 1963, HEI has been in use for nearly 60 years (Hatano & Inagaki, 1991; Itakura, 2021; Roseman et al., 2008), allowing researchers and practitioners to conduct a followup study of more than a decade of student learning outcomes.

## Essential features of IBL and assessment measures

Chinn et al. (2023) note that scholars have often misunderstood key features of IBL, causing confusion about its value. They also express concern about that there are no standardized appropriate measures to assess its outcomes, making it difficult to fully understand what students grasp through their inquiry. They identified three key features of IBL, in contrast with direct instruction: 1) learners have epistemic agency, 2) they engage in interaction, and

3) they engage in complex modes of reasoning and knowledge.  These conditions are not only characteristics of

IBL, but also essential skills for solving complex problems encountered in everyday life. In the context of IBL, these skills are to be acquired integrally with the learning content (Songer & Kali, 2022). Chinn et al. (2023) argue that assessing the impact of inquiry-based environments should  focus on whether students genuinely a dopt the ideas that they are taught, and that appropriate measures are required to evaluate their true understanding and commitment.  Previous studies have shown that IBL is more effective for understanding generative, rich knowledge that can only be  assessed through descriptive questions in contrast to the acquisition of specific, individual knowledge which is better measured with multiple-choice questions (Clark & Linn, 2003). Additionally, their follow-up interviews conducted over a period of four years  demonstrated that these learning outcomes were  not only  retained but also applied and further developed over the long term as students learned new contents.

Miyake & Pea (2007) also advocated for a revised understanding of learning goals and their assessment based on cognitive science and the long-term educational practices of th at time. They proposed a new set of learning goals focused on ensuring the portability, dependability, and sustainability of student learning outcomes. While IBL, where students express and develop their own ideas, can provide a sense of satisfaction, it may also leave students with a sense of incompleteness, such as a sense of non-conformity with established norms, a sense of conflict with others, and a sense of inadequacy as they anticipate the next question to explore. As a result, the learning outcomes of IBL resemble findings in memory research. There, techniques that may feel challenging at the time (such as distributed, non-blocking learning without immediate feedback on correct or incorrect memories) tend to yield better results in the long term, week s or months later, compared to methods that improve memory efficiency in the short term (such as lumped learning, blocked learning, immediate feedback) (Linn, 2007). They caution against prematurely evaluating outcomes  of IBL with standard academic achievement tests during the early stages of implementation as this could undermine reforms that could be effective in the long run. This paper therefore proposes two measures to assess the learning outcomes of IBL. The first measure is that learners, even 10 or more years after their IBL class, remember the learning process of constructing integrated understanding by exerting epistemic agency through interaction (Measure 1: learning process). The second is that, when faced with  a problem that requires such a proce ss, learners can apply integrated explanatory model s (Clement, 2008) using complex reasoning, rather than resorting to a simple algorithmic strategy (Measure 2: EMs). EMs are socially constructed, yet individually understandab le “story” that connects abstract ideas, both original and borrowed from others along with practical rules-of-thumb gained through everyday experience. This model links rules derived from individual experience with scientific concepts.

## Method

Hypothesis-Experiment-Instruction In this section, we outline the framework of HEI, which is a strategy for teaching fundamental scientific concepts (Itakura, 2021). An HEI lesson involves a strategic set of discussion procedures and a “problem” for which the students predict the answer and discuss. An HEI “unit” consists of a series of carefully sequenced  problems or experiments designed to guide students through the development of the scientific concepts underlying the problem set. Each student is expected to synthesize the results of the experiments in their own way, forming an individualized “hypothesis” or rudimentary understanding of the scientific concept.  HEI provides a  learning environment that combine s the three essential features of IBL proposed by Chinn et al. (2023).  I t has been suggested that the sequential cumulative discussions among the students in the HEI learning environment have a strong positive impact on helping learners expand their understanding  adaptively (e.g., Hatano & Inagaki,1991; Roseman, et.al., 2008). Although rigorous research on the long-term learning effects is limited, there are notable instances that students remember the learning process, Measure 1, over 30 years (e.g., Kamisako, 2001) Retrospective survey on long-term effects of HEI’s "Air and Water" unit In the following, this paper focuses on Measure 2 and examine two research questions (hereafter RQ) through a questionnaire survey conducted with a small cohort of university and vocational school students who participated in the HEI class in their third grades. Regarding RQ2, the first author has records of their HEI class which allows a detailed analysis of the learning processes of each student (Saito & Miyake, 2011). RQ1) Do participants continue to use EMs to solve problems more than 10 years after the lessons? RQ2) Are the used models the same as or different from the model they used in the lessons? ## Hypothesis and perspectives of analysis

If the understanding that students gained through t heir lessons has been retained, it is likely that many of the respondents will be able to use the EMs they learned in their lessons to explain their answer choices by referencing the workings of invisible air and its presence. Additionally, all six respondents had taken science classes in junior high schools and high schools, suggesting that they might have been able to explain their reasoning using a more advanced EMs that integrates scientific concepts introduced later and beyond such as atmospheric pressure and surface tension, and can be used to gain a deeper understanding of the principles of the “Air and Water” unit. Given these possibilities, this paper analyzes the answers and justifications from the six respondents using three perspectives: whether they chose the correct answer, whether they applied an explanatory model, and which type of explanatory model they used. These perspectives are applied to learning outcomes from the lessons conducted 10 or 11 years earlier. The EMs are analyzed based on the following three categories as shown in Table 4: “Air” which represents the model  targeted in the unit; “ Pressure”, which refers to  a model incorporating knowledge expected to be developed later  in middle and high school science classes, providing  a deeper understanding of the theme of “Air and Water”; and “Other,” which includes models outside the scope of the unit.

## Discussion

This paper explored the long-term impact of IBL by using key measures in line with the framework of Chinn et al. (2023): the learning process (Measure 1) and the use of EMs (Measure 2). The survey focusing on Measure 2 provided evidence that the tendency to use EMs had been retained or improved. These results sugges t that IBL enables the long-term retention of inquiry skills and usage of scientific models. However, the interaction between the learned models and their usage appears to be complex. It was found that the EMs utilized by participants were not always the same as those employed in class. In some instances, the models underwent further modifications. Theoretical research in cognitive science suggests that scientific skills and disciplinary knowledge interact and are integrally acquired through IBL (Bransford et al., 2000). In our survey, constructi ng an explanatory model is considered a  scientific skill, while the EMs themselves are viewed as disciplinary knowledge. Our results showed that S4 used the “Air” model both in class and in the questionnaire, altho ugh the details varied. In contrast, S6, who used the “Other” model in P10, applied the “Air” model in P7, staying consistent with their classroom use. These findings suggest that over the decade, the students continued to engage in learning that interacted with the disciplinary knowledge and skills acquired, enriching them and making use of the learning outcomes. However, this survey could not pr ovide detailed insights into such long-term processes. For example, S1 used different models during class and in the surve y. Various possibilities could explain this, such as updates to disciplinary knowledge or the use of specific knowledge pieces linked to the skill through later learning in response to the survey problem.

These possibilities highlight the limitations of this paper and suggest future directions. Firstly, the study’s “sparse-ness,” such as conducting a one-time survey after several years and its small scale, including a low response rate, are notable constraints. We need inn ovative approaches to more frequently track the lea rning processes of a larger number of participants with l ess effort, to visualize the interactive processes of enriching domain knowledge and skill development, and to devise metrics that capture these complex processes.
