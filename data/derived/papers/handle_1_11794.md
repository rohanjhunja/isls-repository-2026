# Community-Wide Infrastructuring: Extending Infrastructure Theories Toward Learning Ecosystems

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines community-wide infrastructuring as a framework for fostering equitable learning ecosystems. Motivated by the need to address inequities in access to educational opportunities across learning experiences in a community, we extend prior theories of infrastructuring to community-level contexts. We apply infrastructuring theoretical models to recent work on learning ecosystem mapping and design. This highlights the potential of community-wide infrastructuring to surface invisible infrastructures, identify key social actors, and address systemic barriers. By bridging theoretical concepts with practical considerations, the paper offers a lens for designing learning ecosystems that are locally resonant and collaboratively developed. This work provides an opening for future research on addressing inequities through participatory and sustainable infrastructuring practices.

Broadening the infrastructuring turn in the Learning Sciences Recent events in the Learning Sciences, including s essions at AERA (Hayden et al., 2024) and ICLS (Pinkard,

2024) conferences, have drawn significant attention to the underlying structures which shape learning

environments. Th is interest has led to a call for an “infrastructuring turn” in the Learning Sciences (Pinkard, 2024). Responding to this timely appeal, we aim to contribute to this ongoing dialogue by presenting a theoretical exploration of infrastructuring in community-wide contexts. Recognizing the urgency of this endeavor, this paper reflects a  preliminary theoretical investigation that seeks to expand the field’s perspectives and identify new directions for research.

Our original intent was to construct a comprehensiv e literature review on infrastructuring for the Learning Sciences. However, two key developments have shifted our approach. First, Chen (2024) has recently provided an exemplary synthesis of the existing literature, underscoring infrastructuring's relational and dynamic dimensions and calling for more sustainable approac hes—work that needs to continue in the Learning Science s. Second, Pinkard’s keynote (2024), and her recent work on “Opportunity Landscaping” (2023), drew attention to the multiple levels of learning environments in a c ity and how they interact at different scales. This necessitates infrastructure and infrastructuring at a community and cross-organizational scale (Pinkard, 2019). While this topic is underexplored, existing infrastructuring offers lessons that can inform community learning design. We present here extensions of three frameworks presented previ ously from within  infrastructuring literature (Chen, 2024; Star, 1999; Bielaczyc, 2013) and write about how these frameworks provide guidance (with extensions) towards community-wide infrastructuring.

Our work draws on and unites branches of design work  within the Learning Sciences —infrastructuring and learning ecosystems—which share a literary ancestry deeply rooted in studies of the social, historical, cultural, political, and relational perspectives of learning (see Nasir et al., 2020), studies of informal learning in museums, parks, community centers, and online (such as Ito et al., 2013), and participatory and collaborative design work for learning (Collaborative Education Research, 202 3; Vakil et al., 2016; Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Like how biological ecosystems frameworks have enriched the conceptualization of learning ecosystems by decentralizing individual learners (Hecht et al., 2020), our approach seeks to decentralize classroom and  school notions of infrastructuring to emphasize the interplay of community needs, hype rlocal realities, and geographic conditions which shape a lens for studying community-wide infrastructuring. In this framing, we ask the following research questions:

• What frameworks currently exist that can help us to understand and analyze community-wide

infrastructures?

• How do these frameworks reveal new opportunities or questions for research on community-wide

infrastructures?

By addressing these questions, this paper seeks to contribute both a theoretical lens and practical co nsiderations for advancing infrastructuring work. In doing so, w e invite the Learning Sciences community to imagine  new possibilities for designing and supporting equitable, locally resonant, and interconnected learning environments. Foundations for community-wide infrastructuring Infrastructuring in the learning sciences An approach within the Learning Sciences that empha sizes the intertwined nature of the multiple system s and structures at play within any learning environment or activity is a focus on infrastructure. Star and Ruhleder

(1996) proposed the notion of infrastructure to examine the  links between local practices and larger scale

structures, emphasizing the inherently relational n ature of infrastructure and its ties to people, pra ctices, and resources. Within design-based research, learning scientists have propos ed frameworks aimed at understanding the role of infrastructure in implementing and sust aining learning innovations. Bielaczyc’s (2013) soc ial infrastructure framework offers designers a way to pay closer attention to the social resources in formal learning environments to successfully design and implement t echnology-based tools. This framework includes four dimensions: cultural beliefs, practices, socio-techno-spatial relations, and interaction with the outside  wor ld. These dimensions further emphasize tha t infrastructure is not just physical or material, but also includes social practices, beliefs and relations which are critical  to our work. Within informal learning environments, Hayden and her colleagues (2023), proposed a framework aimed at attending to the ways that people, practices, and objects already present in informal learning environments are leveraged or redesigned toward equitable learning outcomes.

Furthermore, “infrastructuring” has also emerged wi thin design-based research as a lens to move away from static views of infrastructure and instead pay attention to infrastructure as malleable, able to be dynamically renegotiated or designed with and around (Karasti & Syrjänen, 2004). Penuel (2019) builds on Star and Ruhleder’s

(1996) uses of the term “infrastructuring” to talk about efforts that focus on creating the conditions of support for

educators around educational innovations. This incl udes the study of how, where, and when resources are taken up and allows designers to understand how innovations can be implemented equitably sustainably long term. Chen

(2024) proposes the IMPROV framework, a framework that offers theoretical, methodological, and practical tools

for creating sustainable learning innovations throu gh infrastructuring. Through this framework, Chen h ighlights the importance of identifying existing components o f infrastructure in a system to create coherence between a design innovation and the existing or new infrastructure. A key purpose of infrastructuring research has been to look at how existing components, and the (re)design of infrastructures can address (or ignore) pervasive injustices (Shea et al., 2023). But to work on these inequities, Shea, and her colleagues (2023), pointed to infrastructures which exist in-between, and layered on top of individual learning experiences and programs. And, while much of the prior work on infrastructuring can be directly applied to design interventions for out-of-school (OST) learning programs and organizations, researchers and practitioners are left with little specific guidance on how infrastructuring can take place across programs and communities (city, region, or n eighborhoods) to address community inequities, barriers, and challenges. Infrastructuring in community-wide designs (such as through community-based implementation research; Bang et al., 2015) brings about unique challenges which must address how learning happens across spaces, programs, and opportunities.

Learning across spaces: Learning ecosystems Researchers in the  Learning Sciences have long studied learning in non-school environments (Hutchins, 1995; Vossoughi et al., 2013; Marin, 2020, Nasir, 2005; Nasir & Cooks, 2009; to name a few),  and research ers have also focused on informal and after-school learning contexts specifically (including It o et al., 2013; Crowley & Jacobs, 200 3; Keifert, 2021, Bell et al., 2009; Falk & Dierking, 2016). Additionally, some Learning Sciences researchers have advocated studying learning in ways that look across multiple experiences, in and out of school, by emphasizing that learning occurs across a variet y of spaces, and these spaces are interconnected, f orming dynamic learning ecosystems and communities of prac tice (Stevens, Bransford & Stevens, 2005; Akiva & Robinson, 2021; Pinkard, 2019). Pinkard (2019) argues that by looking across multiple learning experiences, we can view learning as a  dynamic process influenced by a range of factors  beyond the classroom, such as family, peers, community organizations, and the physical en vironment. Communities, as learning contexts, rely on learners and families navigating and transitioning between different expe riences and activities, such as moving from school to an after-school program or from a community center to a muse um (Pinkard, 2019). Analyzing these transitions reveals how learners build upon prior experiences, transfer  knowledge, and develop new skills and identities across various settings (Nasir, 2005; Nasir & Cooks, 2009; Thompson et al., in-press; Chew et al., 2023). Research which studies how learners move across spa ces within a community helps reveal inequities not visible in studies of isolated learning environments. This includes revealing gaps and barriers faced by learners in between and across learning activities, such as tra nsportation, safety, cultural relevance, and access  to resources (Pinkard, 2019). Examining learning across settings  surfaces historical and current sociopolitical dyn amics that shape the distribution of learning resources and op portunities, contributing to existing inequities (Vakil, 2020; Halverson et al., 2024). For example, analyzing the  concentration of quality OST programs in specific neighborhoods reveals how historical disinvestment and segregation continue to impact access to learning. Sharpening this view of  learning across spaces is recent work on learning e cosystems (Akiva & Robinson, 2021). Learning ecosystems are complex and dynamic networks of interconnected elements that support learning across various settings and timescales (Hecht & Crowley, 2019). These elements include people (e.g., learners, educators, families, community memb ers, funders, policymakers, and researchers), place s (e.g., schools, libraries, museums, community centers, hom es, online spaces, and natural environments), activ ities and resources (e.g., programs, curricula, internships, books, technology, funding, and social services), and intangibles (e.g., policies, culture, history, power dynamics, social relationships, and community values). Resear ch on learning ecosystems has emphasized that recognizing  the interconnectedness of learning en vironments is not enough; learning ecosystems require approaches that  embrace change, promote equity, and support resilience (Hecht & Crowley, 2019; Akiva et al., 2021; Hecht e t al., 2020; Halverson et al., 2023). Learning ecosystems research also frames assessment of learning around abundance  and empowering youth and community assets, rather than focus on deficits in communities (Halverson et al., 2023). A learning ecosystems approach argues that to support equitable and effective improvements on learning infrastructures, the field needs to move to focus on relational processes among elements in the system  (Hecht et al., 2020). Relatedly, we argue that a community-wide lens to infrastructur e will enable us to make visible these important and complex  relational processes, supporting researchers to move beyond simply creating opportunities for indiv iduals and toward creating robust learning ecosystems.

Collaborative education research Both infrastructuring and learning ecosystems come together around the participatory and partnership-driven nature of designing learning environments (Collaborative Education Research, 2023) and require researchers and community members to form partnerships to map and analyze their communities. This process includes identifying and prioritizing needs and aspirations, ensuring that learning opportunities are relevant, accessible, and culturally sustaining. This participatory approach, which mirrors others in the Learning Sciences including research-practice partnerships (Coburn & Penuel, 2016), desi gn-based implementation research (Fishman & Penuel, 2018), and community-based design research (Bang et al., 2015), empowers communities to advocate for changes that address local challenges, values community practices and expertise, and creates a more equitable learning landscape. It is a necessary  progression of this research to now attend more int entionally to the role of infrastructure and infrastructuring within this community-oriented approach. Researcher positionality Wade is motivated to undergo this research due in par t to their connection of “infrastructure”  in urban planning contexts. For this author, who is a white researcher, infrastructure is a place where communities can collectively and democratically act upon shared resources and structures amidst the more common private (and individualized or siloed) elements of a community. In particular, Wade approaches this work thinking about how collective infrastructuring makes visible the underlying structures as remnants of white supremac y within community structures—forcing this author to review their own contributions to and complicity in perpetuating these structures through their collaborative education research.

Ronni is motivated to pursue these research questions due to her own experience engaging in communitybased research in informal learning contexts. As a white researcher at a predominantly white university, Ronni is aware of the ways that they benefit from, perpetuate, and are harmed by unjust infrastructures. Through m aking visible the ways that communities are engaging in i nfrastructural transformation towards their own jus ticeoriented goals, creating tools that support this wo rk, and advocating for coal ition building toward collective change, they aim to redistribute power away from th e university and toward alternative ways of knowing  and being.

Theoretical contribution: Community-wide infrastructuring frameworks In this paper we aim to harmonize infrastructuring  with theories around learning across  spaces to build a community-wide lens for infrastructuring  which recognizes collaborative approaches to learni ng design and research. We build this ecosystem-level lens to provide guidance for “hyperlocal, community-driven approach[es] to designing robust learning ecosystems, particularly for divested and underserved communities” (Pinkard, 2023, p. 1). A community-wide learning lens is more than plotting educational institutions on a map of a community— instead, we call for a comprehensive analysis of the hard, soft, inf ormational, and relational structures that shape access to learning opportunities across a community  (Pinkard, 2019). This lens provides a more compreh ensive view of infrastructuring —as a practice situated within local realities and a imed at connecting and supporting learning across multiple, interwoven contexts.

Pinkard’s “Opportunity Landscaping” framework (2023, 2024, 2025) can extend infrastructuring beyond singular educational environments and provides insi ght into the underlying infrastructure  that needs to be (re)designed to support community learning. Pinkard (2019) provides compelling evidence  for unseen and underappreciated i nfrastructures in communities (seen in Table 1), and thus  provides target areas for our infrastructuring efforts. For example, soft infrastructures and relational infrastructures (explored more in Chew et al., 2023) stand out as areas not often considered for design work in OST learning initiatives  and key areas of focus for equity-driven redesign. However, we can better connect Pinkard’s work to existing infrastructuring frameworks to provide guidance on  how design can happen  at the community level. We build on both these dimensions of community infrastructure from Pinkard and frameworks drawn from the infrastructuring literature to build our theory of community-wide infrastructuring. We ground our approach to understanding infrastructure at a community-wide level with established infrastructure tools. Star’s “The Ethnography of Infrastructure” (1999) proposes methods for studying the ecological and relational nature of infrastructure using the tools and perspectives of ethnography. While these methods originated within the fields of Human-Computer Interaction and Information Science, the “ tricks of the trade” Star outlines still provide a useful starting point in thinking about making community and geographic level infrastructure visible and actionable. Our proposed approach is also infor med by Chen’s IMPROV framework (Chen, 2024), which provides a model for sustainable educational innovation through infrastructuring, aimed at embedding innovative designs within existing educational systems to enhance thei r longevity and adaptability. Chen advocates for th is framework to address gaps in traditional design res earch, which often underestimates the socio-technical landscapes that affect how innovations perform over time. Bielaczyc's “Social Infrastructure Framework” (2013), while initially focused on classrooms, offers additional value to our community-wide infrastructuring as it draws attention to the  dynamics within hyperlocal, real learning environme nts. By extending Bielaczyc’s four dimensions—cultural beliefs, practices, socio-techno-spatial relations, and interaction with the "outside world"— to a community-wide context, we can glean insights into the unique factors that shape learning opportunities and access within diverse communities.

Though these three approaches include numerous directives, reminders, lessons, and recommendations for infrastructuring, in this paper we propose four example areas to scaffold our investigations into community-wide infrastructure: 1) identifying key social actors in infrastructuring work, 2) making visible the invisible underlying structures, 3) identifying paradoxes in infrastructure  and 4) addressing hyper-local community-wide heterogeneity.

Identifying key social actors in infrastructuring work A consideration for community-wide infrastructuring is identifying who holds influence, access, or insight within the layered systems of learning opportunities in a community. Traditional infrastructure framings often center educators and designers within a bounded environmen t (e.g., the classroom  or a district), but community-wide work demands an expanded understanding —the scale and heterogeneity of communities complicate w ho shapes infrastructures, who benefits from them, and who may be excluded from design processes altogether. In his IMPROV framework, Chen (2024) calls for iden tifying key social actors and their roles in designing and implementing learning innovations (pp. 593–595). He highlights that learners and educators who implement the design (“users”, p. 585) are key to the design process, and not solely a before-use activity (p. 593). Chen emphasizes heterogeneous participation and aims to legitimize the voices of those traditionally marginalized in design processes. This involves recognizing and incorporating diverse structures for learning and heterogeneous ways of knowing. Mapping value networks, as Chen proposes, helps to uncover relationships and power dynamics that shape infrastructure and can guide equitable design. Extending this framework to the community level reveals the need to rethink the scope of social actors. In a community-wide learning context, "users" expand beyond students and teachers to include all community memb ers who participate in and benefit from learning opportunities. This could include families, community organizations, local businesses, government agencies, and residents of all ages and backgrounds. Importantly, expanding beyond educational systems and their stakeholders reveals a wider array of individuals and groups who  have various power and  access to decision-making for the community as a whole or in specific aspects of it. Examples include civic leaders and government agencies, staff at community-based organizations, businesses and employers, as w ell as program officers and staff at philanthropic organizations. Chen’s framing also pushes us to consider residents  in the community, as they are the ultimate beneficiaries and stakeholders in comm unity-wide learning initiatives, and their engagement in shaping these efforts is essential.

At a community level, the multitude of perspectives and ways of knowing (in the present and historically) grows significantly. This includes not only formal academic knowledge but also cultural knowledge, lived experiences, and practical skills valued within the  community. We can examine how the variety of ways of knowing are recognized, valued, and integrated into  learning opportunities, and this is fundamental for equitable community-wide learning. Applying Chen’s call for identifying  key social actors to  a community-wide context means acknowledging the diverse expertise and perspectives held by community members and empowering them to actively leverage this diversity in the design and implementation of learning innovations. Making visible the invisible or underlying community infrastructures Making infrastructure visible is especially importa nt in community contexts, where many critical proce sses are not centralized, standardized, or documented. Infra structures such as time, transportation, and mentor ship development are often taken for granted or hidden w ithin individual organizations —yet they exert significant influence on access and equity. In community-wide infrastructuring work, we must ask: which infrastructures are invisible across a community, and what processes ex ist (or need to be created) to surface them and mak e them actionable?

In the IMPROV framework, Chen calls for infrastructuring to “make visible existing infrastructures and their relations with innovation” (2024, p. 595). This resonates with Star’s (1999) guidance for “surfacing invisible work,” which asks us to uncover the hidden routines, dependencies, and labor that structure everyday s ystems. Star describes how infrastructures often reside in “messy routines” or “boring sameness” (p. 386) and become visible only when they break down. Chen similarly d raws attention to “installed bases”—elements that sink into organizational practice, shaping future design poss ibilities while rarely being questioned (Chen, 2024, p. 595). Extending these insights to a community-wide context requires us to consider what forms of invisibility are structurally produced across organizations, geograp hies, and institutional boundaries  when they  are not coordinated or examined collectively.  One such installed base is the scheduling of when l earning happens —a form of soft infrastructure that is rarely coordinated across a community. In many cities, individual organizations plan OST programs without accounting for how their schedules intersect with other opportunities or con straints in youths’ lives, such as overlapping OST programs,  church involvement,  family responsibilities, or youth employment (which are arguably  integral to a youth’s OST experience). These misalignments remain  largely invisible, managed internally within organizations and normalized through unexamined routines. When communities take steps to make the broader schedule of OST visible, they can begin to address times that could be protected or prioritized for certain youth  and they can identify  groups consistently excluded from certain opportunities due to timing. Other examples of invisible  infrastructures, when seen through a community-wide lens, include how mentors are trained across a region or how curriculum choices circulate and become standardized across programs. These elements, while  often uncoordinated, subtly structure opportunity in ways that reinforce inequities.

Making these structures visible is a political and participatory act of surfacing what communities hav e long managed silently. As Star (1999) explains, inf rastructure is often invisible until it breaks —seen only in moments of disruption or misalignment. In  contrast, intentional infrastructuring work, as Che n (2024) frames it, asks communities to surface these conditions in adv ance, identifying embedded structures before they c onstrain equitable design. When applied at the community lev el, this shift in visibility reframes infrastructur ing not as reactive repair, but as proactive, collective sense making. It opens space for community stakeholders t o examine what forms of coordination, alignment, or redesign might be necessary—not just within organizations, but across the ecosystem. In this way, surfacing invisible inf rastructures becomes a tool for recognizing fragmen tation, revealing patterned exclusions, and creating new possibilities for equitable learning ecosystems. Identifying paradoxes in community infrastructure When infrastructure reflects only one perspective o r set of needs, it can quietly complicate access, r einforce exclusions, or disrupt practices elsewhere in the e cosystem. Pinkard (2023, 2024) echoes this concern, emphasizing the importance of understanding the mul titude of unique needs and resources of each commun ity when designing learning ecosystems.

Chen points to how changes needed to underlying structures may involve addressing tensions. This aligns with Star's (1999) concept of identifying paradoxes  in infrastructure. Infrastructure sometimes become s visible only when it breaks down. This breakdown frequently occurs when two processes are in place simultaneously and create barriers to access for certain groups (Star,  1999, p. 386). This can occur  when a feature meant to improve one part of a process creates confusion and complex ity in an underlying or invisible secondary process. These issues are often driven by standardization efforts that fail to consider diverse needs and contexts and rely on unexamined assumptions embedded in infrastructure d esign—assumptions that can exclude or marginalize specific groups (see master narratives in Star, 1999, pp. 384–385). Extending these ideas to a community-wide context reveals how paradoxes surface not just with in individual systems, but across learning ecosyste ms. A community-wide lens highlights how Star’s c oncepts—such as unintended complexity, standardization, overreliance on master narratives, and the invisibi lity of infrastructure—can be used to identify instances where seemingly minor design decisions in community infra structure create significant barrie rs for other parts of the community. Efforts to streamline or coordinate often bump up against the realities of diverse contexts, needs, and priorities—exposing the complexities embedded in seemingly str aightforward infrastructure. Even well-intentioned infrastructural improvements to community systems can produce unint ended consequences. For example, a museum might offer exceptional exhibits and programming, but its hours of operation could inadvertently exclude working families because the easiest times to visit —the weekends —are also the most crowded. Even a museum’s policy regarding food  and drinks can become a paradox; if a museum offers  food onsite but the options are limited for certain dietary restrictions, or if families are prohibited from bringing snacks or meals, it becomes challenging for them to spend extended time engaging with the museum’s resources.  As communities work instead to build shared systems to support learning, efforts to improve one part of the ecosystem can intentionally and directly address complication s and limits access elsewhere. Design decisions  built on community-wide infrastructure may reflect broader assumptions about use, access, or need  across groups and organizations.

We advocate for a community-driven approach to infrastructuring that continuous ly engages families and community members in discussions about how syst ems and designs address multiple, and sometimes conflicting, needs. By recognizing and addressing these kinds of structural contradictions, communities can move toward more inclusive and equitable systems that better support the diversity of learners across the ecosystem. Attending to hyper-local community-wide heterogeneity Tensions, paradoxes, and perspectives vary from com munity to community, so we must engage in community-wide infrastructuring in ways that acknowledge hype r-local factors. Local characteristics —such as socio-economic conditions, historical legacies, and urban infrastructure—deeply impact the accessibility and quality of learning opportunities. Infrastructuring work must make visible the socio-political and economic dimensions embedded within local infrastructures and acknowled ge how these dynamics both enabl e and constrain educational pathways. Bang et al. (2015) emphasize the importance of local specificity in design practices, noting, “increasingly we worked to develop design practices  to surface and acknowledge our collective experien ces and help us to work toward imagining new possible futur es” (p. 35). Their work reminds us that designing w ithin a community requires attention to histories, relationships, and lived experiences—not just generalized needs. In this framing, place is not a backdrop to learning, it is entangled with histories, politics, and meaning-making—shaping how knowledge is produced and possibilities are imagined (Bang et al., 2015). Bielaczyc’s social infrastructure framework (2013) offers one way to attend to local community uniqueness. Originally developed to describe the in terdependent conditions that support knowledge-building communities, her framework centers four key dimensi ons of infrastructure: 1) cultural beliefs, 2) practice s, 3) socio-techno-spatial relations, and 4) interactions with the “outside world” (p. 266). These were initially framed for classroom environments but align with guidance from Chen (2024) and Star (1999)  that can inform broader infrastructuring efforts. Extending Bielaczyc’s framework to community-wide settings supports Bang et al.’s call for local specificity and collective imagining. Whi le Bielaczyc’s model emphasizes coherence within a learning environment, the community-wide scale requires tools that also support divergence. Each of her four dimensions becomes a site for surfacing what is often invisible in standardized designs: contest ed narratives, uneven participation, and locally valued ways of knowing. This extension reframes infrastructuring not only a s a design task, but as an ongoing, place-based negotiation of meaning, history, and access. A community-wide lens encourages researchers, designers, and community me mbers to engage in infrastructuring that is respons ive to deep local variation—rather than assuming coherence across a learning ecosystem. Cultural beliefs shape the values and assumptions h eld within a community. Bielaczyc defines this as “the ways in which knowledge and learning are conceptualized, students’ and teachers’ social identities, and how technology-based tools are perceived” (p. 266). At a community level, this might include divergent attitudes about OST participation, or competing narratives shaped b y different local histories. For instance, some fam ilies may value OST programs tied closely to school systems, while others may vie w OST as a space that should remain independent from formal education. These beliefs influence where and how OST opportunities emerge—and who engages with them. Practices refer to how learning happens in everyday life —how learners, educators, and families engage in, share, and support learning. In community settings, this includes how people find, sign up for, and travel to programs. For example, two library br anches in the same city may serve demographically s imilar populations but differ dramatically in how families register for and attend programs. These distinctions reflect not only logistical differences but historical and cultural relationships between community members and institutions. Socio-techno-spatial relations point to how physical space, digi tal tools, and infrastructure shape access. At the community scale, this includes mapping the availabi lity of public learning spaces (e.g., libraries, pa rks, maker spaces), access to internet and devices, and transp ortation networks. One neighborhood might lack safe public transit or spaces for STEM learning, while another may have robust resources. Pinkard (2023, 2024) emphasizes the potential of technology to connect learning opportunities across spaces—highlighting possibilities for remote participation, digital mentorship, and interactive mapping tools that make opportunities visible. Interactions with the outside world involve how communities connect w ith regional and national networks. Bielaczyc defin es this as “the ways in which students interact, onli ne and offline, with people outside of their immedi ate classroom context” (p. 266). At the community level, this inc ludes relationships with national curriculum standa rds (e.g., NGSS), funders (e.g., NSF, Meta), and partner organizations (e.g., 4-H or city-wide agencies). These connections shape which infrastructures become available, who b rings them in, and whether local organizations can meaningfully participate.

By applying Bielaczyc’s framework through the lens of Bang et al., communities can critically examine their learning ecosystems and design interventions that remain vibrant and grounded in hyper-local realities. Attending to these intersections empowers communiti es to build infrastructures that are both adaptive and contextually rooted. A community-wide infrastructuring approach that centers local s pecificity does not erase variation; it makes it actionable—opening pathways to more just, meaningful, and enduring learning futures.

## Advancing community-wide infrastructuring: Implications and future

directions This paper has explored the proposed role of community-wide infrastructuring in fostering interconnected a nd equitable learning ecosystems. Through linking infrastructuring frameworks from Star (1996), Chen (2024) and Bielaczyc (2013) to Opportunity Landscaping, we propose a lens for understanding and navigating the relational, socio-technical, and spatial dimensions of community-wide infrastructure. These established approaches gave us a foundation we might use to  address pervasive inequities, identify key social a ctors, surfac e invisible infrastructures, resolve paradoxes, and embrace the heterogeneity of communities moving forward. These insights complement ongoing conversations in the Learning Sc iences, and through this paper, we pull on infrastructuring work to encompass lessons learned from community-wide research and design efforts. We also lean into, and press, recent work on community-wide mapping and design initiatives  to leverage theoretical understandings, frameworks, and guidance from infrastructuring lite rature. Both theoretical approaches have implications for the ways we research how learning happens in communities and what types of designing processes sustain learning ecosystems.

Future work could deepen this exploration by further investigating (empirically) unique infrastructuring needs of community-wide educational research and design. Additional research might also continue to bring together the grammar  and frameworks of community-wide infrastructuring to better define tools and strategies for surfacing invisible infrastructures of communities and navigating the tensions they produce. We suggest future work also address questions of how race, class, gender, language, and  ideology shape or are shaped by infrastructuring processes  in communities and more work must center a rural v isioning of community-wide infrastructuring and the unique circumstances, challenges, and structures  of rural regions and communities. Our intent in this theoretical framing is to lay the groundwork for those inquiries, and as this work evolves, it is crucial to remain attuned to the unique cultural, social, a nd historical dynamics that shape each community. W hile the discussion in this theoretical paper is only a starting point, it aspires to support con tinued inquiry into designing sustainable and context-sensitive learning ecosystems.
