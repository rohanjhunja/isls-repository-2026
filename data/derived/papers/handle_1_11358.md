# Climate Change Science: Sensemaking and Practices of a Maya Muslim Community in Chiapas, Mexico

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores the teaching, learning, and practices of climate change science in an intergenerational setting of an Indigenous Maya Tzotzil Muslim community in Chiapas, Mexico. By focusing on a formal and intergenerational educational setting involving three generations of women from this Maya Muslim community, we show two critical aspects of this climate change paradigm: First, how the community approaches their more-than-human environment as relatives with emotions, intelligence, and agency; Second, how they define care, respect, and responsibility as being central to maintaining sustainable relationships with their more-than-human relatives. We argue that this sensemaking and practices of climate change science are grounded in the ways this community sees themselves in relationship with nature.

## Introduction

Indigenous communities worldwide have long maintained and practiced sustainable and ecologically responsible relationships with their more-than-human environments (Schuster et al., 2019). Indigenous scholars explain how the effectiveness of these relationships, as practi ced by diverse Indigenous communities, is grounded in their relationality with nature (Bang, 2020; Brayboy et al., 2012; McDaid et al., 2023). In these relational paradigms, human and more-than-human environments are seen as braided systems that  exist in a reciprocal relationship. These paradigms position not only humans but also l ands, waters, trees, plants, mountains, and other more-thanhuman beings as alive, connected, and interdependent entities, challenging the binaries of culture and nature often embedded in Western frameworks. They also highlight  how learning from and with diverse Indigenous communities should recognize the ways in which humans can be in caring kinship with their more-than-human environment as a way to address environmental degradation, offering a departure from paradigms that see land as a resource to exploit and nature as an entity to be conquered (Bang & Marin, 2015; Kimmerer, 2013). In this context, this paper explores the teaching, learning, and practices of climate change science in  an intergenerational setting in an Indigenous Maya Tzotzil Muslim community in Chiapas, Mexico. This community has been experiencing rising temperatures, drying rivers, worsening air quality, and the gradual disappearance of plants and herbs that have been central to their di et and medicine for centuries. The community attribute s these shifts to a deteriorating relationship between humans and nature. They speak about climate change as an expression of “anger” that Earth feels towards humans for not showing care and respect to their more-than-human environment. To explore this climate change paradigm, we draw on data from an educational setting involving three generations of women from this community. We show two critical aspects of this paradigm: First, how the community approaches their more-than-human environment as relatives with emotions, intelligence, and agency; Second, how they define care, respect, and responsibility as central to maintaining sustainable relationships with their more-than-human relatives.

As we engage with this climate change science  paradigm, we recognize the global principles of natureculture relationality  shared across many Indigenous nations, tribes, and communities. However, we are also mindful of how these shared values become alive in the specific contexts of the diverse Indigenous communities that can have differentially organized economic, social, and cultural systems (Wildcat & Voth, 2003). For example, the relationship of the Maya Muslim community with land and other more-than-human beings is shaped by a number of contextual factors such as their limited access to agricultural lands and plants and herbs that have been central to their way of life because of their residence in the city of San Cristóbal de las Casas (subsequently referred to as “San Crist óbal”), their current residence on the lands acquired by the Zapatista movement (Maya resistance movement) after their displacement from their ancestral village and lands in 1970s and 1980s, and their conversion to Islam, among other things. We argue that these contextual dimensions are critical to understanding the nature-culture relationships and climate change science of this community. Nature-culture relationality and climate change science Bang and colleagues (2012) highlight how human superiority and human/nature binaries continu e to reinforce racialized hierarchical systems  of dominance and exploitation. Refe rring to  Cheryl H arris’ (1995) concept of settled expectations, Bang and colleagues argue that ways of knowing that are shaped by the racialized categorization of the world have generated systems of governance that equate Whiteness to culture while relegating “others” to nature that can be owned and regulated. Central to this categorization are settler colonial frameworks and systems of ownership and exploitation of natural resources that translate this separation of nature from culture into educational systems, policy frameworks, and common sensemaking. In this context, the relational nature-culture paradigms, as understood and practiced by different Indigenous communities, become important sites not only for sustainable and ecologically responsible practices but also for offering  ways of knowing that assign  personhood to all human and more-than-human entities (Kimmerer, 2013). The Maya people of Chiapas, one of the most biodiverse regions in the world,  have long believed and practiced reciprocal relationality as central to maintaining a balance between and across human and more-than-human environments (Ariana, 2012). According to the Chiapas Maya ontologies, epistemologies, and axiologies, a lack of respect and care for elders, traditions, and nature causes disruptions in both social relations and the natural environment (Ayora-Diaz, 1999). This relational paradigm recognizes all human and more-thanhuman entities as intertwined and interdependent beings with agency and intentionality. In the Maya relational paradigm, the emphasis on certain key relational values to maintain balance does not imply universalizing  them as singular truth. Instead, there is a recognition that these relati onal values might unfold differentially in different contexts  and through different practices and interactions (Ayora-Diaz, 1999). Acknowledging the central role of context in this relational paradigm, here we focus on the practices, interactions, and activities of three generations of Maya Muslim women in an educational setting in order to explore their paradigm of nature-culture relationship and climate change. Our analysis is grounded in the theory of relational epistemology that situates practices, interactions, and ways of becoming as relational, fluid, and intertwined systems that connect human and more-than-human environments (Bang, 2015: Cajate, 1999; Pugh et al., 2019).

## Context and methodology

This paper is part of a broader collaborative project that began in 2021, comprising both university-based and community-based researchers. The university-based researchers are a group of Indigenous and non-Indigenous educational and health researchers who are affiliated with a university in the U.S. The community researchers are a group of Maya Tzotzil Muslim women in Chiapas, women whose identities are deeply intertwi ned with their Indigenous heritage, their practice of Islam, and t heir engagement with th e Zapatista movement. The work presented in this paper  emerged d uring an  oral history ethnographic project in  2022-2023 through which the community identified Maya medicine as a critical ar ea that they wanted our research collaboration to focu s on. This community, like most Maya communities in this region, is facing a physical and mental health crisis because of poor access to healthcare and fresh food,  among other things. The  community s aw Maya medicine as a n effective healthcare system that their ancestors ha d developed and practiced for generations. However, because of the lack of access to communal spaces and agricu ltural land in San Cristóbal, the community felt concerned about their youth not being able to connect to and practice Maya medicine as part of their cultural heritage. The lands that this Maya  Tzotzil Muslim community and other Maya communities live on in San Cristóbal were reclaimed by the Zapatista movement and were distributed to the Maya people who were displaced from their ancestral lands.  This community became familiar with and embraced Islam a lso through their participation in the Zapatista movement. They were invited to learn about Islam by the Spanish Muslims  who were attracted to this region because of this movement. The community converted to Islam in the mid-1990s with endorsement from the leaders of the Zapatista movem ent. The community takes great pride in their ident ities as Maya and Muslims and sees Indigeneity and Islam as complementary ways of knowing. Based on input from community members, we started collaborating with eight women in the age groups ranging from 23 to 60. While they all  employed Maya medicine  in some form in their daily lives, their expertise and knowledge of Maya medicine varied greatly. For nine months, we worked with these community researchers to explore the following areas: critical health issues that the community is facing, knowledge and practices of Maya medicine within the community, community’s access to herbs and plants t hat are used in Maya medicine,  and designing educational settings that could be most conducive for the commu nity. This collaboration led to the  development and implementation of a  workshop that was held in June 2024 in San Cristóbal. The workshop was led by a highly experienced Maya woman teacher  healer who offer ed certified training and has been working with Maya communities and healthcare providers for over three  decades. This paper focuses on the practices, activities, and interactions that occurred during the workshop as a site to explore and understand this community’s sensemaking about nature-culture relationality, foundational to Maya medicine.

## Findings: Healing as relationality with self and nature

The plants are the evidence of the reality of Indigenou s knowledge. They know everything. They know how and where to heal. The plants are innocent and have a lot of wisdom. They are the best sages. But they heal us only if we believe in them. Lillie, the Maya woman teacher, spoke these words i n a soft voice, looking at the community researchers who were seated around a table in the meeting room at h er house. This was the first day of the workshop focusing on Maya medicine. Lillie was invited to co-design and lead this workshop  based on the community researchers’ interest in working with a professional Maya teacher. She had set up a workshop room in her hous e with storage and drying areas for plants to make teas, ointments, and other medicine. In the data excerpt shared above, Lillie discussed why it was  important to learn the “theory” of Maya medicine before identifying di fferent plants and herbs and their medicinal qualities. Lillie called Maya medicine “knowledge of their [Maya] ancestors”  and something all of them as Maya people knew as part of their existence. The healing power of these plants, Lillie shared, was not merely about their  effectiveness in addressing a known imbalance in the human body but also their ability to diagnose the real nature  of imbalances. For example, if someone consumed a plant to address a health issue that they thought was related to their kidneys, but the problem was not in the kidneys but elsewhere in the bod y, the plant would, then, address that imbalance, as it would know what to do,  without causing any damage to the kidneys. Lillie asked the community researchers to see how  the plants and herbs were not merely “medicine” but alive entities with wisdom and agenc y. The healing power of plants became alive for tho se who recognized their essence. She shared, Herbal tea is not going to fix things on its own. It will work only if you trust it, even if you do not know how it works. We have to see plants as our friends. Lillie explained that the powerful qualities of plants and herbs worked only in relationality with humans. Balance, thus, could not be restored merely through consuming “good” herbs and plants. Humans had to know the essence of the plants and herbs the way they would know a friend. In other words, humans had to be in a relationship with plants and herbs rather than using them as a resource distinct and separate from them. It was this human-nature relationality that Lillie called the “truth” of Indigenous knowledge. As Lillie shared what she called the theory of Maya  medicine, the community researchers listened with deep interest, respect,  and curiosity and nodded in approval. The oval-shaped table the community researchers sat at had different dried and fresh plants and herbs on it. Community researchers picked and held them as Lillie talked about the need to trust these plants. Farah,  one of the community researchers in her 20s, share d her anticipation to move to the “practical” part of the workshop  that would focus on using the plants. Farah was keenly interested in Maya medicine and had been lea rning about it from her grandmother. She also used social media to acquire information about using plants and herbs as remedies. Lillie smiled a t this expression of eagerness and shared how knowing the essence of the plants would help Farah to connect with plants as entities that not only have qualities but also “emotions.” It is this connection with plants and seeing them as alive beings, Lillie argued, and not just the qualities of these plants and herbs, that supported the healing and balance in us. Lillie encouraged such questions, comments, and reflections from the community researchers. She stood at the top of the table with multiple posters of hu man body  and Maya medicine models  displayed in her background. At a glance, this physical setting did not stand apart from mainstream Western educational contexts where “knowledge” often flows from teacher to student s in a passive manner. However, the nature of teaching and learning in this workshop was deeply relational and embodied, marked by a shared sense of knowing and trusting each other as Maya people. Lillie positioned Maya medicine as a way of knowing that w as already familiar to community researchers as “knowledge of their ancestors”. Community researche rs, thus, were not merely “students” but fully present and active participants, connecting with the knowledge that belonged to them. This deep sense of shared heritage, ancestry, and embodied kn owledge connected Lillie and community researchers in ways that transcend conventional relationship-building activities in mainstream educational settings.

Throughout the workshop, this sense of knowi ng as Maya people seemed central to the relational teaching and learning processes. This was visible in the expressions of care and tru st that Lillie and community researchers showed for each other as they supported one another to express who they were and how they felt. For example, Lillie explained the nervous system by sharing, The nervous system is more than just the head; it i s the whole body; when the head doesn’t work then neither does the rest of the body. Everyt hing comes from the mind: what we think, our thoughts. … From these thoughts we have emotions and then illne sses. Herbal plants are a way for us to maintain patience. If you don’t have patience, you won’t have peace in your heart. Here, Lillie again emphasized how the herbs and plants worked in relationality with humans, not only cognitively but also in deeply emotional and embodied ways. The  role of emotions was something that Lillie talked about extensively throughout. She talked about being aware of our emotions and being in touch with them to maintain balance and wellness. In doing so, Lillie guided the community researchers to reclaim their agency by connecting to their desire to “ live well” as part of practicing Maya medicine. Here, i t is important to note that there is no word for “health” in the Maya Tzotzil language (Ayora-Diaz, 1999). The closest translation of health is living well, which refers not only to physical health but also  to psychological, emotional, and spiritual balance. Lillie discussed how the foundation of living well was relational trust, that is, humans trusting themselves and their human and more-than-human environments to maintain and restore balance. Nature supports this balance in humans only if humans understand the essence of mor e-than-human species and connect with and care for them as friends. As such, neither living well nor agency  were universal concepts that applied in the same m anner to everyone. Instead, Lillie encouraged the community researchers to have a “dialogue” with themselves to  know who they are and to rethink themselves not merely as resisting challenges but a s capable of living well in a balanced reciprocal relationship with nature.

In discussing this balance, Lillie spoke about the change in climate that was  resulting in rising temperatures and drying rivers, among other things, and how it was making certain plants and herbs inaccessible. These plants and herbs have been central to Maya me dicine for centuries  and Maya communities have worked with these herbs and plants through “caring for and receiving from land.” Lillie called climate change a threat not only as it limits the community’s access to valuable medicinal resources but, importantly, as a threat to the Maya ways of knowing and being. Here, she spoke about ho w humans needed to “know” nature and care for what it needs to receive from it, sharing that climate change result s from our lack of understanding and care for Earth. Throughout the workshop, Lillie emphasized the importance of such knowledge, highlighting the need to be with nature in order to deeply know and care for it. For example, she showed community researchers how the drying process for different plants and herbs had to be aligned with their distinct temperaments and essences. She asked them to touch and feel each plant to “be with the plant” instead of merely following instructions about how to use them. This was a departure from a standardized drying pro cess to make teas and remedies to supporting caring interactions with plants and herbs by learning about who they were. Community researchers spent extensive time each day “being” with plan ts as they learned about their essence, dried them, and made teas  and remedies from them. Whereas many took notes of these processes, Lillie encouraged them to also “feel” the plants’ presence and essence by using different senses like touch and smell to  connect with them, stating: “You have to be with them if you want to know them.” These interactions reveal how a relational paradigm that assigned personhood to all species, including humans, plants, and herbs, is articulated through sensemaking about all  species as having the capacity, intentionality, and emotions to maintain balance wi thin themselves and in their human and more-than-human environments. In this paradigm, climate change is seen as a form of imbalance in the human-nature relationality whereas living well reflects a relational and intertwined balance of human and more-than-human environments. This relationality, thus, is not only about  humans trusting their own agency  to have balance but also seeing the capacity and agency of nature as central to that balance. This relationality is disrupted when people stop ca ring for nature and only expect to receive from it.

Concluding and on-going reflections This paper explores how a group of Maya Muslim women from Chiapas, Mexi co, engaged with nature-culture relationality and climate change science as part of  their learning about Maya medicine. The analysis shows how the paradigm of nature-culture relationality, which is foundational to Maya medicine, assigns p ersonhood to human and more-than-human species. The teaching and learning of Maya medicine were grounded in approaching humans and plants/herbs as entities with emotions, intelligence, and agency. In this relational paradigm, climate change was approached as an imbalance in culture-nature relationships that threatened Maya  ways of knowing and being. The community saw Maya medicine as a way that could address the issues of climate change through re-connecting them with their ancestral heritage of being in a caring relatio nship with nature. This paper show s how the critical issue of climate change can be app roached through focusing on the nature-culture relationality instead of merely focusing on technical information  about sustainable and ecologically responsible str ategies. It also shows how such relational paradigms can be use d to design community-centered and intergenerational learning environments to teach and learn about clim ate change. However, this analysis does not propose  such relational paradigms as a set of universal values. Instead, it highlights how sensemaking regarding nature-culture relationships and climate change unfolds through interactions, activities, and practices within a specific context.
