# Architecture Students Create Balance Structures from Everyday Objects

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how everyday objects can enhance architecture students’ understanding of structural concepts. 186 first-year students designed balancing structures using everyday objects. Data from small group learning diaries and photographs of design outcomes and processes were analyzed to identify recurring concepts (stacking, spanning, cantilevering) used. The findings highlight the use of familiar materials in architectural education for structural learning.

## Introduction and background

Architectural education often faces challenges integrating structural  theory with design aesthetics, as structural considerations have traditionally been the domain of civil engineers (Wetzel, 2012). This division has contributed to a separation of visual building design and the practical requirements of stability and durability (Wetzel, 2012). However, there is growing recognition of the need for interdisciplinary collaboration between architects and civil engineers, particularly in response to sustainability demands in const ruction (United Nations Environment Programme, 2024). Traditional lecture-based approaches provide some theoretical foundation of structural concepts within architectural education (Herr, 2013). However, a lack of bridging structural concepts with design practice within architectural education limits students’ possibilities to f orm important connections between structural reasoning and architectural aesthetics (Delgado et al., 2023). Constructionist approaches to learning provide a way to bridge this gap, emphasizing how manipulation of familiar materials through the design of personally meaningful and shareable projects can lead to internalizing complex concepts (Papert, 1980). In fact, familiar materials foster opportunities to stay with a task for long periods and to approach it with a range of engineering practices (Peppler et al., 2022). In architecture education, leveraging weight, tension, and force in familiar objects enables diverse, stable designs that integrate aesthetics and structure (Vrontissi et al., 2018). Based on the prior work, everyday object s seem to be a promising context for learning structural balancing concepts (i.e., stacking, spanning, cantilever ing). However, how designing with everyday objects as part of formal architectural training supports students  in understanding and articulating structural principles remains understudied. Thus, we asked: Which structural concepts did first-year architecture students design into projects made of everyday objects, and how did the students describe the performed and inh erent structural concepts of their designs?

## Methods

To address the research question, we integrated a design-based learning activity with everyday objects into a semester-long introductory structural design course for 186 first-year undergraduate architecture students (43 small groups) at a German university. The course included 11 face-to-face lectures and three online synchronous tutorials, each lasting approximately three hours. To bridge the ory and practice, students created balancing structures with everyday objects inspired by the work of artists Pete r Fischli and David Weiss (Vrontissi et al., 2018), with a focus on three structural concepts: (1) Stacking, arrangi ng objects vertically by placing one on top of another; (2) spanning, creating a horizontal connection between tw o points (similar to a bridg e); and (3) cantilevering, extended spanning to create a horizontal structure anchor ed at a single point (resembling a crane). Our analysis focused on two activities: (a) Building stacking structures and (b) building spanning or cantilevering structures. These activities were spaced three weeks apart, and students had one month to complete each activity. Students documented their design outcomes and processes with learning diari es (249 pages in total of written reflections to guiding questions) and photographs (522 in total). We anal yzed the recurring structural concepts through iterative coding of the student-created designs and reflections. Guided by prior constructionist theoretical contributions on the role of familiar materials (also for architec tural learning), our analysis examined student design outcomes for structural concepts and how the students’ design proc ess reflections evidenced material interactions that served structural behaviors. We focused on how students integrated structural principles into their design outcomes and processes to advance insights into how familiar objects can support interdisciplinary learning of structural and architectural design.

## Findings

Across both activities, students demonstrated structural concepts wi thin their design projects. Although the instructions encouraged focusing on distinct concepts, many groups initially struggled to isolate stacking, spanning, and cantilevering. In Activity 1, only 2% of groups used stacking alone, while 66% combined stacking with cantilevering, and 27% incorporated all three concepts. This fr equent combination of structural concepts suggests that students relied on stacking as a foundational principl e, using it as a basis for designing with more complex concepts that had not been taught in the course. However, it also highlights students’ difficulties in distinguishing and explicitly naming the structural concepts present in t heir designs, which challenges students from purposefully using structural concepts toward pushing their balancing structures toward a creative edge (e.g., nearly collapsing).

One representative example of this is a group that initially descr ibed their Activity 1 design as a series of stacked elements. The group wrote in their diary: “So we came  up with the idea of laying the scissors openly over the roll. The other stacks of steps followed quite intuitively, a s chopsticks and forks were placed on top.” While they identified stacking, the group also implicitly reflected on how the everyday objects led to the introduction of spanning into their design. This is evidenced by the photograph of their design outcome, showing that the chopsticks bridged across the open scissors (Figure 1, left). In Activity 2, the same group demonstrated conceptual articulation. They intentionally designed a structure where a long ruler spanned across two triangular rulers to extend the distance (Figure 1, right). They wrote: “We us ed two triangular rulers on which a long ruler was placed. Thus, we were able to span a further distance and incor porate another object (set triangle) into our construction.” Unlike their previous description, this reflection expli citly acknowledged spanning. This progression illustrates how iterative engagement with everyday materials supported students to refine their ability to recognize and apply structural concepts more deliberately. ## Discussion

This study contributes to bridging structural theory and design practice  in architectural education through the design of balancing structures with everyday objects. The findings suggest that everyday objects made it possible to design with more than one structural concept and to describe the concepts without naming them prior to having been taught them. This presents a foundational experience that future  activities can build on to support students in advancing designs that purposefully integrate structural  concepts to design balance structures. Designing with everyday objects supported an exploration of the intersection between s tructural function and architectural aesthetics. Future research will explore how the design with everyday objects can foster formal structural analysis, further bridging the gap between conceptual learning and engineering evalua tion in architectural education.
