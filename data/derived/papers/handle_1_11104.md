# “We were doing science, not just talking about science”: Embodied Learning and Science Identity Development

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, we explore the affordances of embodied activities for supporting elementary school students to participate in and develop positive relationships with science. We draw on sociocultural perspectives on identity: viewing identity as dynamic and emerging in interaction between students and their environment. We analyze post interviews in which students reflected on their experiences with and feelings about a ten-day series of embodied science activities, and then focus on three focal students in classroom video to understand how these views may have developed. We found that students both enjoyed and felt competent at the embodied science activities; positive views of science seemed to emerge through: a climate where all students could contribute ideas, expert positionings of students, positive socio-emotional experiences during embodiment, and validating challenges. We argue that foregrounding embodiment can be a valuable way to foster positive science identities.

## Introduction and framing

In science education, increasing attention has been  given to the importance of supporting students to not only learn content, but to develop identities as competent doers of science (Barton & Tan, 2010; Varelas, 2012). Such science identities influence and  are influenced by students’ experiences in and outs ide of the science classroom and have implications for students’ interests and parti cipation in both classroom-based science and in pursuing future science experiences. Barriers to identification with science exist for many students, but especially for youth that have been marginalized in and through narrow c onceptualizations of scientific practice. Statistic s that demonstrate that science fields are disproportionat ely male permeate educational research (National Center for Science and Engineering Statistics, 2023). While such reports refer to undergraduate education and the workforce, students often decide whether science is for them before even entering high school (Ainley et al., 2002; Crowley et al., 20 15). Additionally, while not all students will ente r a science career, all youth deserve to have posit ive experiences as students in science classrooms. Ther efore, it is important to consider how to support s tudents to develop positive views of themselves in relation to science from an early age. Students’ experiences in science are dependent on t he opportunities that they are given to participate. Science identities may be shaped by a myriad of facto rs, including individual interest (Crowley et al., 2015), curriculum (Chu et al., 2017), interactions between  teachers and students (Jackson & Suizzo, 2015), ex periences outside of school (Garner et al., 2016), and systems of privilege and oppression (Avraamidou, 2020). In this paper, we focus on the ways that a particular classroom ac tivity, learning s cience through technology-supported embodiment, contributes to students’ science identity development. Research on embodied cognitio n suggests that the body plays an important role in learning (Danish et al., 2020; Nathan, 2021). Embodiment has been talked about as having the potential to broaden ways of pa rticipating, and thus participation, in science (e. g., Pierson & Brady, 2020), but it is less clear how participatio n in embodied activities supports the development o f new identities in science for stude nts. In this paper, we examine the potential of lea rning science through embodied mixed-reality activities for supporting students to develop positive science identities.

## Theoretical framework

Identity (as it relates to learning) has been explored by researchers in a multitude of ways and from a wide variety of lenses (e.g., from statuses to processes, from i ndividual to collective, etc.). In the Learning Sci ences, many researchers have taken a sociocultural approach to study and define identity. For example, Hand & Gresalfi (2015) define identity as “one’s participation in and acro ss activities and the sense one makes of oneself in  relation to those activities” (p. 191). This definition implies  that identity is made up of two parts: how youth position themselves in activities (sometimes called particip atory identities or embodied identities) and how yo uth view themselves as a participant in those activities (sometimes called narrative identities). Work on narrative identities (Sfard & Prusak, 2005) focuses on identity as stories and beliefs, while work on participatory identit ies (Hand & Gresalfi, 2015; Nasir  & Cooks, 2009) highlights identity as activity or behavior. In t his paper we build on bot h frameworks to explore learners’ science identities in relation to embodied modeling activities. Identity is also closely linked to learning (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Lave (1996) views learning as a facet of social practice. In this view, learning is princ ipally about “the identity-making life projects of participants in communities of practice” (p. 157). Viewing learning  and identity as part of communities of practice po sitions identity as something that goes beyond individual d ispositions or actions. In science education specif ically, scholars (Tan et al., 2013; Caraballo, 2012) have built on the idea of a community of practice to define identitiesin-practice as “identities students acquire or choose to adopt in the science classroom” (Tan & Barton, 2 008, p. 49). An individual student’s ideas about themselves and participation in the classroom are impacted by both how they see themselves and how they are framed by othe rs (Fields & Enyedy, 2013). This positioning and repositioning is ongoing and has been referred to a s identity work (Calabrese-Barton et al., 2013). These sociocultural framings of identity require attending to how students participate in relation to the design of the task and to student/teacher interactions. In viewing ide ntity as something that is co-constructed by youth and their environments, rather than an individual trait, sociocultural perspectives support an asset-based, rather thandeficitbased approach to understanding why youth dissociat e from science. Following Hand & Gresalfi (2015), w e examine the ways in which the foregrounding of embo died acti vity afforded and constrain ed how students participate and the sense that they make of that participation.

## Literature review

This paper brings together two relatively distinct areas of educational research: those of identity in  science and those of embodied science activities. We review bot h how identity and how embodiment have been studied  in science education. We conclude by b ringing together these two areas, asking how embodi ment as a classroom participation structure may foster positive science experiences and identities for young students. Identity in science education In science education specifically, there has been a  move from psychological or cognitive framings of i dentity towards a sociocultural framing (Lee, 2012). Studies tend to focus on understanding barriers to identification with science, or the opposite, understanding how identif ication with science develops, and how to support t he development of positive science identities in both cases. Martin-Hansen (2018) proposes three themes to support students in STEM: supporting recruitment and retention, supporting participation in science projects and courses, and the impact of mentorship on youth. In terms of barriers, youth tend to distinguish between doing o r liking science and wanting to be a scientist (Archer et al., 2010; Wade-Jaimes et al., 2021). While we acknowledge that not all youth will or should become scientists, we believe that all youth should have the opportunity to view themselves of having the potential to explore science if it interests them. Additionally, being successful in school science does not necessarily imply that youth will feel affiliation towards science or engage deeply in it (Carlone et al., 2014). Olitsky and colleagues (2010) found that eighth graders from a racially, ethnically, and socioeconomically diverse school who were interested in science did not achieve at high levels or identify with it because of barriers including: teach er, peer, and administrator discourses, negative st ereotypes, and structural issues. Adams-Wiggins (2020) adds microexclusions by peers in sma ll groups as a potential barrier to the development of disciplinary identities in science. Therefore, while we tak e students’ feelings about science as a starting point for developing positive science iden tity, we also explore how these feelings relate to students’ positioning of themselves and others in relation to science. Embodiment Broadly speaking, theories of embodied cognition po sit that the body plays a central role in cognition. This research ranges in focus from the role of gestures (Alibali & Nathan, 2012) to the role of full-body experiences (Enyedy et al., 2012; Gallagher & Lindgren, 2015) in supporting sen se-making. While embodied cognition scholars often focus on individual movement as a me ans for representing cognitive processes, we focus here on the role of the body in coordinated, group activiti es. Such multi-party, whole-body activities have been found to support the learning of mathematical (Ma, 2017; Vogelstein et al., 2019) and scientific concepts (Tu et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022). Giving students opportunities to move toge ther during learning experiences also has implications for their experiences in the classroom  beyond content learning. For example, embodied sci ence activities have been found to foster agency (Keifert et al., 2017; Steinberg & Danish,  2022) and positive social and emotional development (Humburg, 2020) for elementary school students. It is well established that many classrooms are cha racterized by conversational structures that afford students with very little agency; that is, the Init iate-Respond-Evaluate sequence, in which teachers initiate a question (that they typically know the answer to), a student responds to that question, and the teache r then evaluates the student’s answer (Cazden, 2001). Thes e conversational structures limit possibilities for  developing positive relationships with science. Activities tha t foreground collective  movement may have the potential to disrupt these limiting classroom practices; in embo died science activities, students have agency to ac t in many ways beyond talk, including by choosing where, how, and when to move in the space. In addition to introducing physical movement into t he classroom, embodiment also enables students to take on the perspective of various agents in scient ific systems (Soylu et al., 2017; Wilensky & Stroup, 2013). Such perspective taking has been found to allow students to build empathy towards non-human agents and to see multiple ways of relating themselves to science (Jen et al., 2021). This practice of perspective taking, or pretending to be something else, par allels children’s dramatic or pretend play. Wh ile little research has explored the connection between embodiment in science and learner’s identities, there is a wealth of research that suggests that pretend play supports the exploration of imagined i dentities (Kervin & Maintain, 2022; Wohlwend, 2009). This research suggests that embodied play may support exploration of identity. Finally, there have also been recent discussions ar ound embodied designs and equity. For example, Mathayas and colleagues (2022) questioned the assumption that embodiment is implicitly “good and desired” by participants and asked how designs that foreground embodiment can also be dignity affirming. Embodied physical positioning is also intertwined with social positio ning (Parr et al., 2021), with implications for how student s are positioned in the classroom, and therefore, how the y position themselves in relation to science. These  questions of value and positioning are closely tied to questions of identity, as the ways that students feel and are positioned while participating in embodiment have implications for their relationships with science. Thus, w hile there is little overlap between studies of ide ntity and studies of embodiment in science education, there is reason to believe that embodied  participation structures might help students to ex plore their identities as they relate to science. As such, this paper seeks to answer the research question: How did participation in embodied science activities shape students’ science identities?

## Methodology

This work was conducted at a public elementary scho ol in a suburban area in the Midwestern United Stat es. A class of 22 fifth grade students participated in th e curriculum, with 18 students consenting to partic ipate in the research study. Thirteen students  self-reported demographic information. Students ranged i n age from 10-12, seven students used she/her pronouns, four students used he/him pronouns, one student used they/them pronouns, and one student used both he/him and they/them pron ouns. Students enga ged in a series of ten science lessons lasting approximately forty-five minutes to one hour, supported by the GEM-STEP software, a platform that supports students to engage in mixed-reality modeling (Danish et al., 2022). In general, each day, students worked with a small group to make a plan, and then each group par ticipated in one round of embodied activity followe d by a full class discussion. During the embodiment, students could control agents via either moving thr ough the classroom wearing hats with po zyx tags on them, or through an iPad (Figure 1). Pozyx is a commercial tracking system that provides information to the GEM-STEP software. Figure 1 Students embody worms and bunnies (left) and the projected GEMSTEP screen (right) The first four lessons focused on energy transfer in pond ecosystems, the next four lessons were devoted to energy transfer and decomposition in garden ecosystems, the ninth lesson allowed students to design their own modeling contexts, and the final lesson focused on modeling scientists and their practices. Days one through ni ne of the implementation were part of a larger study on how s tudents could learn about complex science phenomena through cycles of embodied and computational modeli ng. During these lessons, we noticed deep student engagement and excitement about participating, and wondered how they were contributing to students’ id eas about science. As a result, we added one day of instruction to the curriculum approximately three weeks after the initial implementation had ended in which students could explore what it means to be a scientist. Students also participated in individual interviews before the first lesson, after the ninth lesson, and after the tenth lesson. The first two interviews focused on students’ understanding of scientific modeling, while the final interview asked students about their science identities as related to their experiences with all  the lessons. This paper focuses on this final interview, as its goal was to capture students’ narrative identities. Therefore, it asked questions about students’ past experiences wi th and feelings about science, as well as about stu dents’ experiences and feelings about our embodied activit ies and how those were similar and different. The i nterview was semi-structured and lasted between fifteen and thirty minutes for each student, with most taking about twenty minutes. Thirteen students participated in the inte rview (four used she/her pronouns, two used he/him,  one used they/them, one used he/him and they/them, and five did not self-report). Analysis Because we view identity as something that occurs i n interaction between learners and their environmen ts, it is something that is dynamic, fluid, and changing depe nding on the context that a student is in. Therefor e, we look at identity-salient moments; that is, instances that had the capacity to influ ence students’ longer-term ideas about themselves. We do not intend to claim that one thing that happened in the classroom drastically and permanently changed students’ relationships with science. Inste ad, we look at in-the-moment interactions as places that have the potential to contribute to more sustaining views about oneself (narrative identities). These classroom moments make students’ participatory identities visible (Hand & Gresalfi, 2015). Our research question asks: How did participation in embodied science activities shape students’ science identities? Data sources included the identity interview and cl assroom video and screen captures from the entire lesson sequence. We began by watching the identity interviews for each student, as we hoped these migh t point to 1) students who seemed to do significant identit y work during the implementation, and 2) moments in  the classroom video that were significant for that iden tity work. We took an iterative approach to watchin g the interviews. In round one, we wrote analytic memos, focusing on how students described their experience s with science similarly or differently than they describe d their experiences with GEM-STEP. Based on these memos, we rewatched the interviews, iteratively open codin g the data, writing new memos, and refining our hyp otheses. Based on this process, we grouped our codes into th ree themes that described why students had a positi ve experience with our activities. We also identified three students to focus on in the classroom video who, according to literature, were less likely to identify with science (girls and nonbinary students) and who articulated a negative relationship with science but a positive experience  with embodied sci ence in the interviews. We selected these students to better understand the affordances of em bodiment for facilitating this shift. We conducted Interaction Analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) of these episod es, iteratively watching them and writing analyti c memos (Saldana, 2011) about students’ identity work and t he conditions that supported it. To guide our memos, we followed Carlone’s (2012) recommendations for seein g identity work in interaction, taking note of who students were treated as obligated to be, what it meant in the classroom to be successful, who got to be scientific and how, and when students made bids for recognition and how  they were responded to. We also paid attention to embodiment and the social experience it created and  to group work dynamics, as these were highlighted in t he interviews by the focal students. We iteratively reviewed and refined the memos and developed claims about how positive identity work was supported.

## Findings

We report on general trends in the interviews across all students, and then zoom into the classroom experiences of three focal students to understand how the embodied activities supported students to develop these ideas. Narrative identity in interviews Initial analytic memos about the interview data sug gested that nearly all the students had a positive experience with the mixed-reality embodied activities. Eleven out of 13 stude nts indicated that they would like to learn science through movement again, with the other two students indifferent. We identified three overarching reasons that students liked GEM-STEP: it let them move around/engage not just by reading or sitting, it let them work together in productive ways, and it felt like a game.

In terms of moving around, students (n = 10) cited a variety of benefits of movement-based educational experiences. Many students called out the movement as fun and energizing. For example, Tiana stated, “ I like moving around ‘cause like it gets my body energized. Like if I’m sitting down I have to move around, s o it basically just helps me.” Movement was also motivat ing for students. For example, Bob explained: “We u sually stay in our class and watch videos about the ecosys tem and do just plain old boring worksheets, but this time we actually got to move our bodies around and it was a  lot of fun, and it actually made me want to partic ipate more and learn instead of just sitting there writing on a paper.” In addition to being motivating, several students also explained that the movement helped them to understa nd the scientific concepts. Magnoliaway explained: “I feel like it was like more hands on, and the other stuff  that we did was kinda, like the teachers did most of it, and we were just kind of there for them to like just teach kinda, and not really be a part of it, but I feel like with this one, you were more hands on get to be a part of it than just the teachers talk to you. I think that it made sense more and it was more fun than just having people tell you information and doing stuff to process information.” Several students (n = 8) also pointed out their experiences with the collaborative nature of the embodied activities. As a whole, students appreciated the opportunities to work together both in terms of planning and testing their models. For example, Ivy shared, “when I hear d other people’s ideas, I created a bigger idea, and it helped everyone.” One important piece of developing a scie nce identity is positioning oneself and being posit ioned by others as competent, both of which happened in the interviews. Drew positioned herself as competent, explaining, “working together, I thought I was good at that”, and Layla positioned everyone as able to be successful: “I know a lot of other kids that also did a really good job.” Finally, students (n = 7) appreciated the game-like and interactive nature of activities. For Magnoliaway, interacting with the embodied model provided her ac cess to seeing herself as an active doer of science. She said, “I feel like we were doing science, not you were pu tting words into our brains, we were doing science, not  just talking about science.” Another focal student, Jamaican Bobsled had similar sentiments: “we weren’t necessarily learning stuff but discovering it.” The game-like nature was also motivating and helped to reduce frustration for students. Drew highlighted that they had “multiple different chances” when the ecosystem health went d own quickly, and Ivy explained, “it’s like a game, you can pay attention, it’s better.” Participatory identity in classroom video Overall, students seemed to both like the embodied activities and feel good about their ability to par ticipate in them– two indicators of a developing positive science ide ntity. This held true both for students who already articulated positive relationships with science, and for those who felt negatively about science. We f ocus on the latter group of students (Magnoliaway, Jamaican Bob sled (JB), and Alice) to understand the potential o f embodiment to change students’  experiences with science. For these students, we id entified four ways in which the embodied activities afforded opportunities for positive science identity development. Although the re were many moments of each across the lesson sequence, we unpack one illustrative example of each due to space. First, there was a classroom norm of encouraging all students to contribute their ideas and treating those ideas as valued. Embodiment served to create a spac e where students could test their ideas scientifica lly, rather than be evaluated by a teacher. For example, Alice had a hypothesis that algae needed sunlight to grow:

(1) Alice:   I feel like we should put three up there and three on the bottom so

we can see if they actually grow or not.

(2) Facilitator 1:  Does everyone agree with Alice, is that a good idea?

(3) Students:  ((overlapping yes’s))

(4) Facilitator 2:  It’s a good idea if you can test it, right?

On line 1, Alice proposed an idea, holding the floor. Rather than evaluate that idea, on line 2, the facilitator asked for student feedback, positioning students as the ones who held scientific authority in this space. Th e students’ agreement on line 3 ser ved to position Alice as competent, and the facilit ator’s follow-up on line 4 added that Alice’s idea was good because it could be tested, thus positioning it as a valued scientific practice. Relatedly, students were positioned as experts and had ownership over their ideas. They could draw on lived experiences and personal interests, they were  asked follow-up questions, and facilitators played the role of a co-participant in their models. Here, students’ prior experiences with embodiment may have facilitated th is expert positioning. For example, when a facilitator  demoed a model for the class on Day 4, JB volunteered a suggestion without being prompted: “I would change the plant and the bunny to be closer together so yo u don’t have to travel as far and can go a little faster.” JB drew on their prior experience and offered a sugges tion to the facilitator, positioning themself as an expert and the facilitator as a co-participant rather than an authority. Third, the embodied activities often elicited positive social and emotional experiences for students that were directly tied to their scientific actions. During embodiment, all students had a role and their actions mattered. As a result, at the end of a round, students often experienced moments of both individual and col lective excitement/pride. For example, after Magnoliaway and JB’s group modeled on Day 7, Magnoliaway said, “we did great!” and the group exchanged high-fives. This sense of shared accomplishment served t o position all the students as competent and foregrounded the collaborative nature of science. Students’ satisfaction was tied to their actions and to a growing understanding of the concepts. For example, after being a worm, Magnoliaway shared:

(1) Magnoliaway:  I feel like we lived very long. We stayed alive a long time.

(2) Facilitator 2:  How?

(3) Magnoliaway:  Because I felt like, if there was only one bunny, which there was,

that there needs to be one more because a lot of people died. But it took a long time for all of us to die. But I think there could be another bunny. And I really liked being a worm. Oh and I thought the bunny was gonna eat me!

In this interaction, Magnoliaway expressed pride th at both she and her other group members stayed aliv e as a worm (line 1), shared that she enjoyed being a worm  (line 3), and linked those outcomes to her understanding of the scientific system. She suggeste d adding another bunny next time, which suggests th at she recognized that bunnies produce waste that the worms need to surviv e. Thus, through participating as a worm in the emb odied model, Magnoliaway experienced feelings of success as she learned more about the scientific ideas. However, while there were many positive moments lin ked to embodiment, that is not to say that frustration was not present. Yet, when students exp erienced frustration or challenges during embodimen t, they were validated and positioned as something the class could brainstorm around, rather than as shortcomings of any individual. For example, on Day 2, students embodied fish, ate all the algae and then quickly died. They reflected:

(1)  JB:   I was gonna say that in our group, I think it was the person on the

iPad, we had a plan, and then everyone just abandoned the plan.

(2)  Facilitator 1:  So we need a better way of making a plan and keeping track of it.

(3)  JB:   Well, we had a plan, we talked about it, and people just didn’t do it.

(4) Facilitator 1:  And why do you think it was hard to follow the plan?

(5)  JB:   It wasn’t.

(6)  Facilitator 1:  It wasn’t hard? I think it’s kind of hard because you’re trying to

move.

(7)  Facilitator 3:  Maybe another way to think about it, it might not be hard but is there

something in the model that made it tempting to do different things, because we can change that. What made it tempting f or people to not follow the plan and do something else?

Students proceeded to brainstorm around how to make  sure that everyone in the group followed the plan.  They provided suggestions like making the health bar inv isible when it is under half health or making it so that they had a collective goal: for all the fish to survive for a minute, rather than trying to be the last fis h standing like in a “battle royale.” This conversation reframed JB’s initial blame of the person on the iPad for doing i t ‘wrong’ to a way to think about how to refine the model/activity so that everyone could be successful.

## Discussion and conclusion

The embodied activities afforded opportunities for students to have positive experiences with science and to position themselves and others as competent doers of science. Across the whole class, students expressed feelings of enjoyment towards the embodied activities, including students that expressed strong negative relationships with other kinds of science. While we cannot make claims about whether students’ long-term views of science shifted as a result of these activities, we do argue that e mbodied activities have the potential to position students with agency and competence and to spark positive in-the-moment feelings and experiences around science. In future work, we plan to explore whether and how participat ing in embodied science activities impacted how students define science and what it means to participate in it.
