# From Feeling to Regulating: The Role of Meta-Affective Goals in Activating Self-Regulated Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) is a critical determinant of academic success, yet the mechanisms that activate this process, particularly affective ones, remain undertheorized. Contemporary “trigger regulation” frameworks identify external events as precursors to regulation but often overlook the pivotal role of the learner’s internal, meta-affective interpretation of the resulting emotions. This paper introduces the construct of meta-affective goals (MAGs) — internally held goals concerning how to experience and interpret one’s own affect during learning — as a potent, top-down activation cue for SRL. By synthesizing theories of SRL, affect and meta-affect in learning, and affective goals, this paper proposes a framework wherein the adoption of MAGs (e.g., framing confusion as a productive signal) directly initiates the forethought, performance, and self-reflection phases of regulation. This conceptualization has profound implications for measurement, the design of emotionally responsive learning environments, and the extension of SRL theory to socially shared regulation in collaborative contexts.

Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) is a cornerstone of modern educational psychology, widely recognized as a crucial predictor of academic motivation, achievement, and the capacity for lifelong learning (Panadero, 2017; Sitzmann & Ely, 2011). SRL is not a static mental ability or academic skill; rather, it is a dynamic, self-directive process through which learners actively transform their mental abilities into academic competencies. This active, constructive process involves learners setting goals for their learning and subsequently attempting to monitor, regulate, and control their cognition, motivation, and behavior to achieve those goals (Panadero, 2017; Zimmerman & Schunk, 2011).

While numerous models capably describe the phases and components of SRL, a fundamental question remains less explored: what specifically initiates or activates the regulatory cycle? The “trigger regulation” framework offers a compelling answer, defining “activation events” as events, situations, or incidents that may inhibit the learning process and thus necessitate a regulatory response from the learner (Järvelä et al., 2023; Järvelä & Hadwin, 2024). These triggers, which can range from task-related challenges to interpersonal conflicts, provide a concrete mechanism for understanding the activation of SRL processes. However, the application of this framework has often centered on cognitive or behavioral responses to external events, creating a conceptual gap. Concurrently, the field of SRL has undergone a significant evolution, shifting from “ cold,” purely cognitive models to “ hot” models that emphasize the integral role of affect, motivation, and emotion (Ben-Eliyahu, 2019; Efklides, 2011; Pekrun, 2024). Academic emotions are no longer viewed as mere disruptions to be suppressed but are understood as crucial informational signals within the learning process, providing bottom-up feedback that informs self-regulation (D’Mello & Graesser, 2012; Vogl et al., 2021). For instance, confusion can be productive when it is recognized and resolved through adaptive regulation (D’Mello & Graesser, 2012; Sinha, 2022).

This evolution creates an “ affective gap” in activation theory: current trigger frameworks do not fully articulate how a learner’s affective experience of a trigger event serves as the primary catalyst for regulation. The critical juncture is not the external event itself, such as encountering a difficult problem, but the learner’s internal interpretation of the resulting affective response, like confusion or frustration. Drawing from the theory of meta-affect, which posits that the “feeling about the feeling” is often more influential than the primary emotion, the true activation point for productive SRL is the learner’ s meta-affective framing of their emotional state (DeBellis & Goldin, 2006; Goldin, 2002; Radoff et al., 2019). This reconceptualizes the trigger from an external event to an internal, metacognitive-affective process.

The central thesis of this paper is that the construct of meta-affective goals (MAGs) may provide a novel mechanism to bridge this affective gap. A MAG is conceptualized as a learner’s internally adopted goal regarding how to interpret and value one’s own affective response to a learning trigger, thereby functioning as a self-administered gate-keeping mechanism that shifts the locus of activation from external prompts to learner agency. Conceptually, MAGs align with contemporary work on metamotivation, which examines how learners monitor 272 and regulate their motivational states to sustain goal pursuit (Fujita et al., 2024a; Fujita et al., 2024b; Miele et al., 2020). We argue that MAG s constitute a powerful, top-down activation mechanism for the SRL cycle. The subsequent sections will first review the theoretical foundations of SRL, affect in learning, and meta-affect; second, formally define and elaborate the MAG framework; third, explore its implications for research and instructional design; and finally, discuss future directions, including its extension to collaborative learning. Theoretical foundations: A synthesis of self-regulation, affect, and metacognition

## Research question and conceptual approach

This conceptual paper asks: How do meta-affective goals function as internal activation mechanisms that initiate and shape self-regulated learning processes under conditions of affective challenge? To address this question, we conducted a structured conceptual synthesis across three literatures: models of self-regulated learning, research on affect and emotion regulation in learning, and scholarship on meta-affect and metamotivation. Theories were selected for their relevance to SRL activation, explicit tre atment of affect or meta-affect, and applicability to educational settings. We then integrated constructs based on their functional roles in initiating, sustaining, and reflecting on regulated learning—aligning the mediational chain from primary affective responses through meta-affective interpretations and motivational states to self-regulatory behaviors. This approach allowed us to identify conceptual gaps, propose MAGs as a bridging mechanism, and outline methodological and design implications. Models of self-regulated learning: From cyclical phases to social contexts Decades of research have produced robust models of SRL that describe its structure and function. A foundational framework is Zimmerman ’s cyclical model, which organizes SRL into three recursive phases: forethought, performance, and self-reflection (Zimmerman & Schunk, 2011). The forethought phase involves task analysis and goal setting, energized by motivational beliefs. The performance phase encompasses task execution, guided by self-control and self-monitoring. Finally, the self-reflection phase involves self-judgment and self-reaction, which influence subsequent learning cycles. Expanding on this, Pintrich (2000) ’s four-phase model proposed a more granular framework with four phases: forethought/planning, monitoring, control, and reaction/reflection, and specified four areas for regulation within each phase: cognition, motivation/affect, behavior, and context. Offering a different perspective, Winne and Hadwin’s COPES model conceptualizes SRL as an information-processing activity: a learner defines the task by interpreting conditions (both external and internal, including affect), sets goals and plans operations, which yield products that are then evaluated against standards (Greene & Azevedo, 2007; Winne & Hadwin, 1998). This model’s focus on interpreting internal conditions aligns closely with the nature of MAGs. Meanwhile, Boekaerts’ (200 0) dual-processing model makes a critical distinction between a “mastery” pathway focused on learning and a “coping” pathway focused on preserving emotional well-being. A MAG, by shaping how learners appraise their affective state in relation to task goals, would be expected to influence that pathway choice.

More recent models have added further sophistication. Efklides (2011) ’s metacognitive and a ffective model of s elf-regulated learning (MASRL) provides a two-level architecture distinguishing between stable “person” level traits (like self-concept) and dynamic “task × person” level states (in-the-moment experiences and emotions). MAGs can be understood as a mechanism linking these levels, where developmentally formed and enduring beliefs inform the setting of an agentic goal in response to a specific affective experience. Finally, the extension of SRL into social contexts is captured by Hadwin et al. (2017)’ s socially shared regulation (SSRL) model, which distinguishes among self-regulation, co-regulation, and socially shared regulation. SSRL involves interdependent regulatory processes orchestrated by a group, providing a foundation for extending the MAG concept to collaborative learning.

A synthesis of these models reveals the architecture of self-regulation, yet they do not fully specify the delicate lever a learner can pull to navigate this system when faced with a challenging affective state, which is where the role of affect and meta-affect becomes central. The affective substrate of learning: Epistemic emotions and emotion regulation Affect is not merely a byproduct of learning but a core informational system that guides cognition. Critical cognitive states, such as encountering a discrepancy or an impasse, give rise to both metacognitive feelings (e.g., a feeling of difficulty) and epistemic emotions (e.g., surprise, curiosity, confusion, frustration) (Nerantzaki et al., 2021; Vogl et al., 2021). These emotions are directly related to the knowledge-building process itself. While 273 positive emotions like curiosity candrive inquiry, even seemingly negative emotions like confusion can be highly productive, signaling deep cognitive engagement, provided they are successfully resolved (Chinn et al., 2021). Given the power of these emotions, a learner ’s ability to manage them, a process known as e motion regulation (ER) (Gross, 2001; Wright et al., 2025), is plausibly a direct prerequisite for effective SRL (Mega et al., 2014; van Berk & Dignath, 2025). ER and SRL exist in a dynamic, mutually influential cycle; students with strong ER skills demonstrate greater persistence, higher motivation, and an enhanced ability to handle frustration adaptively, all of which are hallmarks of successful self-regulation (Ben-Eliyahu, 2019; Gross, 2001). Meta-affect: Reframing the experience of learning challenges The impact of an emotion is often determined not by the primary feeling itself, but by the secondary layer of interpretation applied to it. This is the core of meta-affect, a construct defined by DeBellis and Goldin as “feelings about feelings,” the monitoring of affect, and affect as a monitoring process (DeBellis & Goldin, 1997, 2006; Goldin, 2002). Meta-affect explains how the valence and consequence of an emotional experience can be radically transformed (Radoff et al., 2019). For example, the physiological arousal associated with fear can be experienced as highly pleasurable on a roller coaster because of a meta-affective frame of safety and excitement. Similarly, the feeling of frustration during mathematical problem-solving can be experienced as debilitating anxiety or as an engaging and interesting challenge, depending entirely on the meta-affective layer (DeBellis & Goldin, 1997). This suggests that a key goal for education is not to eliminate challenging emotions but to foster meta-affective learning — the development of stable, productive dispositions where such difficulties are interpreted as signs of productive engagement (Hidi & Renninger, 2006). This perspective aligns with Kahneman’s distinction between the experiencing self and the remembering self (Kahneman, 2011): the immediate experience of emotion is separate from the retrospective evaluation that gives it meaning. MAGs operate at this reflective layer, guiding how learners label and interpret their feelings. Recent Learning Sciences resear ch has underscored that emotions and epistemic feelings play a constitutive role in inquiry and conceptual change, not merely epiphenomena (Radoff et al., 2019; Camacho et al., 2025; Pekrun, 2024). Developmental origins of meta-affective goals The propensity to adopt a certain meta-affective stance is not arbitrary but is rooted in an individual ’s developmental history. Early in life, children begin to establish a “ fixed style of life ” with corresponding emotional and behavioral habits that form a fundamental relationship between mind and body (Adler, 1931). Each individual’s unique upbringing and experiences create a distinct affective profile, where neurological sensitivities to environmental stimuli are shaped by the richness or scarcity of scenarios encountered during sensitive periods (Boyce, 2016; Camacho et al., 2025), producing individual differences in environmental sensitivity (e.g., via dopamine D4-receptor mediated mechanisms). This long-term affective profile gives rise to what can be conceptualized as a meta-affective orientation: the long-term, overarching preferences and cares an individual holds toward their ideal self. As illustrated in Figure 1, this process can be modeled as a feedback loop where environmental cues trigger meta-affective processing, leading to responses and outcomes that shape self-efficacy and, in turn, reinforce or modify the overarching meta-affective orientation (Komarraju & Nadler, 2013; Schunk & Zimmerman, 1997).

A new construct: Proposing meta-affective goals as activation cues for SRL We formalize the construct of the MAG as the agentic bridge between an affective experience and the activation of a productive regulatory cycle.

From curricular objectives to learner-set goals: Redefining affective goals Traditionally, “affective goals” are defined as curriculum-level objectives focused on shaping students’ long-term attitudes, values, and feelings, often categorized in a hierarchy from receiving and responding to valuing and characterizing (Krathwohl et al., 1964). While valuable, these goals are typically set by the educator for the learner. To understand the activation of SRL, the locus of control must shift. An affective goal in the context of SRL is not a semester-long objective, but a standard a learner holds and enacts during learning, representing an operationalization of their long-term, developmentally-formed meta-affective orientation  (DeBellis & Goldin, 2006; Efklides, 2011).

The meta-affective goal framework: A mechanism for activating regulation A MAG is formally defined as a developmentally formed, enduring goal concerning how to experience and interpret one’s own affect during learning — a guiding orientation that shapes the learner’s response to momentary affective experiences in the direction of their ideal self. It is a goal about how to feel about a feeling. For instance, a learner who holds a productive MAG will, upon encountering confusion, interpret that feeling as a sign of tackling something complex and important — and engage rather than withdraw. When facing frustration, the same orienting goal leads the learner to channel that frustration as productive energy to find a new strategy. Such goals sit squarely within contemporary metamotivation accounts that emphasize monitoring and regulation of one ’s motivational/affective state to sustain goal pursuit (Fujita et al., 2024a, 2024b; Miele et al., 2020). The MAG thus functions as a powerful, top-down activation cue for SRL. It is a direct intervention in Zimmerman’s “forethought” phase and Pintrich’s “forethought, planning, and activation” phase (Pintrich, 2000; Zimmerman & Schunk, 2011). As shown in Figure 2, the MAG acts as a “regulatory keeper,” determining whether SRL is activated or disrupted. When the MAG matches the learning context (e.g., viewing challenge as an opportunity), it activates productive regulation. When it mismatches (e.g., viewing conf usion as a threat), it candisrupt learning. By deliberately adopting a productive MAG, the learner actively redefines the internal conditions of the task (Winne & Hadwin’s model), selects Boekaerts’ “mastery” pathway over the “coping” pathway, and primes themselves for adaptive self-judgments in the self-reflection phase (Boekaerts, 200 0; Winne & Hadwin, 1998). The MAG transforms a potentially inhibitory affective state into a catalyst for strategic action. Empirical precedent for this mechanism exists: Radoff et al. (2019) documented students in science classrooms who spontaneously reframed anxiety as excitement, a meta-affective shift that sustained their inquiry, and Sinha (2022) showed that negative affective states, when processed through failure-driven scaffolding, predicted deeper learning outcomes. These findings suggest that the component processes of the MAG framework are already at work in learning settings, though they have not yet been theorized as a unified construct. ## Implications for research and design

The meta-affective goal framework offers a concrete agenda for both empirical investigation and the design of learning environments.

Methodological pathways: Measuring meta-affective goal adoption and enactment Measuring an internal, enduring construct whose expression is context-sensitive, such as  MAGs, presents a significant challenge that necessitates a multi-pronged approach. One avenue is through questionnaires and selfreport. Existing instruments, such as the meta-emotion scale (MES) which assesses general dispositions toward one’s emotions (Mitmansgruber et al., 2009), could be adapted from a trait-based measure to a state-based one to capture a learner’s propensity to hold and activate certain types of MAGs in specific contexts. Scales designed to measure affective learning (e.g., Rogers et al., 20 18) could also be repurposed to assess the adoption of specific meta-affective stances. To capture the dynamic nature of MAGs, process-oriented measures are essential. Thinkaloud protocols conducted during problem-solving (Ericsson & Simon, 1993) could capture the in-the-moment verbalization of a MAG being activated or expressed. Additionally, microanalytic methods, which involve pausing a learning session to ask targeted questions (Cleary et al., 2012), could probe for MAG adoption with high temporal precision. In digital learning environments, a powerful approach would be to use multimodal learning analytics (Mu et al., 2020). This could involve combining data from physiological signals  like heart rate variability, which provide data on emotional states like excitement and stress, with trace data like clicks and navigation patterns. These data streams can be used to automatically identify likely “activation events,” which can then serve as prompts for targeted self-report measures to determine if a MAG was deployed. Designing for meta-affective learning: Fostering productive emotional experiences The MAG framework implies that learning environments should be designed not just to manage cognitive load (Sweller, 1988) but also to manage “ meta-affective load.” We use this term as an analogy rather than a direct theoretical extension of cognitive load theory, which originates from an information-processing paradigm (Plass & Kalyuga, 2019); meta-affective load instead refers to the regulatory demand that unexamined or overwhelming emotions place on the learner’s capacity to engage in strategic self-regulation (Ben-Eliyahu, 2019; Efklides, 2011). An emotionally responsive environment reduces this load, freeing the learner to activate  productive MAGs. This leads to several key design principles. First, environments should make affect and meta-affect explicit, normalizing the full spectrum of learning emotions by using language that acknowledge s potential feelings and providing learners with tools to reflect on them. Second, instructors should model and scaffold MAGs, verbalizing their own process of reframing challenging emotions as cues for strategic action. Third, it is crucial to create emotionally “safe” and responsive environments where mistakes are framed as integral to learning. This requires fostering positive relationships, providing respectful feedback, and ensuring high usability to minimize extraneous frustration. Culturally responsive teaching (CRT) practices are fundamental to creating this psychological safety (Abacioglu et al., 2020). Finally, designers can use emotional design to elicit productive emotions, leveraging aesthetics, narrative, and surprise to create an engaging context that encourages learners to interpret challenges productively. Extending the framework: Socially shared meta-affective goals (SS-MAGs) The MAG framework can be extended to collaborative learning contexts. Drawing on Hadwin et al. (2017) ’s SSRL model, where groups collectively regulate their learning processes, we can posit the existence of socially shared meta-affective goals (SS-MAGs). Unlike individual MAGs, which are developmentally formed, an SS-MAG emerges through explicit collective  negotiation: the group establishes a shared orientation toward how it will interpret and respond to its shared affective experiences, such as collective confusion or interpersonal friction. 278 For example, a team might establish the shared orientation: “ As a team, we treat moments of disagreement as opportunities to innovate.” Once adopted, an SS-MAG functions as an enduring group norm that activates SSRL, guiding the group’ s collective regulation of motivation, communication strategies, and social dynamics. Investigating the formation and impact of SS-MAGs represents a promising future direction for research in computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL).

## Limitations and boundary conditions

The ability to develop and enact MAGs is a sophisticated skill. Its effectiveness likely depends on certain boundary conditions. For instance, it requires a threshold of metacognitive awareness and emotional intelligence to identify and reflect upon one’s own affective states. Developmentally, young children may have limited capacity for the sustained affective self-regulation involved in MAGs and would likely require significant scaffolding from adults to cultivate this orientation. Furthermore, it is cr ucial to recognize the potential for maladaptive MAGs, such as an enduring goal to suppress all anxiety, which could lead to experiential avoidance and superficial processing rather than productive regulation. Another important boundary condition is that met a-affective orientations are shaped by individuals ’ developmental history and sociocultural norms; learners bring diverse emotional repertoires and socialized interpretations to the classroom. Educators should therefore provide adaptive scaffolds and co-regulation opportunities that account for these differences rather than assuming a uniform capacity to develop productive MAGs.

In summary, this paper proposes the construct of MAGs — developmentally formed, enduring goals concerning how to experience and interpret one’s own affect during learning — as a crucial link between a learner’s affective experience and the activation of strategic regulation. By repositioning SRL activation as an internal, agentic process rooted in the learner’ s meta-affective orientation rather than a reaction to an external event, the MAG framework reconceptualizes the relationship between motivation and self-regulated learning. The framework contributes a two-level architecture in which long-term meta-affective orientations give rise to enduring MAGs that guide learners’ responses to momentary affective challenges, and extends this logic to collaborative contexts through the construct of socially shared meta-affective goals. It offers a mediational model integrating cognition, affect, and meta-affect; an agenda for mixed-methods measurement; and actionable principles for designing emotionally responsive learning environments.
