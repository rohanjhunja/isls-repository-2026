# Towards Decolonizing Agency in Addressing Racialization of Behaviors in Settler Colonial Schools

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper proposes a decolonizing agency framework, emphasizing how a diverse group of policy actors center Indigenous sovereignty, histories, lived experiences, and expertise to address racial disproportionality within a school community. Decolonizing agency builds upon existing frameworks on agency, in particular relational agency (Edwards, 2005; 2007) and expands the concepts in practice by incorporating decolonizing methodologies (Brayboy, 2005; Lomawaima, 2000; Smith, 2018; 2021) and cultural-historical activity theory (CHAT). We have developed the concept of decolonizing agency through a five-year-long transformative research partnership with an Anishanabee (Ojibwe) Nation to address the overrepresentation of Indigenous students in exclusionary school discipline and special education for “behavioral disturbance” at a rural high school. Six dimensions of decolonizing agency emerged based on our research-practice partnership to address the racialization of behavioral problems in a school serving Indigenous students.

## Introduction: Racialization of behavioral problems in the U.S Schools

Schooling has been a site of colonization in the U.S., a nation built on settler colonialism, slavery, and land-based capitalism (Bal & Bird Bear, 2023). In the U.S., Indigenous (American Indian/Alaska Native) students  are punished more frequently and more severely for more  subjective reasons (e.g., insubordination) in scho ol discipline (detention, suspension, expulsion) and r eferrals to law enforcement (Bal et al., 2019; Leung-Gagnè et al., 2022; Office for Civil Rights, 2018; Skiba et al., 2014). Indigenous students are nine and 10 times more likely to receive in-school suspension and out-of-school suspension, respectively, than white student s (Whitford et al., 2019). This is significant, as exclusionary school discipline is a major contributor to detrimental ac ademic and behavioral outcomes, including the loss of instruct ional time (Losen & Martinez, 2020), lowered expect ations, and involvement in the juvenile justice system (Gregory et al., 2010). Indigenous students are also disproportionately pla ced in special education with the disability label of emotional/behavioral disturbance  (E/BD). Despite constituting about 1% of the total K-12 public school population, Indigenous students with disabilities accounted for 19% of stu dents with disabilities in special education (National Center for Education Statistics [NCES], 20 23). Furthermore, among all students with disabilities, Indigenous students with disabilities are more likely to drop out of school and spend less than 40% of their school day in regular classrooms compared to students with disabilities from other racial/ethnic groups (The Office of Special Education Programs [OSEP], 2021).

The overrepresentation of Indigenous students  in special education and disciplinary actions must be examined and addressed within the cultural-historical context of the U.S. education system and the broader social, historical, and spatial contexts of the United States as a settler colonial state (Brayboy, 2005; Lomawaima, 2000; Ko et al., 2022; Ko et al., 2023; Mawene et al., 20 24). Racial disproportionality extends beyond numer ical calculations of demographic categories, reflecting entrenched settler colonialism evident  in disciplinary and disability assessments (Artiles, 2003; Eitle, 2002;  Kramarczuk Voulgarides et al., 2021).  Throughout history, school disciplinary practices and disability identi fications have been settler colonial instruments of  ob ject formation for cultural erasure and forced assimilation of Ind igenous students into Eurocentric ways of being and doing (Brayboy & Lomawaima, 2018; Grande, 2015; Lees & Bang, 2023; Lomawaima & McCarty, 2006; Wolfe, 2006).

This paper offers a decolonizing lens to reimagine a culturally responsive school-wide behavioral support system that honors and nurtures Indigenous sovereignty. We conceptualize the decolonizing framework, followed by a case from Newhope High School (NHS)(1) in rural north Wisconsin, where we collaborated for five years with the NHS and the Anishinaabe (Ojibwe) community. Using a formative intervention methodology, Indigenous Learning Lab (ILL), this inclusive group reimagined a school-wide behavioral support system that honors Indigenous sovereignty, everyday resistance, cultures, and interests, tailored to their specific historical, social, and spatial contexts of injustice (Bal & Bird Bear, 2023;  Ko et al, 2023;  Mawene et al., 2024).  The overarching research question of this study is: How did members of ILL develop the decolonizing agency to confront the legacy of settler colonialism within the behavioral support system? In what follows, we explain the current landscape of research-practice partnerships, followed by our offering on decolonizing agency. Research-practice partnerships Addressing racial disproportionality in special education and school discipline requires a collaborati ve effort between all stakeholders in the school/district to co-design the solutions rather than relying on externa l experts. However, educational decision-making is traditionally the responsibility of educators, particularly administrators. Parents and students, especially those from histori cally marginalized backgrounds, tend to be at the p eriphery of decision-making. To challenge this imbalance, a growing subf ield of Research-Practice Partnerships (RPPs; Penuel et al., 2015) has gained attention for facilitating collaborations. However, RPPs often overlook the complex power imbalances among stakeholders and have not tr ansformed the hierarchical relationsh ip between research and practice, positioning the researchers as subjec ts who design, analyze, and disseminate the interve ntions. Specifically, RPP scholarship has not sufficiently engaged with systemic issues such as racism, coloni alism, ableism, or other  forms of discrimination and oppression that influen ce collaborative efforts (e.g., Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Penuel et al., 2015). To address thes e gaps, some scholars have developed research-practicecommunity partnerships (e.g., Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2018; de Royston et al., 2020; Ishimaru et al., 2022; Ishimaru & Bang, 2022; Lott et al., 2021) that explicitly fo cus on racialized educational inequities and elevat e the voices and agency of historically marginalized families and communities. For example, Ishimaru et al. (2022) proposed three strategies for addressing systemic racism in RPPs: (1) centering racial justice through both ver tical and horizontal systemic change, (2) building equity-focused data systems, and (3) drawing on the expertise of racially minoritized youth, families, and community members. Similarly, Lott et al. (2021) proposed a cross-racial agency. The framework of decolonizing agency we propose in this paper expands the current conversations and efforts in RPPs, particularly building from Edward’s conceptua lization of relational agency (Edwards, 2005; 2007). We expand Lott and Ishimaru’s criticism by arguing that for relational agency to take place, RPP research design and intervention need to consider the unique socio-spatial-historical contexts of discrimination and oppression within the partnership school in the design and intervention.

From relational agency to decolonizing agency The concept of relational agency developed by Anne Edwards (2005; 2007) finds its roots in CHAT (Engeström, 1987; Vygotsky, 1978). Relational agency is agentic  action with others through which one recognizes ot hers as resources for collaborative problem-solving and crafting new tools and practices (Edwar ds, 2007). Relational agency transcends mere collaborative efforts. Instea d, it signifies an innate capability to identify, a ctively seek, offer support, and effectively employ other people’s knowledge as legitimate resources to solve complex problems such as addressing racial d isproportionality. Relational agency explicated two  key actions when working with people from diverse backgrounds, roles, and motives. First, co-design actors must recognize the motives and resources other people bring to the table as they interpret the problem and solution (e.g., racial disproportionality). Second, relational agency occurs in a collective ac tivity system when a participant learns and aligns their interpretation of the problem with others’ interpretations of the problem and solution (Nuttall, 2017). In the context of the U.S education system, we foun d that the current concept of relational agency is limited in its applicability in the racialized educ ational context as it did not consider the roles of racism, settler colonialism, power imbalance, and the multiple, intersecting identities people (Indigenous and settler colonialism) bring when they collaborate. In its current form, relational agency is limited in  capturing power imbalance and intergenerational systemic discrimination within th e U.S. education system. To make the concept of relational agency applicable to the contexts of university-school-family-community collaboration in the racialized U.S. education system, mainly when working with the Indigenous community, we put CHAT in dialogue with decolonizing epistemologies (Brayboy, 2005; Bruynee l, 2007; Smith, 2021; Tuck & McKenzie, 2014). Decolonizing epistemologies highlight the cultural history of racializing behavioral problems in schoo ls and resituate the problem to center Indigenous epistemo logies and value systems. Cultural-historical perspectives consider the roles of place and context in research and coll aboration. The concept of place is expanded beyond a physical location where social life, power dynamics, struggles, resistance, hopes, and future imaginat ions occur. This perspective of place/land is central to Indige nous scholarship (Brayboy, 2005; Bruynell, 2007; Tu ck & McKenzie, 2015). We are attuned to the local social-temporal-spatial contexts, notably the continuous struggle over land and sovereignty for Indigenous communitie s, including the Anishinaabe nation, helping us fur ther realize our axiological commitment to decolonizing the settler colonial education system. Based on our five-yearlong formative intervention, we present six dimensions of decolonizing agency (Mawene et al., Under Review). 1: Acknowledging the historical legacies of educati onal debt and systemic settler-colonial and racial violence entrenched within everyday life and routine school practices and research, including disciplinary measures, spanning historical and contemporary cont exts (Ladson-Billings, 2006; Tuck & Yang, 2014). Local policy actors’ ability to recognize these legacies manifests as drawing upon historical narratives and interpretations of systemic oppression and racism w hen they collectively work to interpret the problem. Such a capacity informs those in bureaucratic roles and fu nctions, such as school administrators and teachers, in interpreting racial disproportionality within their localized socio-spatial-historical contexts. 2: Shifting epistemology regarding inequities, such as racial disproportionality  in special education and school discipline and recognizing these problems as systemic rather than individual. 3: Recognizing diverse and often competing interpre tations of place  and their implications for human interactions. These include racialized experiences that are visible in the present but have historical  roots in the past. This acknowledgment of intricate socio-spatial-historical contexts contributing to racially dispar ate educational opportunities and outcomes for historic ally marginalized students opens avenues for divers e policy actors to actively seek various perspectives, parti cularly those of historically marginalized students  and their families, and to address the issue collectively.

4: Acknowledging and confronting personal vulnerabi lities, including emotional, moral, and epistemological dissonance, white fragility, and bu reaucratic rationality, when discussing racism, set tler colonialism, and related topics. This involves tran scending vulnerabilities to promote courageous dial ogues and encourage others to do the same rather than avoiding di fficult conversations to maintain the emotional and bureaucratic comfort and “safety” of settler colonizers. 5: Recognizing historically marginalized students a nd families have endured and persevered amidst systemic oppression. They possess legitimate knowledge to co-create solutions to enduring inequity issues. This capacity includes leveraging historically marginali zed students' and community members' perspectives, knowledge, and empirical experiences when addressin g systemic i nequities. Placing these policy actors at the forefront of systemic changes acknowledges their ex pertise in navigating racialized experie nces within the educational system and facilitates the development of locally informed solutions. 6: Navigating power dynamics and hierarchy in colla borative, co-design processes. This dimension includes policy actors’ willingness to transgress the taken-for-granted power structures within boundary-crossing works (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011; Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2018). It also consists of understanding and utilizing diverse and often opposing perspectives of others beyond the logistics of time, physical space, or agenda.

## Context

Newhope High School (NHS) had struggled with the overrepresentation  of Indigenous students in behavioral outcomes (i.e., exclusionary school discipline and E/BD identification). When the collaboration with NHS began, Indigenous students accounted for about 20% of NHS’s student population. Still, they received 64.3% of in-school suspensions and 52.2% of out-of-school suspensions (Wisconsin Department of Public Instruction, 2019). Racial disproportionality at NHS had deep socio-spatial-historical roots in Newhope as a settler colonial town (Mawene et al., 2024). The city of Newhope was the epicenter of the anti-Indian movements, locally known as the “walleye war” in the 1980s, which stemmed from a centuries-old struggle over settler-colonialism and Indigenous sovereignty. For Indigenous students, NHS is a transition from their tribal district, where they attended elementary and middle schools, to the unified school districts. This transition was not just a move from one school to another but a shift from being part of a majority group to becoming a racialized minority. We partnered with NHS because both the school and the community of Newhope had a strong desire to solve the issue of overrepresentat ion of Indigenous students in behavioral issues.

Data generation and analysis We utilized ethnographic data collected during the design phase of ILL, which took place during the 2019-2020 academic year. ILL included 14 members representing multiple roles and demographic backgro unds (See Table 1 for participants). For this study, our primary data consists of transcripts from video recordings of the ten monthly meetings facilitated by the authors. Each meeting was 90-minutes long and took place at the NHS and the Trib al office. When the COVID-19 pandemic occurred, we transitioned to virtual meetings. ILL sessions were guided by eight steps of systemic change (See Figure 1). In the first step of the Lab, school leadership and the facilitators established an initial team, which was expanded unt il meeting #4. Once the team was formed, members en gaged in problem identification: questioning and analyzing the issue of racial disproportionality. This analysis involved examining quantitative school discipline, E/BD, and academic achievement data. Members also mapped out the existing system to deepen understanding of students ’ lived experiences and identify breakdowns in the current school disciplinary system. During this stage, ILL members held critical discussions on the cultural and historical roots of racial disproportionality. They reflected on questions like, "What happens during a referral? " and "Who benefits from the current system?" After reaching a  shared understanding, the group moved to problem-solving steps: modeling and examining. Here, they engaged in an iterative process of drafting an ideal behavioral support system and evaluated its feasibility. Finally, duri ng the planning for implementation, the team brainstormed strategies for disseminating the new system and inc reasing buy-in from the larger community, while also reflecting on the Lab process.

Data analysis was a collaborative process informed by data and ou r knowledge of decolonizing agency. We categorized the initial coding and created theme s that best captured the meaning of the data in the  light of decolonizing agency.  Some examples of the codes are: “negotiating power dynamics” and “trauma-informed system, and “epistemic tensions”. Any discrepancy in data coding was solved through extensive discussions until we reached an agreement.

## Results

The overarching research question in this paper is: How did ILL members develop relational agency? In the result section, we use the dimensions of decolonizing agency to present findings that highlight how decolonizing agency shaped the co-design of the school’s culturally responsive behavioral support system. Unearthing the historical roots of racial disproportionality The first dimension of the decolonizing agency emph asizes that a coalition to address racial dispropor tionality necessitates people as policy actors to acknowledge  and center the historical legacies of educational debt and systemic racial violence that per meate routine school practices as the foundation of  understanding the problem. During the ILL, we noticed that centering the historical legacy of settler-colonialism was important for members’ understanding of the problem and designing the new behavioral support system. For some Anishinaabe members, their interpretation of racial disproportionality i n school discipline and special education at NHS wa s shaped by their lived experiences within the socio-spatial-historical contexts of the settler colonial educati on system and their ongoing fight  for Indigenous sovereignty. For example, Diana Prince, an Ojibwe member, recognized the entrenched nature of racism in understanding the ov eridentification of Indigenous students in special education and school discipline. She stated during a discussi on about hopes and fears surrounding the effort of addressing racial disproportionality: My greatest fear and my greatest concern during the learning lab is the magnitude of the issues. Sometimes the ignorance that goes on is insurmountable. These surrounding communities, you might not think so, the racism.

Diana Prince contextualized the overrepresentation of Indigenous students in discip linary actions within the broader historical framework of settler-colonial practices in everyday practices. She allud ed to the current struggles over land and waters that continue to sha dow Indigenous stud ents' and families’ relationship within settler-colonial schools located on ceded lands. Similarly,  when members were modeling/designing the new behavioral support system, Mr. Carson, NHS’s social worker, also alluded to the walleye war that deeply impacted trusts and relationships between settler colonial schooling and the Anishinaabe people. We had teachers in our school who were very active on the boat landings, and Jeremiah speaks to that. He saw those teachers on the boat landing, and then he had to go to class with them. So, those are the perceptions that we have to get over as a district. Both Mr. Carson and Ms. Diana Prince situated the struggle of Indigenous students within the larger social, spatial, historical, and political contexts of settler colon ialism. Trust and a broken relationship between the  Anishinaabe community people and the school stemmed from the intergenerational settler-colonialist violence and Indigenous sovereignty.

From autonomous individuals to system: Surpassing bureaucratic vulnerability The second dimension of decolonizing agency demands  recognizing that racial disproportionality transce nds individual actions but is systemic. The fourth dime nsion calls for policy actors to surpass their pers onal and bureaucratic vulnerability to promote courageous di alogues. In the third ILL session, Mr. Bart, NHS’s Dean of Students, struggled with understanding why the perc entage of Indigenous students in school discipline over the years is much higher than their White peers despite his intentional effort to avoid biases in referral. Mr. Black, an Indigenous Ojibwe whose son was attending NHS when the ILL project took place, made visible the differential treatment in everyday practices of disciplinary exclusion: Mr. Black:  Well, you treat those white students differently th an you treat the Indians. You let them off a lot easier. You know, if you hav e a Native American [student] come into your office, two of these kids did the same thing. You're gonna suspend this one for one  day, or slapped their hands. And the Native, you're gonna suspend them for three days, a week, o r whatever. And that's what happens. They get on that slope and they can't  come back. But I truly believe that you equalize it as much as you can. And I think all these teachers here do the same thing, they're not here to discrim inate against the Natives. They're here to help.

Mr. Bart:  You said that the response to that is different bet ween different kids. It's like some kids are like: “Oh, great, thanks…, I’m glad t o be out of school”. Whereas, where some of our kids are  like, Oh, this is not good. I don't want this to happen again. And so that learning lesson h appens for some of our kids. But that learning lesson doesn't happen for s ome of our kids or it reinforces the negative behavior. Like well if I do this wrong, I get to go home for a couple of days. So, they come back to that process of like maybe this is a reward for behavior as opposed to its punishment for negative behavior. In this conversation, Mr. Black highlighted biases that are embedded in how disciplinary practices occ ur. Mr. Black criticized the punitive system as dysfunction al: instead of “teaching” students, students use th e punitive system to get away from the school.  However, Mr. Black's comments also revealed a partial dissonance in framing the issue. While he acknowledged the inequities in disciplinary actions such as differential treatment, he seemed to attribute these disparities to educators’ individual decisions rather than situating them within the broader context of structural racism inherent in the education system. In this conversation, we also noticed that Mr. Bart, the Dean of Students, did not avoid discomfort talking about one's everyday experience with a disciplinary referral. Instead, Mr. Bart exposed institutional vulnerability throug h the shortcomings of the exclusionary punitive dis ciplinary system. Mr. Bart engaged in courageous conversations with other ILL members, especially Indigenous members, to unpack the complexities of the racialization of discipline. Adding to the conversation, Ms. Jane, an English teacher, brought forward the accumulating educational debts (Ladson-Billings, 2006) in understanding the material long-term implications of exclusionary school discipline: Is there something else we can be doing so that it's not that? For some of the kids, a suspension does work, but I mean if he told us also that suspe nsion is not an effective way to deal with behavior. What can we do differently? and I’m looking from a teacher perspective because a lot of times kids that are suspended are also kids that have attendance issues, and when they're out of the classroom even more, they're even farther behind. They can’t see—even if there is a light at the end of the tunnel, in te rms of getting work done, it is really hard for the m, and it takes a monumental amount of effort to try to help them see  that light at the end of the tunnel. I could do this, get caught up, it's gonna be okay. You jus t need to work but it's hard for a lot of those kids. I understand that there needs to be consequences for harmful behavior but at the same time how do I educate those kids?

Ms. Jane recognized that exclusionary discipline li ke out-of-school suspension could be counterproductive for Indigenous students, particularly those who are already behind in terms of their academic trajectory. By removing students, particularly Indigenous students, from th e classroom, suspensions exacerbate the very challe nges they face. The dialogue between Mr. Bart, Mr. Black, and Ms. Jane exposed a gap in understanding the systemic roots of disproportionality and how entrenched structures  perpetuate inequitable outcomes. This conversation  became a germ cell for a shift toward restorative approach es emphasizing accountability, relationship-building, and ongoing academic support for marginalized students.

When ILL members aligned their common knowledge or interpret ation of racial disproportionality (Edwards, 2007), their gaze on the problem moved fr om individual students, teachers, and parents to th e entire school system. During the modeling phase, wherein m embers continued to design their culturally respons ive behavioral support system, Mr Bart built on the sug gestions from Ms. Gwenwyn, Ms. Lola, and Mr. Jeremi ah about assumed understanding of school cultures and suggested collectively identifying and teaching school-wide behavioral expectations from students and adults in NHS. I think for a lot of our tribal students, their exp ectations of what some of those cultural norm behaviors are way different than some of our non-tribal students because of the whole life experiences that they have. And so I think it's important that we be consistent as to some of the basic behavior expectations in our school and then in our classrooms. Mr. Bart built on the lived experience and expertise shared by her colleagues, Mr. Jeremiah and Ms. Lola (both Anishinabe members), to understand the contradictions in existing disciplinary practices. Mr. Bart was not afraid to unpack the shortcomings of the existing school system. Centering the knowledge of the Indigenous community The fifth dimension of decolonizing agency necessit ates policy actors to center the voice of Indigenou s students and families, and historically marginalized groups in general, in the design of the school system. We observed how ILL members built on each other’s knowledge and expertise in analyzing the issue of racial disproportionality and designing solutions. For example, Mr. Bart built on Mr. Black’s suggestion to center relationship building as one of the critical areas to address in the new system: Mr. Black:  Another big thing that we were talking about was… I  liked the kids finding someone to connect with. Bart:  Like a mentoring system. I like that. Mr. Black:  Or whether it's a teacher, counselor, you know when  they do or hear, or because you need a lot of them people connect with you. Bart:  Yeah, part of that, the way our homeroom system is set up, is supposed to fulfill that, but so many of our kids are like, I d on't I don't mess with my homeroom teacher.

Here, Mr. Bart, the school’s Dean of Students, and Mr. Black, an Indigenous parent, brainstormed some ideas to address the distrust and broken relationship betwee n Anishinaabe students and the school. They propose d a restorative, preventive model instead of a punitive reactionary model. Similarly, Ms. Diana Prince and Ms. Lola, both are Indigenous community members, alluded to a need for a structured mentoring system for Indigenous students to prevent behavioral and academic failures: Diana Prince:  There's one thing that stands out in these innovati ons and improvements for me:  The peer support group. Thinking back when I w as a freshman going into the NHS, I was a very shy person. I hardly ever talked or spoke to anyone. I'm going into these classrooms with a bunch of strangers and I really missed a lot of school due to what was going on at home. And this mentoring system for student to student, where you would have somebody in the classroom who would be a support system for another student who's not doing so well, I think that would be a great idea in each classroom. And i t helps students get to know each other better and it breaks down a lot of barriers. Lola:  So are you suggesting to have a buddy where somebody is starting to fail? Or would you suggest or maybe a suggestion could be to  have a buddy before that happens...both?

As Indigenous people, Ms. Diana and Ms. Lola knew t hat punitive systems do not always work for Indigen ous students. Ms. Diana Prince used her experience to p ropose a prevention system rather than reactive int ervention. For many Indigenous students in Newhope, NHS is the  first time they experience schools outside of the reservation. So many Indigenous students may come t o NHS without a supporting system. Establishing pee r relationships early on could create a foundation of  mutual trust and support, reducing the likelihood of students feeling isolated or unsupported.

ILL members developed a culturally responsive behav ioral support system rooted in prevention, communication, and relationship-building by drawing from the socio-spatial-historical and political contexts of settler colonialism and centering the everyday know ledge and expertise of the Indigenous community.  T his approach represented a shift away from punitive mea sures. Over time, members developed a new understanding of the complexities underlying behaviors. By the conclusion of the first year of the ILL, we noticed that members began to move away from oversimplifying behavioral issues into a nuanced interpretation of the perceiv ed behavioral problems and their solutions.

## Discussion

Decolonizing agency emerged first through recognizi ng the motives and resources that others contribute  as they interpret a shared object. In this study, the share d object was racial disproportionality and, subsequ ently, the behavioral support system. Interpreting this object required a shift in epistemology and an acknowledgment of the historical and ongoing layers of racism, settler colonialism, and other forms of oppression embedded in everyday educational practices. When people shared a common object and  motives, they could align their responses with expanded understandings of both the problems and po ssible solutions (Nuttall, 2017).  They expanded the conversation to examine the multiple marginalizatio ns within schools and communities' historical, political, and spatial contexts. Once the decolonizing agency was built, transformation aimed to dismantle the settler colon ial school system became possible.  The study’s findings highlighted the principles of fostering decolonizing agency within racialized and politicized educational setti ngs. In these contexts, decolonizing agency took sh ape when members shifted their focus from individual behaviors to systemic structures This paper contributes to the ongoing discourse and research endeavors in learning sciences, particularly in addressing systemic inequities in school systems such as behavioral support systems or academic programs and curricula. Our findings advance equity-focused RPPs and other design-based research by demonstrating the importance of historical consciousness, relational accountability, and power redistribution in school reform efforts. First, it proposes a model that emphasizes  process-driven approaches, led and owned by local policy actors, over outcome-driven strategies (e.g., mandating professional dev elopment developed by external experts or consultants) in tackling inequity issues such as  racial disproportionality. In addition, this paper  also enriches the body of literature on school-community-university partnerships with Indigenous communities  by addressing racism, colonialism, and other forms of discriminat ion and oppression through collaborative endeavors.  Finally, the framework of decolonizing agency highlights how  people collaborate to solve complex problems withi n the settler-colonial educational systems.
