# The Impact of Shared Feedback Visualization on Student Reflection During Simulation Debriefing

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examined how a cognitive group awareness tool (RADAR) influences medical students ’ reflective behaviors during online post-simulation debriefing. A between-subjects experiment with 117 medical students compared three conditions: traditional observational debriefing (control), completing a shared decision-making questionnaire before debriefing (light RADAR), and completing the questionnaire with visualized results (full RADAR). Both RADAR conditions increased reflection on decision-making and counseling behaviors. Epistemic network analysis identified three distinct reflection patterns: c ontrol students connected performance review with patient history gathering; light RADAR students linked searching for alternative approaches with communication techniques and counseling; full RADAR students connected performance review with patient partnerships and counseling information. Visualization of assessment discrepancies in full RADAR did not increase feed-forward reflection. This study contributes to understanding how cognitive group awareness tools can enhance reflective learning processes in online simulation-based learning.

Simulation-based training has become integral to medical education, providing immersive, risk-free scenarios that mirror real-world clinical challenges (Chernikova et al., 2020; Popov et., 2024). Post-simulation debriefing, typically guided by instructors, plays a pivotal  role in maximizing learning outcomes by translating simulated experiences into practical knowledge and skills (Kolbe et al., 2023). However, meta-analytical evidence shows inconsistent effects on learning gains due to substantial variation in debriefing quality and implementation challenges (Cheng et al., 2014; Keiser & Arthur, 2021; Sawyer et al., 2016). To address these limitations, there is growing interest in leveraging learning analytics dashboards (Harmer et al., 2026) and group awareness tools (GATs) (Popov et al., 2025). These tools aggregate and visualize performance indicators with the aim of facilitating learning processes and outcomes (Ollesch et al., 2021). GATs provide near real-time, quantifiable feedback on specific metrics while incorporating reference frames that offer learners comparative points (Jivet et al., 2017). The present study investigates how a cognitive GAT called RADAR affects medical students' reflective behaviors during online post-simulation debriefing. While GATs show promise in providing targeted feedback, little empirical research examines how learners cognitively process such feedback during debriefing or how it impacts reflection quality. This study addresses this knowledge gap by comparing traditional debriefing, light RADAR (questionnaire without visualization), and full RADAR (questionnaire with visualization and reflection prompts) conditions.

## Theoretical framework

According to Rudolph and colleagues (2007), learners assign meaning to external stimuli through internal cognitive frames (i.e., mental representations of perceived reality) that remain invisible yet are inferable from actions and results. The primary goal  of debriefing is to make these frames visible by externalizing the thinking behind actions, analyzing their impact, and refining and/or creating new frames. Learning occurs particularly when instructors and trainees explore the frames-actions-results causal sequence in reverse. Feedback during postsimulation debriefing is defined as the transmission of information regarding actions, events, processes, or behaviors relative to task completion or teamwork (Gabelica et al., 2014; Sawyer et al., 2016), and is  recognized as central to both motivation and learning due to its informational value (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). The cognitive processing of feedback typically involves three reflective stages (Gabelica et al., 2014): reviewing performance by evaluating present and past performance strategies; looking for alternatives by considering different approaches; and looking ahead by deciding what feedback to implement for future encounters. These stages are cyclical and intended to be repeated for continuous improvement. Despite metaanalytical evidence establishing debriefing’s essential role (Keiser & Arthur, 2021; Tannenba um & Cerasoli, 2013), challenges persist in achieving effective debriefing due to variation in quality, consistency, and limited evidence-based guidance (Mamede & Schmidt, 2023). GATs have emerged as a technological solution to enhance post-simulation debriefing by visualizing performance data and supporting structured reflection (Buder et al., 2021; Janssen et al., 2011). These tools often incorporate reference frames enabling temporal comparisons or 399 comparisons with others and expectations (Jivet et al., 2017). During debriefing, GATs may visualize students’ self-assessments alongside instructor assessments, creating two comparative points that provide insights into previously invisible learner frames and promote metacognitive processes (de Vreugd et al., 2023; Popov, Tan, & Manojlovich, 2024).

This between-subjects experimental study included 117 medical students (50.4% female, mean age 26 years) enrolled in obstetrics and gynecology clerkships at the University of Michigan Medical School. Students participated in 25-minute online contraception counseling simulations with standardized patients via Zoom, followed by 15-minute debriefing sessions in one of three conditions: traditional observational debriefing (control, n=36), completing a s hared decision-making questionnaire before debriefing without viewing scores (light RADAR, n=44), or completing the questionnaire with access to visualized results and guided reflection prompts (full RADAR, n=37). The RADAR tool (Figure 1) mapped four shared decision-making (SDM) sub-constructs (Kriston et al., 2010): establishing ongoing partnership, information exchange, deliberating options, and deciding and acting on decision. Both students and instructors completed the adapted SDM-Q9 questionnaire immediately following simulation (reliability: 0.81 for students, 0.79 for instructors). All debriefing sessions were recorded, transcribed, and segmented based on students’ turns of talk. Two independent coders analyzed reflective behaviors using a coding scheme based on Gabelica and colleagues’ framework (Table 1), identifying three reflective levels: reviewing performance, looking for alternatives, and looking ahead. Inter-rater reliability was strong (Cohen ’s kappa = 0.77). Analysis employed non-parametric tests (Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U) for frequency comparisons and Epistemic Network Analysis (Shaffer, 2018; Popov & Rochlen, 2025) to examine temporal co-occurrence patterns of codes.

Reviewing Performance was the most frequently observed reflective behavior across all conditions (75.5–85.1%), followed by Establishing Partnership (42.9–55.3%), with Looking Ahead occurring rarely (0.5–1.3%). Significant differences emerged across conditions. Students in full RADAR and control conditions reflected more frequently on establishing partnership (55% each) than light RADAR (42.9%, p<.001). Control students discussed patient history more frequently (35.2%) compared to full RADAR (20.7%) and light RADAR (15.8%, p<.001), while full RADAR students reflected most on counseling behaviors (36.3% versus 21.1% in control and 28.3% in light RADAR, p<.001). Both full and light RADAR conditions showed increased reflection on deciding and acting on decision compared to control (11.1% and 9.2% versus 5.5%, p=.004). Full RADAR students reviewed overall performance more frequently (85.1% versus 77.8% control and 75.4% light RADAR, p<.001), while light 400 RADAR students discussed alternatives most often (29.3% versus 17.7% in full RADAR and 16.8% in control, p<.001). No significant differences appeared for domain general communication, deliberating on options, or looking ahead across conditions.  Epistemic Network Analysis revealed distinct reflection patterns by condition (Figure 2a,b,c). Control students primarily connected performance review with patient history gathering and establishing partnership. Light RADAR students frequently linked searching for alternatives with communication techniques, patient rapport building, and counseling. Full RADAR students most often connected performance review with establishing patient partnerships and counseling information. Contrary to our expectations, visualization of assessment discrepancies did not increase feed-forward reflection. This study contributes to understanding how cognitive group awareness tools enhance reflective learning in online simulation-based education. The results demonstrate that both RADAR conditions shifted reflection toward counseling and deciding and acting on decision compared to traditional debriefing, indicating that structured selfassessment tools direct student attention to specific shared decision-making competencies that may otherwise be overlooked in unstructured debriefing. This attention-directing function represents a key affordance of GATs by structuring assessment around specific competencies, these tools provide cognitive scaffolding that breaks down complex clinical interactions into atomic components for feedback, practice, and remediation. While both RADAR conditions increased reflection on these decision-making domains, they maintained existing reflection areas such as establishing partnership and patient history. Light RADAR stimulated the highest frequency of higher-order reflection (looking for alternatives), suggesting that questionnaire-based prompts alone offer substantial educational value. The mechanism behind this pattern may relate to how students internalize assessment criteria through questionnaire completion. The cognitive act of self-evaluating performance against specific criteria appears to foster e xploration of alternative approaches. In contrast, the full RADAR condition heightened level 1 reflection (reviewing performance) but not higher stages (looking for alternatives and feed-forward reflection), possibly because the visualization of discrepancies between student and instructor assessments creates coordination costs that consume cognitive resources otherwise available for deeper reflection. This finding suggests that visualization complexity, while intended to enhance reflection through explicit discrepancy highlighting, may limit the reflective process when learners must coordinate interpretatio n of multiple information sources during livedebriefing discussion.  Our future will examine how individual differences in self-assessment accuracy and the direction of self-assessment bias (overestimation versus underestimation) influence reflection patterns, particularly concerning deliberation and feed-forward planning behaviors.
