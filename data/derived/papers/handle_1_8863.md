# The Effect of Different Sequences of Examples and Problems on Learning Experimental Design

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Example-based learning refers to a pedagogy in which learners are provided with a step-by-step solution to novel problems early in the learning process. Worked examples are most effective when used in tandem with open problems. However, there are still questions regarding the ordering of examples and problems. We compared the effect of sequencing examples and problems on learning and cognitive load. One hundred thirteen middle-school students were randomly assigned to one of three conditions that varied presentation order within each example-problem pair: example first, problem first, or simultaneous presentation. We evaluated students' performances on knowledge application and the transfer of learning on the topic of experimental design. We found no effect neither on learning nor on cognitive load. We compare these results to parallel studies in the literature and identify possible reasons for the null effect.

## Introduction

Worked-Examples (WE) is a teaching approach in which students are invited to study the problem formulation, its solution, and the steps leading to this solution prior to receiving open problems to solve (Renkl, 2005). Studies show that for novices, WE improve learning and reduce cognitive load (Gerven et al., 2002; Sweller et al., 2011; Van Gog et al., 2006). Optimal use of WE interleave s them with Problem-Solving (PS) in which learners apply the taught procedure to a new problem (Renkl, 2005; Sweller & Cooper, 1985). Alas, while the benefits of using WE and PS are well documented, results about the timing of WE in relation to PS are inconclusive. The question of sequencing WE and PS is an interesting one, as it helps clarify (and optimize) the rol e of examples in learning. Which element within each example-problem pair should be introduced first? One option is to provide learners with WE prior to PS. The rat ionale for this sequence is allowing learners to de vote their cognitive capacity to studying the worked-out solut ion procedure and construct a cognitive schema for solving similar problems (Sweller & Cooper, 1985). Another option is to introduce PS prior to WE. The rationale for this sequence is that providing challenging questions to learners prior to instruction was shown to better prepare them for future learning from subsequent instruction (Loibl et al., 2017; Sinha & Kapur, 2021). The third o ption is to present WE and PS simultaneously. The rationale for a parallel presentation is that when learners are challenged with a particular problem, giving an example in parallel could guide them in the solution process, thus could help them develop strategic knowledge. This approach is based on the analogical reasoning literature (Gick & Holyoak, 1980; Renkl et al., 2013).

The current study explores the effects of different  sequences of examples and problems on knowledge application, the transfer of learning, and cognitiv e load. We do so with middle-school students learni ng about research competencies and focus in particular on le arning how to design experiments. To date, most stu dies of WE have focused on topics such as physics and mathematics (cf., Renkl et al., 2013; Salden et al., 2010). To the best of our knowledge, the effectiveness of this approach to learning research competencies has yet to be studied.

## Theoretical framework

Worked Examples WE include the problem itself, the steps of its solution, and the final answer (Atkinson et al., 2000). Empirically, learners who receive WE followed by PS performed better than learners who receive problems only (Renkl et al., 2013; van Gog et al., 2011). Learning from WE allow learners to form problem-solving schemas that are stored in long-term memory, and these schemas can later be  retrieved when solving related or advanced problem s (Sweller, 2006). In addition, WE reduce students' cognitive load compared to PS (Sweller et al., 2011; Van Gog et al., 2011).

The WE effect is particularly strong for novice lea rners (Kalyuga et al., 2001). Example-based learnin g may become redundant for students with a higher level of prior knowledge as they no longer need the guidance ICLS2022 Proceedings 727 © ISLS provided by WE (Kalyuga et al., 2001). This effect is commonly referred to as the Expertise Reversal E ffect (Kalyuga et al., 2003; Salden et al., 2010).

Most research on WE has been done in well-structure d domains such as mathematics, physics, and programming (Glogger-Frey et al., 2017; Renkl 2013; Salden et al., 2010). Recent studies show the impact of the WE also in less structured domains such as writing, literacy, and music (Kyun et al., 2013; Nückles et al. 2020; Owen & Sweller, 2008). However, the effectiveness of this approach to learning research competencies has not yet been studied.

The combination of WE and PS In recent years, extensive research has documented the beneficial impact of the combination of WE and PS on learning (Renkl, 2005; Sweller & Cooper, 1985). How ever, there is still uncertainty regarding the timi ng of providing WE in relation to PS. Should the WE be pr ovided before, during, or after the PS? Results var y, and sometimes conflicting results were found by the same researchers. For example, Van Gog et al. (2011) found that WE before PS is most effective for learning, but se veral years later received different results showing no effect for order (Van Harsel et al., 2020). Similar null results were also reported by Chen et al. (2020). Reisslein et al.

(2006) reported a more nuanced finding, where low-p rior knowledge students benefited from example-problem

pairs, while high-prior knowledge benefited from pr oblem-example pairs. There are strong rationales fo r the benefits of each sequence: PS before WE (PSàWE) When starting with PS, learners search for ways to solve the problem without having sufficient knowledge. While learners may experience this order frustrating, studies show that grappling with novel problems supports learning from subsequent instruction (Chowrira et al., 2019;  Sinha & Kapur, 2021). This sequence of PS followed  by Instruction (such as WE) is commonly known as PS-I (Loibl et al., 2017; Sinha & Kapur, 2021). These ea rly solution attempts facilitate activation of prior knowledge and recognition of the deep features of the problem, thus preparing students for future learning (Loibl et al., 2017; Sinha & Kapur, 2021). PS-I instruction esp ecially improves students' ability to transfer the taught k nowledge, for example, when learners are asked to c orrect a given faulty model (Holmes et al., 2014). In addition to these cognitive benefits, several studies suggest that initial WE can increase learners’ motivation as well as the ir focus on the unsuccessful steps (Hausmann et al., 2008; Reisslein et al., 2006). However, to be effective, learners need to be able to diagnose their own atte mpts, which may not be the case with complex skills such as experimental design. WE before PS(WEàPS) Presenting WE followed by PS follows a teaching app roach known as Scaffolding (Tropper et al., 2015). Scaffolding refers to providing students with support during early stages of learning before removing support and shifting the responsibility to them (Jumaat, 2014).  In the context of WE and PS, providing learners wi th WE before they attempt the problem can equip them with resources to draw upon as they construct their answers. WE have been shown to reduce the demands of complex pr oblems while allowing students to construct their o wn knowledge schemas (Sweller et al., 2011).

There are several reasons for the advantages of a scaffolding approach for teaching experimental design. The initial use of WE gives learners the opportunit y to acquire the correct processes and knowledge, t hereby reducing the probability of mistakes and misconceptions (Nigam & Klahr, 2004). In addition, when learners lack the knowledge necessary to solve a problem, they may solve the problems through resource-intensive processes such as trial and error, which increases their cognitive load (Atkinson et al., 2000). However, this approach may be less effective, especially for students with hig h prior knowledge (Reisslein et al., 2006), for whi ch learning from examples can be ineffective or even impair the  learning process (Kyun et al., 2013). In addition,  and according to Cooper and Sweller (1985), learning from WE may not support skill transfer to other topics, a crucial feature in learning about experimental design.

Concurrent presentation of WE and PS Learning from WE and PS is essentially a process of  Analogical problem-solving (Gick & Holyoak, 1980),  as learners identify key elements in one domain using a source problem and transfer these to a parallel (yet seemingly very different) target problem. The analogical processes are fundamental to human cogni tion and learning (Gentner, 2010). However, mapping targets on source s is challenging even across short time periods (Gi ck &Holyoak, 1980; Cushen et al., 2018). To optimize this transfer, one may present the source and the target side by side (Ngu & Phan, 2021). This presentation can s upport the mapping process and reduce cognitive loa d. On the other hand, providing a single WE based on a si ngle solution may lead learners towards a single so lution ICLS2022 Proceedings 728 © ISLS process rather than considering alternative solutions. Furthermore, parallel presentation of source and target may encourage a rote process of read-and-apply, without in-depth encoding of the solution process that supports future independent performance (Pol, 2008).

The present study As presented above, despite substantial research on example-based learning, the sequences of PS and WE remain an open question. In addition to both types of sequential presentation, as far as we know, no studies to date have contrasted the traditional sequencing with a parallel presentation of PS and WE. The present study aims to investigate the effect of different sequences of examples and problems on novices learning research competencies, a topic that has yet to be studied in WE research. Specifically, we address the following two research questions: RQ1) What is the effect of the sequence of WE and PS on learning, as assessed using application and transfer items? RQ2) What is the effect of the sequence of WE and PS on students’ cognitive load during the learning process, as reported by the learners?

## Method

Design We evaluated the three approaches using a pre-treatment-post experiment. The topic of the study was experimental design, as it covered learning outcomes that are re quired by the curriculum presented by the Israeli m inistry of education for seventh-grade students. The curriculum emphasized aspects that students need to consider, such as fixed factors, sample size, control group, measuring a dependent variable, manipulating independent variable and repetitions in a specific context.

## Participants

214 seventh-grade students from two Arabic-speaking schools in Israel began the study. All Arab schools in Israel are run in Arabic, and only Arab students attend them. The study randuring the last few weeks of the school year (with a post-test being administered during the las t week of classes) and thus suffered a high attriti on rate. Our inclusion criteria were that a student had attended  school during the study and completed at least hal f of the activity in the training phase to be included in th e analysis. This leaves us with 113 participants ov erall. There was no relationship between condition and attrition (WE first: 74 students began the study, 40 complet ed it; PS first: from 68 to 38; PS in parallel with WE: from 72 to 35). To maintain anonymity, students were assigned an arbitrary code on their participation form and the exam.

## Procedure

The experiment took three days, with a week between each two days. During Day 1, students were administered a pre-test and confirmed their participation. A week later, during Day 2, participants were randomly assigned to one of 3 conditions: Example-Problem pairs (n=40; W EàPS), Problem-Example pairs (n=38; PSàWE), or an example and a problem in parallel (n=35; WE || PS).

Each participant was given 2 sets of WE-PS pairs. The only difference between the three conditions was the order within each set. After finishing each set, a cognitive load rating scale was administered. Each task had a limited time to finish (see table 1). During the learning phase, there was no intervention by the researchers, and the tasks were solved individually by participants under the supervision of the teacher and the resear cher. The post-test was administered a week after the learning phase. ## Data Analysis

All items were analyzed using a multidimensional rubric that mapped onto the taught features. For each feature, students received a grade of 0 if they neglected to  mention it or 1 if they used it correctly. For exa mple, the following answer: "to have two people, one with diet, one without diet, to check their weight before and after the experiment ", received 0 for the feature "number of individuals ", and 1 for the features "measuring dependent variable" and “control group”. The pre-test and application questions included 5 features that were analyzed. The transfer items had 9 features combined. Scores were scaled to 100% on each scale for convenience. 20% of the items were analyzed by the first author and another researcher from the research team. For each of the  features, the interrater agreement was greater than 87%, showing a high degree of convergence. Each student was assigned four values: (i) pre-test score, which was used as a covariate; (ii) post-test application score; (iii) post-test transfer score; and (iv) cognitive load rating, which was the average of the two cognitive load measures (during both sets).

## Discussion

The present study investigated the effect of differ ent sequences of examples and problems (i.e., WE àPS, PSàWE,  and WE || PS) on learning (as assessed using application and transfer items) and on cognitive lo ad among novices learning experimental design. Results showed no effect for condition on learning nor on cognitive load, and there was no significant interaction between condition and prior knowledge. The question, therefore, is: why was there no observed effect on learning or on cognitive load? One possible explanation is that the study materials or procedure did not support learning. Furthermore, the post-test was delivered a week following the study, and the short manipulation might not have been sufficient for the effect to carry forward. Alas, analysis fou nd a significant increase from pre-test to post-tes t, one that corresponds to a medium effect size, regardless of condition. This suggests that the learning material s helped students learn this set of skills without any additional intervention by the teacher or the research team. This also shows that the measurements used in this study were sensitive enough to identify this improvement. Thus, while assessment timing may play a role, we look for a fuller explanation. A second explanation may be that indeed there is no  effect of the sequence of WE and PS on learning each of the studied conditions supports learning by  triggering a different cognitive mechanism, thus h aving its own benefits: scaffolding students, offering opportunities for exploration, or supporting analogical transfer. It may ICLS2022 Proceedings 732 © ISLS be that for this scenario, all approaches are similarly effective. This result echoes earlier findings (Van Harsel et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2021). This is not to say that order never matters, as also suggested by previous results (e.g., Van Gog et al., 2011; Reisslein et al., 2006). Perh aps there are other factors that moderate the effec t of the sequence of WE and PS, such as motivation, domain, attitudes, etc. One of these factors may be type of knowledge. Studies that have examined the impact of the order of PS and WE mainly focused on procedural rather th an conceptual knowledge. In this study, we investigate d the skill of experimental design which is much mo re conceptual. A study of Chen and colleagues (2021) i nvestigated the effect of types of knowledge compar ing different order of WE and PS (WEàPS, PSàWE). Interestingly, also they found that there were no significant differences between WEàPS and PS àWE for learning simple and complex conceptual knowl edge. Thus, the interaction of various factors on example-based learning needs to be further studied. A third explanation has to do with factors specific to this study. One of these is timing. The study ran in June (the last month of the semester), and many stu dents chose not to engage with the study materials.  While students who completed the study devoted enough att ention to learning the basics, they might not have truly challenged themselves. One indication that this may be the case is the fact that students were far from mastering the skills even on the post-test. This also explain s the lack of effect on the measures of Cognitive L oad. Selfreported cognitive load was similar across conditio ns not because instruction had no effect but rather  because students regulated (and limited) their effort. Future studies should explore the role that factors such as motivation, interest, and attention play in example-based learn ing. While certainly not ideal, this aspect highlig hts the ecological challenges of everyday teaching.

The study has several limitations. One is timing, with post-test being delivered during the final week of classes. Engaging in learning activities during thi s period is uncommon, which probably affected stude nts’ motivation. Another limitation is that this was a single-topic study, and the topic was a challenging one.

## Conclusion

A study of different sequences of examples and problems found no effect for order. In this, it adds to the divergent results that similar studies have found over the past few years. Taken together, these results suggest that additional factors that mediate learning from examples and pro blems should be investigated and studied. It may be  that topical factors (such as type of knowledge and comp lexity), contextual factors (such as timing), and c ultural factors (such as school norms and culture, as this was the first WE study in Arabic) affect example-based learning in ways that remain to be explained.
