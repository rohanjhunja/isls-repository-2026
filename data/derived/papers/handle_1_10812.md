# Pedagogical Dilemmas and Decision Making within a Decolonial Writing Space: A Teacher's Perspective from India

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, we as teachers and researchers present our analyses of pedagogically productive talk within a decolonial writing space. As mentors, we volunteered for a not-for-profit organization and worked with students on their writing, who come from an indigenous community in India. We analyze our bi-monthly mentor meetings, where we worked through course design, our challenges, and strategies. Our reflections were mediated by classroom videos that we watched together in these meetings. As part of our findings, we present two pedagogical deliberations that created the most tension from a decolonial lens. These deliberations included decisions around using the frame of counter stories, the scope of our intervention as mentors viz a viz linguistic norms and navigating the layers of distance between us and our students. Our findings have implications for training mentors and educators who are interested in designing decolonial learning environments.

Decolonizing language arts pedagogy Even though some people would like to believe that the colonial effects of English are over, its imper ialistic tendencies live on(Phillipson, 2009).The way English language classes in India continue to discourage use of local languages (Anderson & Lightfoot, 2021) and educational institutions fail to engage with th e knowledge systems of indigenous communities (Sundar, 2012)  are seen as clear illustrations of the coloniality of our education systems. Given these historical, structural, and ev eryday moments in which coloniality of thought and being is perpetuated through language, there has been a growing movement towards decolonizing language arts (Cushman, 2016). One way in which decoloniality is embraced is to re cognize the geopolitical and biographical coordinat es of sensemaking (Mignolo, 2000). Educators have called to decolonize the ESL curricu lum by adopting plurilingual pedagogies and indigenizing English fo r epistemic access (Zhou et al., 2020). Given that designing decolonial learning environments are riddled with c omplexities, because teachers are also implicated w ithin colonial logics (Menon et al., 2021), this paper argues that pedago gical dilemmas and decision making within such spaces need to be examined. In the next sectio n we look at how dilemmas and decision making have been understood in existing literature.

Teacher’s decision making and dilemmas Teaching is a complex practice that calls for a nuanced skill set, including sensitivit y to identify critical events, ability to interpret and understand them and respon d flexibly using a diverse repertoire of strategies  (Lefstein & Snell, 2013a). Some of the challenges faced by teachers within Language Arts entail decisions around the use of dominant language  and creating a balanced environment that values diverse language s (Canagarajah, 2015). Balancing these choices at that very moment of inst ruction in teaching calls for making decisions whic h should ensure that students can participate actively, able  to comprehend the content and develop proficiency in the language of instructions. What complicates decision making within decolonial learning environments is that we are trying tonegotiate from within the colonial he gemony, and thus decisions often take up the qualit y of dilemmas, sometimes ethical ones. While the literature on ethical dilemmas encountered by teachers who are after decolonial learning environments is limited, we have some va luable insights into the multifaceted nature of  predicaments from multilingual classes and student teaching. Studies shed light on the concept of 'role strain,' indicating the various role dimensions student teachers must fulfill during their practicum (Allen & Peach, 2007). Juggling roles and responsibilities as colleagues, teachers to a diverse student body and facilitators  of learning underscores the intricate ethical landscape these e ducators navigate within multilingual classrooms. M oreover, ethical challenges arise from the tension between t heoretical pedagogical recommendations and practica l experiences in diverse classroom settings. Addressing  ethical dilemmas and decision making necessitates navigating complexities that are related to the soc iopolitical context withi n which decoloniality is enacted. We describe some of the layers of the socio-political complexity of English in the next section.

## Sociopolitical context of the project

Three layers of sociopolitical complexity of this debate are particularly relevant to our study. The first and perhaps the most dominant layer is that of English being pr omoted as the language of opportunity and social mo bility. A second related layer to this context is how English has become a fanatic obsession which perpetuates the processes of ‘othering’ (Kumaravadivelu, 2003). A third complicated layer to this context is caste. Some see caste practices as a greater internal oppression that predates Brit ish colonization. The proximity of the upper caste with the British, and the English language, helped them furt her their advantage in Independent India. Due to th is complicated history, some South Asian critiques of coloniality are called out as a way of hiding privilege behind an obfuscating theory (Chakravarty, 2019). It is in this layered backdrop, that this study was designed as part of a not-for-profit organization in India.  For this project six of us volunteered and were con nected with 12 Santhali youth from Chakai. The project started in January 2022 and ended in July 2022. There were two intertwined goals of this project, learning to write and, in the proc ess of writing, engaging with English and other lan guages. We say ‘with’, because by design we wanted students to use their full linguistic repertoire. The classes were conducted through Zoom, two to three times a week. We met twi ce every month to brainstorm and plan the pedagogic al shifts required for the project. Our intention was that both these goals would serve each other, which  means we would be able to respect student’s aspirations towa rds English and engage with the funds of knowledge students bring with them. The possibility of student publica tion was included to make the project more authenti c, challenging, and desirable for all of us. Inspired by our framework of decoloniality, in this study we  explore two related research questions. Out of the 12 students who initially enrolled, 5 of them published their w riting on different online platforms, three of them did write but did not publish and four students dropped out. None of the teachers knew student’s mother tongue, Santhali and  were not familiar with their cultural background. In this study we explore two interrelated research question s-What kinds of pedagogical dilemmas and decision mak ing emerged for the mentors designing a decolonial English language space and how were they resolved? Data The decolonial methodological orientation of this p aper is based on a humanizing aspiration; it is a c ollaborative sense-making experience that is creative and edifying  (Boveda & Bhattacharya, 2019). We attempt to use a transformative methodology which will have implicat ions for our own transformation and for our decolon ial praxis in the course. To reflect on the project, we  had two kinds of data-interviews and Zoom recordings of the mentor's meeting. For this paper, we analyzed the mentor’s meeting to  get a sense of the issues presented in its dynamic element. Each meeting was roughly two hours long and we looked at 16 of them in total. These meetings had a typical structure of video based professional  learning communities, where each of us would bring  small clips of our  classrooms to mediate our discussions. We started by open coding and quickly noticed that given the aim of these meetings, much of the rich discussions  were organized around challenges and strategies  (Charmaz, 2014)After the first phase of open coding, we wrote some analytic memos to get a sense of emerging themes and ideas (Emerson et al., 2011). At this stage some of the prominent themes that eme rged were-ethical dimensions of mentoring, navigating distance and familiarity b etween students and mentors, shifting pedagogical understandings, and negotiating student’s expectations. From the data, we finally developed 8 broad categories of pedagogical decision making that were particularly complex from the point of view of decoloniality. Su ch decision making centered around our implicit and explicit assumptions about coloniality and decoloniality. In that sense the decolonial moves we aspired to make were not a given, but rather something we continually discovered and negotiated amongst ourselves.

## Findings

Our analyses of the teacher's meeting were moments of learning for the teachers, which means that the understanding around these issues evolved through pedagogically productive talk (Lefstein & Snell, 2013).Some of the main categories of decision making that generated the most discussion were: what kinds of learning material to be used, how writing is to be introduced, what kinds of language norms obstruct or support students expression, decisions around explicitly engaging with counter s tories, how to navigating different kinds of distan ce between us and our students, our perceptions about student’ s engagement and what we valued in class, the scope  of our own interventions as mentors, and decision’s around  student’s translanguaging. In our preparation meetings before we started taking sessions with students, we explicitly used counter stories as a g uiding framework for our own understanding of decol oniality. We wanted to share this concept with the students, to orient them towards the larger issues around wri ting and expression, especially from an indigenous context. After the concept was introduced, Pramiti in the ve ry next class showed the students a film about eating field  rats. Pramiti mentioned how students were ‘confused by the concept’, in the sense they were not able to recognize the film as an example of a counter story. In response to her, Gautam felt that the counter story is “ a researcher's perspective or let’s say a conceptua l perspective on someone's work.”  Taking the discussion forward Aparajita felt that “ the name counter story can act as an emotional barrier” between her and the students. Here, Aparajita seeme d more concerned about the foreignness of the term to the context of the students. A few t urns later, Aparajita adds that we should not negle ct the desires of students, because one  of her students was more interested to learn about LinkedIn than to discuss counter stories. Was this insistence on counter stories reinforcing a one-dimensional perspective about them? Even though it emerged from the margins in the global north, its recontextualization in our project in India prompted Aparajita to ask, isn’t counter story a mainstream construct?

As concepts migrate, they are repositioned in the local matrix of power, where our use of counter stories perhaps gave it a colonizing force. Given that all the mentors were and had been part o f university spaces that operate within coloniality, where ideas around crit icality are often too distant from those it claims to stand with, our positionalities and ways of thinking were too c lose to slipping into what Freire had called vangua rdism. A few turns later, Ankita added, “I am from the outside and I might have come across those stories, but it would be imposing to say what other people think about them.” There is some difficulty in introducing dominant narratives or what Ankita calls “what others think about them (indigenous culture).” It gets into the difficulty of  being “from the outside” and talking about domination without creating the necessary conditions of trust. Counter stories were discussed in a few meetings before it was decided t o not use the concept explicitly, for everyone agre ed that the term itself did not add much value. Going ahead, two pro minent ways to resolve this dilemma emerged. Ankita shared, “I think I got the right words or the concepts from  them because I wasn't able to come up with the Hin di word for perspective and they said “Nazaria” This move retained the basic affordance of the concept but localized it by rephrasing it in the words of the students. Another resolution was to simply support the students “in writing about their experiences in a more authentic manner. ” This move assumed that the best way to reach stor ies that students really want to tell is to not let theories of negation mediate the process. Navigating the distance between mentor and students In a decolonial framework within a multilingual writing space, a teacher's positionality serves as a critical axis for shaping the dynamics between teachers and students.  Within our meetings, we felt different kinds of di stance between ourselves and our students. Some pedagogical decisions elevated the cultural, caste, class and academic distance between us, and some others helped us brid ge and navigate it.  We frequently attributed this distance to their perceived unfamiliarity with the students' li ved exp eriences, revealing the multifaceted nature of the distances prevailing in diverse settings. The disco nnect manifested in various ways: uncertainty surro unding the interpretation of articles, differences in expectat ions between teachers and students, and t he struggle to connect deeply personal stories to broader societal discourse. For example, Sahil in of the teacher's meeting, reflected, “I was looking at food habits from my own academic interests... And this is what I asked him, also…. But then what he came up with is a very cultural piece. It's not at a ll what I had imagined in my head. ” At the forefront of this conversation is the academic distance, illustrated through the teacher's articulation of expectations. The pursuit of creating substantial academic outputs, such as articles, is deeply ingrained in this paradigm. Taking the conversation further, Ankita pointed out that in moments like this she would be happy if  her students could come up with any topic “And somehow, I'm trying to compromise it with the fact that I would really be happy if they just come up with an articl e.” Here, Ankita's reflections reveal a strategic attem pt being made to mitigate the distance arising from the cont rast between her expectations and the student's wor k. Her conscious "compromise" signifies a deliberate effor t to reach a middle groun d between the academic ideals she holds, and the practical challenges faced by her students. In response to our deliberations around different k inds of distance, we want to highlight two pedagogical decisions that bridged this distance. To understand what writing in a new language, translating and translanguaging feels like, Gautam shared, “What I have been doing for the last  two weeks is that I'm writing something in Santhali in the class itself…And first, I have written my article in English…But then I work around with whatever Santha li I knew... And then the students help me a little, like the students help me a little to add more words and tha t's how it creates a common experience of translati on.” By reversing their roles in taking interest in Santhali, this move assumed that teachers would understand the labor of students more closely and more importantly it would  become one way in which cultural distance is reduc ed. Another mentor in the meeting  shared, “So I showed her like my creation when I was a se ven-year-old. This is what I used to write. And so showed her some of the  recent ones… she was so excited about it.”  By deciding to share personal experiences and mentor’s displaying their own trajectory of growth, this mov e assumed that students would feel encouraged in terms of their writing.

## Implications

How candecoloniality, which sounds like a negation  of coloniality, be enacted without the pitfalls of  negation and emerge from a place of desire? As decolonial educators we continue to wrestle with such questions and work through the pedagogical dilemmas that continuously emerge in our work. In this paper  we shared some of the most complex decisions we made within a decolonial language art space and these findings contribute to  the existing literature on the complexity of decolonial  work for teachers. The collective decision making,  and the deliberations had a bearing on our learning as ment ors. Since our perspectives on decoloniality, what teaching and learning looks like, established norms and our positionality, kept shifting through our collective sensemaking, our work also adds to the literature on professiona l learning communities. What we learned from this experience has three broad implications for educators, designe rs and researchers who are interested in designing decolonial learning environments.  Although we descriptively present some of the dilemm as, subsequent research can generate analytical categories and vocabulary aroun d teacher’s sensemaking vis a vis challenges within decoloniality as it relates to particular contexts.  Our projects highlight that mentor meetings emerged as a space for self-reflexivity, navigating complex decisions and co-learning. Such a space also forged an internal community for us, and even though most of us have never met a nd volunteered for a project, we still continue to think and work together. Our study can provide insight for tr aining programs that could design specific modules and activities around some of these  pedagogical dilemmas. Another design related implication is to extend the space that mentors share with students. Looking back, we could have shared some of our complexities with the students themselves and engender more transparency and student’s agency in designing the curriculum.
