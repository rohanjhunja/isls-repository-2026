# Why Making Still Matters

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
School-making is not simply a replication of real-world making; it undergoes recontextualization, shifting tools, practices, and meanings to fit institutional priorities. This paper centers on objects as mediators, shaping student engagement with materials, epistemic practices, and disciplinary knowledge. School-making is not a deficit but a generative space for hybrid-making that sustains exploration, creativity, embodied learning, and relational engagement while embracing institutional constraints and possibilities.

## Introduction

Makers make things, while students learn to make things, which is not the same. To be taught, making must be recontextualized into “school-making.” The integration of making in schools has gained prominence for fostering creativity, problem-solving, and technical skills by allowing students to engage in hands-on, project-based learning that connects abstract concepts to tangibl e outcomes (Blikstein, 2018, 2020; Kafai & Peppler, 2011). Maker education enables students to build shareable  artifacts that embody powerful ideas and foster epistemological pluralism (Turkle & Papert, 1991). However, making in schools often differs significan tly from its original contexts in workshops, industry, or studios. Making in the “real world” is not a singular practice; it emerges in diverse ways: as a pragmatic response to  necessity, such as carpenters building homes (Pye,  1968; Schon, 1983), an exploratory engagement with materials where creativity allows form to unfold or ganically (Ingold, 2013), or an embodied, sensory practice wh ere body and material are in dialogue (Malafouris, 2013). Making is collaborative and relational, fostering shared meaning (Lave & Wenger, 1991) while carrying cultural significance, as seen in ritualistic practices (Tur ner, 1967). It transmits knowledge and values (Maus s, 1934), connects makers to beauty, permanence, and purpose (Arendt, 1958; Ruskin, 1907), shapes identity, merges craft with technological innovation (Buechley, 2013), and invites ethical reflection (Mezirow, 1990). Making, in all its forms, is embedded in relational, cultural, and material contexts. Yet when it enters schools, it is inevitably altered and recontextualized to align  with institutional structures and constraints. This  transformation raises critical questions about what is gained and what is lost. Do es recontextualization retain the essence of making  or reduce it to a sanitized, scripted, incomplete version? As  making is adapted to educational priorities, “school-making” defines what making looks like to students, yet str uggles to capture its full complexity; it shifts fr om deep engagement to structured, assessment-driven tasks (Bevan, 2017). We present a framework for understanding how making transforms through recontextualization, centering objects as epistemic mediators, and argue, drawing on anthropology and learning sciences, that it should be understood as generative. Objects at the center A potter’s wheel in a studio, a multimeter in a workshop, or a 3D printer in a classroom does not merely facilitate making; it structures engagement, embodies knowledge, and carries cultural significance. Objects are not neutral; they shape and are shaped by practices, relationships, and institutions (Coupaye, 2013). As Kopytoff (1986) notes, objects are never static; their significance evolves as they move across contexts. Tools and materials acquire new biographies aligned with curricular goals. A weaving loom may no longer transmit heritage but serves as an entry point for teaching algorithms and pattern recogniti on. This shift reflects how schools reframe objects  to fit their environments. Objects are entangled with  materials, which further shape learning. Material transformations are epistemological: a laser cutter replacing hand-carving, or a loom adapted for coding, alters how making happens and how learners relate to materials  and processes. As objects enter school-making, their function is shaped by institutional goals (Appadurai, 1986). Schools resh ape making to serve certain goals, from innovation and assessment to efficiency and skill-building. A 3D printer in a classroom is more than a fabrication tool; it embodies creativity, literacies, and problem-solving, often aligned with future and career readiness. Seeing objects as “good to think with” (Lévi -Strauss, 1962) reveals their epistemic roles. This interplay between objects and contexts challenges that dichotomy. School objects undergo a daptation (Coupaye, 2013); balancing preservation a nd change is not unique to schools but is also seen in  museums. A soldering iron that signifies precision  in professional contexts may become a beginner’s  tool for circuitry, raising questions about whether  this limits or expands its role. To understand objects in making, we must trace their evolving biographies, how they change, and what knowledge is gained or lost. Looking at cr aft-based education (e.g., Nordic Slöjd), which emphasi zes materiality, helps us ask what such traditions preserve that school-making struggles to maintain, and how we might sustain relational depth and embodied knowledge.

From making to school-making In contrast to a maker community, studio, or professional practice, the classroom presents a very different context; therefore, making undergoes institutional translati on when it enters schools (Bernstein, 1990; Sharma & Anderson, 2009). Recontextualization shapes practic es, tools, and meanings. Schools, like museums, decontextualize artifacts and knowledge, repurposin g them for institutional goals (Clifford, 1988; App adurai, 1986). Transitioning from making to school-making presupposes a reframing process (Clifford, 1988), which often alters its relational, cultural, or material depth. Similar to math and science education, makin g is adapted, raising questions about what is lost or gained in t he transition (Sharma & Anderson, 2009; Bernstein, 1990). However, authenticity is not a fixed state but a relationa l construct (Handler, 1986; Bendix, 1997). Thus, ra ther than being “lost” in translation, we can understand  it as continuously negotiated. In this sense, scho ol making does not necessarily dilute an original form of making but generates its own, unique, authentic practices. Recontextualization alters not only the act of maki ng but also the objects and materials that define i t. School-making reflects a layering of new purposes, from technical to economic, as when a 3D printer shifts from a professional tool for precision to an  educational tool for scaffolding learning. Recontex tualization isn’t linear but a spiral, where tools, practices, and meanings shift as they move across domains. From traditional workshops to classrooms, making retains some elements while acquiring new roles to align with institutional priorities. Once embodying communal heritage and utility, in schools weaving may become a simplified exercise in computational pattern recognition, stripping away its cultural depth. The  spiral framework highlights what is lost and how making is actively reshaped. As making moves across  contexts, the shift is neither purely positive nor  negative; it depends on how one engages with an object’s mult iple dimensions. On one hand, recontextualization c an broaden access. Laser cutting, for example, serves as a gateway to computer-aided design, material properties, and prototyping. On the other hand, simplification flattens meaning. The abundance  of “keychains” (Blikstein,

2013) reflects how technical skills are introduced but not deeply explored.

Rethinking school-making The goal is not to preserve making as it exists out side schools, but to ask which forms of making are valuable in educational settings and how recontextualized makin g can foster meaningful knowledge construction. Ins tead of viewing school-making as a “deficit,” we should see it as generative. Schools cannot replicate traditional practices, but they can transform them intonew hybrid experiences. Bringing making into schools requires reimagining; since original practices cannot be faithfully replicated. What students encounter is always “in absentia” (Coupaye, 2013), a mediated version shaped by curricular goal s, time constraints, and institutional priorities. Perhaps replicating professional or traditional contexts in K-12 classrooms is not even desirable. Instead, we should view recontextualization as an opportunity to realign ma king with the broader ethos of maker education. If making involves exploration, creativity, and relational en gagement, these principles should guide its integra tion into education. Schools need not mimic professional workshops or studios to engage meaningfully with materials and ideas. How can we design an educational model that embraces recontextualization while resisting flatte ning? What might those hybrid forms of making look like?  How can recontextualization become an active design principle rather than a passive constraint? Maker e ducation need not apologize for differing from real-world making. While the original contexts may remain abse nt, the values and aspirations at its core can thrive, recontextualized, not diminished.
