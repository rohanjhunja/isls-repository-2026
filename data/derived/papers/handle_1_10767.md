# Toward Onto-epistemic Heterogeneity through Culinary Making: Insights from a Learning Narrative

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Recent discourse within the learning sciences toward onto-epistemic heterogeneity challenges static, hegemonic notions of disciplinarity in learning and their resulting inequities for historically marginalized learners. We present an exploratory autoethnographic learning narrative and reflect on how three sensibilities of onto-epistemic heterogeneity—multiplicity, horizontality, and dialogicality—present in an account of cooking situated in a relational history between the co-authors. This paper operationalizes onto-epistemic heterogeneity as a framework within the understudied domain of culinary learning to surface the following insights on learning: 1) how analogy enables complex, culturally situated, cross-disciplinary maneuvers, and 2) the ways that relational histories sustain and are sustained by learning that centers on them. We emphasize the implications and potential of future work around such learning processes in the quotidian context of culinary learning towards proliferating ontological, epistemic, and axiological shifts in what constitutes disciplinarity and learning.

## Introduction and conceptual framework

Recent discourse in the learning sciences surrounding disciplinarity has called for a move towards onto-epistemic heterogeneity—toward approaches that acknowledge the relationship between the nature of being (ontology) and knowledge (epistemology) to interrogate colonial hi stories of disciplinarity and “enable expansive and  insurgent ways of learning, being, and acting in, with, and a cross disciplines” (Warren et al., 2020, p. 278). R ooted in decolonial and indigenous perspectives, such work c enters on relationality. As such, it reevaluates “p aradigms that position the disciplines themselves as settled and exempt from reproach or historicity”—rejecting notions of neutrality or objectivity in knowledge generation a s built on “the epistemic, ontological, and axiolog ical assumptions of Western normativity in service of empire building” (Warren et al., 2020, p. 278). No discipline or topical domain is untouched by hegemonic onto-epistemologies founded through histories of subjuga tion— implicit commitments to a Descartean mind-body split, for instance, or views of the category of the Human that rest on Western philosophical conceptions of “Man” as an idea detachable from environments, social cat egories, cultural contexts, and non-human relations (Wynter, 2003). Nonetheless, schola rs across disciplines continue to challenge the origins and s ustained harm enacted by ethnocentrisms by practici ng “epistemic disobedience” (Mignolo, 2009)—linking nondominant ways of knowing to knowledge pr oduction in the natural sciences and engineering (Rosebery et al., 2010; Tan, et al., 2019). Yet, onto-epistemic heterogeneity in disciplinarity —for example, the move in microbiology to understand human-bacteria relationships as necessary partnership rat her than through Pasteurian germ theory eradication—demonstrates how de-settling expands knowledge generation (Warren et al., 2020). Within Warren et al. (2020)’s conceptualization of onto-epistemic heterogeneity, there are three guiding se nsibilities, which we operationalize as conceptual framework: multiplicity, horizontality, and dialogicality. Via Warren et al., we define multiplicity as an attunement to the “heterogeneity of [discipli nary] knowledges and ways of knowing” —an attunement that reveals knowledge production as imb ricated with identity and positionality (2020, p. 2 80). Horizontality refers to the dynamic spatio-temporal and cultural axes in which learning occurs —considering “cultural repertoires of practice within activity s ystems as resources that are always available for d isciplinary learning” (Warren et al., 2020, p. 283). Finally,  dialogicality can be understood as the endeavor of reframing the “discursive, layered aspects of meaning-making as a critical site of refusal, delinking, and re-imagining the normative chains that structure disciplinary learni ng”—undermining neutral notions of language to formulate ways of relating to concepts and beings beyond legacies of conquest (Warren et al., 2020, p. 285). Drawing on constructionist approaches to learning (Papert, 1991), we highlight making in the culinary arts as a possible cross-disciplinary mechanism toward cultivating onto-epistemic openness—one that promotes “imagining radically different horizons of possibil ity for disciplinary knowing and learning as the ma king and sharing of worlds woven with the making and sharing of selves (Morrison, 1993; Smith, 2012, as cited in Warren et al., 2020).” The culinary context, a domain to w hich all learners arrive with prior histories, hold s immense potential for accessible, quotidian, (cross -)disciplinary (un)learning across space, time, and relationships (Chew, 2023; Chew et al., 2023). We reach toward multiplic ity in culinary learning by focusing on decisions l earners make when cooking. We refer to these decisions as ethnogastronomic (Nazarea-Sandoval, 1991) maneuvers — maneuvers indexical of cultural culinary knowledges  active within or across various ethnic groups. Ethnogastronomic maneuvers are rooted in different culturally informed rationales and outcomes, which provide opportunities to cultivate situated understandings of knowledge across multiple disciplines. Take for example, the application of Chinese velveting meat practices (using egg and a starch or baking soda, paired with a flash frying technique) to make the Peruvian national dish lomo saltado. Scaling ingredient ratios and heat to yiel d desired chemical processes (i.e. starch gelatinization or m anipulating pH levels), formatively assessing resul ts through sensory observation, and moving between different e thnogastronomic techniques, tools, and the histories of migration they belie, present opportunities for embodied, met acognitive learning across disciplines like the culinary arts, math, science, and history. The culinary context can thus be used to position individuals as “agentive thinkers, poised to not only participate in but to imagine, articulate, and reshape disciplinary activity” (Warren et al., 2020, p. 280).

Considering relationality in the project of onto-epistemic heterogeneity pushes us to consider two unique learning assets of the culinary context: analogical  movement (the relationship between culinary concep ts and practices) and relational histories (the relationships between people). As the term ethnogastronomy suggests, the various sociocultural practices surrounding food po sition the culinary space as conducive for the inte gration of various perspectives and techniques, and for what w e refer to as analogical movement between them. Cross-disciplinary scholars have pointed to the potential  that analogy holds in bridging disciplinary perspe ctives (Griffiths, 2016) toward cultivating civic-minded scientific dispositions (Leal, 2022). Analogical movement relies on structural correspondence: on understanding “abs tract properties of situations that are revealed wh en we consider how objects, people, places, or things rel ate to one another” (Krawczyk, 2017, p. 227). Consi dering relationality between culinary practices, ingredien ts, and approaches opens opportunities for critical, reflexive assessments of culinary practices expressive of per sonal histories, in relation to situated cross-disciplinary knowledge. Additionally, learners’ ethnogastronomic maneuvers “carry the trace of prior social relations” around food, and as such are rooted in the extended spatio-temporal axes of learners’ relational histories (Vossoughi et al., 2020, p. 187). Individuals often learn techniq ues, ingredients, or recipes from family or friends, and incorporate them in their cooking to yield live, re mixed, culturally heterogeneous  practices. Relational histories therefore serve as inseparable repertoires learners draw from when they make ethnogastronomic maneuvers. We explore the ways learners draw on analogical mov ement and relational histories through embodied ethnogastronomic maneuvers, as mechanisms toward on to-epistemic heterogeneity in learning. We present an autoethnographic, self-directed learning narrative i n the context of the co-authors’ relational history. The lead author makes the staple South Asiandish dal tadka.  She reflects on the process to glean practice-driven insights into the ways the culinary context functions as a s pace of learning—and, crucially, as a site in which hegemonic conceptualizations of what knowledge is and looks like break down, thus requiring a more capacious framework: onto-epistemic heterogeneity. We offer this work as a co ntribution to a growing, vibrant literature within the learning sciences that questions and deconstructs dom inant narratives of knowledge production to arrive at more agile analytical tools. By leveraging and dignifyin g nondominant learners’ rich cultural and relationa l histories rather than effacing them, we aim to model onto-epistemic heterogeneity in learning as both a nimble intellectual practice and a generative social good. We ground this effort in the following exploratory research question: What can analyzing a self-directed culinary learning narrative through the framework of onto-epistemic heterogeneity teach us about the role of analogy and relational histories in cross-disciplinary learning?

## Methods

Just as onto-epistemic heterogeneity critically questions the so ciohistorical roots of learning and disciplinarity, autoethnography as a method uses personal storytelling as a window into broader cultural phenomena to challenge notions of data and methods that seek to present a false objectivity through the exclusion of the rese archers’ background as bias within cultural studies (Ellis e t al., 2011). We chose autoethnography as a means t o include and utilize researchers’ personal and cultural hist ories as funds of identity (Esteban-Guitart & Moll, 2014) in the endeavor of surfacing insights around sociocultural learning inherent in the culinary making process. We collected personal memory data —researchers’ “self-observational” and “self-reflective” (Chang, 2 016) writing and drawing—to allow deep access into not only often non-externalized thinking processes in-vitro, but also the complex and often invisible tapestry of a relational history. To draw insights around learning and disciplinarity outside its traditional contexts, we chose to focus on self-structured culinary practice that demonstrates the horizontality of learning across “the boundless var iety of places, cultural contexts, and pra ctices through which people learn” (Warren et al., 2020, p. 283). After Aakriti recounted the recipe for dal tadka, Phebe commenced on a self-directed making of the dish. Phebe jotted down the main components of the recipe, made the dish, w rote out a recip e as a post-making reflection, and then collected personal memory data around the entire process—recounting and then reflecting on the making process from Aakriti’s instructions, and well as the relational history  between the authors. We then engaged  in conversations and joint ethnographic drawing reflec tions (Causey, 2017) to reflect and corroborate the  learning narrative. We then deductively coded the narrative for multiplicity, horizontality, and dialogicality, and wrote reflective memos around the coded excerpts to understand the ways analogical movement and relational histories supported different components of onto-epistemic heterogeneity in the learning narrative. Learning narrative In this section, we switch to a first-person perspective to walk through Phebe’s autoethnographic learning narrative in making dal tadka, in the context of her relational history with Aakriti. Our relational history Throughout the duration of my friendship with Aakriti, food has been a consistent practice leading to the creation of artifacts to anchor our interactions. As two peo ple obsessed with the quotidian art of feeding ours elves, we quickly found a shared, endlessly renewable domain of interest. Our interests started with chai—a shared interest in hot tea started a chai circle where each friend seemed to take turns making their specifically-proportioned brew of spices. One lazy Sunday, she invited me to eat fluffy rounds of pani puri with a tofu sabzi, saying that “puri wasn’t a dish you ate alone.” I joined, bringing along dried chrysanthemums, jujubes, and goji berries to make a n herbal tea to usher in the afternoon in the style  of yumcha (the Cantonese brunch tradition involving dimsum di shes digested alongside copious amounts of tea and social conversation, and which literally translates to “dr ink tea,” which my Cantonese-speaking father one day exasperatedly told me upon repeated inquiry)—knowing from our chai afternoons together that hot tea was always welcome. Another day, I invited her to join me on a spontaneous Korean-inspired feast—featuring crispy daikon pancakes based on inspirations from Taiwanese radish cakes (which my Taiwanese godmother loved to make for me) but prepared with Korean crispy pancake techniq ues (first shown to me at a squid pancake party my best friend led with the other Korean students), and an assorted series of other pan-Asiandishes: sweet garlicky soy-braised potatoes, cucumber kimchi, and Thai-style fried egg-battered eggplant slices. Aakriti likened the daikon pancakes to the Indian pancake dish chila, just one of the many times our food sharing would prompt us to venture into cross-cultural resonances and motifs, amidst appreciation for what was distinct and new to us. Aakriti’s infectious enthusiasm for making and shar ing the South Asian food (particularly Indian food) she ate growing up in India —and now missed in the Western suburban sprawl she m oved to—meant I had been steadily learning about the vast and regionally div erse arena of Indian gastronomy through firsthand (and firstmouth) experience. She mentioned dal in passin g many times when we talked about dinner making pla ns, establishing it as a South Asian staple dish. She shared different types of dal with me on multiple occasions: each a foray into different legumes, viscosity, spices, and the regional or ethnic associations of their uses. As someone with Southeast and East Asian heritages comfortable  with techniques based in these and assorted Wester n cuisines—but who had with little to no familiarity around South Asian cuisine—I found that my relationship with Aakriti slowly transformed a domain once unknown to  me into a place of tentative, reverent exploration. In accompanying her to food spaces, I began to see bot h equivalencies between gastronomies I’ve grown up with and encountered in the past —as well as distinctly Indian techniques and feature s. Dish by dish, unfamiliar food terms melted into recognition, now marked by memori es and cultural mnemonics shared with Aakriti. It became apparent to me that restaurant and supermarket visi ts were sociocultural places of education —as were the home visits where we shared dishes we made, talked through their properties and how we accomplished certain results, and abstracted cultural and local-personal memories we associated with them. The making process Getting an approximate recipe It started as chatter to pass the time during a lab  task one morning. I had only eaten dal made by Aakriti, and never tried making it myself. Through many supermarket visits and Aakriti’s generous offers of the delicious foods she made, I found myself ready and curious to explore making the staple dish she spoke about always: Phebe:  “Aakriti, how do you make dal?” Aakriti:  A good one, like how my mother makes? Or one that I just make every day? Phebe:  A good one. I want to make a good one.

Aakriti:  Oh, okay. Well for a good one you cook the dal separately. First you boil the dal. These two are used together a lot, the red ones you got the last time at the store, and the yellow one.

Phebe:  You don’t need to soak the red ones?

Aakriti:  No, you don’t. They cook very fast. Maybe cook the yellow one first for ten minutes. And you also cook tomato, whole, my mother says so that it doesn’t get sour, with the dal, and mash it. I don’t even do this, but my mother does. Then you put ghee and your spices, and add in the dal, and any vegetables. You can use any vegetables you have.

Phebe:  Which spices do you use?

Aakriti:  That depends. Ginger, and sometimes I just use turmeric and cumin. Phebe:  What about the tadka?

Aakriti:  Yes, that is the tadka.

Phebe:  Ohhh, okay. That sounds doable! I’ll give it a shot. With the memory of Aakriti’s recounted recipe, I set to work making for the first time for myself what is a staple dish for billions of people. I treated it as a project of the evening—one to de-stress from the week, and a creative opportunity to make something new to me, with improvisations in line with the structure and assignment of dal. Getting organized I first jotted down on paper, in broad strokes, the  steps Aakriti had outlined for me. I did this for two reasons: first, to relieve myself of all the pressure of rec alling reasonable ingredient ratios and next steps while cooking, knowing my mind would be split between the cognitive load of making a new dish and balancing multiple components over an open flame. Secondly, I used it to familiarize my mind with the steps, tryi ng to get myself into the specific culinary logic of Indian cuisine undergirding the steps Aakriti explained to me, and reconciling it to the approaches and techniques I had in my arsenal, to hopefully lead to a smoother making experience. Cooking the dal After getting organized, I set to work to use my ti me efficiently. I had the split yellow peas soaking  for half an hour in advance as I jotted down the steps, thinkin g they’d need more time relative to their thinner, smaller, split red lentil counterparts which Aakriti mentioned didn’t need soaking, which would take less time to cook. I dug into my mental repository around how to cook dry legumes: remembering how my Thai-Chinese mother soaks in advance dried red or green beans fo r sweet soups, or dried black beans for braised bla ck bean pork hocks, to make for quicker and more even cook ing; remembering one time I recreated an Armenian l entil soup a good friend shared with me, realizing they r equired deceptively ample time and water to cook th rough. I figured to add the whole tomato at the same time I added the red lentils, as it sounded it needed to be intact enough toneed to mash, yet tender enough to mash with the  back of the spatula, based on Aakriti’s instructions. I threw the split yellow peas in a pot with plenty of salt and bay leaves, knowing they’d develop a pleasing m ellow, yet savory flavor through diffusion by salting at this step rather than later, and figuring this solution would be my main source of salt for the dish. I let the split yellow  peas cook for about 10 minutes before I threw in t he red lentils and the tomato. I h ad never thought to cook a large tomato whole in a dish with this sort of stewed consistency but thought of the Vietnamese uncles I had seen throw whole onions in their legendary phở  broths, undergirded by a prioritization of the ‘sweetness’ of the ingredient rather than the more abrasive fla vor notes— whether spicy or sour. I set the timer to 30 minutes and moved to gathering my spices for the tadka. Making the tadka The concept of tadka, or tempering, as it was trans lated, excited me. It was a new addition to my culi nary techniques: understanding that the function of temp ering was familiar —toasting spices, like toasting nuts and sesame seeds before using to bring out their flavor, or shocking aromatics with hot oil as a basis for  flavorful sauces in Chinese cuisine (such as the ginger scall ion sauce emblematic of the Hainanese chicken rice I grew up eating)—but that the concept of tempering was both distinct and prominent within Indian cuisine. Aakriti had told me to use ghee for the tadka. I di dn’t have ghee, but I had butter. Knowing that ghee was just its clarified equivalent (strained milk so lids), but knowing I enjoyed the caramelized taste of browned butter from my forays into baking —why not? I added the butter, being mindful of the heat, a ware that because the butter wasn’t clarified, its remaining milk solids could go from brown to burnt quickly. Now, which spices to incorporate? Based on our conv ersations, it seemed like dal varied in terms of thickness, spices, vegetables, across each househol d. In addition to the ginger, cumin, and turmeric Aakriti mentioned, I remembered our varied conversations and gatheri ngs around chai, sabzis, and dal in the past — remembering the ways many of the same spices were used in differing proportions, rotating main features by dish, regionality, and household. I added half a cinn amon stick, 2 bay leaves, masala, cloves, fenugreek  seeds and leaves, ground coriander seeds, dried chilies, garl ic, and onion. Drawing from certain spice memories —my mother’s love for strong, bergamot-adjacent cloves speckling a roasted ham around Than ksgiving celebrations, and a Nepalese friend’s skillful use of bitter fenugreek seeds in a refreshing achar—I knew the strong flavonoids in these spices would call for more restraint and was mindful to add less of them. I made sure to watch the spices and aromatics so th ey wouldn’t burn, before dumping in the cooked lentils. I chopped up whatever diced vegetables I had in the fridge, which coincidentally formed a French mirepoix base: carrot, onion, and celery. Only instead of a base cooked down until texturally unrecognizable, t hese vegetables in dal were to hold their own flavor and  structural integrity as main components of the dish. This was an intentional pivot here, to stay true to the culi nary logic (where local ingredients may diverge) of  Indian food as I was currently understanding it: to use the tad ka as the base, and to focus on the vegetables them selves—the way the word sabzi refers to vegetable dishes, and also means “vegetable” itself. Sharing and feedback I gave the dal a taste. It seemed good in broad str okes, though as this was my first dal, I had no real point of reference as to how it measured up to an Indian palate. I called Aakriti over to try it, and get her feedback—what spices were too prominent, and what was right and w rong with it? Did I use too much fenugreek seed ? Masala? Was it too runny, or too thick? Perhaps my consistency was different sin ce I didn’t have a pressure cooker, and couldn’t mark the done-ness of my dal from a number of whistles the way Aakriti would. Aakriti:  Phebe, this tastes just like how people would make it at home. The texture is perfect, I can’t get it this thick. And celery, I have never thought of putting celery in dal, but it is so good and crunchy.

Phebe:  Oh, I’m so glad. I had no idea what it tastes like because this is the first Indiandish I’ve ever made. I’m glad the taste was right, and the consistency worked out…you told me I can put whatever vegetables, so I put celery. When you buy celery there’s always so much of it, so it’s nice to get rid of. Aakriti:  With some rice and ghee it would be perfect. Dal is the best with ghee. Phebe:  I made some basmati rice. Would you like some? Let me pack you some and you can eat it tomorrow. I’m just so glad it turned out well. Thank you for teaching me how to make it, again.

Post-making reflections and relational extensions That same day while the experience was still fresh,  I wrote down the recipe now to remember what I had  done, and to be able to capture the techniques and approx imate amounts of ingredients that worked well toget her. Usually when I make something I would ma ke again, I like to do this as a reflective activit y that allows me to remember the making process, what went well, and think about what I might do differently next time so I can tap back into the different way of making which runs th rough each culturally specific cuisine. The techniques and logics of each cuisine are pivotal to making sure i t comes out right. This is a space to include the t echniques, flavor profiles, and proportions that led me to where I have come this time—and to help me better the next time. A few days later, Aakriti called me to give me ghee she had made, filling the container I had packed rice in for her, making good on her promise to show me what she had told me: that dal really is the best with ghee. At a later point, through moments of transit between v arious research and school-related commitments, we found ourselves talking about what we were making for dinner. Aakriti said she would probably have dal, but not before slapping my arm and adding with a smile, “Now I always make dal with celery.”

## Discussion

In this section, we return to a third-person analytical perspective to synthesize the pre ceding learning narrative and discuss two insights from the culinary learning  context that can be applied in the design of learn ing environments geared towards fosteri ng onto-epistemic heterogeneity. Specifically, we draw atte ntion to 1) analogy as a critical tool to foster movement across multiple ways of knowing cultivated across expanded spatiotemporal learning contexts towards critical meaning-making processes, and  2) how, when centered in learning, relational histories provide infrastructure that both sustains and is sustained by onto-epistemic heterogeneity. Analogical movement as a critical tool to connect and expand disciplinarity In this section, we discuss the ways analogical mov ement served as a critical tool through which Phebe was able to access situated cross-disciplinary knowledge (e.g. history, culinary arts, chemistry, etc.) in tandem with metacognitive thought and personal/cultural memory.  Specifically, we highlight a two-part process possible through leveraging analogical movement towards onto-epistemic heterogeneity in learning: 1) the ways an alogy can be leveraged to cultivate dispositions of learn ing inclusive of multiplicity and dialogicality, and 2) the ways such processes can lead learners to contextualized decision points in their learning allowing them to subvert histories of “onto-epistemic violence” (Mignolo, 2009). Analogical movement as a tool towards multiplicity To discuss analogical movement as a tool towards mu ltiplicity, we first establish the multidimensional  and concentric nature of multiplicity within the learning narrative—scientific ways of knowing and doing in culinary practice, cultivated in relation to personal and cultural memory. We understand Phebe’s learning process as movement across these domains, facilitated by, but not ending with, analogy. For example, Aakriti’s direction to cook the tomato whole to reduce its  sourness is analogized through Phebe’s own understanding through personal and cultural memories of “the Vietnamese uncles [she] had seen throw whole onions in their legendary noodle broths, undergirded by a prioritization of the ‘sweetness ’ of the ingredient rather than the more abrasive flavor notes—whether spicy or sour.” Here, we see how situated chemical understandings are informed by and bridged through analogical movement  across personal and cultural culinary experiences. Similarly, Phebe’s understanding of pre-soaking larger dried dal for quicker and more even cooking reflects situated understandings of diffusion/water absorption, analogized across experiences such as her mother’s sweet bean soups and braised black bean pork hocks,  as well as a friend’s Armenian lentil soup. In ano ther example, Phebe’s movement across multiple domains—scientific domains behind the ways heat applied to oils in spices and nuts fortify and change ingr edients’ aroma, and personal and cultural memories around Hainanese ginger scallion oil—demonstrate how moving through analogy was a critical mechanism for Phebe to understand the cultural culinary concept of a tadka in South Asian cuisine. Of importance, as demonstrated through these examples, is seeing analogy as a tool and not an endpoint in efforts towards multiplicity. Though analogical movement across these culinary terms and concepts a llowed for meaning making towards understanding a tadka fr om an outside perspective, Phebe uses prior and varied cultural knowledges from her personal history to lo calize the conceptualization of a tadka, towards th e endeavor of understanding a tadka as a concept on its own terms—within its own onto-epistemic context. Analogical movement as elucidating processes toward dialogicality We speak to the potential of leveraging analogy across multiple ways of knowing towards reconfiguring discursive meaning-making spaces “as a critical site of refusal, delin king, and re-imagining the normative chains that structure disciplinary learning” (W arren et al., 2020, p. 285). Phebe’s choices when incorporating vegetables to align her techniques with South Asian onto-epistemic concepts of a sabzi rather than a French mirepoix that is more familiar to her, and dominant in Eurocentric c ulinary narratives, presents how the mechanism of a nalogy can elucidate the histories of knowledges learners toggle between, thus enabling opportunities for cri tical reflections on dominant epistemologies. A mirepoix constitutes a combination of onion, carrot, and cel ery that serve as an aromatic vegetable base to layering fla vor in French cuisine. Phebe recognizes this combination of vegetables captured under the term mirepoix and uses this understanding to pivot instead to conceptualizations of sabzi—of making room for multiplicity in ways of conceptualizing vegetables in dishes, and making a conscious choice to center an approach towards culinary techn ique that run counter to the dominant, culturally h egemonic narratives of making that denote mainstream and the refore ‘co rrect’ conceptualizations. In this instance, analogical movement opened metacognitive space to consider multiple cultural concepts, lineage of techniques, and agency to embrace onto-epistemic heterogeneity through culinary endeavor. Furthermore, Phebe’s assessment of her own dish reflects a respect fo r subjectivity across cultural metrics which reflect different ways of knowing —acknowledging that what may be “good in broad strok es'' to her, with her repertoires across (South)east Asian and Western cuisines, is no indicator of “how it measured up from an Indian palate.” This speaks to the ways reverent learning through ethnogastronomic avenues and contexts can cultivate understandings of multiplicity toward s opportunities for critically assessing the assumptions of the metrics that dictate correctness across systems of knowledge to be as subjective as a culturally guided palate. Relational histories as dynamic infrastructures that sustain learning In this section, we focus on the reciprocal relationship between learning and relational histories within the learning narrative. We discuss three learning infrastructure s (Ludvigsen et al., 2010) that relational historie s provided in culinary learning which served to sustain learning,  towards the importance of centering relational his tories in learning endeavors predicated on onto-epistemic heterogeneity. Relational histories as reference, creative, and scaffolding infrastructures Aakriti and Phebe’s very infrastructures of learning are predicated on their cultural and relational histories, which leverage diverse ways of knowing (multiplicity) as resources across extended axes of learning through domains of life (horizontality). Phebe’s learning narrative demonstrates how learning i s reliant and predicated on cultural “funds of identity” as reference infrastructures across which complex cross-discipline analogical movement is possible (Esteban-Guitart & Moll, 2014). Throughout the le arning narrative, co-authors’ relational histories provide them with cultural practices and techniques which they engage and share with one another. When pressed for a “good” dal recipe, Aakriti’s knowledge stems from her relational history with her mother —distinguishing the recounted recipe from her everyday one-pot dal through steps like cooking a tomato whole t o result in a less sour taste, and cooking the dal in a separate pot, rather than in the same pot with the tadka. Phebe’s  relational histories—whether through historicized interactions with her mot her tied to culinary techniques and cultural dishes around dried legumes and cloves, memories of  a friend’s skilled achar featuring fenugreek seeds —are similarly accessed as historicized infrastructures of reference in her learning how to make dal. All in a ll, both authors’ knowledge of foods and the customs around them (puri as “[not] a dish you ate alone,” regional variances in main legumes used and properties, yumcha/chai as significant socio-gastronomic rituals, etc.) reflect movement of these practices across personal and cultural mem ory into the present, through spaces and interactio ns across oceans that coalesce to form new ways of knowing and relating (i.e., rotating chai circles, puri, yumcha brunches). The learning narrative also highlights how creative infrastructures—concrete project ideas in the form of dishes or recipes —result from centering relational histories, which a re threaded by learners’ interests. The project of making dal, itself, came up through thei r friendship and shared interests in food. Within t his project was “a creative opportunity” for “improvisations in  line with the structure and assignment of dal” —creating the situation and parameters within which Phebe’s rich learning was made possible. Furthermore, relational histories also served as scaffolding infrastructures in the learning of different culinary onto-epistemologies. Multiplicity, instantiated in the learning narrative as “the specific culinary logic of Indian cuisine,” are brokered to Phebe through Aakriti’s funds of identity—Phebe’s reflections indicate that her friendship with Aakriti “transformed a domain once unknown to [her] into a place of tentative, reverent exploration.” We see the ways this process yields new ways of making and doing as “people, ideas, and practices of different communities meet, collide, and merge” (Engeström, 2005, p. 46) —whether it is delving intonew ethnogastronomies, or incorporating local ingredien ts like celery which are not typical in Indiandish es, into existing staple dishes.

## Conclusion, implications, and future work

The necessary task of making food to nourish the bo dy provides opportunities for learning in relation to various cultural repertoires of knowledge, across domains, environments, social positionalities, cultural cont exts, and languages. Our work demonstrate s the multiplicity, horizontality, and dialogicalit y afforded by analogical movement in tandem with learners’ relational histor ies. The many possible connections between disciplinary concepts across writing, computational thinking, music, STEM, ethni c stud ies, etc.  and the growing ecosystems/technologies around food can comprise endless culinary-based curricula. Particularly for marginalized learners navigating inhospitable educational terrain, such curricula candisrupt “the maintenance of such static conceptualizations” of disciplinarity w hich “reproduce racialized and ideological anxietie s and projects” (Warren et al., 2020, p. 280).

Additionally, this work demonstrates how relational histories supply the infrastructure that learners adapt and transfer through cross-disciplinary movement and improvisations —activities that not only reveal the culturally-specific knowledges that texture everyday life, but also sustain and extend them. Such insights highlight the importance of what we call a relationally sustained  learning: learning that centers marginalized learners’ complex histories in the creation of culturally-sustaining (Paris & Alim, 2017) learning environments that honor suppressed knowledges and challenge the dominant lo gics of conventional disciplinarity. Just as “liber atory education ought to be deeply rooted in the pasts, p resents, and futures that sustain and imagine multi ple values, purposes, and arcs of human learning,” (Warren et a l., 2020, p. 278) we look to future work around relationallysustained culinary learning that takes seriously the project of onto-epistemic heterogeneity toward rich, accessible opportunities in everyday contexts that push on dom inant ontological, epistemic, and axiological narra tives of learning.
