# Centering Youth’s Contexts to Acquire Political Information Literacy

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Effectively navigating political information online and distinguishing it from mis/disinformation has become crucial. Designers of educational programs should consider the unique perspectives of youth, especially those marginalized, that can often explain why and how youth engage with information and what makes them susceptible to misinformation. This paper discusses the learning outcomes immigrant youth achieved through community out-of-school learning centered on their contexts. Youth were invited to lead the learning process as design protagonists, which urged educators to serve in multiple roles to support them.

## Introduction

The spreading of political information online makes distinguishing between facts and misinformation challenging. People often avoid discussions (online and offline)  with those with whom they disagree and tend to spr ead more information (even misinformation) if it aligns with  their beliefs and attitudes (Bowyer & Kahne, 2019). These issues emphasize the need for educational programs to provide youth with opportunities for political socialization, such as engaging in meaningful discussions (Gil de Zúñiga et al., 2022; Warren & Wicks, 2011) and acqu iring literacy to make informed decisions on political ma tters (Cooke, 2022). Scholars identified a dimensio n of Information Literacy, Political Information Literac y (PIL), that can help youth tackle these political mis/disinformation, recognize the role of technology, identify political agendas, understand the context of political information, and assess the credibility of sources (Attama & Igwe, 2015; Singh & Brinster, 2022). Educ ational programs can promote PIL but  are often designed and led by adults with different  contexts and beliefs of youth (Bessant et al., 2016), which is essential to under standing how youth make sense of and navigate polit ical information (Koren, 2023). This paper discusses a p roject that centered on the historical and cultural  contexts of youth through Participatory Design (PD) and Asset-Based Community Development (ABCD) and brought together community assets-immigrant youth and educators who serve and support  them (Iversen et al., 2017; Haines, 2014). I discuss the PIL learning outcomes im migrant youth acquired as they led their learning p rocess and the roles of the educators who supported their learning.

## Literature review

Understanding the origin of political information and distinguishing facts from mis/disinformation is a tedious task that requires individuals to learn the history, perspectives, and potential agendas or  alternate motives of sources and those involved, especially on digital means. Nonetheless, identifying, understanding, and acting on political information in today’s political climate is crucial to protecting democracy (DiGiacomo et al., 2021; Cooke, 2022). Youth, who are still formulating their civic and po litical attitudes and beliefs, can significantly benefit from educational responses to the widesprea d political mis/disinformation and learn how to na vigate contextual political information (Papapicco et al.,  2022; Warren & Wicks, 2011). Scholars in the social sciences have conceptualized different types of literacies t o help youth assess  and make decisions on  civic and political information, like Media literacy (DiGiacomo, 2021), Online Civic Reasoning (McGrew et al., 2018), and Political Information Literacy (PIL) (Singh & Brinster, 2022). In this study, I use the conceptualization of PIL, a literacy developed by Library and Information Science (LIS) scholars as a dimension of information literacy. PIL attends explicitly to the cultural context of information a nd the communities that create and make sense of it  (Attama & Igwe, 2015). PIL is essential in this work, as it e xplicitly emphasizes the role of inform ation, those who control it, and identify misleading, false, and malicious intent behind it (Koren, 2023; Singh & Brinster, 2022). Existing programs and resources that teach youth how to navigate political information rarely consider youth perspectives and forms of participation, especially those marginalized (Bessant et al., 2016). Out-of-school initiatives with and for marginalized communities found that asset-based approaches provide a meaningful ground for de veloping youth’s agency and positive outcomes in civic engagement (Ito & Cross, 2022). Other projects found that directly involving youth in designing learning experiences, allowing them to critique and play a leading role i n their learning, is crucial for political socialization and developing skills, knowledge, and confidence to p articipate (Bessant et al., 2016; Penuel, 20 19). This project draws from these approaches to el evate immigrant youth’s cultural and historical contexts through Participat ory Design (PD) to identify their design recommenda tions and the PIL learning outcomes they can achieve by desig ning their learnin g experience. Although being engaged, informed, and knowledgeable is helpful, it is insuf ficient for youth to avoid cognitive barriers compl etely and spread misinformation when content aligns with thei r attitudes and beliefs (Bowyer & Kahne, 2019). The refore, this project extensively uses political discussions  in a safe, established community. Discussions can help youth gain knowledge and confidence, provide opportunities to confront contradicting opinions, and even change one’s perception of political information (Gil de Zúñiga et al., 2022).

## Data collection and analysis

Using the Asset-Based Community Development approach, I identified organizations (libraries and nonprofits) serving as assets to immigrant youth, providing opp ortunities, information, and services (Haines, 2014) and invited three educators to participate. Six immigra nt youth (non-U.S. citizens) between the ages of 15-17. The cohort met for a design day and participated in three 90-minute PD sessions. Following Iversen et al.’s (2017) process, the day started with a design brief that invited youth to design a learning experience in which they will obtain PIL. The brief discussed issues identified i n the state around civic engagement, service learning, educational policy, and political polarization. Each session focused on one factor that shapes PIL (see Koren, 2023). Educators and youth were unfamiliar with the plan and did not prepare ahead of time. Session 1 (historical and cultural contexts): Participants played a card game. Each participant picked a card and responded to a prompt about their family story, experiences, or challenges. Session 2 (attitudes and beliefs): Each youth sorted cards with sociopolitical issues (e.g., Xenophobia), into categories (e.g., issues I can’t control), and were encouraged to ask for help as they needed. Educators were instructed to help only when asked. T hen, youth engaged in discussions to identify similarities and differences  in their classifications. Session 3 (media use and exposure): Participants watched a video about power structures  following a gallery-walk activity in small groups where they analyzed articles/posts shared on online platforms (e.g., CNN, Reddit), examined power struc tures, and opportunities of learning about the issue on the platform. A focus group discussion was then conducted with yo uth in which they reflected on their experiences, and online interviews were conducted with the educators. Session data, interviews, and focus group recording s and transcriptions, images, and physical artifacts were analyzed. I took analytic memos to capture reflections and moments worth documenting during the sessions, and a fter the sessions, I audio-recorded think-aloud memos to document my reflections (Saldaña, 2021). I analyzed the collected data using Thematic Analysis  and Interoperative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA)  to consider different aspects of the same data (Spiers  & Riley, 2019). The thematic analysis started duri ng data collection, where I attended to unique or repeating moments. I consid ered conversations, behaviors, statements, and reflections. I sought visual cues of youth such  as gestures (e.g., glances at ad ults), moments of silence, language switching, and other moments relevant to i nteraction (e.g., laughing). I started with open co ding, producing initial observation notes when revieweing artifacts and  watching the recordings. To answer RQ1, I coded examples of youth answering questions and how  their answers change or develop between activities, participant interactions, and examples where educat ors share information, explain terms, or provide co ntext. To answer RQ2, I focused  on the youth’s participation in  the activities, the issues they identified in previ ous programs, and their expectations and experiences developing PIL. Then, I revisited the data and grouped the open codes into categories based on similarities and overlaps in conceptual meaning (Saldaña, 2021). Data for the IPA analysis included the responses of youth to the pro mpts of the card game in the first design session p rimarily (personal stories, experiences), conversations between youth during the second desig n session as they were comparing their classifications, and data from the focus group discussion. I read and re-read each specific data unit individually and coded specific statements with my initial reactions. Then, I used the initial codes from each data unit and compared them to other units to identify themes. IPA codes shed light on some themes that emerged during thematic analysis through the lens of youth experiences. In the finding section, I use YP to describe youth and AP to describe adults.

## Findings

Participants could strengthen and gain a more nuanc ed understanding of complex, controversial issues, gain knowledge, learn new terminology, and improve their  reasoning and confidence. The process helped draw youth deeper into conversations about unfamiliar issues. The youth pulled educators into conversations to help confirm their perspectives. For instance, YP1 asked AP1 to define the “student loan crisis,” as she was unsure she had the definition correctly, which made more sense to her after hearing the explanation. YP2, YP3, and YP4 di scussed the “digital divide” and struggled to define the co mplexity of the entire issue. YP2 defined it as: “P eople who live...in the projects, trailer parks, stuff like t hat, and didn’t really get the opportunity to have access.” This demonstrated that the youth had a partial understanding, but prompting helped them develop a better understanding. The staff’s personal experiences hel ped the youth understand the contexts of issues and  sparked interest. For example, YP4 almost constantly worked  alone but occasionally stopped listening to AP2’s stories about living in Los Angeles in 1992 during a political controversy. The staff sometimes helped youth clarify issues by slowly reviewing their details, explaining the contexts, or repeating information. YP1 and YP2, who were often vocal, silently listened when AP1 presented the sto ries more straightforwardly instead of reviewing th em independently. In each small group discussion, it w as observable that the staff helped reduce the info rmation overload to help youth digest the information by breaking down stories into smaller parts. This allowed the youth to think through the issues carefully and process the available information to respond. Once youth understand socio-political issues and their deeper contexts, they also develop their ability to reason about them while discussing and examining th e information. For instance, YP1 mentioned that her conversation with AP1 about one of the stories  on Reddit helped her learn what hate speech is, ide ntify more nuance in controversial issues in the media, and gain the context she needed to understand how they are developed: “[the] LGBTQ [community] has a lot of hate stories…and those people who hate them would believe in anything that anyone else would say against them.” Like YP1, YP2 was able to deepen her understanding of a certain issue, which surprised her and helped her learn how to pay more attention to nuanced contextual information: “I think I knew about the hate that LGBTQ+ people received, but I feel like I didn’t really know about how much it was. It was shocking to find out.” As conversations about controversial issues deepened, the quality of youth’s reasoning improved. AP2 mentioned: “It  [youth’s reasoning] did get a little more sophisticated the more we tal ked about it.” What helped the youth provide a more thoughtful response were the staff’s prompting and group conversations with peers who challenged each other to clarify the ir statements. As the design da y progressed, youth became more careful during conversations. Youth started to  develop a more balanced critical perspective and s pent a shorter time engaging with controversial issues or online stories. Their perceptions and reasoning reg arding the trustworthiness of sources were often justified, but their actions examining the information were inconsistent with their reasoning. From the first to the last political discussion, yo uth seemed to listen more, talk less, and ask questions to understand complex issues and terminol ogy. Youth had multiple opportunities to converse w ith people with contradicting perspectives, which helped them learn the nuance of some issues and identify how some digital political information is partial and should be examined more carefully. During the design day, multiple occurrences showed that the youth felt confident enough to participate and handle complex matters. Youth were confident in  stating their opinions throughout the day. They sa id that some conversations will be more challenging  but nonetheless essential for learning. YP2 said: “ Most…people are…misinformed and ignorant, so this is your chanc e to tell them the information, educate them, and r eally just give them the chance to see your perspective.” Some  youths were more confident than others; for instance, YP1 said: “I won’t feel uncomfortable. I’m just going to say what I believe in. I don’t care about what others are going to think.” Others, like YP5 and YP6, who speak Spanish, found it more comfo rtable participating in small group discussions with the Spanish-speaking educator AP3, as they could switch back and forth between Spanish and English. Youth were also confident that although ad ults could be more experienced, this does not necessarily mean that young people are less informed. They may even feel more informed than some adults. YP2 said: “Sometimes some younger people, as crazy as it is, might have more experience and knowledge in certain stuff.” These and other instances shed light on youth’s confidence level during the design day, but the level at which the activities helped it develop remains to be explored.

## Discussion

The current study sought to identify PIL learning o utcomes youth can gain in a community-based learning experience as they engage in PD activities that center their contexts and beliefs. Giving youth autonomy as design protagonists (Bessant et al., 2016; Iversen et al.,  2017; Penuel, 2019), including a mixture of activi ties that centered on youth’s perspectives and contexts (espe cially the card game and the political discussions), involving community educators, allowing youth to explore thei r attitudes and beliefs, share their personal exper iences, discuss their different backgrounds, and interact with various media, helped them better understand contextualized political information, gain k nowledge and terminology, improve their reasoning a bout information, and even develop more confidence. Activities allowed youth to discuss issues with those with entirely different perspectives and practice real-life political discussions, were meaningful to developing PIL outcomes (Papapicco et al., 2022; Warren & Wicks, 2011). Nonetheless, as PIL learning  outcomes developed stimulatingly, were intertwined, and hard to distinguish. Future research should identif y assessment tools to examine the development  of skills, knowledge, and confidence. The study highlighted the types of educational programs that can resonate with youth and inspire designs of future learning experiences.  Previous studies suggested that youth might have d ifferent perspectives and insights on political participation than adults (Bessant et al., 2016). Youth were more dedicated to developing PIL when they had a chance to express  their opinions and even “educate others,” which th ey perceived as a form of civic engagement.  Allowing youth to leave the echo chamber by engaging in meaningful political discussions with peers with entirely diff erent perspectives is a powerful way to participate  (Reichert & Print, 2017). Elevating youth voices and using thei r perspectives to design future learning experience s is meaningful for youth participation.

The study sheds light on the roles that educators can lead during a community learning where youth lead the learning process.  During the study, educators  served as moderators during contentious discussions, helped redirect discussions, and supported youth in clarif ying their perspectives and statements  (sometimes by translating). This helped youth consider the perspectives of thei r peers, use more conscious language, and de-escalate difficult conversations. Educators  shared from their experience (not as exp erts), provided relevant context, explained terminology, and stepped in to increase youth’s suspicion of information, which improved their reasoning and critical examination of online inform ation. This helped youth improve their reasoning ab out political issues. At the sa me time, youth had the autonomy to choose when to r eceive help and by what means, which made the balance between the autonomy of the youth and the learning process challenging. Future research should develop support systems for educators to  switch between multiple roles as they support youth  without taking over their learning process.
