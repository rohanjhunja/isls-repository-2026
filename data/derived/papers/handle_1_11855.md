# Teacher Orchestration Load in Collaborative Learning Activities in Robotics

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates teachers’ orchestration tasks during collaborative robotics activities, focusing on the cognitive, physical, and emotional components of orchestration load. Using a mixed-method approach, we compared a self-orchestrated session to one structured by the Pyramid script, a collaborative learning flow pattern. Findings indicate that structured scripts effectively reduce emotional and cognitive load for junior teachers by clearly guiding orchestration, enabling a shift from continuous monitoring to strategic classroom management. In contrast, senior teachers experienced increased emotional and cognitive loads due to conflicts with established routines. By highlighting emotional load as an orchestration component, this study deepens the understanding of teacher orchestration load in CSCL environments. Further research involving diverse teaching contexts is needed to validate these findings.

## Introduction

In today's rapidly evolving educational landscape, teachers play complex roles extending beyond tradit ional instruction. Like orchestra conductors, teachers harmonize various elements of learning, balancing direct guidance with student autonomy—known as orchestration in Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) contexts (Dillenbourg et al., 2009). Martin, a primary teach er in Switzerland, vividly describes orchestration: “Orchestration sounds like an orchestra conductor. Except that here the conducto r is sometimes passive because normally he always gives everything. Here, there are certain moments when you [the teacher] give something and then the students must play.” The integration of new digital technologies, such as robotics, significantly increases orchestration demands, as teachers must simultaneou sly address cognitive, physical, and emotional chal lenges (Dillenbourg & Betrancourt, 2006; Schenkenberg, 202 4). In Sw itzerland, recent educational reforms have incorporated digital competencies into th e curriculum using Thymio robotic kits. While these kits offer valuabl e learning opportunities, limited resources and the c ollaborative nature of robot use add layers of comp lexity to orchestration, requiring teachers to manage group d ynamics, troubleshoot technical issues,  and bridge the gap between abstract concepts and concrete experiences (Chevalier et al., 2016; Shahmoradi et al., 2020). Existing research on teacher orchestration has predominantly addressed cognitive and physical aspects (Prieto et al., 2011), often overlooking the emotional dimension despite its critical role in cl assroom dynamics. For instance, while emotional load has been examined in other contexts such as teacher stress and student interaction (Frenzel et al., 2021), its direct role in orchestr ation—especially in technology-enhanced classrooms—has not been explored. This study directly addresses this gap by incorporating emotional load into the orchestration model, offering a more comprehensive perspective. The addi tion of emotional load is important, as teaching inherently involves emotional interactions that influence both  teacher performance and student outcomes. This stu dy investigates teacher orchestration tasks and loads during collaborative robotics activities, specifically addressing:

1. What tasks do teachers perform when orchestrating collaborative robotics activities?

2. What types of loads, particularly emotional, do teachers encounter?

3. How do collaborative scripts impact teacher orchestration and associated loads?

Teacher orchestration and orchestration load Teacher orchestration involves managing simultaneous classroom activities within constraints such as curriculum requirements, time, space, and safety, to foster effective teaching environments (Dillenbourg, 2013). This process requires coordinating tasks a cross multiple learning levels, emphasizing deliber ate management of interactions (Rothstein-Fisch & Trumbull, 2008). Researchers have proposed designing Technology-Enhanced Learning (TEL) environments, where teachers actively conduct and adapt classroom  interactions (Kollar & Fischer, 2013; Dillenbourg, 2013). To address gaps identified in p revious research, our study proposes an orchestrati on model (Figure 1) based on Prieto and his colleagues’ (2011) model consisting of four  dimensions: planning (designing instruction and preparing resources), management (r esource and behavior management, clear expectations, positive relations; Gaudreau, 2024), awareness (mon itoring students’ progress), and interventions (ad dressing unexpected events, providing feedback).  Unlike prior models that predominantly focused on c ognitive and physical aspects, we explicitly incorporate emotional load. Teachers act ively contributed to shaping this model, emphasizing orchestration as adaptive and improvisa tional, akin to a bassist in a jazz band continuous ly responding to immediate student needs, thus supporting flexible learning environments. Orchestration load represents the effort teachers e xpend to effectively implement classroom activities, reflecting the gap between ideal scenarios and classroom realities (Cuendet et al., 2013; Dillenbourg et al., 2018). It encompasses three dimensions: co gnitive, physical, and emotional. Cognitive load in volves the mental effort required for planning, adapting, and making real-time decisions while monitoring multiple group inte ractions (Sweller, 2010). Physical load refers to exertion r elated to mobility, material distribution, or class room reorganization, which can accumulate and affect tea cher effectiveness (Kerangueven & Claudon, 2023). Emotional load captures the demands associated with  regulating emotions and managing interpersonal interactions in dynamic teaching contexts (Hellemans et al., 2014; Frenzel et al., 2021). ## Method

This qualitative exploratory study aimed to capture  teachers' experiences of orchestration and orchestration load in collaborative, technology-enhanced classrooms. Two primary teachers from scho ols in Vaud and Neuchâtel, Switzerland, participated, differing in teaching ex perience and familiarity with digital technologies.  Martin, a senior teacher with over 20 years of experience, regularly integrated digital tools and robotics in his 5-6P classes (ages 9-10), identifying time management, progress tracking, and resistance to collaboration as key challenges. Pauline, a junior teacher with limited robotics experience, taught 7P classes (ages 11-12) and focused on managing time, technology, progress monitoring, and equitabl e task distribution.  Each teacher conducted two robotics teaching sessions: one self-orchestrated, where teachers independently managed collaboration, and one structured using the Pyramid Collaborative Learning Flow Patte rn (CLFP). The Pyramid model  was chosen over other models because it strikes a balance between individual accountability and group interdependence  (HernándezLeo et al.,  2006), making it particularly suitable for robotics activi ties requiring iterative problem-solving. It is particularly effective for fostering collaboration while providing structure to manage complex interactions, which is key for novice teachers who may struggle with classroom management. To capture orchestration load comprehensively, we d eveloped the Orchestration Task Questionnaire to help teachers allocate their perceived effort acros s planning, management, awareness, and intervention dimensions, distributing 100%. Stress and emotion s cales (PANAS) measured stress (0 –10 scale) and emotional states (1 –5 scale), including physical symptoms. A modified N ASA-TLX assessed perceived cognitive and physical load. Approximately two weeks after each s ession, self-confrontation interviews were condu cted using selected video excerpts to highlight specific orche stration demands. Teachers reflected freely before responding to structured questions on orchestration tasks and associated loads. All qualitative data collected du ring the interviews were systematically analyzed using thematic analysis to identify recurring patterns related to cognitive, physical, and emotional loads. The qualitative find ings were triangulated with quantitative questionna ire data to provide a comprehensive view of each teacher’s orchestration experience.

## Results

To address the research questions, we analyzed both  sessions to examine how teachers allocated their orchestration efforts and how these tasks and loads  were influenced by collaborative scripts (Figure 2). The use of the Pyramid script significantly influenced cognitive and emotional loads. For the novice teacher, the structured guidance of the script helped reduce cognitive load  by providing clear steps and minimizing the need f or spontaneous decisions. However, for the experienced teacher, the same struc ture led to increased cognitive load as it conflicted with established routines, forcing  adaptation and adjustment that felt unnatural. Sim ilarly, emotional load varied: whi le the structured approach reduced anxiety and stre ss for the novice teacher, it heightened frustration for the experienced teacher who perceived it as restrictive. Role distribution within groups emerged organically rather than being pre-assigned. This choice aimed to support student agen cy and foster collaborative problem-solving, allowing students to naturally assume roles based on comfort and skill level. Pauline, during the unscripted session, focused mainly on awareness (40 %) and interventions (40%), actively monitoring and frequently adjusting. Management and plannin g tasks were minimal (10% each). In the scripted se ssion, management and planning involvement increased to 30 % each, while awareness dropped to 10%, indicating reduced monitoring needs. Intervention tasks also d ecreased to 30%, reflecting a lesser need for  adjustments. Although the script provided structure, it heightened stress as he struggled to balance group supervision and overall classroom dynamics. Emotionally, Martin reported increased frustration and embarrassment during the scripted s ession, feeling constrained by the lack of flexibility to adapt to spontaneous situations. ## Discussion and conclusion

This study suggests that the benefit of structured CSCL scripts, like the Pyramid model, could vary depending on teaching experience. It enables, novice teachers  to reduce  emotional load and reallocat e cognitive resources toward strategic planning and classroom management (Dillenbourg & Tchounikine, 2007; Kirschner et al., 2006). The junior teacher leveraged the script to decrease immediate monitoring demands, aligning with Carroll’s (1963) Model of Sch ool Learning, where structured repetition lowers st ress through iterative opportunities for understanding. Conversely, the senior teacher experienced increase d cognitive and emotional loads when implementing the structured script, as it conflicted with his established teaching routines. This finding aligns with the expertise reversal effect, where support mechanisms beneficial for novices can impose additional demands on experts (Sweller et al., 2003; Kollar et al., 2007). These contrasting experiences highlight the need for professional development focused on script customization, allowi ng digital tools to adapt to the established practi ces of experienced educators. Rather than expecting teache rs to modify their approaches to fit rigid technologies, it is essential to design tools that are flexible and res ponsive to diverse pedagogical context s. Both teachers initially reported minimal physical exertion, yet self-confrontation interviews revealed they underestimated their physical engagement. This aligns with Csikszentmihalyi’s (20 05) concept of flow, where teachers underreport phy sical effort when deeply engaged. Future studies employing objective measures, such as electrodermal activity (EDA), could yield more precise physical load assessments.  Despite shedding light on orchestration practices a nd loads, this study's limitations include its small sample size and short duration, which may limit generalizability. Further research with larger, diverse populations is necess ary, incorporating objective physiological indicato rs and exploring the relationship between CSCL script usage, orchestration load, and student outcomes, notably student engagement and well-being.
