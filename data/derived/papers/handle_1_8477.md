# Comprehensive Student Records as a Tool for Networked and Connected Learning: Using Social Media Research to Frame Inquiry

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Comprehensive Student Records (CSRs) are an emerging model for life-long and life-wide learning transcripts. As they are currently conceived, CSRs would allow students to track and curate learner profiles aligned with industry recognized skill frameworks, making them potentially powerful tools for promoting pipelines to future learning and employment. How CSRs could support connecting and networking within these pipelines is, however, undertheorized. In this paper, social media research is used as a frame to inform future work of designing CSRs as tools for connecting and networking students to learning and employment opportunities. Special attention is paid to the need to focus on the design of CSRs to promote equitable access to employment.

## Introduction

In 2015, the American Association of Collegiate Registrars and Admissions Officers (AACRAO) partnered with the National Association of Student Personnel Administration (NASPA) to implement the Comprehensive Student Record Project to imagine the future of student records for lifelong and life-wide learning (Green & Parnell, 2017). Comprehensive Student Records (CSRs) differ from typical student records in that they allow for richer accounts of skill and ability by shifting the measure of learning away from seat-time metrics to competencies demonstrated through experiential learning. CSRs also take advantage of the affordances of digital technology to include microcredentials and other digital evidence of learning. It has been argued that CSRs can be a powerful tool for students to construct learning profiles that can be aligned to industry-recognized skill frameworks (Shendy et al., 2019) making them useful tools for scaffolding future lea rning and connecting students to learning employmen t opportunities.

Research on CSRs has so far focused on demonstrating their viability as a medium for communicating more explicitly about experiential learning. This has been demonstrated through the CSR Project that saw CSR models expand from twelve models in the pilot study to over 150 models in Phase II of the project. Another line of research has sought to understand student (Parish, Parks, & Fryer, 2017) and employer (Cederquist, Fishman, & Teasley, 2022) perspectives of CSRs as tools for communicating learning. Findings from both studies suggest CSRs are perceived as more informative than traditi onal transcripts in that they more holistically com municate the broad range of skills and competencies students are developing within and outside classrooms. While this line of research has done well to demonstrate the viability of CSRs as an alternative transcript model, additional work must be done to understand their viability as a too l for connected and networked learning. In this pap er, I use social media research to frame challenges and oppor tunities researchers should consider when designing  and scrutinizing CSR systems for their ability to equit ably and capably connect and network students to fu ture opportunities. What emerges is the importance of understanding producer (student) and consumer (e.g., employer) behavior as it relates to CSR curation and consumption. Before moving into this discussion, I first describe CSRs as a tool for “connected learning” (Ito et al., 2013) by highlighting the unique features of CSRs. CSRs as a tool for connected learning As they are currently conceived, CSRs largely refle ct existing static academic transcripts with the ad dition of experiential learning outcomes documented on the tr anscript. To understand the potential of CSRs as a tool for connecting and networking to opportunity, it is important to highlight two key features: 1) the emphasis on their interoperability with different employment and learning platforms, and 2) ownership of the record is vested in the student rather than the credentialing institution (Torres, 2020). Torres (2020) argues this is particularly useful for those students who ultimately leave postsecondary education with some credits but no degree; with a CSR system, any learning achieved or competencies demonstrated could be stacked and credentialed. This would allow students to “translate their full education, training, and work experience to a record of transferable skills” (Torres, 2020, p. 46). As a personally-managed digital record of one’ s achievements and interests across time and space,  CSRs would support models of connected learning: socio-c ultural models of learning that are interest-driven, peersupported, and production-centered and powered by networked technology (Ito et al, 2013). This interpretation of CSRs as a tool for connected learning is further supported by ethnographic work on social media as a tool for jobICLS2022 Proceedings 1321 © ISLS seeking—notably the work of Gershon (2017). Gershon  documented how the changing technological infrastructure of networking and hiring (e.g., LinkedIn) presents a scenario in which the individual is encouraged to view themselves “as a business” in which their bundle of curated skills are viewed as business solutions publicly displayed for the purpose of networking and gaining employment (p. 11). The possibility of curating one’s bundle of skills is supported by a feature of CSRs often highlighted by their proponents—the possibility for collecting rich and granular data on student activity through digital learning tools (Black, 2016). Black argues that an increased level of detail in data collection and representation “can lead to a fuller analysis of strengths, weaknesses, and opportunities” in a world in which continuous asses sment of skills is becoming increasingly important (Black, 2 016, 4th para.). In theory, data collected on student behav ior could be analyzed by admissions officers, hirers and applicant tracking systems to support decision-ma king in applicant screening and evaluation. While intriguing, Teasley and Kelly (2020), in their report on the potential of linking learning and employment data, have noted th ere are short and long term issues that must be add ressed before this idea can be fully realized in practice: for example, defining higher education outcomes as competencies and developing methods for communicating these competencies. Ultimately, as issues of data measurement and communication are addressed, it will become increasingly important for researchers to understand how questions related to CSR platform features may impact consumer and producer behavior. In the following sections, I point to research from social media studies that can help scholars examine these issues. Framing CSRs through social media research As discussed above, CSRs have the potential to be a tool for managing one’s self-presentation as employable, or ready for future learning, through data derived from a wide variety of learning experiences. From this perspective, we can assume there are implications for how students (producers) would utilize the affordances of CSR systems to document their learning and connect prior experiences tonew opportunities. There are also clear implications for how both humans and machines will interpret the  data contained within CSRs to support recruitment and hiring decisions. In the following sections I use social media research to further explicate the two perspectives of producer and consumer, showing how both are vital t o understanding the potential opportunities and pit falls of CSR systems as tools for connecting and networking to opportunity. Self-presentation: Audience and affordances Social media research into self-presentation has a long history and finds its roots in Goffman’s Presentation of Self in Everyday Life (Donath, 2020; Goffman, 1959). An issue often addressed by self-presentation research is whether the unique environmental cues of social media result in distinct affordances for self-presentation (Donath, 2020). This question is crucial because it points t o two social media theories—audience and platform affordances—that may help researchers better unders tand how CSR-users might manage self-presentation i n networked environments. For example, Duffy and Chan  (2019) documented emerging adults’ response to “imagined surveillance.” Duffy and Chan’s participa nts described using privacy settings on social netw orking sites to manage an imagined audience’s access to ce rtain content, thus controlling their identity pres entation. (Duffy and Chan, 2019, p. 125). Viewed through the lens of affordances—the “multifaceted relational structure between an object/technology and the user that enables or constrains potential behavioral outcomes in a particular context” (Evans, Pearce, Vitak, & Treem, 2016, p. 36)—Duffy and Chan’s participants were able to control their visibility on platforms because of these privacy features. This allowed the participants to manage who saw what information, and as a result the impressions they b elieved potential employers would form about them. The framing of affordances will be particularly useful for understanding the unique advantages a digitally networked record of student learning has over traditional transcripts. Additionally, as CSR designs evolve over the course of students’ lives, taking an affordances perspective will allow for a contextually stable object of inquiry. This points to the moving target problem in social media resear ch, i.e., “the challenge of researching media artif acts and effects that are constantly in flux” (Bayer, Trieu, & Ellison, 2020, p. 472). Employing an affordances perspective with CSRs will help researchers and designers under stand how changing features of the technological sy stem either support or hinder the presentation of one’s self as competent in order to align with evolving standards and expectations of professional communities.

Impression formation: Signaling and warranting theory Proponents of CSRs typically frame the process by which evidence of competence contained in CSRs could help people find jobs as follows: 1) CSR data connects a student to an opportunity, 2) a student presents e vidence of competence, 3) the evidence is interpreted by human  or machine, and 4) the student is accepted as comp etent. CSR research would benefit from a consideration of how information cues, or the hidden signals given o ff by characteristics of the information and channel, specifically affect impression formation. Additionally, while hirers ICLS2022 Proceedings 1322 © ISLS do review work portfolios, less is known about how they would interpret the granular data on learning that differentiates CSRs from portfolios. Interpreting t hese issues through signaling and warranting theory  can help explain how a focus on CSR design can help us understand how impressions are formed through digital media. Signaling theory argues the perceived reliability of information relates to the perceived cost of producing the information; two types of signals are noted in the literature. Assessment signals are those that are more costly to produce and are thus deemed more reliable because “their production mandates the possession of the quality.” Conventional signals, because they are believed to be more easily produced, are viewed as arbitrary signals of a quality (DeAndrea, 2014, p. 197). Warranting simila rly describes how people interpret information cues, but unlike signals which convey reliability through pro duction cost, warranting conveys reliability based on cues presented by the source of the information. The mor e control a user has over the source of the informa tion cue describing them, the lower the warranting value. As  a result, third party warrants are generally belie ved to be more reliable.

Taken at face value, we might assume evidence within CSRs would be interpreted as assessment signals with high warranting value: student competency data and examples of work can be reviewed to determine if a job applicant is qualified for the job, and the validation of the credential issuer would imbue the evidence with high trust. However, findings from CSR research suggest additional research into the signaling and warranting powers of CSR features is needed. For example, to understand the advantages and challenges of implementing CSRs into entry-level hiring, Cederquist et al. (2022) presented hirers with a publicly available exemplar of a CSR designed around mastery-and competency-based learning known  as the Mastery Transcript™. While perspectives wer e generally positive, two potential challenges emerge d from the study. First, how complex non-cognitive skills could be presented through the CSR’s data visualizations was unclear to the hirers—they were unsure how such a contextually situated competency like teamwork could be represented through data on a digital transcript. The second challenge relates to the similarities hirers drew between the Mastery Transcript™ and LinkedIn.  Due to the school’s verification of learning, hirers viewe d the personally curated competency profile in the Mastery Transcript™ as a more reliable presentation of competence than what students curate on LinkedIn. While we can interpret this as a potential advantage CSRs could contribute to social media professional networking sites such as LinkedIn, it is unclear if this opinion would be held by other hirers. Cederquist and colleague’s population size was too small (n=12) and the background of informan ts too similar (participants came from one professi onal pathway) to draw generalizable findings. Future res earch should include larger and more diverse profes sional pathways to determine if CSRs maintain their high warranting value. The importance of designing CSRs for equity and inclusion Before concluding, it is important to note why it i s of vital importance to consider the social networ ks through which job seekers access labor markets when studyin g CSRs. Referring back to Gershon (2017) we see tha t professional networking sites such as LinkedIn are designed with a specific type of worker in mind—white collar professionals. Similarly, research on low-resource workers’ use of social media for job-seeking shows that when using Internet-based tools to look for work, low-re source workers tend to rely on social network sites  like Facebook and Instagram and job posting sites like C raigslist to find information about work (Lu & Dill ahunt, 2021). Lu and Dillahunt (2021) show that this has implications for equity as the design of communication channels on sites like Facebook reinforces power asymmetries between employers and low-resource job seekers; employers interviewed in the study framed Facebook recruitmen t as a process of “accessing a pool of low wage workers” (sec. 5.3, emphasis original). Lu and Dillahunt argue that this framing potentially makes low-wage workers both “more accessible” but also “more expendable and rep laceable” (sec. 5.3). A focus on equity and inclusi on also highlights the necessity to vest control of CSR data with the student. Failure to do so opens low-wage workers to potential exclusion through surveillance of the digital traces of their education and employment records. This asymmetry between hirers and job-seekers echoes McDonald and colleagues (2019) study of two online labor markets—Monster.com and LinkedIn. The former site was described by hirers as “black holes ” in which the ease of uploading resumes to the site resulted in information overload as thousands of applications came in for only one position. LinkedIn, however, was de scribed as a site for looking for and recruiting “s pecialized superstars” away from their current jobs. Sites lik ed LinkedIn are not designed with the great diversi ty of job seekers in mind. Thus, designing CSRs with only Lin kedIn in mind would likely obscure the needs of a w ide swath of the labor market. These findings suggest it would be prudent to focus the work of designing CSRs with those typically left out or disadvantaged by the ex isting technical infrastructure of job-seeking. Dil lahunt and colleagues’ body of participatory design work with underserved job seekers provides a useful starting point for the design of such an agenda (see Dillahunt, Lam, Lu, & Wheeler, 2018; Wheeler & Dillahunt, 2018).

## Conclusion

ICLS2022 Proceedings 1323 © ISLS As CSRs develop as tools for connected learning, it  will be important to develop a more rigorous theoretical understanding of their design and use within networks. Findings from social media research can help in framing studies with these objectives. Studies of CSRs shou ld pay particular attention to the affordances offe red by features unique to CSRs, particularly the affordanc es offered by interoperability and student ownershi p (as opposed to institutional ownership). Additionally, research on the imagined audience can help scholars understand how CSRs help students curate self-presentations of  competence and network within communities of practice. Research into consumers of CSRs should examine how both humans and machines interpret evidence, including granular data on learning, to form impressions of the individuals represented through CSRs. This should include the unique environmental cues communicated by diver se information sources and channels. This also stron gly suggests a focus on developing CSR systems that support the job-seeking behavior of those typically left out of the labor market. Such a focus will forefront the n eeds of low-resource job seekers and help promote t he development of equitable pathways to employment.
