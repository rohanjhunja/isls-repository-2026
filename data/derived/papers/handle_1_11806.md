# Fostering Sociopolitical Awareness in Genetic Education: Investigating Racial Health Disparities in Diabetes

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Schools are not neutral sites of learning, and (re)produce white supremacy through seemingly neutral teaching and learning interactions. Heeding the calls to incorporate the sociopolitical into science education, we developed a high school biology unit that critically engages genetics education. The unit highlights environments as not neutral and not benign, and that the inequities plaguing these environments were created through structures and forces of oppression. We analyzed post interviews to understand the extent to which students developed non-deterministic views of genes and sociopolitical awareness of the nature of the environments in gene-environment interactions. Our findings suggest that students understood trait variation as resulting from interactions between genes and the environment and were able to reason about the environment in these interactions as non-neutral and harmful for marginalized communities. However, they struggled to explain how gene-environment interactions work at the biological level and the intentionality of environmental inequities.

## Introduction and motivation for the research

There is a growing body of research in the Learning  Sciences that attends to the importance of address ing issues of inequity (e.g., settler-colonialism, racism, homophobia, classism) through science education (Duncan et al., 2024; Bang et al., 2018; Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020; Morales-Doyle, 2024; Pugh et al., 2019). This scholarship has shown the ways in which schools are not neutral sites of learning (Morales-Doyle et al., 2020; Morales-Doyle, 2024; Tan & Calabrese Barton, 2023), but rather purport dominant Eurocentric ideologies that (re)produce white supremacy through everyday seemingly neutral teachi ng and learning interactions (Bang et al., 2018). Importantly, there has been a significant push towa rds developing science curricula that explicitly en gage with the sociopolitical, towards desettling Eurocentric ways of knowing and being (Bang et al., 2015; Learn ing in Places Collaborative, 2021; Tzou et al., 2019), and  in which science learning is aimed at fighting his torically sedimented injustices that youth contend with in their lives (Tan & Calabrese Barton, 2023; Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020; Morales-Doyle et al., 2020). This paper builds on these ongoing efforts, attuning to the ways in which critical approaches to teaching genetics can support students’ understandings around race and racism as it shapes their everyday lives in and out of school contexts.

Over the past decade, genetics educators have been arguing that current genetic s instruction engenders problematic and misleading views of genetic phenome na (e.g., Donovan, 2014; 2017; Dougherty, 2009; Has kelIttah & Yarden, 2021; Kampourakis, 202 3). That is, current genetic instruction leaves yout h with misleading understandings of: a) genetic determinism—the idea that genes are the sole determinants of in ternal and external features and b) genetic essentialism —that human social or racial groups have unique and immut able genetic essences that differentiate them. Specifically, res earch has found an alarming correlation between gen etic determinism and essentialism, and racial prejudice (Dar-Nimord & Heine, 2011; Willia ms & Eberhardt, 2008). Put bluntly, genetics educators are finding that cu rrent genetics instruction is likely reproducing, if not fostering, racial bias and racist thinking (Donovan, 2014). Whil e alarming in its own right, this trend is particul arly worrisome given the current political climate in th e United States and globally, where notions of gene tic determinism and essentialism are likely contributors to racism, white supremacy, and homophobia. The prevailing instructional recommendations for countering such e ssentialist and deterministic thinking call for fos tering understandings of trait variation as being caused by many genes in interaction with the environment (multifactorial inheritance) as well as understanding trait variati on within and across human groups (population genet ics) (e.g., Donovan et al., 2024; Dougherty, 2009). As we have argued elsewhere, countering essentialist and deter ministic thinking through a deeper understanding of multifac torial inheritance and population genetics is not s ufficient (Duncan et al., 2024).  This is because understanding that racial groups ar e not genetically distinct and that environments influence trai ts does not automatically explain racial disparitie s in health, wealth, education attainment, etc. Rather, to explain racial health d isparities, students need to understand the sociopo litical nature of the environments that impact human health and we ll-being (Duncan et al., 2024; Morales-Doyle et al., 2020). Towards this end we developed a high school genetic s curricular unit designed to counter these problem s in genetics education through a focus on gene-environment interactions, with an explicit recognit ion of the environment as structured through sociopolitical, historical, and cultural inequity. We next describe the theoretical frameworks that informed the design of the genetics unit. Theoretical framing We draw on the Rightful Presence (RP) framework tow ards unsettling entrenched epistemic hierarchies th at consider science knowledge as separate from the soc iopolitical by recogniz ing that learning about injustices students experience in the world is integral to their classr oom science learning and knowledge creation (Tan et al., 2019). As such, designed into the curriculum a re ‘making present practices’ (Calabrese Barton & T an, 2019) that explicitly incorporate knowledge about racial and economic inequity as deeply consequential to students’ science learning. We contend therefore that learning about genetics necessarily involves learning about racial and economic injustices that youth experience in their community. These ‘making present’ classroom practic es— structured into the curriculum unit —invite students’ whole selves to be rightfully pres ent as part of science learning, while also gearing the science they learn towards making consequential changes in their lives, for example, through advocacy and activism (Tan & Calabrese Barton, 2023). Complimenting this approach, we build on our theorizations of consequential science education through an explicit focus on environments as the product of  historically sedimented inequity structured into o ur communities (Duncan et al., 2024).  By this we mean that a) environments are not-neutral and are experienced differently by racialized people (e.g., the non-neutrality of police interactions with members of different ra cial groups), b) they are not benign in that some environments are harmful and this harm is experienced predominantly by minoritized groups (e.g., dispossessed neighborh oods and food deserts), and c) envi ronments are not happenstance in that they are intentionally created by sociopolitical forces such as racism (e.g., redlining policies and their impact on neighborhoods and their dweller s). In this sense, designing for students ’ RP through science education necessitates ‘making present’ injustices,  contextualized within their emergence through hist ories and ongoing reproductions of inequity in non-neutral environments. Specific to genetics education, when considering observable racial differences in health and physiol ogy, it is important that students understand both what is not causing these differences —genetic differences between racial groups or the inevitability of particular ou tcomes given particular genes —and what is causing them —non-neutral differences in environments that result in physiological harm to minoritized racial groups. As  noted by Graves and Goodman (2022), racism, not ra ce, explains health and other disparities. Therefore, anti-racist genetics education that invites youths’ RP into science classrooms needs to foster both more complex unders tandings of genetic phenomena (i.e., multifactorial  model) and a sociopolitical awareness of the environment in gene-environment interactions.

## Research questions

Our [project name blinded] research group has taken initial steps towards this goal by co-designing a high school genetics unit with science teachers. This unit was implemented by the collaborating teachers in two high schools with a mixed 9 –10th grade class (n=21) and an 11 th grade class (n=30). Our research questions thus exp lore the extent to which our designed unit supported student s in: a) developing non-deterministic views of genes (gene-environment interactions), and b) sociopolitical awareness of the non-neutral, harmful, and designed nature of the environments that impact our genes, physiology, and  ultimately our health. We address these questions through our analyses of post-implementation interviews conducted with small groups of students in both schools. Design of the genetics unit on diabetes Taking an explicit political turn, the unit focused  on inequities and disparities associated with diab etes, a known health concern for the community served by the two schools. Type 2 diabetes is profoundly influenced b y environmental factors such as diet, stress level, and activity level, which in turn are  impacted by racial and economic segregation (Doucet-Battle, 2021). Over one year, our collaborative design team developed eight lesson sets, with each lesson between 1 –6 periods of instruction (~60 min pe riods). The overall aim of the unit was to teach students genetics concepts, while weaving in the sociopolitical through explicating the non-neutrality of the environment as it shaped different communities’ experiences of and with diabetes (see Figure 1). The first lesson set introduced students to various  dimensions of the diabetes phenomenon and initiate d the Driving Question Board  (with student generated questions) that motivated t he rest of the unit. The second lesson set engaged students in evidence-based modeling of the physiology of diabetes, inclu ding at the cellular and molecular levels. The third lesson set involved  more direct instruction on the central dogma of mo lecular genetics (how genes code for proteins). In the four th lesson students developed a model to explain a r are monogenic form of diabetes and its inheritance patt erns (MODY). The fifth lesson set focused on Mendel ian inheritance patterns (this was needed to mee t the district’s requirements) and polygenic inheri tance including polygenic risk scores for diabetes. In the sixth le sson set, students analyzed survey data from their community that they had collected at a cultural festival at t he school. These data exam ined the conditions and lifestyles of members in their community (e.g., weekly food expen ditures, health insurance coverage, types of foods consumed, income, etc.) The aim of this lesson was to help students understand how environmental factors drive health disparities in diabetes and how policies and stru ctures (e.g., access to health care) impact diabete s burden on different communities. The seventh lesson set ex amined the relationship between genes and environme ntal risk factors and how living in particular environme nts and being racialized in particular ways can be detrimental to your health. The eighth and final lesson set was  devoted to efforts towards activism and advocacy r elated to what students had learned in the unit. ## Study context

The unit was implemented in two high schools in the  same district in a sanctuary city in Northeastern USA. The schools serve the same predominantly immigrant comm unity, with 90% of the students identifying as Hisp anic and most families (77%) eligible for free and reduced lunch. One of the collaborating teachers taught in the smaller STEM magnet school in the district and implemented the unit with 21 students (mixed 9th and 10th grade) in an elective course. The second collaborating teacher taught at the larger main high school and implemented the unit with a class of 30 honors biology students in the 1 1th grade. Both teachers were involved in co-designing the curriculum with university researchers and implemented the units in spring 2024. Data collected The data analyzed for this paper included eight post-unit interviews: six were with small student groups of 3 –4, one was with a pair of students, and one was with a n individual student (five, including the singleton  were with students from the 9–10 grade class and the other three were with students from the 11th grade class at the second school). Interviews were conducted over three days during the last few days of the unit. The first interview was conducted primarily by the first author (an established researcher) as the second author (a graduate student) observed and asked some  of the follow up questions. The second interview w as conducted by both authors, and the remaining six were carried out by the second author. Interviews lasted between 30–45 minutes. Students’ responses to three of the int erview questions were analyzed for this paper, selected to answer the research question foci of this study. The first question asked students “why is it that certain groups of people, Black and Hispanic  people for example, have more diabetes or other health iss ues like heart disease or high blood pressure compared to other groups like white European people?” The two other questions were based on a short clip from a PBS documentary called Blood  Sugar Rising, which students had viewed in class (and reviewed during the interview). In the clip, Dr. Anthony Hatch, a sociologist and public health expert, states: “It is very important for communities to understand that our genes do not work in a vacuum. They always on ly ever do what they do in a given environment. So, we’ve got to turn back and look very carefully at t he environments that we live in and how they are built, how they ar e structured, to create inequity.” Students were first asked to explain what was meant by the statement “genes do n ot work in a vacuum; they always only ever do what they do in a given environment.” We then asked them to explain what was meant by t he statement “the environments that we live in and, how they are built, how they are structured to create inequity.” Analysis The interviews were transcribed and analyzed predom inantly by the second, fourth, and fifth authors. W e conducted a content analysis of the groups ’ responses to the three questions (each question wit h its own coding scheme). Initially, the second, fourth, and fifth authors reviewed the transcripts and through constant comparisons of responses to a specific question developed a coding scheme and shared the themes they noticed in the responses. The entire team then reviewed the coding scheme and excerpts from the transcripts to refine the coding schemes. The three authors then coded the entire data set and settled any disagreements. In terms of the first question regarding racial hea lth disparities we coded the data to capture studen ts’ understandings of: a) the environment as non-neutral (did they understand environments as differ ent due to segregation), b) what environmental factors were seen as contributin g to the higher prevalence of diabetes (harmful nature of environments), and c) relative contributions of genes and environmental f actors to the disparities observed. For the  second question (genes do not work in a vacuum) we coded responses to  better understand students’ ideas about how genes and envi ronments actually interact. For the third question (environments structured to create inequities) we c oded student responses to identify whether and how they conceived of environmental conditions as non-neutral (systemic inequities due to racist policies) and by  whom (who contributed to the current conditions).

Codes were ascribed to utterances so that we could tell how many students in a group explicitly expressed a particular idea (brief statements of agreement th at did not articulate the point of agreement, e.g.,  “I agree with her” did not get coded). We were particularly interested in identifying the variation and range of ideas within a group rather than how many students in a group subs cribed to a particular idea). Coding thus allowed u s to determine the prevalence of ideas across groups. In  the next section  we illustrate the themes that emerged from this analysis in relation to our research questions. a) To what extent did the unit support students i n developing non-deterministic views of genes? and b) to what extent did the unit support sociopolitical awareness of the nonneutral nature of the environments in gene-environment interactions?

## Results and discussion

Countering genetic determinism: Students’ views of gene-environment interactions While the genetics unit engaged students with the m ore traditionally studied Mendelian inheritance pat terns, we did emphasize that most traits do not have a binary phenotype as suggested by Mendeliandominant and recessive terminology (e.g., attached versus detached earlobe s or having sickle cell trait versus not having it). We also engaged students with explorations of the molecular  basis of diabetes (and some other traits) to illus trate the complex interactions between several gene products (proteins) in the production of a phenotype (visible trait). In the latter lessons we discussed polygenic risk scores and the idea that there are many hundreds of genetic variants that contribute to such a score and no single gene that determines trait variation. Those lessons also presented examples of how environmental stimuli such as stress can result in altering molecular interactions in cells, leading to changes at higher organization levels (tissue, o rgan, and ultimately organism). Thus, there were mu ltiple opportunities in the unit that countered a simplist ic deterministic view of gene-trait relationship towards a more complex multifactorial relation ship (multiple genes in interaction with the environment). Two of the interview questions we analyzed provided insights about how s tudents viewed the nature of genetic influence on t raits (Q1 about racial health disparities and Q2 about genes not working in a vacuum). Our analysis showed that none of the groups attribu ted racial health disparities to genetic difference s alone. In seven of the groups there was at least on e student who espoused the contributions of both ge nes and environments to traits (with a larger infl uence attributed to the environment). Only one stud ent in one of the groups attributed trait variation solely to the environment. It is important to note that even those students who saw a role for genetics in racial health disparities di d not ascribe genetic differences to races. Rather, they assumed a genetic predisposition if there is a family history of diabetes. For example, Delia explained that: “I would think it would mostly be environmental. Bu t sometimes genes does play a part in it because of what their parents have. For example, for diabetes, there can be parents that could have like type two diabetes, and then their kids can end up with type one.” An interesting finding that emerged was students ex plaining genetic variation as arising from differen t environmental conditions and the need to physiologi cally and genetically adapt to harmful conditions a s leading to inherited predisposition. For example, Elissa responded that: “It’s because in an environment, if your parent lik e gets to... adapted to that environment they're in, then most likely their bloodline is also going to have, like, the same problem. Like, that's where mostly people get diabetes from their genes because  of what the parent first did in that environment so then it kinda overlaps.” Such adaptation-type explanation involved either one or two generat ions (parents and children) or a longer evolutionary time frame in which ancestors that were used to one type of environment had genes that were maladaptive in a new environment. These responses were not frequent (five students across four groups) and seem to reflect students' attempts to reconcile the know n ‘family history’ risk factor (discussed in the un it) and the understanding that races are not genetically distinct. While not accurate, their explanation of physiological/genetic plasticity given harmful environmental conditions i s a reasonable response to this dilemma. However, s tudents generally knew that even inherited genetic predisposition is not a prescription for diabetes, as noted by Aaron: “So like, if you have diabetes, and like I'm your dad, we're kind of going to live the same lifestyle, which can– which is going to increase your chance of, like, ge tting diabetes. But if that person does change their lifestyle, I believe that it will be different.” Overall, based on this data, students understood that one’s phenotype (expressed traits) depends on both genes and the environment—a non-deterministic view of genetic traits. We next analyzed how students conceived of this interaction.

To address that aspect of the research question we turn to the analysis of the second question: what is meant by the statement genes do not work in a vacuu m. Generally, students struggled to explain this st atement but in all but one interview there was an agreement that this means the environment plays a large role in how we turn out and what traits  are expressed (i.e., it is not only genes). In that  one interview students offered an evolutionary adaptation explanation similar to what  we described in the prior section. Two groups, both in the 11th grade class, suggested that there is some reaction between the genes and environment, that the environ ment changes the genes somehow, or that it activates the  gene. Their conceptualization indicated that genes  would behave differently in different environments. As examples, consider Devon and Aaron’s explanations: Devon:  I think what he's referring to is that genes– that they only react when they're met with something environmental, and that the environmental factors and the genes together umm, give a reaction like diabetes or mayb e preventing diabetes, or something like that. Aaron:  So if you put your genes, let's say, through this t ype of environment, well this is what's going to happen. Or this type of environm ent, this is what’s going to happen. So, depending on which kind of path you tak e, let's call it healthy or unhealthy, is what your genes are gonna react.

Thus, students did not fully understand how exactly the environment “gets under our skin,” but they did seem to understand that different environments do interact with genes and thus lead to different health outcomes. Fostering sociopolitical awareness: Students’ views of the nature of environments The unit offered multiple opportunities for students to learn about the ways in which environments impact health as they explored correlations between social determ inants such as income and diabetes through maps of New Jersey that illuminated these relatio nships. Students also explored the relationship bet ween various risk factors for diabetes and environments (e.g., availability a nd cost of nutritious food compared to processed fo ods or the geographic correlation between activity level and d iabetes). To better understand their own community’ s risk factors and environments, the students developed, administered, and analyzed a survey for community members. Analysis of the survey responses revealed important  information about community members’ food budget a nd diets, health insurance status, prevalence of diabetes, and activity level, along with demographic data. In order to explain some of the observed differences in income and racial segregation on the maps we also shared a  video clip from the series Glad You  Asked about redlining and the ongoing inequitable o utcomes of that policy. Our aim was to help students understand three important  aspects about the environment as a key component o f the gene-environment equation: a) the environment is non-neutral, b) it is not benign and can be harmful to people’s health, and c) existing inequities are not happenstance but rather are a result of intentional systemic racism. We present data from the first question about racia l health disparities to illustrate students’ views of the environment and its relationship to different healt h outcomes for different communities. In response t o this question, all students noted environmental differen ces as contributing to health disparities and could  point to specific differences such as lo wer income neighborhood with less access to nutriti ous food, as noted in Enrique and Claudio’s responses: Enrique:  Yeah, so like, as I know we saw that in the maps, t hey showed, like, typically for the Black people living in communities that had  lower income and that can really affect them, not just on a stress level, but also, like their actual food, like the food that they eat.

Claudio:  My answer to that question would be that it mostly due to environmental reasons, because, in like– in America– like in the whole planet, the case that it’s a fact that Latinos and African Americans, we are m ostly…[pause] When it comes to environment, we live in worse – how you call it? in worse… [Interviewer: conditions] in worse conditions. And we have, like less, less people, less Latinos and African Americans have,  how you call it? like insurance, health insurance. Students readily pointed to aspects of people’s lif estyles that were affected by inequities and could lead to diabetes. Multiple students across seven of the groups noted income disparities, multiple students across five of the groups noted stress, students in three groups mentioned disparities in health insurance, and multiple students across seven groups mentioned dietary choices and disparities associated with finding healthy food. Thus, students were, unsurprisingly, aware of their communities’ s truggles and injustices as related to  inequity across environments experienced by marginalized people (i.e., non-neutral and non-benign nature of the environment). We present data from the third interview question in which students were asked to explain the statement “environments are structured to create inequity” in the video they viewed. We wanted to see whether, and in what ways, students saw the environmental injustices the y were aware of as happening by chance or as happen ing by design as a result of white supremacy and institutional racism. Students in seven of the eight groups claimed that the inequities in environments were purposeful and that minoritized people were forced into these harm ful environments. Multiple students in two groups also acknowledged that people tend to live in areas with  others like them (segregation by affinity) but both groups  also claimed that segregation was forced and that it was both due to force and affinity that we have segregated neighborhoods. Students claimed segregation as occurring along racial and/or economic lines. The responses from Cesar and Devon illustrate the notion of forced segregation: Cesar:  L ike I think it's – that clip is trying to say that, like, maybe like, some racial groups are like, forced into these local areas or l ike, they're forced into like certain environments which end up affecting their health in some sort of way. Overall, students readily understood and had experi ence with the environment as non-neutral as evidenced by their discussion of inequities prevalent in the environments of marginalized communities and largely missing in the environments of affluent white commu nities. They also understood the harmful nature of these inequitable environments and their impact on health due to increased stress, fewer material resources, higher costs of nutritious food, less access to healthcare and i nsurance coverage. A substan tial number of students saw these inequities as intentional and were able to reason, to some extent, about the structural and institutio nalized nature of racism and racist policies. This reasoning was c ircumscribed given their limited knowledge of histo ry. While some of the students did learn about redlining in t heir social studies class, not all the students (ev en in the 11 th grade class) knew that these policies were largely based on race.

## Conclusion and implications

Engagement with race, racism, and racial disparitie s is rare in science classrooms given the prevailing and problematic perception that science and science education are (and should be) a-political (Morales-Doyle et al., 2020). However, if we want to create a more just and equ itable society it is imperative that we support stu dents in developing critical consciousness (Freire, 1970)  within and across academic disciplines. Heeding th e calls to take a political turn in science education (Morales-Doyle et al., 2020), we developed, in collaboration with expert teachers, a genetics unit that brings together a fo cus on required three-dimensional learning in genetics (NGSS, 2013), weaving in a  focus on the social, historical, and political dime nsions of genetic phenomena. This unit explicitly addresses the complexity of genetic phenomena in order to counter deterministic views. It also brings a sociopolitical lens to understanding societal structures  and forces that shape our environments and consequently how our genes might function in these environments and lead to profound health disparities. The specific genetic phenomenon we chose for the unit —diabetes—was selected due to its relevance to the community the schools served. While students were not familiar with the p hysiological and genetic mechanisms of diabetes, th ey were familiar (to varying extents) with the disease. The y also had ample lived experiences and knowledge ab out environmental factors —diet, stress, family history, and activity levels —that are known r isk factors for this condition. Collecting survey data from their community furth er enriched their knowledge of these factors as related to the larger community. In this sense, the unit not only focused on science content knowledge, but invited students’ RP into the classroom through ‘making present practices’ that included: a) a unit focus that was relevant to their community (i.e., diabetes), b) lessons which integrated sociopolitical dimensions of the phenomenon (diabetes) that ‘made present’ systemic injustices as they shaped o ur physiology (i.e., gene expression) and c) curricular structures (the community survey) that desettled epistemic hierarchies by inviting and valuing students’ and their community’s knowledge about both the science of diabetes and systemic inequities (as it related to health care access, for example). Furthermore, as we illustrated in our findings, students not only developed a sens e of multifactorial genetics, but were able to connect their learning with a nuanced understanding of systemic inequity structured into the non-neutral environments within which we live. Our implementation of the unit in two classrooms pr ovided encouraging results as well as highlighted shortcomings of the design (that we will be address ing in our revision of the unit). First, overall students understood that both genes and the environments con tribute to traits and trait variation. Second, they  understood that these contributions are due to an interaction between genes and the environment. That is, that the environment impacts gene function and therefore the same gene c ould lead to different outcomes in different environments. This is a fairly sophisticated idea that is not tri vial for even college level students to understand (Haskel-Ittah et al., 2020). Third, students understood the environment, in gene-environment interactions, as non-neutral and not benign. They could readily describe differences in the physical and social environments experienced by different racial and socioeconomic groups and the harmful imp act of some of these environments on people’s menta l and physical health. Four th, students generally viewed environmental inequit ies as intentional in the sense that minoritized communities were forced to live in harmful environments. There were two areas in which students struggled to  provide more nuanced explanations. First, while they understood the idea that genes and the environ ment interact, they struggled to explain how this h appened at the biological level, meaning how the environment “gets under our skin.” As noted earlier, such explanations are complex and difficult even for older students (Haskel-Ittah et al., 2020; Todd & Romine, 2018) because they require understanding mechanisms of gene expression and regulation or the intricate epigenetic modifications (the latter are not part of the science standards expectations for high school). In the unit we did provide a few examples of gene-environment interactions, but it seems they were insufficient to engender a r obust understanding that would allow students to reason about them. In diabe tes, the mechanisms of gene-environment interactions are highly complex, and some are not fully understood in the scientific community (Cornelis & Hu, 2012; Kido, 2016; Cornelis & Hu, 2012). Thus, we elected not to delve  into diabetes-specific mechanisms within the unit. This is a decision we likely need to reconsider, and we argue  that fostering deeper understandings of the mechan isms by which the environment influences trait variation is  critical if we want students to understand the maj ority of genetic phenomena.

Second, while students understood environmental ine quities as not happenstance but intentional, their ability to provide examples of how policies, expect ations, or structures caused these differences was limited. Students did use redlining as a key example, but on ly a handful of students shared this example (likel y other students did not mention redlining either because t hey did not recall or fully understand it). As note d earlier, teaching about the political, especially historical social policies, is not common in science classrooms. However, understanding these policies and their ongoing rami fications can help students to both identify the so urce of the problem and to understand that the current status q uo is not inevitable but can be changed, as it was created, through social polic y. This study builds on efforts in the Learning Scienc es to develop science teaching and learning experiences that equip students with the t ools and resources to change our worlds for the bet ter. Doing so—that is, enacting the consequentiality aspect of critical science learning—necessitates that students and adults develop political clarity around inequity  (McKinney de Royston & Sengupta-Irving, 2019), work together as sociopolitical allies in changing systems that repr oduce harm (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020), and as we argue in this study, afford students opportunities to lea rn science concepts as entangled with nuanced under standing of inequity structured into our environments.  This can provide a target for action and a sense of  critical hope (Duncan-Andrade, 2009). We argue, then, that we need to find ways to incorporate the sociopolitical, including relevant histories and policies that impact our bod ies, our minds, and our well-being. Separating the social and political from the biological is a disservice to students learning biology in secondary education and beyond.
