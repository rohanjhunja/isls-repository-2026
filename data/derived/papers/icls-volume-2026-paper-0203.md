# Dilemmas of Customization: Navigating Goals and Guidance

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Curricular customization is one means for teachers to achieve their own equity and pedagogical goals by tailoring the curricula to address a range of needs. However, teachers must balance potential contradictions between the goals and guidance of the curri culum and their own goals and understandings of students’ needs. Using data from a professional learning community of science teachers customizing curriculum for equitable sensemaking, we investigate how the dilemmas they face, the reasoning they employ, and their resulting customizations are shaped by these contradictions. We use these findings to highlight the complexities that underlie curricular customization, particularly when the goals and experiences that teachers bring to the classroom are at apparent odds with the guidance they receive in curricula. We intend to show that teachers do not enact their equity goals in a vacuum. Rather, they are shaped by the context within which they teach and the curricula.

Conventional views of teaching assume that teachers simply implement prescribed teaching practices in alignment with curricular materials and district directives. In actuality, teachers modify curricula in myriad ways, as influenced by their beliefs, experiences, and preferences (Kazemi & Hubbard, 2008). This results in an inevitable gap between how designers intend for teachers to use curricula and how teachers actually enact it (Penuel et al., 2014). This gap has frequently been problematized (Penuel et al., 2014), where focus is placed on how well teachers’ enactment aligns with curricular goals and principles. However, recent shifts in the literature focus on teachers’ modifications of curriculum as an asset for student and teacher learning (DeBarger et al., 2016). Curricular customization involves teachers tailoring existing curricula in order to achieve their pedagogical goals (McNeill et al., 2026). This requires balancing the goals of standards-based curricula with their goals. This case study shows t eachers grappling with customization dilemmas, where teachers must reason through multiple reasonable pathways (Kavanagh et al., 2020) in ways that are productive for student learning. In navigating dilemmas during curricular customization, teachers use pedagogical reasoning to ensure their goals are reflected in their customizations. Pedagogical reasoning is the unseen cognitive activity through which teachers “attach their actions to the purposes that undergird them” (Kavanagh et al., 2020, p. 3). Pedagogical reasoning is used in response to dilemmas that emerge at the intersection of tensions between the teacher, their students, the curriculum, and the context within which they are teaching (Kavanagh et al., 2020). In customizing, though teachers may not always arrive at a ‘correct’ solution to these dilemmas (Lampert, 1985), they use their pedagogical reasoning to respond in ways that reflect negotiations between their own goals, the goals of the curriculum, and their students.

In this paper, we present a case study of science teachers customizing for equitable sensemaking to show the pedagogical reasoning they employ during customization. We show that these dilemmas emerge as they grapple with how to address the potential mismatch between the guidance of the curriculum and their own equity commitments and understandings of students' interests and questions. Teachers engage in pedagogical reasoning in order to move through these dilemmas, shaping their final customizations. We do so in order to highlight the complexities of customizing curricula for equity, particularly when teachers’ own equity goals and their understanding of students’ interests and questions do not immediately align with the goals of and guidance from curricular materials. We see this as having broader implications for curricular design, particularly in designing with the intention of supporting teacher customization.

## Context

The data presented in this paper follows three eighth-grade Midwestern science teachers, Sandy, Erin and Michael, as they participate in a series of optional researcher-organized professional learning community (PLC) meetings. In PLC meetings, teachers focused on customizing the district-mandated curriculum, OpenSciEd, to increase equitable sensemaking opportunities. Equitable sensemaking was initially framed by the district science 1497 coordinator as helping students feel comfortable sharing their ideas out  loud, feel their ideas matter, and see the curriculum as connected to their lives.

Sandy, who has over 20 years of science teaching experience, self-selected to lead all PLC meetings, after participating in researcher-led meetings on the same topic the previous school year. She received researcher support in planning meetings and drawing  on outside resources, but led and recorded meetings independently. The PLC meetings were organized around a curricular customization cycle, which involved creating equitable sensemaking goals, analyzing curriculum and planning customization, implementing customizations, and reflecting (McNeill, 2026). In the first PLC meeting of the school year, the teachers revisited their previous ideas of equitable sensemaking, narrowing their goal for the year to increasing relevance. The customization presented in this paper was situated towards the end of the eighth-grade genetics unit, designed around the genetic factors that influence the heavily muscled phenotype in cows.

Data includes video recordings of seven PLC meetings, around 40 minutes each, artifacts from PLC meetings, and roughly one hour of follow-up interviews per teacher. Additional data includes audio from a 30-minute interview with one of the curriculum writers of the unit. All participants are referred to by pseudonyms. Researchers transcribed videos of PLC meetings and interviews, creating a structured memo template to analyze each recording. Initial memo templates included guiding questions about interactions with curricular resources, the nature of customizations, and the learning that occurred around their own teaching, customization, and the curriculum. We began by individually viewing the transcript and video of the first two PLC meetings and filling out structured memos. We then revised the initial memos together for consistency and updated the guiding questions to better align with the research questions. We then completed structured memos for the remaining PLC meetings independently. These structured memos were used to identify recurring themes throughout the customization process.

Here, we present an illustrative case (Hayes et al., 2015) of the dilemmas that these teachers faced when customizing for increased relevance, defined as honoring students’ interests. We used thematic coding (Miles et al., 2014) to understand the specific dilemmas that emerged in customizing for increased relevance, the pedagogical reasoning teachers employ in response, and how they resolved the dilemmas. This case study presents the dilemmas that emerged when science teachers customized for increased relevance. We present these dilemmas, and the reasoning that teachers did, to show that teachers must work to interpret the guidance of curricular materials w hile navigating differences between their own equity and pedagogical goals, students’ interests and questions, and the goals and guidance of the curriculum. After setting their equitable sensemaking goal as increasing relevance to students, Sandy, Erin, and Michael sought to respond to students’ questions and interests, which they described as interest in humans and their own genetics. This becomes the conflic t at the heart of the dilemmas the teachers face throughout customization – how do they honor students’ desire to learn about human genetics while following curricular guidance that expressly recommends avoiding it.

In pursuit of increasing relevance, Sandy, Erin, and Michael add a lesson to the middle of the unit centered on the genetic inheritance of the Fugate family of Kentucky. The Fugate family were carriers of a recessive genetic blood disorder that turned their skin when the gene was expressed (Cawein et al., 1964). The teachers’ customization shows the work they did to interpret the guidance from curricular materials and resolve the manifold design and pedagogical dilemmas that emerged as they sought to increa se relevance. Dilemma 1: Balancing relevance while avoiding students’ personal genetic histories The first dilemma was how to be relevant and interesting to their students without drawing on students’ own genetic histories. Students expressed interest in their own genetics, but the curriculum explicitly recommends against it, with little explanation as to why (Figure 1). Teachers had to balance their goal of increasing relevance with avoiding “explor[ing] inheritance with their [students’] families” (Housman & McGill, 2022; p. 43; Figure 1). The curriculum includes specific guidance on why animal phenomena were chosen over human phenomena, noting the potential for students to believe in genetic determinism and essentialism, as well as guidance on being inclusive for students with unknown genetic backgrounds (Housman & McGill, 2022). Present throughout t he Teacher Edition for the genetics unit (Figure 1), this guidance focuses on what to not do, rather than what to do. In striking a balance between being relevant and interesting to students without drawing on students’ own genetic histories, Sandy says that they would need to “bring in a human element to it, because the kids have been asking for that” while also being “not, like, specific to them, because that was part of why, what we were 1498 avoiding with this curriculum was the whole like, why am I, like, my parents kind of thing, questions”. They needed to find a way for their customization to be “human, but not specific or personal to [the students].” This emerges as a primary dilemma: how do you pick a phenomenon that is relevant and interesting enough to all students without using their own genetic inheritance as examples? Figure 1 8.5 Teacher Edition: Attending to Equity & Working toward more Powerful and Equitable Genetics Education To resolve this, they choose the Fugate family as a human phenomenon that is relevant enough but not from their students’ genetic history. The Fugate family is both spatially and temporally removed from them. In a PLC meeting, Erin says that the phenomena is “good because it then introduces a human component, but not personal, like we don't have any blue people in [Midwestern town].” Sandy echoes this, saying that the phenomena is “just kind of a distant, happened in the past, example of people in a different location from them, of how these genetics were passed on.” She describes the Fugate family as “a very neutral, external example… there wasn't going to be any kind of, like, overt discrimination, or, you know, like, within our community”. To them, the phenomena is relevant enough but also removed enough to avoid going against the curriculum. By adding a lesson on a human phenomenon, the teachers respond to this dilemma in a way that aligns with their relevance goal. By aiming for distance, they respond in a way that also aligns with the curriculum’s guidance: avoiding students’ genetic histories. Though attempts to make science content spatially and temporally distant depoliticizes and dehistoricizes science content, not aligning with discourses of equity that acknowledge science content as inherently non-neutral and non-benign (Philip & Azevedo, 2017), this concern is not reflected in the curricular materials. The curricular materials provide little explanation for the why behind their guidance, instead avoiding topics to remain equitable. Thus, teachers reasoning through this dilemma shows a balancing of their own equity goals and the curriculum’s goals.

Dilemma 2: Balancing relevance with mitigating extrapolations The teachers must increase relevance while interpreting and accounting for the curriculum’s warning of students’ potential extrapolations of simplified human genetics toward racism and genetic essentialism and determinism (Figure 1). The teachers grapple with student’s interests in human phenomena and its apparent misalignment with the guidance of the curriculum on why the chosen phenomenon is not about humans. Sandy says that “a lot of the kids have been really interested in doing human related things, although I told them, like, we can't really focus on humans and gave them the spiel that the OpenSciEd people gave us, you know, but I'm wondering if it's like, far removed from us?”. The teachers are working to understand the curriculum’s guidance but are still committed to their own equity goals. Sandy’s reference to the ‘spiel’ from the curriculum and wondering about whether being ‘far removed from us’ addresses  the concern, speaks to the interpretive work that the teachers must do. In referring to the ‘spiel’, Sandy communicates that she is familiar with the content of the curriculum’s guidance, going as far to recite it to her students, but also distances herself from it, implying that she isn’t taking the guidance up as the be-all and end-all about what she will teach. Sandy turns toward distance to resolve this, interpreting the underlying point behind the guidance, and whether it can be upheld without strictly avoiding human phenomena. As described above, they settle on a phenomenon that is spatially and temporally rem oved from students but  are still concerned about students’ interpreting the Fugate family phenomenon. Sandy warned Michael and Erin that they would “get a lot of the comments, like, the hillbillies and backwoods and incest, and all that kind of stuff, because that's just where people go” and that “there might be some hint of, like, incestuous relationships, and I want to try and avoid that as much 1499 as possible, because the kids are going to, like, just latch on to that.” They reason through the particular prejudices that the Fugate family phenomena may incite in their students as their next step in the process. Rather than ignoring this concern or abandoning their relevance goal, the teachers add supports the lesson to mitigate this outcome. They add supports to “dismiss the idea of this, like, inbreeding as the reason for this mutation, and swing it back to like it was a genetic thing that just kept getting passed on”, making “a point to ask, like, so why do you think that these people had this blue skin? Was it because they were marrying their cousins?” This was their solution to prevent potentially harmful outc omes from the Fugate family phenomenon while honoring students’ interests and following the guidance from the curricular materials. Their resolution to this dilemma reflects their commitment to increasing relevance and to the guidance of the curriculum. They reason through the possible outcomes of honoring students’ interests, acknowledging the guidance on why the lesson is not built around a human phenomenon  in the first place. Rather than taking the guidance as the definitive decision maker, the teachers engaged in pedagogical reasoning to balance the intention behind the guidance with their own relevance goals. Having to interpret the intention behind the curricular materials in the first place seems to be a result of the material’s reliance on avoidance as a way of dealing with essentialist views, rather than providing teachers with a more nuanced description of what they intend to avoid and why or providing other ways for teachers to convey the content. Because the curriculum takes a stance of avoidance, the teachers must interpret the intention behind the curricular materials to integrate it with students’ interests. Still, they rea soned through this dilemma to honor students’ interests, with added supports to mitigate the possible effects.

## Implications

In presenting these dilemmas, we intended to show that teachers use pedagogical reasoning to resolve customization dilemmas, shaping their customizations. These dilemmas emerged as teachers interpret the curriculum’s guidance, which in this case takes an avoidance approach. Thus, teachers are faced with a mismatch between students’ interests and the guidance built into the curriculum. At first glance, the teachers’ added lesson on the Fugate family may not seem more equitable than the original lesson, instead playing into genetic essentialism and determinism and relying on spatial and temporal distance. However, the teachers made considered attempts to interpret the goals that undergird the curriculum’s guidance. They made well-reasoned decisions in response to each dilemma that emerged and reasoned through the intricacies of following the intent but not the letter of curricular materials, while honoring students’ interests and questions. We see this case as contributing to our understanding of the complexities that underlie curricular customization, particularly in customizing for equity, in two ways. First, dilemmas arise when the goals and experiences of teachers seem to be at odds with the goals of standards and curricula. The pedagogical reasoning they use is multidimensional and often emerges as they grapple with mismatches across the curriculum and their own goals and experiences. Though it is possible to view the final customization through a deficit lens, viewing it through the series of dilemmas that they grappled with, and the pedagogical reasoning they employed, pushes us to see how it came to be within a larger decision space. Second, complexities arise in customization when teachers have to reason through and interpret the intent behind curriculum’s guidance, particularly when materials advise against intuitive ways of teaching, not providing actionable alternatives. Here, the curricular materials note that students are likely to show interest in human genetics and bring in their own relevant examples. However, no actionable solution is provided for teachers about avoiding humans while noting that students will be interested in humans and their own genetics. By not resolving this discrepancy, teachers are left to resolve it on their own, meaning they must weigh their own goals and students’ interests against the direct guidance from curricular materials. This case speaks more broadly to the intricate work that teachers do in wrestling with misalignment between top-down guidance on teaching and their own goals and experience and students’ interests. Teachers do not enact their equity goals or respond to students’ interests in a vacuum – they do so in ways that are shaped by the context within which they teach and the larger education system within which materials are filtered down. Curricular materials avoiding certain phenomena as a strategy means that teachers must reason through the intent behind the materials, working to integrate the guidance with their own knowledge of teaching and the classroom. The guidance in the curricular materials (Figure 1) makes it clear that curricular designers are aware of the complicated sociohistorical dynamics that are at play within science content and practices, and how this gets conveyed to students. However, avoiding these topics leaves teacher s to have to make sacrifices between making their teaching relevant and following the letter of the guidance.
