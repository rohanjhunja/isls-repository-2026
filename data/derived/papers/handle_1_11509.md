# Developing a Theory of Conceptualizations of Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This conceptual paper presents a theory viewing conceptualizations of learning as complex systems. Synthesizing design experience, analyses (legislation, research, interviews), and theories (metaphor, frame, complex systems), it posits conceptualizations comprise interacting, self-organizing elements (interdependent ideas) generating emergent practices. These systems seek equilibrium but allow transformation through strategic leveraging of core ideas. This framework aims to understand and shift how learning is perceived and enacted.

## Introduction and background

As a learning scientist, I became increasingly convinced that a set of fundamental questions is crucial: What do people think learning is?  and How do different frames of learning relate to practices?  This conceptual  paper describes the development of a theory of conceptualizations, a theory of distributed and culturally, historically, linguistically, and contextually situated complex dynamic conceptual systems (Delahunty & Kimbell, 2021) of large numbers of interdependent ideas that together form a conceptualization. In one early study, I analyzed metaphors in federal legislation documents (Donaldson, 2017), and in another I collected audio clips of 200 people in the general public talking about the nature of learning (Donaldson 2018). After collecting and coding data in the  conceptual metaphor studies  with simple frequency analysis resulting in  findings supporting arguments such as those by Frei re (1970/2005) that the dominant “banking” conceptualization of learning involved metaphors of  acquisition, accumulation, and transmission, I became dissatisfied at the oversimplification inherent in frequency analysis. I also realized that conceptual metaphor theory alone was insufficient for explaining the complexity of what I was seeing in the data, especially when I saw that large portions of the data remained uncoded because they did not contain any metaphors. Inspired by the critical pedagogy work of Kincheloe (2007) and Giro ux (2013), I wanted to find a way to analyze data n ot only for conceptual metaphors, but also for experiences,  worldviews, and paradigms.  In a subsequent study of 315 articles from educational research journals, I coded the data for metaphors, worldviews, paradigms, and practices and then used code co-occurrence analysis to identify patterns (Donaldson & Hammrich, 2018). That study found the acquisition and transmission frames of learning  were dominant even in educational research. More importantly, it pointed clearly to relationships between the dominant frames and disempowerment of learners. My findings from coding co-occurrence analysis were encouraging, so in a desir e to visualize these co-occurrences in more expansive ways, I was drawn tonetwork analysis. Network analysis is a key complex systems methodology (Dan & Shunkun, 2022), although I was unaware of complex systems theory at  first. In a study of 211 articles from the Journal of Educational Psycho logy (JEP) and 78 articles from the International Journal of Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning  (ijCSCL), I found that a transfer/acquisition conceptualization of learning was dominant in JEP, but a construction conceptualization was dominant in ijCSCL (Donaldson, 2019a). During my most important study —an analysis of in-depth interviews with 10 leading learning scientists and 10 STEM educators in higher education —I was led to the insight that  conceptualizations of learning are complex systems entailing numerous elements, including not just metaphors, but also non-metaphorical characterizations, stories, value statements, and paradigmatic stances, and that conceptualizations of learning are inextricably linked with marginalization, oppression, and epistemic vio lence (Donaldson, 2019b; Donaldson, 2020a; Donaldson, 2020b). This study was my first to use Girvan-Newman cluster analysis (Girvan & Newman, 2002) as a means of identifying and describing self-organization of ideas into distinct conceptualizations.  Through further studies, I refined and formalize a theory of conceptualizations of learning (Donaldson & Allen-Handy, 2020) and grounded it as a sub-theory within the context of complexity science (Donaldson & Allen-Handy, 2023).

## Theoretical framework: Complex systems theory

The current formulation of the theory is situated within a complex systems ontological and epistemological stance (Kaplan & Garner, 2020) suggesting that conceptualizations exist within com munities. The core principles are described below, but more details will be published elsewhere (Donaldson, 2025). According to this theory, conceptualizations constitute complex systems in whi ch interacting elements self-organize. These elements are not isolated in individual min ds but are contextually, culturally, linguistically, and socially distributed. Individuals participate t o varying extents in these complex conceptual syste ms within their own situated activity systems. Complex conceptual systems possess self-organizing emergent properties in the form of linguistic, action, perceptual, and valuation potentials. The primary focus of my own research efforts has been on action potentials. These emergent prope rties produce conditions for emergent phenomena to arise. Complex conceptual systems give rise to various emergent phenomena. Most importantly, emergent practices are guided by action potentials. My research suggests complex conceptual systems may have attractor states, that these attractor states are conceptualizations, and that complex conceptual systems tend toward s elf-organized equilibrium around these attractor states (conceptu alizations). Complex conceptual systems in equilibr ium have particular ideas playing a uniquely crucial role in  connecting other ideas: these ideas can be framed as leverage points. I am currently investigating analytical techniques for identifying  leverage points, but network analyses that include betweenness centrality measures seem m ost promising because they pinpoint ideas with high er structural importance in terms of serving as the “g lue” that connects other ideas. Leverage points in problematic conceptualizations can be disruption focal points t hrough questioning assumptions. Leverage points in empowering conceptualizations can be generative foc al points to pull complex conceptual systems toward  new attractor states. Changing conceptualizations requi res well-designed data-informed change processes. The first step in changing a complex conceptual system may be  to identify leverage points in both problematic and empowering conceptualizations. Second, working with  leverage points entails applying pressure to compl ex systems, which must be maintained o r the system will revert to its initial attractor s tate. Third, the pressure must be sustained for a long period of time. During this  prolonged strategic pressure, we may not observe c hange because the system remains anchored to the initial attractor state. Eventually, self-organized criticality (a tipping point) will be reached and the system will undergo a phase transition, reorganizing around the new attractor state (a new conceptualization).

## Implications and future directions

My work on complex conceptual systems theory was motiv ated by a desire to design learning environments and experiences through which to facilitate learner cri tical consciousness, epistemic agency, and authorsh ip of self and world. This necessitates transforming conceptualizations because I am engaging learners in practices that may not naturally be emergent from their current conceptual systems. Due to equilibrium tendencies, certain complex systems are resilient to change efforts. Complex conceptual systems may have this nature, necessitating long-term design-based studies. I have been operating under this tentative assumption. If problematic practices are emergent from a dominant conceptualization, efforts to repla ce them with empowering practices will fail unless we first disrupt problematic conceptualizations and transfor m the complex conceptual system toward more empowering attractor states. When a conceptualization becomes widespread and “common sense,” emergent practices become reified as traditions and policies (Berger & Luckma nn, 1967). Practices emergent from a different conceptualization will be seen as irrelevant or even danger ous (Kincheloe, 2007). Although this proposition remains tentative, I believe that practices for powerful learning and ju stice cannot emerge within contexts where problematic conceptualizations are dominant. They c an only emerge after radically disrupting those conceptualizations. Understanding complex conceptual systems is crucial  to address problematic conceptualizations and emergent practices leading to injustice. However, conceptualizations a re one type of complex system within a larger ecosystem of many interdependent complex systems (Lemke & Sabelli, 20 08). This conceptual paper presents the  proposed starting point for development of a comple x conceptual systems theory. A lot more research is needed to refine and revise it, and potentially refute the fundamental assumpti ons and principles. Furthermore, the only analytical me thods our studies have utilized involve various for ms of network analysis, and innovative uses of other analytical methods will be needed.
