# Making in the Magic Valley: Historical Actors and the Making of Speculative Props

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, I explore how Latinx participants in a making-based activity create speculative props that represent a combination of their histories, experiences, and desired futures. I do this in the context of a making space within a history museum whose focus is on telling stories of a largely Latinx region in the U.S., the Lower Rio Grande Valley. I position participants’ work as materializing a double bind, one of the indicators of becoming a historical actor. Specifically, participants are creating speculative props, which are tools for imagination and enacting futures in the present. I present three examples of speculative props and how they bring together pasts, presents, and futures. From this work, I argue that we candesign making-based activities that facilitate reflection towards imagining desired futures.

## Background

The two major concepts I draw on in this paper are learners as historical actors (Gutiérrez et al., 20 19) and speculative design (Arada et al., 2023; Dunne & Rab y, 2013; Wargo & Alvarado, 2020). I discuss these i n the context of a making space (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2018) where participants are invited to craft representations of memories, futures, and current experiences in and around the Valley.

## Conceptual framework: Historical actors as speculative designers

The process of becoming a historical actor is relev ant for how learners, especially those from margina lized backgrounds, come to “develop the agency to become designers of their own social futures as they engag e and develop historicized and collective sensibilities and a commitment to a larger social good” (Gutiérrez et al., 2019, p. 293). Gutiérrez et al. (2019) have outlined four  indicators of becoming a historical actor, meant t o illustrate how it is that people come to develop this agency across mome nts in their lives (Gutiérrez et al., 2019). In this paper, I specifically focus on how learners’ work i s a materialization of a double bind, referring to how learners problem-solve and/or take action around “dilemmas, contradi ctions, and conflicts” towards “collectively enhancing their life circumstances” (Gutiérrez et a l., 2019, p. 293). In this double bind, learners ar e at once recognizing that a circumstance or dominant narrative is oppres sive in some way, and begin imagining what it might mean to be otherwise (Gutiérrez et al., 2019). I build on this idea of historical actors as “des igners of their own social futures” by incorporating tools from spe culative design in the discussion of how the double  bind is experienced as learners’ make and describe their ar tifact. Specifically, I look at the creation of spe culative props (Dunne & Raby, 2013), and further consider how arti facts can be created that represent and enact desir ed social futures. In this way, speculative props can be considered as tertiary artifacts (Cole, 2019; Gutiérrez & Vossoughi, 2010). In bringing these lenses together, my aim is  to ground the making space, the makers/learners, a nd the artifacts that were made within the work of “building new worlds” (Garcia & Mirra, 2023, p. 12). Making speculative props In this paper, I focus on speculative prop making a s an approach to speculative design (Dunne & Raby, 2013). Speculative props’ purpose is to “facilitate imagining;” in “facilitate” we see that props have an active role in the process of “imagining” (Dunne & Raby, 2013, p. 92).  Yet, the role of a speculative prop is to be part of enacting future worlds, where we are invited to “entertain n ew ideas, thoughts, and possibilities for an altern ative world from the one we and the prop coexist in” (Dunne & Raby, 2013, p. 92). Thus, the use of speculative props is more for play or make-believe, rather than a stage production; there is n o requirement, then, that the props must be polished or in a ‘final’ state (Dunne & Raby, 2013). Speculative props only require that viewers “must  become active ‘imaginers’” who are also part of the prefigurat ive activity of the space (Dunne & Raby, 2013). Thi s articulation of a speculative prop is in line with Cole’s (2019) consideration of tertiary artifacts, which play a role in “guiding and informing human imagination” (Cole, 2019, p. 320). Making, the “creative production of artifacts [...] through analog and digital practices,” is the frame I use for learners’ work in the CVB (Halverson & Peppler,  2018, p. 285). Thus, the CVB is a  making space, a term I use to address the larger culture in which the maki ng is taking place; in my case the history museum (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2018). I would posit there is a particular immediacy to creating future-oriented artifacts in what is ostensibly a past-oriented space, as a history museum, that creates a  generative site for considering learners’ speculative work. On this point, I also bring in the speculative potential of making (Wargo & Alvarado, 2020), as a context where play, imagination, and creativity a re at the forefront of the space.  Finally, I posit ion this work within the tradition of attention to culture and power within making, namely how there is a need to conceptualize toward what ends we are working towards in creating these spaces (Vossoughi & Vakil, 2018).

## Research design

At the core of the research design were the Community Vision Board (CVB) stations, where people at the museum could participate in the activity. ## Methodology and data generation

This study was designed under the paradigm of desig n ethnography (Pink et al., 2022), which holds that  we as researchers have a “responsibility and commitment t o gaining deep and theoretically coherent understandings of how possible futures might be imagined, anticipated  and comprehended from the sensory, embodied and fe lt moments of the everyday and how these unfolding fut ures are formed” (Pink et al., 2022, p. 11). Design ethnography also makes explicit that “learning is understood phenomenologically, as part of an experiential world of everyday meaning and action” and takes seriously  “elements of [participants’] everyday world as rel evant to conceptualize and study” (Pink et al., 2022, p. 8). Taken together, this methodology allows for ethnographic ways of knowing while also recognizing that the work is “ interventional activity which does not stop at pres cribed deliverables, but which moves forward towards unkno wn futures” (Pink et al., 2022, p. 3). Concretely, this approach allowed me to generate insights from all parts of the intervention, such as the interactions that take place and the way people use materials, rather than just the outcomes that come from the intervention. Thus, my role in the actual space of the CVB can best be described as a participant-observer, where I cofacilitated the craft area while also taking notes of how people approached the area, how they arranged themselves, and how the making proceeded (where  possible). The primary data generation activities w ere the creation of artifacts at the stations, and short semi-structured interviews with participants about their  experiences and the artifact. Interviews took place individually when the participants were ready to share about what they made. Each time, I asked participants to describe what they cr eated, and why. Then, in listening to their respons es, I asked follow up questions like why they chose the prompt,  why they made certain choices in what they mad e, and if they made anything else. If they made something els e, I would ask them the same general questions abou t the other creations. I always asked if they were fine w ith me recording their responses digitally, and som e were not. In cases where they want ed to share but did not want to be recorded, I took  notes about their responses and recorded them later in my field notes. I took reflective field notes for each event, recording times, how participants approached the space, the order in which people came and left, and other events of note. These field notes helped me keep track of how people were moving in and arou nd the space, and look at how they might have influ enced each others’ works. I took photos of the set up of the CVBs stations at the beginning, in the middle, and at the end of the events, to understand a little more about ho w the progression of work took place. I also took p hotos of the artifacts that were created in the moment, especial ly when the participant wanted to take what they ma de with them instead of leaving it on the wall.

In sum across the four events that data was generated at, 46 people made 64 unique artifacts, I conducted 39 interviews, 20 of which were recorded, ranging i n time from 30 seconds to 5 minutes. In the analysi s section below, I outline how I approached the analytic process, including the construction of the analytic memos and high level synthesis.

Analysis The analysis process started with analytic memos, f rom which I generated a set of five codes to identi fy how the artifacts responded to the CVB prompts/theme (Salda ña, 2021). These codes were: Diagram, Kinesthetic, Props, Photograph, and Written. I applied  these codes to all 64 artifacts, and in the finding s I describe a subset of the artifacts that were coded as props. Below, I provide more descriptions of each of the steps in my analysis process. The analytic memos, which were documents that contained photos of the artifacts, interview transcripts, my high level noticings about the artifacts, and th oughts on emerging patterns, were the first step in  the analytic process (Saldaña, 2021). I construct ed these memos in stages; in the first stage, I pla ced the photo of the artifact in the top left corner of the document, and to the right of the image I added either the interview transcript (if there was one available) or the field notes of the conversation. I opted for this layout based on Marin’s (2020) intentional placement of childrens’ video capture on the left o f the parents, given the left side’s prominence as “inception of a statement or entire discourse” in the West (Ochs,  1979, p. 49, as cited in  Marin, 2020). For my part, this was meant to place analytic priority on what participants made, followed by what they had to say about it. Accordingly, in the second stage, I placed my first descriptive notes below the photo and transcript. These descrip tions often include information about the artifact in my own wo rds, what I noticed about it, and any additional in formation that was not included in the transcript or field notes. I repeated stages 1 and 2 in the same document for “related” artifacts as well. By related, I mean artifacts tha t were either created by the same person or created  by people in the same group. Thus, the memos contained anywhere from 1-4 artifacts. Finally in stage three of the memos, I wrote at the top of the document my thoughts on eme rgent themes, patterns, and general noticings acros s the artifacts. In total, I wrote 10 of these analytic m emos, reflecting artifacts made at the first three events, the toplevel information of which were used to generate the codes I applied to all of the artifacts. I synthesized my top-level notes related to what mode  participants constructed their artifacts to create the five codes: Diagram, Kinesthetic, Props, Photog raph, and Written (Saldaña, 2021). My top-level notes in the memos reflected a few dimensions of the artifacts a nd the participants who created them, such  as what was represented in the artifact, how people arranged themselves as they created the artifacts, how those arrangements were salient to what was created, and what mode of representation the artifacts were created in. The c odes I generated speak to this last dimension, where I con sider how the artifacts respond to the prompt, eith er through being a written response (Written), representing a relationship between ideas/objects (Diagram), or as an invitation to interaction (Props).

For the purposes of this paper, I expand on the “pr ops” that I identified in this process. Ultimately,  I identified 12 “props” across the four events. Defin ed by the relationship between the artifact and the  viewer, typically an artifact coded as a prop wo uld not be drawn, but crafted using various materia ls, and would have an “interactive” element, where the viewer is invited to “use” the artifact. I include “interactive” and “use” here in quotes because I am taking up prop making for its imaginative potential, rather than its ability to be picked up and used, as in a stage prop.

## Findings: Speculative prop making as materializing the double bind

One way that participants responded to the CVBs was  through the creation of speculative props. Props w ere typically defined by the relationship between the a rtifact and the imagined viewer, in that they were typically interactive or invitations to imagine oneself using the prop. Typically, a prop would not  be drawn but crafted using various materials. In figures 4a,c,d, we can see examples of this from two of the events. Figure 3b, as I will describe later, was the first version of the orange  tree seen in f igure 3c. I include both of these artifacts here to discuss how this layered, iterative creation allows  for understanding these works as props that invite  interaction and imagination. Further, I argue that these props “made visible and objectified” the double bind experienced by historical actors, as they created these props in conversation with historical knowledge while also resisting linear, single-voiced accounts of how this history impacts them (Gutiérrez et al., 2019, p. 293). I n considering how youth become historical actors th rough looking at their everyday experiences, Guttierrez et al. (2019)  argue for looking within everyday moments. For articulating a double bind in particular, the everydayness of their action is an important context for their work as it shows how people from marginalized communities are constantly reckoning with inequities at different scales. Considering these works as speculative allows us to see their work in the context of becoming historical actors. That is, these works were created in part t o reflect what they learned as part of their participation in  the event, in part to respond to the prompts/questions placed around the arts and  crafts tables, and in part to project the significance of this history and work into the future. What participants created was not a passive reiteration of something they saw, but a living, textured prop wit h which we can imagine multiple relational histories bei ng played out in front of. Even so, none of these creations are larger than 9-by-6 inches (about 22-by-15 cm), and all three of these examples are constructed in two dime nsions, facts which I offer to say that these “props” do not look or ‘act’ like a prop that could be seen in a s tage production. Understanding them in this way, th en, requires a shift to imagine these constructions as mock-ups of props, sets, and backgrounds that could be u sed in an envisioned future. Speculative props “can be a fully working prototype or not. This is not the issue; its purpose is to facilitate imagining” (Dunne & Raby, 2013, p. 92). Taken together, the objective of looking at thei r work as speculative emphasizes both the “not yet here”-ness of the envisioned future, and the props as wor ks in progress to this end.

## Discussion and conclusion

By understanding the creation of the speculative props as part of the process for people becoming historical actors, I aim to link their work not only to their individu al lives but to the broader contexts in which they create. Expanding on speculative props in this way invites us to think about the worlds that these props could  exist in. While I would not argue that these props represent a unified, cohesive vision of a progressive future, they do serve as windows into the types of resistance being articulated. That is, they challenge simplifi ed, white-washed narratives of how the creation of the “Magic Valley” impacted the communities of the Rio Grande Valley before, during, and since it was coined by White businessme n in the early twentieth century (Brannst rom & Neuman, 2009). Yet, they do so by drawing on the very same or similar imagery as exists within popular advertising at the time, placing the importance of these signs, indust ries, and economies into the context of their daily  lives, as things they recognize as ubiquitous, changing, and worth sharing with future generations. At least two of the props are responding directly to the same prompt: “What is your favorite memory of nature in the Valley?” While this prompt was writte n as a way for people to reflect on their experienc es and memories, it became a way for Esther and Ce leste to suggest their desires for the future becau se of the nature of the prop itself. In Celeste’s case she uses her mem ories to talk about what the citrus festival meant to her, while creating, through her prop, the world where they we re all in it toget her. In Esther’s case, she remembers a particular part of nature, the orange tree, and creates a way for others to experience the richness of the orange tree by re-making it into a prop, manifesting a future where others can know that part of nature as she does. In creating artifacts that bring us into a world, informed by memory, the CVB context, and desires for the future, we can see that there are potentially multiple temporal standpoints from which to start creating speculative props. Considering these works as materializing a double bind troubles what it means for speculative props to reject “mimic[ing] reality or allow[ing] us to play act” (Dunne & Raby, 2013). That is, I still argue these artifacts allow us to “entertain new ideas, thoughts, and possibilities for an alternative world from the one we and the prop coexist in” in how they are taken up as imagining n ew futures by people in the process of becoming his torical actors. Thus, these props can be considered speculative because of their position as tertiary artifacts (Cole, 2019; Gutiérrez & Vossoughi, 2010) that orient us in different ways to the past, present, and future. This is an important aspect of being a historical actor, where part of b ecoming involves “invoking the past in order to re-mediate it into a resource for current and future action” (Gutiérrez et al., 2019). A limitation of this work is that in locating my wo rk singularly within STARHM, I did not follow participants across time, and therefore cannot make  arguments about how their development as historica l actors was taking place across longer time scales. Thro ugh a focus on the micro-genetic scale of creation facilitated by the CVBs, though, I argue that we can still see som e of the signs of becoming historical actors, such as materializing the double bind. Additionally, looking across artifacts created in these settings gave way to thinking about how patterns emerged in the types of response s created. Notably, the creation of speculative pro ps was an emergent response to the CVB prompts, in that participants were not told to respond in any particular way. Rather, they were given open-ended questions to start their thinking, but were invited to let it go where it would. Creating these props is one of the ways that we can see orientations to history or “the past” be shifted to the present, as being in the making. Through conversations about what they made, we can also see that while what they made may be based on memory or experience, it is also projecting futures where others share that experience together. I argue that this is prefigurative in the sense that we are being invited to play make believe in imagining what a future could look like, but in a way that is n’t necessarily “stable”—we are not being asked to live in this space, merely to take on the role of being there for the time being. Ultimately, I see this work as useful not only for the space it creates, but how what we know from it can inform the design of learning environments within the community that it originated in. This type of activity could be particularly useful when designi ng with marginalized communities, because of the wa ys that it can surface non-dominant beliefs, values, and cultural backgrounds.  Thus, this project exists at least in part as an e xcavation of ‘Tejano and Mexican views’ of the industry and the ways that it impacts them (Brannstrom & Neuman, 2009). It does so in the context of reclamation, which is to say that we get to determine what these things mean and what impact they have had, and will continue to have, in the Valley.
