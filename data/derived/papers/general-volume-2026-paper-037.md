# Visualizing Curricula in Higher Education: A Semantic Similarity Approach

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Many institutions give students some freedom to choose courses across disciplines. Yet, existing course registration technologies often present courses as a textual list by discipline, leading to extraneous cognitive load and a lack of metacognitive awareness of how course decisions relate to the broader curriculum. To support students in making well-informed course choices, we develop a tool to visualize the entire curriculum based solely on course content. We describe its methodology, features, theoretical underpinning from learning sciences research, and ways to mindfully embed the tool into advising sessions. Problem and motivation In many institutions of higher education, particularly liberal arts colleges, students have considerable freedom to select courses from a broad range of fields. This freedom is intended to allow students to shape their own learning goals proactively and choose courses accordingly (e.g., Amherst College, n.d.; Brown University, n.d.). In practice, however, the exercise of this freedom is mediated by administrative technologies for course browsing and registration, which are often used to guide advising sessions. By structuring the information available to students and faculty, and by guiding their conversations in advising sessions, these tools play a sociomaterial role in shaping course selection and learning outcomes. Many institutions rely on commercial, off-the-shelf registration platforms that often organize courses in a long list by discipline. While these technologies streamline administrative processes, they raise two challenges. First, reading through a long list can impose extraneous cognitive load (de Jong, 2009) on users, hindering their ability to make fully informed course selection decisions. Second, disciplinary boundaries and a lack of interactivity limit students’ ability to develop metacognitive awareness (Flavell, 1976) of how their learning relates to the wider curriculum and their overall learning goals. Moreover, these two points are in fact related: the extraneous cognitive load imposed by such course scheduling tools candivert students’ cognitive resources away from big-picture, metacognitive thinking. Together, these constraints can limit the scope and richness of conversations between students and advisors, raising the risk of making uninformed or unsatisfactory choices. Especially in liberal arts programs, such constraints may cause students to inadvertently gravitate toward familiar disciplines, contributing to a mismatch between students’ exercise of freedom in course selection and these institutions’ missions of promoting interdisciplinary learning. Against this backdrop, we created the Visual Open Curriculum, a supplemental web application for course exploration and academic advising that is especially conducive to liberal arts curricula. First, the app lessens students’ cognitive load by replacing lengthy course lists with a visual and interactive curriculum map, a semantic search engine, and a recommender function. Second, it organizes courses based on content rather thandisciplinary classifications and includes a reflectional note-taking tool, which together promote conscious selection of courses according to learning goals. Moreover, recognizing that effective use of technology depends on both the users’ mindful engagement and surrounding social contexts (Salomon et al, 1991; Rook et al, 2024), we provide explicit suggestions on how students and faculty can best integrate this tool into the broader sociomaterial environment of their advising sessions. Overall, the app aims to provide students with increased agency in navigating extensive curricular choices, informed by theories of learning.

Previous studies within the field of learning analytics have developed visualizations and algorithms to map, predict, and recommend courses. They draw on different sources of information to analyze the curriculum: prerequisites (e.g., Parameswaran et al, 2011), past students’ course-taking patterns (e.g., Pardos and Jiang 2020), grades (e.g., Jiang et al, 2019), course evaluations (e.g., Farzan and Brusilovsky 2006; Bendakir and Aïmeur 2006) among others. The choice of which information source to use hinges on the objectives of the tool. For instance, if the objective is to recommend a series of courses that prepares a student well for some target course X, the tool should model prerequisite structures and how past students who have taken X fared in their courses prior to X (Jiang, Pardos, and Wei 2019).

In contrast to these studies, our objective is not to predict students’ course selection or recommend the most viable path, but rather to facilitate discussion and exploration by identifying interesting courses for the student without being bound by disciplinary boundaries, grades, or prerequisites. We thus focus on content alone, drawing on course descriptions. In liberal arts institutions with more freedom in course-taking, or in humanities departments with fewer prerequisites, raw content similarity may very well dominate other factors in course choice. Approaches used in prior work fall short in this aspect, as none capture course content directly. For example, while students’ course histories are correlated with content similarity (courses often taken successively are likely to be related), it may bias the algorithm toward courses in the same discipline and leave out courses that are similar but infrequently taken together. While traditional text analytics (e.g., bag-of-word models) struggle to capture semantic similarity, modern language models, which attend to the broader context of textual descriptions, are especially well suited for these purposes.

We employ publicly available course data from Amherst College, a selective private liberal arts college in Massachusetts, USA. Importantly, however, our methodology is general and can be used in any institution with textual course descriptions. Our procedure begins by extracting the descriptions of every course from the course catalog and cleaning these descriptions using OpenAI's GPT-4o, such that it retains only information about course content. We then calibrate a custom language model to convert these text descriptions into embedding vectors. An entirely zero-shot approach, e.g., using Open AI's GPT embedding model directly off the shelf, can be suboptimal precisely because they are trained on enormous corpora of internet text (Dell, 2025) and are prone to missing the subtle nuances in course content that students and faculty may care greatly about. We address this issue by conducting contrastive learning to tailor a smaller, open-source embedding model (Sentence BERT) to our particular dataset. We use cosine similarity to measure the similarity between two courses, and implement a combination of supervised contrastive learning (Khosla et al, 2020), alignment loss and uniformity loss (Wang and Isola, 2020) to make the model more attuned to the variation in course content. Finally, we define distance between a pair of courses simply as one minus the cosine similarity. Since distances are defined pairwise, we have close to one million dimensions from the 13 thousand courses with valid descriptions offered in our 17-year sample period. To visualize this in 2D space, we use tdistributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE; Hinton and Roweis, 2002; Maaten and Hinton, 2008). While all dimensionality reduction techniques are inherently distortionary and involve trading off preservation of local vis-a-vis global structures, our experimentation reveals that t-SNE with a perplexity parameter of 50 offers good balance. Figure 1 shows an intuitive global spectrum of academic fields from the arts to the natural sciences, with similar courses and majors clustered near each other. There is also significant overlap of fields (as shown by the manually drawn rectangles in Figure 1) and mixing of departments, confirming that our map focuses solely on course content in a discipline-agnostic way. Further technical details and validation exercises are omitted for this short paper but are available in our longer documentation online. Tool description Visual map of the curriculum Many course scheduling tools do not allow students to compositely see all of their course options. Instead, they are presented with hundreds of courses in list form, alphabetically organized by disciplinary category. This places extraneous cognitive load on students, especially those attempting to explore multiple areas of their curriculum. It is well documented that extraneous cognitive load leads to disengagement in learning (de Jong, 2009; National Academies et al, 2018). Anecdotally, this sort of disengagement in the course exploration process can manifest as students ‘settling’ for courses within disciplines they are already familiar with. In contrast to existing course registration tools such as Workday, the Visual Open Curriculum app was designed to decrease students’ cognitive load by providing them with an interactive, visual map of the curriculum. The courses the student selects are emphasized visually, and for any mapped course, users may select the corresponding marker to view associated metadata: faculty, teaching schedules, course descriptions and course codes (Figure 2, left).

We also explicitly provide students with the “My Course History” view, where students and advisors can see in a single glance how the student’s previous courses fit into the broader scope of the college’s courses (Figure 1). This relational view supports their awareness of how they are constructing their path through higher education. Students can use the “My Course History” view to engage mindfully with the app and visualize their individual learning journeys holistically, supporting their decision to select courses that will build on previous courses or that will allow them to explore a new area. This sort of metacognitive awareness can support students in becoming conscious of their autonomous ability to set learning goals and select courses accordingly (National Academies et al, 2018). Furthermore, when students recognize that they have made an informed, autonomous decision, it s upports their motivation and commitment to these learning goals, even when they may encounter obstacles (Murayama et al, 2015).

Semantic search Existing course scheduling platforms are often characterized by restricted or underdeveloped searching functionality. Users can only look up courses for which they already possess the relevant course codes or words contained in the titles. To facilitate user exploration, we build three additional features that help redirect cognitive energy away from navigating the interface to actually engaging in course exploration. First, we augment traditional keyword search by integrating a semantic search engine, applying the same might search “working with optics & lenses” and receive metadata on both an Advanced Astronomy elective (in the physics department) and an introductory course in photography (in the art department). Second, in the popup for each course, we display the most closely related courses according to semantic similarity. For instance, a student, having particularly appreciated a course on Behavioral Economics in a past semester, can search for that course and immediately identify similar courses across all departments (Figure 2, left). This feature is not only immediately useful, but also addresses an important limitation of the map. Because dimensionality reduction distorts pairwise distances, the courses shown near a given course on the map are not always its true closest neighbors in the embedding space. Lastly, we build a recommender function that combines the personalized insights of an academic advisor with an encyclopedic knowledge of all available courses. Specifically, we embed a chat-based LLM to return courses outside of the user’s most frequently explored disciplines while maintaining the common thread that unites their areas of interest. For example, a physics or art student who might not usually consider taking a chemistry class could potentially be interested in “CHEM-110: Color Study” (Figure 2, right), but would likely never discover that class when perusing the traditional, opaque course registry. Reflection and note-taking Goal setting, often considered a key component of metacognitive thinking (Stanton, 2021), can improve learning outcomes both in terms of overall learning trajectories (Schippers et al, 2020) and specific learning tasks (Paulson and Bauer, 2011; Stanton, 2021). To this end, we provide students with an in-app space for reflection and notetaking, where they can bookmark courses of interest and add notes regarding their goals and motivation for taking each course. By connecting students’ learning goals to a tangible map, we scaffold students' abilities literally “map out” different pathways they could take through the curriculum to reach these goals. Students will also have the option to share a snapshot of their mapped courses and reflections with their academic advisors. In this way, the app can not only provide material support through presenting information, but also aid in enhancing social relationships between students and their advisors.

Example usecCase Prior to the advising session, students will a. Conduct initial course exploration using the Visual Open Curriculum, e.g., zoom in on their past courses and look at neighboring courses to identify common themes, use the semantic search function to compile a list of courses that relate the student’s key interests to other fields. b. Respond to prompts asking the student’s motivations for their proposed course selection. Share that document with their advisors, improving knowledge sharing ahead of the actual advising meetings. During advising meetings, the app functions as a visual support to enable better communication. a. Advisors will review the students’ course choices to date in the “My Course History” tab. The map makes it clear when their studies are concentrated in one corner of the curriculum. Areas lacking highlighted markers stand out as potential areas of exploration. b. Advisors can propose alternative classes with common themes identified using the visual map and semantic search functions to avoid over-concentrating in specific departments. Interactive demo session We will project our web application on a screen. After briefly reviewing the methods, we will ask the participant to either play the role of an academic advisor or that of a hypothetical student. The author will play the other role. Together, we will review the app functionalities and conduct a mock advising session. Traditional platforms often lack an overarching view of curricular offerings, hindering students’ capabilities to align their learning journeys with personal goals as well as institutional ones. The Visual Open Curriculum app attempts to provide students with an accessible metacognitive scaffold, allowing them to chart their own paths through the map of possibilities that their education offers.
