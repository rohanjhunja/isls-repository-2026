# Teaching Programming to Pre-service Teachers through Pair Programming

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Programming is becoming more prevalent in primary schools. However, we still do not know how to effectively train teachers in programming. On the other hand, prior research on pair programming has been shown to support learning in the literature of computer science education. Therefore, in this study, we examine the role of pair programming in pre-service teachers’ (PSTs’) learning of programming in an elective course at a Finnish university. Our data sources are video data, semi-structured interviews, and course artefacts. Our analysis showed that pair programming supported PSTs’ learning when they engaged in collaborative decision-making processes and took up collaborative roles as they preferred. Pair programming practices were more meaningful when the pairs worked on open-ended programming tasks. The gaps in programming knowledge and skills were reported to hinder learning when each partner did not share their programming progress with each other.

## Introduction and background

Programming has been introduced as a compulsory sub ject in the primary school curricula in several cou ntries, making it an integral part of primary teachers’ daily instruction (Balanskat & Engelhardt, 2015). To equip teachers for this evolving educational landscape, teacher education programs are progressively integrating programming into their curricula. This inclusion not only familiarizes future teachers with programming but also provides them with practical, hands-on experience. However, the development of effective training practices for programming in pre-service teacher education remains an area yet to be  fully explored, largely due to the emerging nature  of the field. Given this gap in the existing research,  in this proposal, we  aim to contribute to the understanding of how commonly used teaching strategies in computer science departments such as pair programming can be used in primary teacher education programs in training f uture teachers. Therefore, in this study, we invest igated the impact of pair programming on pre-service teachers’ learning processes of programming  in an elective course at a Finnish university. Specifically, we address the following research questions: 1) What elements of p air programming practices were meaningful for pre-service teachers? 2) When did elements of pair programming practices support or hinder pre-service teachers’ learning of programming?

## Theoretical background

At the primary school level, programming education often includes block-based programming platforms such as Scratch. This accessible tool enables students to create interactive media like games and animations. The primary aim is to familiarize students with computational concepts and practices and foster skills like algorithmic thinking (Grover & Pea, 2013).

Learning programming fundamentals can be challengin g for both young people and adults, especially novices (Ben-Ari, 1998). However, pair programming has shown benefits in learning, such as improved program quality, reduced defects, and increased motivation (Wei et al., 2021). Empirical studies indicate that compared to solo programming, pair programming can be more effe ctive in developing solutions for complex tasks (Liebenberg et al., 2012) and fostering creative th inking (Zhong et al., 2016). One common p ractice in pair programming is to employ the roles which are typically divided into the 'driver', who writes the program, and the 'navigator', who reviews and suggests changes. This  approach not only enhances learning but also provi des opportunities for pa rticipants to engage in coordinated activities, sol ve shared problems, and create new knowledge (Goodyear et al., 2014). The critical attributes of collaborative pair programming, especially in Scratch activities, include working on a common task, maint aining interdependence, and ensuring individual accountability (Fagerlund, 2021).

## Methods

We collected video and interview data from eight Finnish pre-service teachers. These pre-service teachers engaged in pair programming during a three-hour session of an elective introductory programmin g course at a Finnish university. Three instructors taught the session, and  the programming activities involved three Scratch programming tasks, which  the participants completed simultaneously in pairs on the same computer. We employed the Use, Modify, Create progression (Lee e t al., 2011) in the design and development of the t asks. Participants completed a highly structured tutorial (30 minutes), debugging tasks (30 minutes), and a self-designed mini-game (60 minutes).  Each pair's work was recorded with a GoPro camera a nd a screen capture software. Students formed pairs by choice, except for one pai r, which was formed based on their previous programming experience levels.  The instructors also informed them of what pair pro gramming meant and the roles in pair programming (driver/navigator).  We organized a reflection session for the participants one week after they attended the pair programming session. In this sess ion, the participants individually watched the reco rdings of their pair programming practices and listed the moments that supported or challenged them in their programming and learning of programming experience. Then, we conducted semi-structured pair interviews (approximately 30 minutes each) to obtain further details on the list ed learn ing moments.  In analyzing the data, we employed thematic analysis to identify and inductively categorize recurring themes across interviews and the recorded videos (Braun & Clarke, 2021). All authors subsequently re viewed and discussed themes and sub-themes and further elaborated on their meanings and definition s in the data. In reviewing and discussing themes a nd subthemes, our aim was to explore how various learning moments under each theme or subtheme either supported or hindered the participants’ pair programming experience.

## Preliminary findings

Our preliminary analysis identified key themes infl uencing pre-service teachers' learning experiences in pair programming. These include role dynamics within pairs, the impact of varying programming skill levels, decisionmaking processes, peer support mechanisms, and the nature of programming tasks. Roles in pairs and use of computer The role allocation between pairs varied, with each  finding different arrangements that supported thei r learning. For example, one pair maintained the same roles (navigator or driver) throughout the session, which allowed them to concentrate more on the task rather than shiftin g their focus to the role. Contrarily, Pair 3 belie ved in the significance of alternating roles, aiming for an equal role distribution. As Student 2  from Pair 3 noted, “I think it went really smoothly... that I didn't have the feel ing that I had done too little or too much.”  Another example is Pair 2, who took turns without strictly adhering to  the d river/navigator roles. Their task involved developi ng a football game in Scratch, and they had challenges in programming the ball. The following excerpt illustrates how Pair 2 flexibly took turns at the computer, each contributing to the task by building on each other's ideas and skills: Pair 2/Student 2: The player cannot influence where  the ball is  as [Y value]  would change anyway.

Pair 2/Student 1 (in control of the computer): So, should this [Y value] come from the starting point of the ball or from characters?

Pair 2/Student 2: Let’s see. The ball, yes.

(Student 2 takes control of the computer) Pair 2/Student 1: Just click on the variables first and name it "direction." Pair 2/Student 2 (in control of the computer): Direction? Pair 2/Student 1: Yes, and that’s where we put this (Points to “Add an input – Boolean” on the screen).

The interview data showed that almost all pairs were happy with the role allocation  and the time they spent using the computer. However, there was an exception in Pair 1, where Student 2 indicated difficulty in being a passive observer while her partner controlled the  computer and undertook the programming tasks. This  aspect of the pair programming experience is further discussed in the next section. Learning levels and engagement Student teachers reported that pair programming  supported their learning when they had similar leve ls of programming knowledge and experience: “And I somehow feel that we were at least on the sam e level when it came to Scratch. The fact that we were able to thin k together on the same level, on the same line, als o helped” (Pair 3/Student 2). There was one pair (Pair 1) that varied in their understanding of programming, and one student (Student 2) teacher in the pair expressed her experience of pair programming as negative and even panicking. She claimed her partner spent more time programming, and she did not have the chance to build on her knowl edge. She felt that "because (Partner) has played so much, (Partner) would have been clicking on things [so quickly] that [she] wouldn't have [been able] to follow” (Pair 1/Student 2). Although Student 2 did not feel comfortable with the pair work but it did not lead to lower levels of engagement with the task; she wa s engaged with the task throughout the session. However, it was also observ ed that she did not feel comfortable using the comp uter and wanted her partner to use it throughout the session.

Pair and peer support Mutual support within each pair was pivotal, as stu dents built upon each other's strengths. This colla borative environment significantly boosted motivation and the willingness to explore programming. As one student put it, “It [pair programming] certainly increased that kin d of motivation and perseverance to try and try fir st yourself and try bravely” (Pair 3/Student 1). Furthermore, a  culture of shared learning emerged among all parti cipants. Students exchanged project ideas and progress with other pairs, and this inter-pair interaction was not just limited to session times but extended into breaks and post-session discussions. One student highlighted the be nefits of this broader collaboration: “...try different kinds of games....then look at the others’ [games], because there were some quite nice games” (Pair 2/Student 2).

## Task and context

The pairs completed three tasks in the session. The first task was strongly guided. The second task provided some choice in programming. The last one was an open-ended task allowing for multiple ways to progress. Pair programming was clearly more meaningful with the open-ended type of tasks as those allowed pairs to have more space for discussion and planning together: “kind of forced us to discuss with each other what w e wanted to do and how we wanted to do it and what we wanted it to  look like” (Pair 1/Student 1). It was observed in the video that the pairs talked to each other most when they were working on open ended tasks.

## Discussion and conclusion

In this study, we aimed to reveal meaningful pair p rogramming practices in the pre-service teacher education context in Finland. Pair programming supported stud ent teachers’ learning when they engaged in collabo rative decision-making processes and took up collaborative roles as they p referred. Any type of support (instructor/pair/peer) comforted the pairs and enab led them to develop their skills in programming and  to experience different ways of programming. Pair prog ramming practices were more meaningful w hen the pairs worked on open-ended tasks. In addition to these supportive elemen ts, the gaps in programming knowledge and skills were reported to hinder learning when each p artner did not share their programming progress wit h each other. This study shows t hat pair programming can be a beneficial teaching p ractice for pre-service teachers; however, pair programming cannot become a standalone practice. Teacher educators need to carefully design and develop teacher training program that will support every pre-service teacher’s learning of programming.
