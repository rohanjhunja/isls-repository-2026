# Critical Action Learning: Empowering Students in An Uncertain World

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents a recent whole-school study of Critical Action Learning, a form of sustained critical inquiry for K-12 classroom communities. Through partnerships in North America, Europe, and Asia, we pursue a deeper understanding of how Critical Action Learning occurs in classrooms and how it influences students, teachers, schools and communities. This paper presents a study of a school located in Bangalore, India, in which teachers and school leaders worked to adopt Critical Action Learning in numerous classrooms. We report on teacher knowledge and practice, features of curriculum, and the growth of the school community.

## Introduction

This paper presents a recent whole-school study of Critical Action Learning, a form of sustained critical inquiry for K-12 classroom communities. Building on a decade of p rior research on learning communities (Slotta, Quintana & Moher, 2018), the Critical Action Learni ng Exchange (CALE) has advanced a framework to guid e the design of sustained critical inquiry, with the goal of empowering students and teachers in the face of oppressive socio-environmental issues. CALE was designed as a  teacher professional learning community and has eng aged hundreds of teachers in North America, Europe, and Asia. Through these partnerships,  we pursue a deeper understanding of how Critical Action Learning occur s in classrooms, and  how it influences students, teachers, schools and communities.

The adoption of any form of critical pedagogy requi res a teacher to engage in autonomous design and enactment of curriculum, including dialogical practices and critical reflections (i.e., about what hap pens in the classroom and with each student). To help  CALE teachers engage in such autonomous innovation and experimentation, CALE research now explores the  infrastructuring (Penuel, 2019) of Critical Action Learning, including: (1) the physical and material learning environment, place-based learning, location and co-location of learning interactions, and a wide range of material  and embodied interactions; (2) the role of technology  in scaffolding student and teacher interactions within a community of learners, fostering critical action, and ensuring a climate of care for all participants; (3) the local and global educational context  including school, district, and national level curriculum policies and expectations, as well as cultural and historical factors; (4) t he journey of a teacher, including initial ideas about pedagogy and practice, pathways through curriculum design, and expectations about student experience and development; (5) interactions with the school and local community, as critical action engages teachers, school leaders, s tudent families and the wider community. In order for CALE to succeed in its vision of supporting autonomous teac her engagement, we must understand the infrastructu re of critical action and the processes through which the classroom and school community may be transformed. This paper presents a study  of one school, located in Bangalore, India, in which t eachers and school leaders worked to adopt Critical Action Learning in numerous classrooms. We report on  teacher knowledge and practice, physical and material elements of the lea rning designs, student interactions with peers, tea chers, and learning environment, and the influence of such cur riculum on students, teachers and school communitie s. We investigate how the curriculum designs utilized curriculum time and classroom spac e, what forms of discourse and interaction occurred, and how learning was medi ated through material and embodied interactions. We  also examine teachers’ professional growth, in terms of the challenges encountered and solutions explored, as well as the growth of the overall school community.

Rationale and prior research CALE is a community of educators responding to a gl obal scenario that has been described in increasing ly dire tones. UN Secretary-General António Guterres recently referred to the c urrent state of wars, climate crisis, growing economic inequalities and geopolitical divi sions as "a confluence of challenges unlike any oth er in our lifetimes" (Guterres, 2023). In 2021, one of the fi rst global-scale investigations of climate anxiety in the yout h surveyed 10,000 children and young people (aged 16 –25 years) in 10  different nations (Hickman et al., 2021). 60% of respondents reported being extremely worried  about climate change. Over 50% reported feeling sa d, anxious, angry, powerless, helpless, and/or guilty about the climate crisis, and more than 45% said these feelings are negatively affecting their daily life and funct ioning. Similar effects have been attributed to oth er pressing issues, such as systemic racism and other forms of discrimination, war, poverty and inequality, etc. (Ridley et al., 2020; Williams, 2018). Against this worrying backdrop, a growing body of research has suggested that engaging in some form of committed collective action can be an effective way of empowering children and young people in relation to their concerns or anxieties caused by their stressful and  uncertain world (Hoggett & Randall, 2018; Nairn, 2019). The high state of anxiety and passion among student s is a wake-up call to educators everywhere. We must find better ways of helping our teachers he lp our students find meaning and purpose in learnin g, build strong identities as learners and future citizens, and understand the problems in their world and thei r lives as something they can respond to through action. This paper presents a study that deepens our understanding of how teachers adopt Critical Action Learning. By situating our study within the rich context of a single school, we can examine the interplay of factors relating to teacher knowledge, educational systems, school leadership, curricular activities, classroom interactions and physical and  technological elements.  This will shed light on the specific experiences of teachers and students and inform our further infrastructuring of Critical Action Learning, Theoretical foundations CALE draws from Knowledge Building theory and Critical Pedagogy to advance a framework for Critical Action Learning. Knowledge Building (Scardamalia & Bereite r, 2006) places a fundamental value on epistemic ag ency and collective responsibility, together with (a) kn owledge advancement as a community rather than indi vidual achievement; (b) knowledge advancement as idea impr ovement rather than as progress toward accurate or warranted belief; (c) discourse as collaborative pr oblem-solving rather than as argumentation. These princip les intersect with key ideas of Critical Pedagogy, whic h criticizes the traditional process of schooling a s one that alienates students from their sense of agency by denying them opportunities to establish their own aims and goals, and advocates for educational ideals and practices that would empower marginalized people as transform ative agents capable of lessening their oppression (Freire, 1970, Kincheloe, 2004). Freire (1970) proposed a “problemposing model” of education that values the importance of student experience and a dialogical method of teaching and learning. While Critical Pedagogy has been cele brated by educators for more than half a century, i t remains largely absent from schools today. Effective as they may be shown by research studies, most inquiry and dialogic pedagogies fail to gain wide adoption, and classroo ms of today look and behave much the way they did i n the 1950s (Collins & Halverson, 2018; diSessa, 2000). The reasons for this intransigence are complex and interwoven, including structural aspects (i.e., separated class  topics, daily class schedules, grades, etc), as we ll as curriculum standards and expectations that pressure teachers t o cover  the breadth. Another barrier is concerned with the fundamental challenges of changing one’s profession al practices, especially when situated within a str ong community of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991). The sp ecific forms of practice required by critical i nquiry methods, as well as the burden they place on curriculum time, make them quite challenging for teachers to adopt. KCI: Knowledge Community and Inquiry For nearly two decades, our research group  has advanced the KCI model to leverage the power of collective inquiry and learning communities while ensuring cov erage of content  (Slotta & Peters, 2008). By designing a curriculum unit in which the classroom is conceived  as a learning community where peers contribute to and receive from the wider collective, KCI guides teach ers to embed inquiry and still ensure learning of c ontent, practices and epistemic forms (Slotta, Quintana & Moher, 2018; Fong & Slotta, 2018). Technology can play an important role in helping the teacher orchestrate c ollective inquiry by scaffolding students in buildi ng a community knowledge base and then scaffolding their applications and extensions of that community knowledge within subsequent inquiry. Because learning community approaches are challenging for teachers to adopt, as with most forms of inquiry (Blumenfeld et al., 1994), KCI provides scaffolding through a structural model of collective inquiry, together with actionable approaches and examples. CALE: The Critical Action Learning Exchange In 2019, we established the Critical Action Learning Exchange (CALE) based on a key insight that the affordances (epistemological, technological, material) of a lea rning community approach could be helpful in suppor ting teachers’ adoption of critical pedagogy  (Carvalho et al., 2021; 2022). We refer to this fusion of learning communities with critical pedagogy as Critical Action Learning, wherein the goal is to empower students in the face of social and environmental issues like climate change, gender inequity or economic uncertainty. CALE has developed a curriculum framework and professional d evelopment model, and has engaged hundreds of teach ers from dozens of countries in professional developmen t workshops where they design and share critical ac tion curricula (Carvalho et al.,  2022; 2023). Over the past four years, we have esta blished partnerships in Canada, Egypt, India and China, providing CALE with a global perspective and representation (Carvalho et al., 2022). CALE has articulated five  approaches to make Critical Action Learning more practicable and offer design pathways for teachers: (1) Arts-based Critical Action allows students to communicate abstract or complex ideas and express their identities in an intellectually and emotionally engaging way, developing richly connected understandings of the diversity of viewpoints and v alues among their peers (Keifer-Boyd, 2011); (2) Critical Making emphasizes the situated, context-specific, and material aspects of the design proces s, and helps students explore the relationship between process and produc t, the entanglement of theory and practice and conn ections between makers and communities (Ratto, 2 011); (3) Critical Action Games  afford simulation or enactment of distinct realities and outcomes that can be critica lly analyzed. Games don't need to adhere to real-world social conventions or structures, allowing experimentation  with alternative realities, identities and concept s (Dhiman, 2023); (4) Storytelling allows students to craft their own stories and connect the learning with emotions, thoughts and creativity, bringing forward critical perspecti ves and artistic interpretations in which they inte rpret and respond to world issues (Burk, 2000, Honwad et al., 2020); and (5) Community Engaged Critical Inquiry engages students directly with the issues that concern them  personally, cooperating with organizations and com munity members to generate positive transformations (Ozer,  2017; McKoy et al., 2021). For each approach, CALE has curated a set of examples and teaching strategies, to help teachers integrate the approach into their design. A CALE framework describes Critical Action Learning along two axes: The vertical axis, “Depth,” includes three levels: (1) Knowledge, where students develop abilities to acquire, asse ss, and build new knowledge; (2) Criticality, in which students understand their own position i n relation to forces and structures that affect the issue; (3) Action, in which students gain a sense of agency, shifting from “that's just the way things are'' to “that's a problem that I can act upon.” The horizontal axis, “Scope,”  also includes three levels: (1) Individual, where students explore their own intersections with the issue, creating personal meaning and purpose in learning; (2) Community, where students recognize the value of community in any action, making some impact on their local community. (3) Globe: Students understand that problems such as climate change, social injustices and economic inequalities are global, and that their actions are connected to the wider community and world. For each axis, CALE provides a set of scaffolding questions to guide teacher des igns. These questions, together with the CALE approaches and examples, form a Desig n Guide that supports teachers in developing critical action curriculum units. While some teache rs can use the CALE Guide autonomously, most benefi t from guidance and peer collaboration for their design a nd enactment of critical action learning. CALE professional learning supports must be tailored and responsive t o each teacher’s background, knowledge and wider co ntext (i.e., country, culture, school, topic and student age level). CALE has developed a formative interven tion model for teacher lea rning, following Engeström's (2011) ChangeLab, in which the teacher is engaged in reflexive dialogue concerning their current practice and their goals or vision for change (Carvalho et al., 2022). Infrastructuring Critical Action Learning Beyond the dissemination of ideas or resources (the approaches, framework, etc.), CALE aims to foster the cocreation of conditions in which practitioners can take ownership of those ideas. This is consistent with the notion of "infrastructuring" (Chen, 2022; Penuel, 2019), a process-oriented approach where the negotiation between users' needs and environmental affordances plays a critical role in fostering autonomous engagement of practitioners. By engaging a diverse cohort of teachers, the present study inv estigates two infrastructural dimensions of Critical Action Learning: (1) Teacher s’ professional growth through adoption of critical ac tion learning, and (2) Physical, material and technological elements of critical action learning environments. Teacher professional growth Following Jones and Swanson (2022), we understand a  teacher’s readiness for pedagogical innovations in terms of their own self-efficacy and motivation (Bandura, 2001) as well as the influences that may constrain agency and prevent change (Grenny et al., 2013). For example, a teacher might be earnestly ready for innovation, but find themselves in a depar tment, school, or broader educational climate that is averse to such change. This paper examines the tensions between teachers' interest, motivation s and ability to change their practice and their perceived structural constraints or affordances for such changes. Physical, material and technological elements of critical action learning Any classroom learning has a physical context, which includes aspects of emplacement (i.e., in a particular room or setting), materiality (i.e., of learning materials and products), and embodiment (i.e., of student engagement, copresence, and location-dependent learning). In previous partnerships, we have observed how teac hers’ designs depend on their local context, and typically entail some physical and material elements (see Figure 1). This paper examines the role played by such elements to understand their impact on students, teachers and communities. ## Method

Two research questions are addressed by this paper: (1) What forms of professional growth occur as teachers adopt critical action learning? This question entails the study of teachers’ incomi ng ideas regarding inquiry methods, student learning, and the support of students who feel oppressed and anxious in an uncertain world. We examine the curriculum designs and enactment s of  each teacher, as well as their change process, including engagement in critical reflections.  Finally, we articulate the teacher’s broader instit utional and sociocultural contexts and how those may influence their  adoption of critical action learning. This includes peer interactions among teachers and leaders within the school and any form  of “public intellectualism” (Giroux, 1988) in which teachers participate actively within their community. We exa mine the impact of these activities within a school, as measured by the uptake of ideas, level of engagement by stakeholders, and sustained critical inquiry of teachers.

(2) What are the physical, material and technologic al dimensions of Critical Action Learning?  Specifi cally, we

examine the role of the physical space (i.e., locat ion of students, materials or activities), as well as the broader materiality of learning (e.g., in support of activities, design, making, community interactions, or artistic products). We also captured the role of any technology in scaf folding critical inquiry within a learning communit y and helping the teacher achieve a dialogic pedagogy and leverage space and material elements.

## Participants and settings

Over the past several years, CALE has developed a p artnership with Richmond Road Public School (RRPS) in Bangalore, India, which serves more than 1500 students (grades k-14) from low and middle-income backgrounds, and over 40% who identify as first-generation learners. More than 40 teachers at RRPS have participated in CALE workshops, nine of whom implemented Critical Action Learning curricula in their classrooms for purposes of this study. The school principal and all participating t eachers have expressed an interest in continuing th eir journey and are active in maintaining their own communications and design processes (Carvalho et al., 2024). Participants include teachers, school leaders, and the broader c ommunity relating to our partner school in Bengalur u, India. Teachers varied greatly in terms of their backgroun ds, pedagogical knowledge, experiences, motivations  and readiness for change. This provides an entry point for the formative intervention and an important infrastructural dimension of teachers’ readiness for change. Participating teachers received support from school leaders as well as from the research team but remained firmly in the ownership position with regard to the design and enactment of curriculum, including the physical, material and technological aspects of the learning environment. Material and technology environments As illustrated in Figure  1 above, prior CALE  curriculum designs  have revealed a profound intersection with physical, material and technological elements. Stud ents are in motion within the classroom, the makers pace, or out-of-doors – interacting with place and space, working with a variety of materials to embody their critical inquiry through constructed environments, community innovations, storytelling, and public galleries. While teachers and schools differ in terms of available resources and curricular requirements for space and material, there were some common material elements of arts and makerspace sup plies, chart paper, writable surfaces, floor space, and furniture. With regard to technology, the school pr ovided access to personal computers and a computer lab for teachers, as well as a smart board for video and technology presentations. Addressing the research questions The research questions are addressed in an interwov en fashion, using complementary methodologies. For example, to understand teachers’ trajectory in adop ting Critical Action Learning (RQ1), we employ a mi xed method approach including surveys, interviews, and analysis of curriculum designs and design processes. We employed surveys of students and teachers, as well as semi-structured interviews for teachers and selected students. Prior CALE research (Carvalho et al., 2023) has developed a grounded coding of curriculum enactment, to capture the role of infrastructural elements, including the role of physical space, material interactions, embodied elements, teacher-led discourse, and technology environments. This co ding yields themes and supports further qualitative analyses of curriculum designs, student-made artifacts, classroom interactions, and communi ty-level factors. Moreover, those same analyses of curriculum design and enactment can also inform our understanding of the role of physical, material and technological el ements in Critical Action Learning (RQ2). We employ designbased implementation research (DBIR) to support par ticipating teachers through regular design meetings as they develop activities, assessments, and learning envir onments. We captured all designs, as well as enactm ents in terms of teacher and student interactions with peer s, teacher, and physical, material and technologica l elements of the infrastructure. For each teacher, we examine  (1) Curriculum design of inquiry activities, mater ial and technology; (2) Curriculum enactment, including the role of physical space, material and technological elements, as well as the influence of the curriculum on students and teachers; and (3) Teacher journey, in which we capture the teacher’s readiness for change and their experience of working with these ideas, enacting them with students, critically reflecting on the curriculum enactment, and making plans for the next iteration.

## Findings

Curriculum design outcomes CALE researchers collaborated with 9 RRPS teachers to develop 5 lesson plans. Teachers implemented the se lessons in over a two-week time span. Except for the Grade 8 Math teacher, all teachers worked in pairs. Teacher’s curriculum designs utilized four of the CALE approa ches (all except Storytelling), and most combined m ultiple approaches. Below, we describe each design and discuss how each approach was utilized and how they connected with local issues that affect students. All teacher s started from curricu la prescribed by the ministry based on textbooks and expanded from their traditional metho ds (i.e., lecturing with the support of the textboo k) to incorporate elements of collective inquiry, experiential learning and Critical Action Learning. Urbanization-Grade 8, Social Sciences.  Two teachers co-designed this lesson that built on a textbook curriculum on the concept of urbanization. The desi gned lesson connected the subject matter with the r eal issue of the accelerated growth of Bengaluru. To accompli sh that, teachers mixed two CAL approaches, communi ty engagement and critical making. Students surveyed t heir communities about problems created by the incr eased population in recent years. All students created a large collective collage together to represent these problems and possible solutions. Finally, they used critical making to create interactive models with ideas for how the city could solve those problems (Figure 2, below).

Percentage-Grade 8, Math.  The Math teacher was able to expand this lesson far beyond the prescribed curriculum about the application of percentages. This lesson engaged stu dents with the concept of smart cities. Students worked in small groups to survey their com munities to identify urban problems, which they wer e later asked to categorize. Based on these categories, stu dents defined sectors of their envisioned smart city in  which they wanted to invest. Next, they were given a fict itious budget that they would allocate among the sectors, thus applying percentage concepts. Finally, students used Minecraft to realize their vision of a smart city (Figure 2). Figure 2 Urbanization Unit (Left), students engaged in critical making to demo nstrate ideas of sustainable urban futures; in the percentage unit (right), students created Minecraft renditions of their ideas. Water pollution-Grade 5, Science.  This textbook curriculum focused on concepts such a s solubility (i.e., the ability of two substances, the solute and the solvent, to form a solution). Teachers were a ble to connect this concept with the problem of water pollution, e specially the pollution of local rivers. Teachers used an Arts-based approach to engage students with critical act ion towards this issue. First, students designed po sters with their own messages and opinions about the importance of keeping the local bodies of water clean. Teachers asked the school leadership to post pictures of the students’ posters on the school's social media channels, to amplify the impact of students' action. Students also created a dance performance about r iver pollution that was presented in an event that gathered the whole school community and visitors from other local schools (See Figure 3, below). Fibre to fabric-Grade 6, Science.  The textbook curriculum aimed to familiarize students with different natural fibres (i.e., cotton, wool, silk, etc.) and  processes used to produce fabric. Two teachers wor ked on this lesson and significantly expanded it, connecting it  with the issue of plastic waste, which is a serious problem for the local community. One activity of this curriculu m that students found very impactful was a rally ar ound the neighbourhood with posters about avoiding excessive  plastic waste and discarding it correctly. During the rally, students also collected plastic garbage from the streets and sidewalks, engaging directly with the problem. Finally, students used the collected plastic garbage to crea te an artistic installation about the problem  of plastic pollution and a culminating fashion show with clothes students designed using either natural fibres or recycled plastic. Wind and Storm-Grade 7, Science.  The textbook curriculum, which focuses on the processes that form wind and storms, was expanded to address the broader problem of the management of natural disasters. This was a timely subject, as (during the unit enactment) th e school experienced three days of mandated closure  by the government due to heavy rains and flooding. The teachers relied on experiential learning methods for this lesson, with many classroom experiments demonstrating air p ressure, wind speed, etc.  they also engaged students in critical making for the construction of models that  demonstrated how natural disasters could affect th e city, as well as some forms of prevention or remediation.

Our five co-design teams each created a lesson that reflected the particular strengths, interests, and knowledge of the respective teachers. Material and technologi cal elements emerged as solutions within the design process, and  some activities were traditional and di dactic, while others were more dialogic and inquiry-oriented. In each unit, students were supported in various forms of critical action. They participated in 21st-century competencies, problem-solving, collaboration, and communication and learned to make connections between ideas, issues, and topics for learning in school. Classroom space was a striking element of all curri culum designs, as the physical classroom (furniture, windows, etc) has a profound impact on the kinds of  teaching and learning that happen in that space.  Inquiry methods (like Critical Action Learning) re quire flexibility. Teachers must be able to quickly  rearrange the space by moving students and furniture so they can adapt to different types of individual or group work with the use of various materials. The space must allow the teacher  to move freely and learn from interacting with students, and the technology support must facilitate different ki nds of interactions.  Unfortunately, the physical and technological infrastructure at RRPS w as not ideal in this aspect. Classrooms are furnished with long and heavy benches with attached writing surfaces, which can n ot be easily reconfigured, and teachers tend to use  the smartboard only as a whiteboard or a TV to play videos. Teachers and students alike were struck by the mismatch of form and function as soon as the didactic lecture was left behind and peer interacti ons became the mode of learning. Teachers resorted to any number of innovative approaches with regard to physical space, some of them utilizing hallway space or sending students to work  in the library or auditorium. One outcome of the w ork was a new awareness of the importance of the physical space, and the school director promised to develop an innovative, active learning classroom where teachers could apply new forms of pedagogy and practice Material was a powerful feature in all lesson desig ns, as students and teachers worked with various media, paper, paints, electronics, and modelling, e ven technology environments. All teachers utilized a smart board, although most only as a form of projector.

Another important infrastructural element was that of school leadership.  From the outset, the Directo r, school principal and vice principal of curriculum at RRPS were all deeply involved in this partnership. CALE was brought into the school at their invitation, and th ey have been steadfast in their support and encoura gement of teachers to adopt critical action learning. The sch ool had a reputation as being quite stringent in su pport of the formal curriculum and textbook. Teachers had been i nstructed to follow the curriculum as a rule. However, this position shifted over the course of our study, and school leaders began talking about the curriculum as an object of inquiry and critical thinking: that teachers nee ded to be selective about topics and choose how to use their classroom time. Hence, school leaders have grown together with their teachers and made decisions to support the continued professional growth of all community members. Teacher professional growth Since 2021, CALE researchers have collaborated with RRPS teachers to develop curricula that empower students through Critical Action Learning. Over 40 teachers at the school have participated in CALE workshops o ver the last three years. In October 2024, CALE researchers collaborated with 9 RRPS teachers to design 5 new lessons. Teachers enacted these lessons over two weeks, with positive results. Teachers were all seen to step w ell outside their comfort zone: quite literally outside the “comfort zone” of the front of the clas sroom. Most teachers had limited experience with conducting classroom discussions, and no experience facilitating discussions that engage students’ critical thinking. Teachers universally e xpressed discomfort and unfamiliarity regarding suc h forms of discourse, yet all teachers expressed excitement that they had made progress in this area.  Here, we present a case study of one teacher, Ms. Manisha (a pseudonym), focusing on her professional growth as a critical educator. Ms. Manisha has worked at RRPS for the past seven y ears as a math teacher in grades 8, 9, and 10, and has participated in all design workshops and other CALE events in the Bengaluru community. In the past  two years, Ms. Manisha has successfully designed and implemented two lessons using the CALE framework, with an apparent preference for the Games for Critical Acti on approach. This is remarkable because Games for C ritical Action is the least frequently used of all five app roaches we systematized, probably for being one of the most difficult to implement (Carvalho et al., 2024). Additionally, Ms. Manisha teaches math, wh ich is also a notably challenging subject to integrate with Critical Action Learning. The first curriculum design created by Ms. Manisha was titled “Fractional Finances,” in which she redesigned a grade 8 curriculum on fractions that was usually taught through a traditional lecture-based approach. Instead, Ms. Manisha devised a simple game in which groups of students are given different amounts of "money" (in the form of plastic beads), representing the in come ranges of Indian households. Students were the n asked to plan the families' budgets by allocating fractions of their income to each expense category (e.g. heal th, food, housing, leisure, etc.). Throughout the lesson, stu dents were given opportunities to reflect on econom ic inequalities, career choices, and the cost of living by comparing the budget of each different “family.” The second design developed by Ms. Manisha expanded  a prescribed curriculum about the application of percentages to engage students with the concept of smart cities. In this lesson, students worked in small groups to survey their communities to identify urban problems, which they were later asked to categorize. Based on these categories, students defined sectors of their envisioned smart city in which they wanted to invest. Next, they were given a fictitious budget that they would allocate among the sectors, thus applying percentage concept s. Finally, students used the game Min ecraft to realize their vision of a smart city. Bes ides improving as a designer, Ms. Manisha has also engaged with conceptual and theore tical understandings of Critical Action Learning. S he articulates the importance of students developing a n “understanding of social issues and social awareness” and sees critical action learning as an approach that h elps to “link the subject matter with social issues.” This has led Ms. Manisha to assume a leadership role within the Bengaluru teacher community, which will be instrumental in future efforts to expand the community to other local schools.

## Discussion

Teachers at RRPS encountered four main challenges d uring this project: the first was that of curriculum time, relating to the trade-off between depth and breadth of coverage. Teachers did not see enough time for large inquiry units, with the looming presence of exams. Also, th ey had a difficult time creating lesson designs tha t replaced the lecture, rather than just complementing it. The second main challenge was that of designing act ivities and assessments. Teachers had little experience in creating activities that engage students and still have them learning content and competencies. They were challenged to k eep the content learning centred within their desig ns, such that students would not get distracted by art proje cts or community trips. The third key challenge was that of interacting with students.  Many teachers expressed frustration that students w ould not speak within class, and could not be engaged in critical discussions. Stude nts, just as teachers, are accustomed to more tradi tional forms of teaching and learning. Some feel nervous or shy and do not want to talk. All of the teachers found this challenge to be an important focus of future professional dev elopment. The final challenge was that of using the physical and material space and most teachers found themselves thinking creativ ely to make the best out of the physical limitations described above. Many solutions were ad vanced in relation to these challenges. There was c reative use of time around major holidays where students br ought cultural elements into their activities. Teac hers found the CALE approaches to be helpful, referring to the asso ciated examples and other materials. In several cas es, multiple content areas were connected within a single curriculum unit, allowing a sense of curriculum integration. Teachers and school leaders agreed, at the end of this study, to make continued professional progress on these four challenges. With regard to curriculum time, every teacher should be a critical thinker about curriculum requirements, and about helping students learn more deeply.  Teachers resolve to “think of themselves as learners and experimenters,” trying new activities to see wh at works best. They also committed to updating thei r designs based on things they noticed not to be working, and  to collaborate  with peers and mentors wherever possible. With regard to student interactions there was some insight about possible “quick methods" that can be used at any point in time, such as think-pair-share and reflective discussion. It was recognized that students and teachers would get accustomed to these, promoting a more gen eral climate of dialogue and communication. It was also clear to all  teachers that practice would be helpful: in asking questions, encouraging students to express their ideas, and in their own learning from students.
