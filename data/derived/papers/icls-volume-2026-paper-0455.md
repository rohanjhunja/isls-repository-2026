# Sequencing Immersive Simulations for Cybersecurity: Task Complexity and Efficiency

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
VR is increasingly used for safety training, yet how the order of instruction and VR learning influences outcomes remains unclear. We invited students (N = 44) to complete two short vehicle security lessons using PowerPoint and VR materials presented in different orders. Although the order did not significantly affect test scores, students who studied PowerPoint first tended to perform better with fewer attempts, while those starting with VR often showed confusion by revisiting nonessential panels.

Autonomous driving features have advanced rapidly by integrating sensors, algorithms, and software, and are moving from speculation to everyday reality—bringing new safety and cybersecurity concerns alongside promised benefits. It’s essential to give learn ers the right knowledge and skills to defend digital infrastructure against potential attacks  (Rahman et al., 2020; Wusylko et al., 2024). Traditional approaches to cybersecurity education, such as presentation-based lectures and video modules, have long been used for their effectiveness in delivering structured, factual content. However, these methods often fall short when it comes to helping learners develop situational awareness or hands-on competencies required in real-world applications (Alnajim et al., 2023). VR-based training has shown potential in promoting active engagement, deeper cognitive processing, and stronger long-term retention (Kanade & Duffy, 2022). It is particularly beneficial for high-risk or complex learning contexts where learners can observe and interact with critical situations without real-world consequences (Sacks et al., 2013; Scorgie et al., 2024). However, a lack of clear conceptual scaffolding can lead learners, especially novices, to confusion and inefficient navigation in VR environments. Seo et al. (2019) found that although students viewed their immersive cybersecurity training as engaging and effective, many struggled to identify correct tool functions, timing, and basic controls within the simulation.  Taken together, these findings suggest that rather than using VR solely, it may be more effective to combine it with separate information delivery tools, rather than treating them as competing methods. Yet, an important instructional question remains: is it more effective to “do first,  read later,” or to “read first, then do” in safety-critical domains such as cybersecurity? Therefore, we need to examine when and for whom particular orders of pre-instruction and VR are more helpful, given the learning content, context, and learner characteristics (Yılmaz et al., 2025).  To address this, we pose the following research question:  How does instructional sequence (PPT → VR vs. VR → PPT) influence students’ performance and efficiency across two cybersecurity training scenarios of differing complexity? Undergraduate students used an Oculus Quest 2 headset to explore a Unity-based VR simulation that demonstrated vehicle signal jamming. Using the right-hand controller, they adjusted sliders and toggles to set noise and jamming parameters and initiated or stopped attacks with buttons. The system provided real-time feedback so that learners could manipulate parameters and observe the outcomes. Two perspectives were implemented: attacker and victim. In the attacker scenario, users sat in a vehicle following the victim’s car and operated the jammer. In the victim scenario, users drove behind an attacker vehicle without knowing that hacking was occurring. Scenario 1 included higher cognitive complexity than Scenario 2 to test sequencing effects under different task demands. Forty-four undergraduates from a southeastern U.S. public university (M_age = 22.19, SD  = 2.77; 35 male, 8 female, 1 undisclosed) participated for course credit or equivalent compensation. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two conditions: PowerPoint followed by VR (PPT→VR: n = 22) or VR followed by PowerPoint (VR→PPT: n = 22). Both groups received identical instructional content differing only in order. After consent and a pre-survey, participants completed two learning rounds, each followed by a short survey, and finished with a post-survey. Self-efficacy was measured using the scale by Pintrich et al. (1991). Learning 2794 outcomes were evaluated using a five-point rubric (inter-rater reliability = 0.91). High-performing learners connected frequency range, distance, and signal direction to jamming effects on sensors, while lower-performing learners showed only partial unders tanding. From the victim’s view, advanced learners recognized that a white screen represented sensor failure requiring immediate manual action. Baseline measures on their prior knowledge did not differ significantly between groups (p >.05). In Scenario 1 (more complex), which modality came first did not significantly affect performance or effort. Mean rubric scores  of the knowledge test were comparable (PPT→VR: M = 3.55, SD = 1.41; VR→PPT: M = 3.18, SD  = 1.62; t(42) = 0.795, p =.431). Median attempts did not differ reliably (Mdn = 2 vs. 3; U = 274.5, p =.430), although VR→PPT learners showed slightly longer interaction sequences (e.g., extra clicks/panel checks). Taken together, for the more complex scenario, sequence order showed no measurable advantage in scores, attempts, or interaction time. In Scenario 2 (less complex), a clearer sequence pattern emerged, suggesting a modest advantage for the PPT→VR condition in efficiency and accuracy. The proportion of students achieving the highest knowledge test score (5/5) was greater for PPT→VR (50%) than for VR→PPT (31.8%). Mean rubric scores also trended higher for PPT→VR (M = 3.73, SD = 1.45) than for VR→PPT (M = 3.09, SD = 1.57). Learners in the PPT→VR group required fewer attempts to reach success (Mdn = 1 vs. 1; U = 314, p =.033), indicating a tendency toward quicker and more efficient completion.

After completing both components, students in the PPT→VR group (M = 4.59, SD = 0.67) reported marginally higher perceived ease than those in the VR→PPT group (M = 4.09, SD = 1.02), showing a trend toward significance (p =.061). In the post hoc survey, the majority of students in each group indicated that the order they experienced “worked best” for them.  Notably, however, several VR-first students volunteered that PPT should have come first. Their comments capture a consistent tension: VR-first felt engaging and hands-on, but also confusing until PPT filled in the gaps. Representative responses include:  “I think doing the VR first was better because it gave me a chance to try it out without expectations, but I think I would’ve understood more if I had the context from the PowerPoint beforehand.”  “Yes because doing the simulation first I could get hands-on experience, but I was confused until I saw the PPT.” Three tentative patterns seem to emerge. First, sequence did not appear to uniformly change outcomes; its impact may depend on task demands. In the more complicated Scenario 1, both groups seemed to rely on trial-and-error regardless of prior PPT exposure. The problem itself may have required substantial in-VR figuring-out, potentially minimizing any advantage of entering with a roadmap. Second, once the task became simpler (Scenario 2), the order appeared to matter more. Students who had already internaliz ed key ideas via PPT tended to execute the VR task with fewer sidesteps, achieving mastery in fewer attempts, whereas some VR-first students appeared to carry over a “search mode” into an environment that no longer required it.  Third, students’ perceptions of the “best” order both aligned with and complicated the performance data. Most students defended the order they experienced, which could reflect a basic confirmation tendency or simply that both sequences were acceptable. Notably, a subset of VR-first students explicitly endorsed PPT-first, valuing VR’s immediacy and novelty while noting that an upfront explanation might have made the process “less confusing” and “helped me  know what to do better.” This suggests prior framing may help students calibrate their confidence to the task’s actual demands. From a design standpoint, these findings suggest two practical moves: (1) Match scaffolding to task complexity. When a scenario is dense with steps or obscure affordances, both groups will likely explore extensively; sequence alone may not simplify that work. In such cases, layering in targeted, in-VR hints or short checklists may benefit everyone. When a scenario is straightforward, however, a concise pre-VR briefing can streamline student action and reduce redundant attempts. (2) Support calibration, not just engagement. VR-first can spark interest, but some learners will misjudge what matters. Post-hoc comments show they noticed this mismatch. Lightweight orientation (e.g., a two-minute PPT or infographic) before VR —or embedded cues that surface only when students loop—could help students align their confidence with the task’s actual requirements. 2795 Alnajim, A.
