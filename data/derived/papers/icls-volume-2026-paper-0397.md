# AI Literacy in CS Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study systematically reviews 27 peer-reviewed studies on AI literacy in computer science (CS) education, including five broader STEM studies as comparative cases. Using PRISMA, it synthesizes evidence across conceptual, ethical, and pedagogical dimensions. Results show advances in data competencies but persistent challenges in ethics integration, assessment, and teacher preparation. The study highlights both STEM-wide trends and CS-specific needs, offering actionable guidance for designing coherent and sustainable AI literacy curricula.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has rapidly evolved, influencing sectors from healthcare and manufacturing to business and education (Spector & Ma, 2019). As AI increasingly permeates daily life, the need for AI literacy has become urgent (Grassini, 2023; Holmes & Tuomi, 2022). Long and Magerko (2020) define AI literacy as the ability to evaluate, collaborate with, and use AI across contexts. Beyond applied competency, it includes critical judgment, responsible use, and human –AI collaboration (Chiu et al., 2024; Grassini, 2023; Holmes & Tuomi, 2022). Computer science (CS) education is central in this transformation, since graduates design, deploy, and govern AI systems (Holmes & Tuomi, 2022; Slimi, 2021). Thus, CS programs must extend beyond programming to integrate technical skills (e.g., machine learning, data practices, explainable AI) and social-ethical competencies (e.g., bias mitigation, reasoning about societal impacts, collaboration with AI) (Chiu et al., 2024; Nuder & Dai, 2025). Labor market dynamics reinforce this need: early-career workers in AI-exposed fields, including software development, face declines in routine roles susceptible to automation, which highlights demand for skills that AI complements, such as critical thinking, ethics, and creativity (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025; Peng et al., 2023). Recent scholarship  synthesizing reviews reveals both advances and challenges. Zhang et al. (2025) identified progress such as definitional consensus, accessible tools, and project-based pedagogies, while noting gaps in ethics integration, standardized assessments, teacher development, and attention to non-STEM contexts. Similarly, a CS-focused synthesis (Nuder & Dai, 2025) echoed these gaps but further emphasized shortages of K–12 resources, weak assessment standardization, under-integration of ethics and e xplainable AI, and limited professional development. Such constraints hinder the development of scaffolded curricula needed to prepare AI innovators. Yet umbrella reviews rely on secondary analyses and may overlook emerging primary research (Zhang et al., 2025). To address this limitation, this study systematically reviews primary literature on AI literacy in CS education, including five broader STEM studies to provide comparative context. By doing so, we highlight both common patterns across STEM and unique emphases in CS, thereby distinguishing AI literacy in CS education from broader AI-related work in STEM. Our guiding research question is: What are the current approaches, opportunities, and challenges for cultivating AI literacy in CS education within the STEM context? This study employed a systematic literature review of AI literacy, guided by the PRISMA framework (Page et al.,

2021) to ensure transparency and rigor. The review focused on how AI literacy is cultivated in CS education, with

five broader STEM studies included in the final corpus for comparative context. Peer-reviewed studies published between 2010 and 2025 were searched  across four databases: Web of Science, ERIC, APA PsycNet, and EBSCOhost. The Boolean string combined terms related to AI and computational methods, education, and ethics or culture: (“Artificial intelligence” OR “AI” OR “Machine Learning” OR “Deep Learning” OR “computational thinking” OR “XAI” OR “explainable AI”) AND (“education” OR “curricul*” OR “Teach*” OR “pedagogy” OR “STEM”) AND (“ethic*” OR “cultur*”). These terms were derived from foundational AI competency frameworks (Chiu et al., 2024; Long & Magerko, 2020) and a meta-review highlighting persistent gaps in ethics and explainability (Nuder & Dai, 2025).

Inclusion criteria required studies to (1) focus on AI or related computational methods in education, (2) explicitly address AI literacy or competency, and (3) be English-language, peer-reviewed research. Exclusions applied to non-educational studies, dupl icates, reviews, or work lacking methodological transparency.  Because terminology in this area remains inconsistent, the search strategy was broader than the final inclusion criteria, but studies were retained only when full-text review confirmed an explicit focus on AI literacy or AI competency. 2617 The search yielded 16,105 records, which were reduced to 13,390 after duplicate removal. Title and abstract screening narrowed the set to 71, and full-text review produced a final corpus of 27 studies. A qualitative thematic synthesis was conducted, using deductive coding from research questions and inductive refinement to capture emergent themes (Thomas & Harden, 2008). This enabled systematic comparison across studies while highlighting novel insights into technical, ethical, and cultural dimensions of A I literacy. The final corpus of 27 studies spanned 2021– 2025, with a sharp increase in recent years: 24 published from 2022 onward. The corpus covers diverse regions, including the USA, East Asia, and Europe, reflecting global interest. Methodologically, the body of work is dominated by empirical, classroom-based designs (n = 21), including mixed-methods (n = 10) and qualitative research (n = 8). The remaining six studies are conceptual papers that propose frameworks or synthesize evidence. This distribution demonstrates both diversity and the emerging character of current AI literacy research.

Three central themes emerged from the synthesis of 27 studies, revealing a multifaceted and evolving understanding of AI literacy.  First, across the reviewed studies, AI literacy was increasingly framed beyond technical skills as a socio-technical construct. In 24 studies, AI literacy extended beyond programming or technical knowledge alone to include ethics, uncertainty, and broader social impacts. Here, socio-technical refers to understanding not only AI tools, data, and models, but also how human judgment, ethics, and social consequences shape AI systems. AI literacy is commonly framed as an integration of conceptual understanding and applied competence, distinguished by its focus on data, uncertainty, and complex systems (Chiu et al., 2024; Sanusi et al., 2022). Only six  studies explicitly adopted formal AI literacy definitions, while many framed AI-related learning through curriculum or activities rather than a formal construct definition. While broader STEM contexts emphasize foundational data and computational thinking, CS education extends these concepts to advanced topics like machine learning, explainable AI, algorithmic bias, and AI governance, treating ethical reasoning as an inseparable component of technical practice (Sanusi et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2023). Second, the reviewed studies emphasized active, authentic, and learner-centered approaches, especially project-based and hands-on learning using tools such as robotics, block-based programming, and ML platforms to foster deep, contextualized understanding (Dai et al., 2024; Naya-Varela et al., 2023). Project-based and handson approaches appeared in 23 studies. However, there is a critical emphasis that tool-driven engagement must be paired with conceptual and et hical reflection. Assessment was less consist ently articulated than pedagogy, and only a minority of studies reported fully developed assessment tools. Five studies described authentic assessment approaches such as grading rubrics, project portfolios, final presentations, exhibitions, and capstone projects that captured applied skills and ethical dispositions (e.g., Chiu et al., 2024; Dai et al., 2024). Third, the successful implementation of AI literacy is critically dependent on human and systemic factors, namely educators and policy. The literature positions educators not merely as instructors but as co-designers, facilitators, and technology brokers who adapt curricula to local needs and learn alongside students (Chiu et al., 2024). Human and systemic supports were emphasized in 22 studies.  This emergent role highlights the need for sustained professional development, although only a few studies examined it in sustained or evaluative ways. On a macro level, policy initiatives like AI4K12 are crucial drivers, but a persistent tension exists between rapid technological advancement and slower policy adaptation, underscoring the need for coordinated frameworks that ensure equity, sustainability, and a healthy balance between technical and ethical education (Touretzky et al., 2019).

This study contributes to the literature in three key ways. First, building on the three themes, it clarifies how CS both shares common challenges with STEM education and emphasizes unique competencies such as machine learning, explainable AI, and algorithmic governance. Situating CS education within the broader STEM landscape offers a comparative perspective that previous reviews have seldom provided.  Second, the findings on curricular and pedagogical approaches underscore the pivotal role of educators and policy frameworks. Project-based and inquiry-based designs in CS require systemic support and targeted professional development, while assessment remains less clearly articulated across the reviewed studies, highlighting the need for institutional and policy-level investment to sustain AI literacy initiatives. Third, recurring gaps across CS and STEM studies, including uneven ethics integration, a lack of standardized assessments, weak theoretical grounding, and limited detail on sustained teacher professional development, point to the necessity of unified frameworks and validated measures.
