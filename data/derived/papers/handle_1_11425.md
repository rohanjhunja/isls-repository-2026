# Towards Better Understanding Students’ Choices in Advanced Learning Technology

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
One main goal of research in the learning sciences is to promote autonomous, self-regulated learners who can make strategic choices during learning. Yet, prior studies have not systematically conceptualized, measured, and supported students’ various choices in a way that enhances our understanding of how students make choices and how strategic choices can be supported. We present the Metacognitive Choice-Making Model of Learning to better understand and support students’ fine-grained choices in advanced learning technologies. We demonstrate the applicability of the model using an example from an algebra intelligent tutor where students made choices on when to use visual scaffolding to aid their problem solving.

## Introduction

Choice-making as an important construct in learning research One major goal of education is to help students become self-regulated learners who can strategically choose to engage with resource s around them to advance their learning  (Schwartz & Arena, 2013).  When self-regulated learners make strategic choices during learning (e.g., choosing to ask for help during math problem solving), they would assess their own state of knowledge and the difficulty of the task (self-assessment: “Is this step too difficult for me?”), monitor their own performance (self-monitoring: “Am I doing this right?”, “Am I stuck?”), make informed choices (self-control: “I’ll ask for teacher’s help”), and reflect on their choice behavior (self-reflection: “did I really need help?”).  This cycle mirrors the three-phase model of self-regulated learning (i.e., forethought, performance, and self-reflection) where learners engage in processes such as self-assessment and planning, selfmonitoring and self-control, as well as self-reflection and self-evaluation (Zimmerman & Campillo, 2003). Measuring and supporting choices using advanced learning technologies Advanced, interactive learning technologies provide opportuni ties for better understanding and supporting students’ strategic choice-making behaviors during learning. These technologie s, such as Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITSs), enable the collection of fine-grained data on learner-system interactions (Kulik & Fletcher, 2016). This data captures  not only the detailed choices students make but also the outcomes of those choices, such a s whether a particular choice leads to correct /incorrect attempts. Further, ITSs could measure students’ behaviors in real-time and provide adaptive scaffolding by  recommending certain choice behaviors. For instance, a system could detect a student’s problem-solving struggles and recommend targeted behaviors, such as choosing to use visual scaffolding (Nagashima et al., 2023) to enhance their understanding. Past studies using advanced learning technologies have explored and supported students’ choice-making during learning  (Roll et al., 2011). For example, in studies on “choice-based assessments”  in Posterlet, an interactive learning environment where students could design a poster and get feedback (Cutumisu, et al., 2015), authors have found that, when students were given a choice of seeking either positive or negative feedback  on their poster design, the frequency of seeking negat ive feedback was positively correlated with learning gain on poster design principles. Past work, however, has not yet conceptualized students’ choice-making behaviors systematically. One reason lies in the inconsistent terminology used across st udies, such as “choice,” “help-seeking,” and “tool use” (Chin et al., 2019; Roll et al., 2011, Schwonke, 201 3), which influences how studies situate their findings in a theoretical background. Another reason is that these studies do not necessarily investigate students’ fine-grained, complex choice making in the environment  but rather focus their measurements on the aggregated data (e.g., overall mean frequency of a certain choice behavior) (Duffy & Azevedo, 2015). Relatedly, despite the importance of using temporal data in ex amining students’ self-regulated choice behaviors (Greene et al., 2021), past studies do not provide any insight s into how students’ choice behaviors might change over time. It is reasonable to think that learners’ adoption o f self-regulated choice behaviors is not consistent (Berna cki, 2017). For instance, learners may first use ineffec tive strategies to explore the learning environment  and may gradually learn to use ef fective strategies as they gain relevant skills. Aggregated data might obscure such meaningful changes in the choice behaviors. Addressing these gaps requires a more robust concep tual and methodological approach that considers the dynamic and temporal nature of choices during learning. Metacognitive Choice-Making Model of Learning To conceptualize students’ fine-grained choice making (that may change over time) a nd to guide future studies, we propose the Metacognitive Choice-Making Model of Learning  (Figure 1), a conceptual model that illustrates metacognitive steps in learners’ choice-making processes and behaviors. The model divides the choice-making processes into four phases (Pre-Intend, Intend, Choose to Implement/Choose not to Implement, and Reflect):

(1) Pre-Intend: A lea rner, when presented with a choice-making opportunity initially in this phase, is

still not sure whether to invest their effort to ad opt a new strategy. (2) Intend: Once a learner recognizes the benefits of using the strategy, they would then mov e to th e Intend phase. In th is phase, the learner is generally interested in using the strategy and understands  its benefits and potential drawbacks but has not yet used/implemented it. They may also assess the utility value of the task and/or self-efficacy. (3) Choose to Implement/Choose not to Implement: In this phase, a learner would show the behavior of either choosing to use/implement the strategy or choosing not to use/implement it. (4): Reflect: In this phase, a learner would reflect on their choice behavior —of using/implementing the strategy or not —to inform what they would do in the next choice-making opportunity (hence feeds back to the Intend phase). Figure 1 Metacognitive Choice-Making Model of Learning The Metacognitive Choice-Making Model of Learning was developed by integrating two existing models of self-regulated learning and behavior change. Specificall y, we integrated Oppezzo and Schwarz (2013)’s four-stage behavior change model of self-regulation and Zimme rman and Campillo’s cyclic self-regulation model (2003). Oppezzo and Schwartz (2013) abstracted behavior change models to create a four-stage model  (preintend, intend, implement, and inhabit stages) and discussed its application to self-regulated learning. Under this model, the goal of the behavior change is to reach the “inhabit” phase in which learners would sustain the behavior so that they would be able to use the strategy without being motivated to do so anymore. Zimmerman and Campillo (2003) describe self-regulated learning processes through a cyclical mod el that consists of “forethought,” “performance, ” and “self-reflection” phases. In the forethought phase, students analyze the presented task and set learning goals accordingly. In the performance phase, students carry out the learning task, monitor their own learning processes, and regulate their behaviors based on their observations. Finally, in the self-reflection phase, students evaluate their own performance and then react to it to improve their performance. If they identify areas for improvement, they may adaptively adjust their strategy use to enhance their performance. This cyclical process enables continuous learning and refinement of skills and strategies. We based our own model on Oppezzo and Schwatz (2013)’s behavior change model, adapting it to reflect the cyclic nature of self-regulated learning in Zimmerman and Campillo (2003) ’s model. The Metacognitive Choice Making Model of Learning  incorporates two distinct characteristics that address critical aspects of students’ choice making during learning. First, the model captures the  metacognitive processes involved when students choose not to engage with a given opportunity for action. For example, in the context of help seeking in an ITS, students may strategically decide not to use hints if they think that they can solve the presented proble m without additional scaffolding (Aleven et al., 2004). This type of strategic no implementation of a strategy is not typically outlined in previous models in an explicit way. Yet, explicitly recognizing these moments of strategic disengagement helps researchers capture a richer range of student  behaviors, as opposed to only accounting for instances when students choose to engage with a pre-defined ideal behavior (e.g., Uesaka et al., 2010). Second, the model also visualizes the cyclic nature of stud ents’ choice making, in accordance with Zimmerman & Campillo (2003). As stated earlier, students’ choices are dynamic and evolve over time (Greene et al., 2021), and it is hard to capture such changing behaviors with linear models that are widely used in behavior change literature. Traditional behavioral change models focus on maintaining a fixed target behavior (e.g., to quit smoking), rather than supporting the process of change/learning, which is central to learning science research (Ajzen, 1991). Application scenario: The case of algebra tutoring software We demonstrate the applicability of the model using an example in an algebra ITS where students make choices of whether/when to engage with visual scaffolding to aid their conceptual understanding and problem-solving.

## Study context: Algebra tutor with tape diagrams

We developed an ITS for solving equations in middle-school algebra where students can choose to ask for visual scaffolding. As shown in Figure 2, when solving the problems, students can, for each problem-solving step, choose to use diagrammatic scaffolding (called “diagrammatic self-explanation,” Nagashima et al., 2021) to aid their understanding of how to solve the symbolic equation (by using the “Use Diagrams?” button). If students choose to use diagrams, they will then select one option that depicts the correct next problem-solving step in the visual form and use it as a reference to work on the same step in the symbolic format (Figure 2, a). As the choice decision is up to the learners, they could also keep solving equations without requesting the diagrams (Figure 2, b). ## Method

We conducted a classroom  experiment with 140 5th-8th graders in the US. Students were randomly assigned to either the Scaffolded Choice (n = 69) or Un-scaffolded Choice (n = 71) condition. In both conditions, students used the tutor introduced above, but only in the Sc affolded Choice condition, they received additional metacognitive scaffolding that supports choice making. These metacognitive sc affolding included  (1) an interactive tutorial at the beginning that describe s how diagrams could benefit algebra  problem solving, (2) adaptive recommendations reminding students that they could consider choosing to use diagrams, and (3) a selfreflection learner dashboard that visualizes the learner’s own problem-solving success rate on steps where they requested diagrams vs. steps with no diagrams to facilitate students’ active comparison of their choice behaviors (the dashboard did not recommend certain choice behaviors). The dashboard was designed to appear after every four problems, showing  students their aggregated performance data from those most r ecent four problems. We prepared a pretest and posttest on conceptual and p rocedural knowledge in algebra. The study took place across five consecutive days. Students first worked on the pretest (for 20 minutes). Students then used the respective version of the tutor to practice algebra problems. On the last day, students worked on the posttest (for 20 minutes).

## Findings

This study explored whether the metacognitive scaffolding would help students make strategic choices on when to use diagrams and whether it would enhance learning outcomes (see Nagashima et al., 2023). We first compared students’ pretest score and posttest score on conce ptual and procedural knowledge of algebra in each condition, finding that students in the Scaffolded Choice cond ition outperformed their peers in the Un-scaffolded Choice condition on conceptual (𝛽 =.95, t(137) = 2.52, p =.01) and procedural learning (𝛽 =.53, t(137) = 2.08, p =.04). We then examined how students made choices within the ITS. On average, students in the Scaffolded Choice condition chose to use diagrams less frequently for each step (M =.12, SD =.10) than those in the Un-scaffolded Choice condition (M =.16, SD =.12), t(135.3) = 2.03, p =.04.  However, when we investigated students’ diagram use  over the course of learning, we found that students in the Supported Choice cond ition seemed to make choices strategically; they tended to use diagrams more often when encountering  new problem types, with little difference in frequency compared to t he Un-scaffolded Choice condition. Conversely, students in the Scaffolded Choice condition quickly reduced their use of diagrams when repeatedly encountering the same problem type, whereas those in the Un-scaffolded Choice condition continued to use diagrams more consistently. In other words, students in the Scaffolded Choice c ondition focused their diagram use on the tasks that are ne w or difficult to them, whereas those in the Un-scaffolded Choice condition did not show any such pattern. The application of the Metacognitive Choice-Making Model We then annotated our model with descriptions of the metacognitive processes exhibited by successful learners in the Scaffolded Choice condition. As shown in Figure 3, students were well-scaffolded (from the Pre-Intend phase to the Intend phase) to understand the benefits of using diagrams, there fore smoothly entering the cyclic process of self-regulated learning (i.e., from the Intend phase, Figure 3, a). Once in the Intend phase, learners then made strategic choices of using diagrams in situations where they thought they would benefit from diagrams (e.g., when they encountered a new  problem type, (Figure 3, b)) and strategically chose not to use diagrams if having diagrams would not offer a strong benefit (e.g., when they kept practicing t he same type of problem s, (Figure 3, c)). Finally, learners had an active opportunity for self-reflection using the dashboard, which visualized their problem-solving performance based on whether or not they had chosen  to use diagrams (Figure 3, d). This reflection process might have led to less frequent overall use of diag rams in the Scaffolded Choice condition. In some cases, the dashboard may have shown students that using of diagrams is not helpful for their performance. Those students might have formed an intent that they would not use diagrams but still benefited from their focused problem-solving practice without diagrams, leading to better learning outcom es. In contrast, students in the Un-scaffolded Choice condition  lacked metacognitive scaffolding and might have ove rly relied on the visual scaffolding without engaging in strategic choice making. As a result, they might have missed  the opportunity to test and strengthen their algebra problem-solving skills through reflective and intentional practice. Also, it is important to note that students’ choice decisions may have changed over time as they used the tutoring system (Figure 3, e). For example, it is possible that students initially (in the Intend phase) were interested in using diagrams and chose to do so (in the Choose to Implement phase). Yet, as they made progress, they may have noticed, through self-reflection (in the Reflect phase), that they could actually solve problems without using diagrams and therefore chose to use diagrams less frequently in the later opportunities. ## Discussion and conclusion

We introduced the Metacognitive Choice-Making Model of Learning  as a new model to better make sense of students’ strategic choice-making behaviors during learning. Unlike previous models, our model captures strategic choices of using and not using a certain strategy, making it possible to understand students’  strategic disengagement. As well, the model accounts for the cyclic nature of choice making, which helps ide ntify how students’ choice making may change over time. As illustrated above, the example case highlighted the importance of capturing these processes explicitly. However, we acknowledge that the case illustration of the model does not guarantee that the model can be applied to various other forms of choice making or to a wider range of adaptive learning technologies. Still, the model offers a guide for future research and in terventions that focus on deeply understanding and better supporting students’ strategic choice making using advanced learning technology.
