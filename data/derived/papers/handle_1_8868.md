# Investigating Synchronous & Asynchronous Feedback in an Elementary School Maker Education Classroom

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Providing and receiving feedback is a crucial aspect of K-12 education, and is foundational to building a strong student-educator relationship in project-based learning. Previous research has highlighted the importance of student documentation, thus we study the implementation of a mobile application prototype (CoCreator App) that encourages student documentation along with features for asynchronous educator feedback. We observed and analyzed how educators provided synchronous feedback when conversing in-person with students. Our analysis indicates a variety of differences between synchronous and asynchronous feedback. Asynchronous feedback was typically used for praise and encouragement, but also offered additional opportunities for educators to notice student struggles that may not have been visible in class. Synchronous feedback was largely used to provide process-oriented feedback. Looking ahead as the COVID-19 pandemic prevails, we suggest practices and workflows for maker educators to consider as they provide both synchronous and asynchronous feedback in interdisciplinary (and potentially hybrid) learning environments. Key terms: feedback, formative assessment, maker education, synchronous, asynchronous

## Introduction

Feedback is regarded as a primary component of form ative assessment (Black & Wiliam, 1998), where the intention is to communicate new information to the learner to modify his/her thinking to improve learning (Shute, 2008). Using Hattie & Timperley’s definition, feedb ack is information provided by an agent (such as a person, experience, or self) regarding aspects of one's performance or understanding, a “consequence” of performance in a spectrum from instruction to feedback (2007). For feedback to be helpful, the provider needs to take into account the many ways learners might interpret feedback, and characteristics like timing and how information is delivered (Havnes, Smith, Dysthe & Ludvigsen, 2012).

In maker education, a particular form of project-ba sed learning where students are tasked with using a variety of traditional and highly technical tools t o create physical artifacts, feedback is vital to e nsure students progress down a productive path. Feedback has typically been given synchronously, where teachers visit students' work areas, providing feedback and encouragement th roughout the construction process. In interdiscipli nary settings such as a makerspace, synchronous in-perso n feedback has demonstrated huge benefits to studen ts, as students and educators can troubleshoot problems quickly by using demonstrations and sharing tools. However, the prevalence of digital portfolio systems and the  expansion of remote learning experiences (especial ly due to the COVID-19 pandemic) has meant that maker educato rs are often providing feedback asynchronously. Asynchronous feedback has also positively impacted students’ self-efficacy and self-regulation too (Tseng, 2016; Litts et al., 2016). We are interested in exploring situations in maker education where there are avenues for both synchronous and asynchronous feedback.

In this study, we explore the following research questions: What types of feedback do maker educators provide at various stages of students’ interdisciplinary maker projects, and how do asynchronous and synchronous modalities impact the nature of this feedback?

## Literature Review & Prior Work

Educators often use technology as part of their ped agogical and formative assessment practices. Using learning management systems or collaborative editing softwar e has helped educators support their students’ need s asynchronously and synchronously (Ames, Harris, Dargusch & Bloomfield, 2021; Irons & Elkington, 2021). In Hattie and Timperley’s (2007) systematic review of literature on feedback processes, they identified four main types of feedback students typically rece ive: Task Feedback, Process Feedback, Self-Regulation Feedback, and Praise/Self Feedback. Task feedback provides students with information about whether their work is correctly executed or not; Process feedback enco urages deeper reflection on the extent of success t o which a task was done and areas for improvement; Self-Regulation Feedback challenges students to self-assess their work and develop self-directed learning skills; Praise/Self Feedback focuses more on the student rather than the task atICLS2022 Proceedings 759 © ISLS hand, and may not necessarily encourage students to invest more effort in a task (Van Boekel, Weisen & Hufnagle, 2021; Hattie and Timperley, 2007). Later in data an alysis, we use Hattie and Timperley’s categories to  identify instances of synchronous and asynchronous feedback that we observe. Synchronous In-Person Feedback as Formative Assessment During in-person maker experiences, educators are g enerally able to quickly determine students’ progre ss and struggles. These formative assessment opportunities  emerge either when students come to educators for clarifications or when educators notice students’ progress while pacing around the class, particularly when similar misconceptions surfaced across a sizable proportion  of students (Jayathirtha, Fields, Kafai & Chipps, 2020). Maker educators also often facilitate peer-to-peer feedback so that students interact with and learn from each other by discussing each other’s code or prototypes. This mode of feedback has its advantages because educators can command all students’ attention to troubleshoot or clarify their misconceptions immediately (Jayathirtha, Fields, Kafai & Chipps, 2020). The in-person setting also allows educators to improvise in the moment with just-in-time ideas (Vossoughi, Escudé, Kong & Hooper, 2013).

Asynchronous Online Feedback as Formative Assessment Educators might use online platforms for formative assessment, as online platforms afford higher visib ility of patterns across artifacts that students upload (Won g & Yang, 2017). A maker education class may take p lace online too, as observed during the COVID-19 pandemi c when schools switched to emergency remote teachin g, or in distance learning when students and educators  are at different geographic locations. In such cas es, the educator’s feedback would usually be personalized f or each student. Educators may be able to spot patt erns in students’ submissions, and thus identify common struggles (Jayathirtha, Fields, Kafai & Chipps, 2020). However, there may be missed opportunities where the entire class could learn together, as there are fewer or m inimal opportunities for peers to view each other’s work, depending on the type of platform (Fields, Jayathirtha & Kafai, 2019).

## Methodology

Our literature review has demonstrated affordances of both synchronous in-person and asynchronous onli ne feedback, thus we decided to conduct a study where maker classes occurred in-person during formal scho ol curriculum periods, with an added component of asynchronous online documentation for students and educators to use. This was an eight-week in-person classroom study with 24 fifth-grade students (11-year-olds) and 2 maker educators (one primarily taught music, the other vi sual art) in a public school in Singapore. All part icipating students had prior experience in maker education from activities in third and fourth grade. In these previous maker experiences, students kept a booklet to occasionally jot ideas or sketches down and had intermittently used Seesaw, a commercial mobile application, to share photograp hs and videos with other teachers and their parents  of the work they do in school for various subjects. However, the students were generally new to regularly documenting their own work and receiving feedback via a mobile application. The participating maker educators are strong advocates of documentation and iterative feedback, and were very familiar with using Seesaw to document their students’ activities. The maker education classes were held once a week for 1h 20 minutes during the time these teachers would normally provide visual art and musi c instruction. The first author observed the in-per son classroom sessions during the study.

Content & Curriculum The participating teachers Mrs Lee and Mr Tan (name altered) created this maker education curriculum for their fifth-grade students three years ago when this scho ol’s makerspace first began running. The school its elf was designed by Singapore’s Ministry of Education to be   a lead school of maker education, therefore the s chools’ teachers participated in additional professional development with the ministry’s Education Technology Division. As Mrs Lee is a visual art teacher and Mr Tan is a music teacher, both decided to collaborate to devel op an interdisciplinary curriculum for the term (2-3 mont hs long) where students would incorporate both elem ents of visual art and music in their projects, and also learn other concepts such as computer programing, circuitry, and material science.

For this eight-week study, Mrs Lee and Mr Tan’s pr imary learning objectives for their students included:

1) to understand and implement the design thinking process that the Stanford d.school established-Em pathy,

Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test; 2) learn to compos e Singaporean folk songs with the right chords and notes through computer programming with Scratch; and 3) apply aesthetic design principles and knowledge of various types of art materials to sculpting. For the first two lessons, students embarked on individual assignments to learn computer programming with Scratch and circuitry with the Makey Makey microcontroller, then worked in groups ICLS2022 Proceedings 760 © ISLS of 4 to 5 to develop their projects for the remaini ng weekly lessons. The maker educators tasked stude nts with using the design thinking process, requiring studen ts to interview non-teaching staff members to ident ify their hobbies and interests, and then construct a musical  instrument using recycled and craft materials foll owing the non-teaching staff members’ preferences. Each group assigned one or two students as “documentation lea ders” who were responsible for taking and uploading photographs of work in progress and self-reporting their group’s motivation levels.

Features of the CoCreator App The CoCreator App (Figure 1) was developed to encou rage students to upload photographs of their works-inprogress with hashtags related to content and emoti ons, to self-report motivation levels throughout th eir maker classes, and to input comments or descriptions about their work in a text box below. Educators are notified about new posts that students make on their account, and also have text boxes where they are encouraged to p rovide feedback to students. Students receive a notification when the educator comments on their posts. Figure 1 Screenshot of the CoCreator App’s interface In class, the educators instructed students to use  the CoCreator App throughout the 1.5-hour period t o document activities and assignments they are workin g on. The educators encouraged students to document photographs of works-in-progress, instead of only s howing finished work. To provide a bit more structu re to documentation, the educators assigned at least one student per group to be the “documentation leader” so that this student will be responsible for taking photographs of his group’s work and reminding his group mates to document any individual work. The educators also suggested that students should take at least five photographs spaced out throughout the 1.5-hour period, and not a bunch of photographs during the last few minutes. The objective of the CoCreator App is to assist ed ucators in noticing information about students or groups that they may not have observed during in-person cl asses, through data from students’ photographs, com ments and/or self-reported motivation levels. From this study, we observe that educators choose the modality in which they provide the feedback (i.e. either synchronous or asynchronous or both), then ponder over the feedback they are about to provide. Usually, the modality where they encounter students’ work prompts them to consider certain aspects for feedback, thus the type of feedback the y provide synchronously and asynchronously usually differs for similar tasks or stages in students’ projects.

## Data Collection & Analysis

We examined three primary types of qualitative data: educators’ asynchronous feedback on students’ posts using the CoCreator App, educators synchronous feedback g iven to students as captured by in-class video-reco rded observations, and field notes that the first author took during the in-person observations. For in-class synchronous feedback sessions, we obs erved how educators interacted with students to deliver the feedback. Although the first author took field notes during the class, we added additional context and observations to these notes  by watching video recordings of in-class sessions. We base our analysis on traditional interaction analysis (Erikson, 2006), where we ground our observations focused on modalities of interaction such as pace of speech, tone of speech, shifts in body language and posture, changes in facial expressions, hand gestures etc. We generated transcripts of selected observati ons of feedback sessions to document their content and attributes related to student-educator interaction.  From these transcripts, we locate key moments wher e the educator modifies tone and expression to emphasize certain points while providing feedback, and where the students ask clarifying questions or respond to the educator’s questions. We would mark a phrase or behavior as “feedback” if the educator provides additional ideas, support, clarification, or correction to a student’s activity or work in class. Examples are demonstrated in the next section. We categorized educators’ feedback into the four m ain types of feedback that Hattie and Timperley had identified: Task Feedback, Process Feedback, Self-Regulation Feedback, and Praise/Self Feedback (2007). Next, for each type of feedback, we further categorized t hem into sub-codes based on emerging themes we obse rved from content and tone: encouraging, where the educator wrote positive comments meant to motivate the student, checking in, where the educator suggests that the student foll ows up either on the application or in class, and troubleshooting, where the educator points out where a student may  be stuck or is doing something wrong, unplanned, or unexpected.

For select situations where data is available for both synchronous and asynchronous settings on the same stage of a group’s project, we compared how educato rs delivered feedback via two different modalities,  using field notes to capture synchronous feedback observed during in-class sessions and log data on the CoCreator App to capture asynchronous feedback.

## Findings & Discussion

The following examples show educators providing stu dents with feedback synchronously in class and asynchronously through the CoCreator App, where stu dents document their project work by uploading photographs they had taken in class and self-reporting motivation scores for specific tasks. First, we tallied the types of asynchronous feedback we observed from the CoCreator App. Overall, there were 10 occurrences of asynchronous feedback-2 of  which were task feedback, 0 process feedback, 1 se lfregulating feedback, and 7 praise/self feedback. The total number of asynchronous feedback occurrences paled in comparison to the total number of synchronous in-cl ass feedback (100+ occurrences) across 5 observatio n sessions. On average, both Mr Tan and Mrs Lee individually rotate to each of the 5 student groups at least twice during each class to provide feedback. This means each group should receive at least four instances of synchronous feedback in one class, and over the 5 observation sessions, each group will have received at least 20 instances of synchronous feedback, hence totaling to 100+ expected occurrences overall. During each weekly class, we observed at least 1 ou t of 5 groups. For synchronous sessions that we observed in detail with fieldnotes, task feedback o ccurred overall 18 times, process feedback 5 times,  selfregulation feedback once, and self/praise feedback thrice (although one of which was criticism, not praise). Task feedback occurrences were in the majority, and task and process feedback mostly intersected with the sub-codes checking in or troubleshooting. Synchronous self-regulating feedback happened the least number of times overall, and the particular instance we observed intersected  with encouraging, where the educator told a group of past emotional challenges that a student group from last  year had faced. Two instances of self/praise feedb ack intersected with encouraging because the educators praised students for completing a certain stage of their project; the third instance of self/praise feedback intersected with troubleshooting as the educators realized students ran into issues in their project, not because they did not understand a task, but because they executed th e task in a lackadaisical manner (as perceived by the educators). For synchronous feedback, they are task feedback + troubleshooting (an example shown in the quote from Mr Tan). ## Discussion & Conclusion

This study explores feedback given from an educator  to students in an in-person maker classroom where both asynchronous and synchronous modes are available. The educators used synchronous feedback to provide detailed process-related suggestions for all groups and to offer immediate help when students ran into difficulty, whereas they used asynchronous feedback mainly for encouragement. However, there were a couple of occasions where students indicated confusion in concepts or process es in the CoCreator App, rather thanduring face-to-face interactions with the teacher.

In this study, it seems that the educators mostly used the CoCreator App to check students’ work briefly and provide simple feedback, leaving more detailed feedback for synchronous time in class. During a po stimplementation interview, educators indicated they worried asynchronous feedback might not be noticed by ICLS2022 Proceedings 764 © ISLS students as they are only able to use the CoCreator App when using the school’s iPads during their once-a-week class sessions. Results from this study may have be en different if the educators allotted more time to  provide various kinds of feedback on the CoCreator App, and if the educators structured the in-person session for students to review feedback they received through the CoCreator App. More attention to in-person and synchronous feedbac k, when it’s available, is a good thing!  Future iterations of the CoCreator App could optimize this  face-to-face feedback by automatically evaluating student posts and notifying educators of potential concerns. For example, the app could detect a drop in motivation levels or increased use of hashtags related to emotions such as frustration or dejection. Another area for potential research is to study sel f-regulation feedback-educators providing feedback to students about self-assessment of their work. For this participating school, the educators had organized a peer feedback session where students rotated to different groups to provide feedback to one another based on rubrics and criteria. Although we observed minimal feedback from the educators about students’ participation in this peer feedback session, in another study, we can investig ate students’ processes and perspectives of peer-to-peer feedback (Parenti, 2013). For future design iterations, the CoCreator App could also support asynchronous peerto-peer feedback by providing students with feature s to comment on other groups’ work. This would hope fully enhance the amount of self-regulation feedback that occurs in the maker classroom, as educators will have more opportunities to identify how students are assessing their own and their peers’ work.
