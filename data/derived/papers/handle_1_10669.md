# Visualizing Observation: The Role of AR App-Supported Science Learning in Fostering Families’ Scientific Observation

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To support scientific observation, we conducted a research study using a place-based, mobile augmented reality (AR) app to assist families in learning about healthy habitats for pollinators. By analyzing videos and transcripts from 17 families, we found the significant effects of seven learning activities on families’ scientific observation along four dimensions. The study contributes findings related to how engaging with various features of an AR app can influence aspects of families’ scientific observation.

## Introduction

Scientific observation is a crucial science practice (Eberbach & Crowley, 201 7) that develops from everyday noticing (Ericsson, 2014). Eberbach and Crowley (2009) provide a descriptive delineation of four components of scientific observation — offering a valuable perspective for examining this practice. Additionally, investigations into the developmental trajectories have identified a continuum for scientific observation: everyday, transitional, and expert (Eberbach & Crowley, 2009). Furthermore,  researchers argue that developing from novice to expert observers increases learners’ capacity to engage in scientific reasoning (Eberbach & Crowley, 2009; Hmelo-Silver et al., 2007; Hmelo‐Silver & Pfeffer, 2004). Learning to become a scientific observer requires complex, deliberate educational engagement (Eberbach & Crowley, 2009).  Research has shown that mobile technology can scaffo ld learners with scientific perspectives, guidance, and interactive experiences to enhance scientific obs ervation and engagement in informal science learning (Land et al., 2022; Zimmerman et al., 2021). Building upon this literature, our research focus es on  understanding the effects of  designed learning activities of a n augmented reality (AR) app on  scientific observation. We elucidate how learning a ctivities influence scientific observation and provide recommendations for the design and study of learning to observe. Conceptual and analytical framework We adopted the four components of the Biology Obser vation NEOP framework proposed by Eberbach and Crowley (2009) for analyzing scientific observation: Noticing (N) refers to picking up pertinent aspects of a phenomenon connected to disciplinary knowledge at a n individual and species level; Expectations (E) refer to separating observations from beliefs and opinions w hile beginning to coordinate hypotheses, theoretica l frameworks, and evidence; Observational records (O) refer to developing the ability to generate records that can be used as data within investigations; Productive dispositions (P) refer to seeking out opportunities for observation and information while developing an interest in the discipline. We drew on the findings of two studies (Eberbach et al., 2012; Hmelo-Silver et al., 2007) to distinguish between expert and amateur observers  within the NEOP framework  based on the Structure-Behavior-Function framework (SBF)  concepts related to complex systems in science. The SBF framework consists of three components: Structures (S) represent the elements within a system; Behavior (B) denotes the processes through which these system structures accomplish their goals or purposes; Functions (F) describe the specific roles played by elements within the system (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2007). The SBF framework is recognized as a representation of how biological experts understand complex system s (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2007). In our AR app, we designed seven learning activities as scaffolds to direct and guide learners’ exploration and engagement in  scientific observation. Consequently, we are interested in the  effects of these learning activities on  the family’s scientific observation. Specifically, in this study, we ask: Are there differences in the influence of  different learning activities on  learners’ overall scientific observation  practices? Are there differences in the influences of the learning activities on each dimension (i.e., NEOP) of scientific observation?

## Study design and participants

The study employs  a placed-based, AR app-assisted outdoor science learning experience to investigate how families learn about pollinators at an environmental center. The app is GPS-triggered and can track families’ location on a digital map. The app provides scientific content about pollinators through text and photos, uses AR to show facts that are not visible to the naked eye and uses a camera to allow families to take pictures of pollinators and record their observations. In the app, there are seven learning activities (Se e Figure 1) designed to facilitate the learning process.

Families with children aged 5-12 were recruited on-site at a nature center serving rural areas, small towns, and one university community. Adults provided written consent, and children provided verbal assent. Each family completed a 30-45-minute exploration across five micro-learning sites: the Pollinator Garden, the Milkweed Patch, the Front Yard Stage, the Rockwall Garden, and the Pond. Families’ learning experiences were captured via hat-mounted GoPro cameras and iPad screen recordings. Data sources and analysis The study analyzed 17 families with 48 people in total (25 adults, 23 children). The families’ videos (12 hours) were professionally transcribed and checked for accuracy by the researchers. The transcripts were coded according to the behavioral descriptions of NEOP, with corresponding scores assessed based on the SBF score (Table 1). The seven activities were used as the seven levels of the independent variable: learning activity. NEOP was used as the four dependent variables. NEOP, as the four components of scientific observation, are inseparable, interrelated, and mutually influential (Eberbach & Crowley, 2009). Therefore, they can be treated as a  unified entity for measurement. Thus, their sum can be seen as a manifestation of the overall scientific observation ability and was used as another dependent variable. We performed an analysis of variance (ANOVA) using mean values (Table 2).

## Findings and discussion

By conducting a one-way ANOVA, we found (a) a significant difference in the effect s of the seven learning activities on learners’ scientific observation and (b) significant differences in the effects of the seven  learning activities on each aspect of the learners’ NEOP scores (Table 2). As shown in Figure 2, regarding overall scientific observation, the Checklist activity exhibited the highest mean at 6.94, while the scores across the four dimensions of scientific observation  were remarkably close, demonstrating a relative balance  of each NEOP  aspect. Conversely, Text and Graphic Content yield the lowest mean at 2.75. The t-test for both yields a significant result with t=6.536 and a p-value <0.001, underscoring a discernible disparity in the impact of various activities on overall scientific observation capability.

In the Checklist activity, learners engage d in a cumulating activity that included reviewing th eir photographs while reflecting and responding to the research team’s pre-generated questions. This activity fostered sensory (observational), emotional, and cognitive engagement, which are indicators of  effective learning (Fredricks et al., 2004; Vedder-Weiss, 2022). In contrast, other activities that were more singularly focused, such as reading text and graphic content, tended to have a lesser influence on the learners’ observation proficiency. The three learning activities, Photo-taking, Checklist, and Bringing It All Together, also scored higher than other activities in the O and N dimensions  of NEOP, indicating that generative activities played a favorable role in fostering the development of scientific observation. This finding underscores that generative activities are instrumental in cultivating the ability to document scientific observations. These findings imply that the influences of AR learning activities (excluding the checklist) on th e four dimensions of scientific observation were not uniform or evenly distributed. A careful exa mination of these data reveals that different activities placed varying emphasis on promoting NEOP. ## Implications

Developing scientific observation practices demands  purposeful guidance and technological support. Thi s study has two major implications  for learning designers and researchers. First, desi gners and researchers can consider using multimedia, photo-taking, and other technological affordances to faci litate scientific observation and its four dimensions, NEOP. The mobile app we designed i ncluded various functions such as GPS tracking, photo-taking, and AR scanning. By integrating these technological resources into a  nature center experience, we supported the learners’ scientific observation s of pollinators. When families were provided with scientific information and guidance on scientific observation practices, they tended to notice and reason about natural phenomena in more expert-like ways.

Second, while our study is still preliminary, our findings suggest designers can consider integrating various observation activities. We found it beneficial to incorporate a varied set of activitie s, as they effectively supported the four facets of scientific observation to differing degrees. For example, learners acquired scientific knowledge through reading text and graphic informat ion, which advanced families’ scientific re asoning ability; the photo-taking activity directed families’ attention to notice more scientific phenomena. Additionally, designers can incorporate summative, reflective activities that draw upon learners’ cognitive and sensory engagement, such as the Checklist used here, to advance scientific observation development and foster productive dispositions.
