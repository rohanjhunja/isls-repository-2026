# Qualitative Research in the Age of LLMs: A Human-in-the-Loop Approach to Hybrid Thematic Analysis

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper introduces a hybrid (deductive-inductive) human-in-the-loop (HHitL) framework for thematic analysis (TA), leveraging large language models (LLMs) while prioritizing human interpretation and decision-making. Grounded in reflexive TA, our resulting six-stage framework is guided by design principles of interoperability, human-LLM transactivity, scalability, and transparency. This method enables mixed-methods displays (e.g., network graphs, code frequency/diversity/magnitude metrics) supporting productive data triangulation. We demonstrate this HHitL-TA approach by analyzing engineering students’ motivations based on Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT), illustrating the framework’s application, principles, and triangulation potential. While acknowledging potential risks, the HHitL design actively mitigates passive researcher roles through transactivity and transparency, promoting humans as generative, active directors of the analysis. This work contributes a structured methodology for rigorous, scalable qualitative inquiry using LLMs.

## Introduction

The introduction of large language models (LLMs), t ransformer-based generative AI, into qualitative research is a debated yet seemingly inevitable development in the social sciences. While concerns about methodological rigor and preserving human subjectivity exist  (e.g., Lissack & Meagher, 2024; Roberts et al., 2024), the  potential benefits of scale and efficiency drive adoption, reinforced by promises of enhanced quality and reproducibility. Recognizing LLMs as potential disruptors of qualita tive inquiry, this paper explores thematic analysis  (TA)—a traditionally flexible and human-centered method —as suitable for LLM integration. We propose a si x-stage Hybrid Human-in-the-Loop Thematic Analysis (HHitL-TA) framework grounded in reflexive TA principles (Braun & Clarke, 2019) to mitigate risks and mainta in rigor. This approach aims  to iteratively build AI trustworthiness while preserving researcher subject ivity and ensuring a rigorous, transparent process.  We demonstrate this framework by applying inductive co ding within the deductive  Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT; Lent, 2002) structure to understand engineering students’ motivations, summarizing the opportunities and risks of using LLMs to scale reflexive TA.

Theorizing LLMs in qualitative research: A reflexive lens Integrating LLMs into qualitative research sparks critical debate. Proponents see potential for enhanced analytical breadth and systematic coding (e.g., Bano et al., 2023; Tai et al., 2024), while  critics raise concerns about hallucinations, biases, and compromising human-centric inquiry (Roberts et al., 2024). We adopt a pragmatic perspective, viewing human-LLM partnership as potentially synergistic. Discrepancies between LLM outputs and human interpretations are accepted as not only inevitable (Bano, Zowghi, et al., 2023a) but as productive sites for negotiating richer meaning. Key opportunities for incorporating LLMs, therefore, include expedited an alysis, broader contextualization, improved internal coding  consistency, and LLMs acting as “third-party” perspectives for researcher reflection (Bano, Hoda,  et al.,  2023). Conversely, significant challenges include privacy,  bias mitigation, contextual limitations, over-reliance, transparency, and intellectual property rights (Bano, Hoda, et al., 2023). The risk  of “sloppy science” and epistemological issues rega rding LLMs as meaning-makers without nuanced human experience and embodied cognition are valuable concerns (Lissack & Meagher’s, 2024). Despite this, LLM applications are rapidly evolving (Rothschild et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024)  that continue to demonstrate efficiency, transparency, accessibility, and inter-rater reliability. As integration of LLMs appears inevitable, critical  design for its use in the social science s is essential. This paper adopts reflexive thematic analysis (RTA; Braun & Clarke, 2019) as a central theoretical framework to guide the design and evaluation of our HHitL-TA approach. In contrast to codebook thematic analysis  (e.g., Boyatzis, 1998), which focuses primarily on  objectivity and inter-rater reliability  (IRR) within a pre-defined theoretical structure, RTA champions an active, ref lexive, and generative process of meaning-making and storytelling, valuing researcher subjectivity as an integral re source. For our framework, although selectively incorporating IRR, we choose RTA as our primary theoretical target for this work. We adapt RTA, however, for human-LLM interplay, using the subjectivity of both to iteratively refine interpretations and enha nce trustworthiness, while striving to keep the researcher in an active, generative position. Objectives This paper aims to: (1) Introduce and explicate a six-stage Hybrid Human-in-the-Loop Thematic Analysis (HHitLTA) framework designed to address critical debates and methodological challenges concerning LLM integr ation in qualitative research; (2) Describe the custom so ftware tool  developed to facilitate this HHitL-TA process, highlighting its essential role in this implementation; (3) Illustrate the frameworks’ application through an analysis of engineering students’ motivations using SCCT; and (4) Discuss the potential benefits and inherent c hallenges of this approach for enhancing the rigor and scalability of reflexive thematic analysis (RTA). System design: Supporting hybrid human-in-the-loop thematic analysis To facilitate HHitL-TA, we are developing a software tool that incorpor ates dedicated LLM clients for various stages of analysis. Key components include: definin g the LLM persona (system instructions); preparing relevant data payloads (e.g., text, codes, and research question); specifying the analytical task;  defining structured output formats (e.g., JSON schemas); and integrating these into prompt templates. The Python tool processed docx files, chunked large texts, called an LLM API (Gemini 1.5 Pro via Vertex AI), and saved the structured output  to files (xlsx) and visualizations (png) for human review, modification, and transparency. The open-source project, under ongoing development, is available on GitHub (https://github.com/encorelab/ai-theme-analyser) (Wiebe, 2025). Design principles: Interoperability, transactivity, scalability, and transparency Four key design principles have guided the tool development:

1. Interoperability: Ensuring seamless data exchange via dynamic conversion between human-readable

(docx/xlsx/png) and LLM-readable (markdown/JSON) formats.

2. Human-LLM transactivity: Maintaining human oversight through staged analysis with access to

intermediate LLM outputs/reasoning, continuous opportunities for intervention, and progressive prompt priming with approved resources (e.g., codebook, research question, themes, etc.), culminating in a human-led synthesis and final report.

3. Scalability: Leveraging LLM efficiency by programmatically saving structured outputs to reduce

manual data management, enabling analysis of hundreds of documents while necessitating deliberate human engagement with staged outputs to avoid passive reliance.

4. Transparency: Emphasizing methodological rigor and trustworthiness from both the LLM and

researcher by: (a) designing LLM interactions that output justifications for key decisions and intermediate analytical steps for human review and intervention; and (b) promoting clear reporting guidelines for researchers to document their roles in the HHitL-TA process, ensuring accountability. The framework and development of this tool emerged from the practical need to analyze a large dataset efficiently for timely actionable responses to findings. The adoption of LLMs and subsequent development of this framework and tool were primarily driven by the scale of our dataset, compr ising nearly 1000 student reflection documents, desired for timely student feedback and program eva luation and implementation. Of those reflections, 175 from the initial time point were included in this paper’s thematic analysis. Applying HHitL-TA: An illustrative analysis of engineering student motivations We illustrate the HHitL-TA method by analyzing undergraduate engineering students’ motivations for  joining their program, drawing upon SCCT and factors identified by  Wint (2023)  in the  engineering context. The identified factors encompassed influences on students’ self-efficacy, learning experiences, and career expectations (e.g., family, prior experiences, STEM affinity/efficacy, building efficacy, career goals, etc.). Prior to TA, a latent class analysis (LCA) based on 11 SCCT-related factors verified the presence of nine factors within our data and determined five distinct profiles of students. HHit L-TA was used to qualitatively elaborate the characteristics of each profile. This paper includes results from Class A, a single profile from that analysis. This TA aimed to understand: How are undergraduate engineer ing design students' choices to become engineers motivated by elements of social cognitive  career theory (SCCT)?  We examined the five latent class profiles independently, then performed a cross-case analysis. The following outlines the six HHitL-TA stages that were employed.

A hybrid human-in-the-loop framework for thematic analysis While widely used in qualitative research, t hematic analysis continues to face critiques for its common methodological ambiguity of implementation in practice, necessitating continued explication and theoretical grounding (e.g., Braun & Clarke, 2006; Byrne, 2022; Saldaña, 2021). Despite the significant influence of Braun and Clarke’s (2006) well-established six-stage framework for thematic analysis ([1] familiarization with data; [2] generating initial codes; [3] generating themes; [4] reviewing potential themes; [5] defining and naming themes; and [6] producing the report), this framework emphasizes flexibility, enabling researchers to tailor it to the specific demands of their inquiries. Our HHitL-TA framework (outlined in Figure 1) adapts this model, strategically integrating the capabilities of LLMs. While building upon Braun and Clarke’s (2006) model, we combine the theme generation stages (3-5) and incorporat e distinct stages for full dataset coding and within-case analysis, while emphasizing theory-driven grounding for inductive coding and theming. Stage 1: Familiarization and determination of constructs The primary goal of this initial stage is twofold: for the researcher to gain familiarity with the dat aset and to establish and verify a set of theory-driven constructs providing a deductive framework f or subsequent inductive analysis. This hybrid approach integrates familiarization with developing an a priori conceptual structure, aiming for greater efficiency and theoretical grounding th an purely inductive familiarization, while ensuring  chosen constructs are relevant to the specific dataset. Th is stage i s primarily human-led but can be assisted by large language models (LLMs). The implementation involves  researchers identifying relevant theories (e.g., S ocial Cognitive Career Theory [SCCT]), defining initial high-level constructs, and then reading a sample of the data to assess the applicability of these constructs, poten tially involving manual note-taking or initial coding. An LLM may optionally assist in coding a data sample accor ding to preliminary construct definitions, using LL M model prompts similar to Stage 2 (for initial coding). Based on this familiarization and verification process, researchers refine the construct definitions, potentially mergi ng, modifying, or discarding constructs lacking rel evance or sufficient presence. Achieving inter-rater reliability on sample coding ca n strengthen confidence in the final constructs. The main product of this stage is the v erified construct codebook containing the definitio ns of the overarching theoretical dimensions guiding the analysis. In our illustrative study applying this stage, fami liarization was guided by SCCT (Lent, 2002), specifically factors identified by Wint (2023). The  primary researcher initially defined 11 SCCT-related factors. To verify these constructs within the engineering student reflections, the researcher and LLM independently coded a 20% sample (n=39 documents). Based on low prevale nce (zero and one occurrence), two factors were eliminated. Robust inter-rater reliability (Cohen’s Kappa > 0.7) was achieve d between the human and LLM for the remaining nine constructs on the sample. These final nine constructs (e.g., Subject Affinity, Prior Experiences, Social Influences) with their refined definitions f ormed the construct codebook, which was subsequently formatted as JSON for LLM use in Stage 2. As an opt ional step separate from the core HHitL-TA process but useful for later comparative analysis in this study, the LLM coded the full data set based only on the presence/absence of these nine verified constructs, enabling a Latent Class Analysis (LCA) to group students into distinct motivational profiles. Reporting for this stage would typica lly summarize the outcome, for instance: "Stage 1 resulted in a verified 9-dimension construct codebook derived from SCCT (Win t, 2023), confirmed via initial coding (IRR Kappa > 0.7) on a data subset, which framed the subsequent inductive analysis.” or alternative RTA familiarization (Braun & Clarke, 2006, 2019) may be reported. Stage 2: Initial inductive coding within constructs The goal of Stage 2 is to generate an initial set o f inductive codes capturing the specific nuances and variations related to each theory-driven construct dimension identified in Stage 1. T his HHitL-TA stage focuses inductive elaboration within these predefined constructs, ali gning the analysis with the research questions and theoretical grounding by leveraging the LLM’s pattern-recognition capabilities under close human supervis ion. Implementation employs an iterative process using t he construct codebook (from Stage 1) and a representative data sample. Generally, the researcher prompts the LLM to generate potential codes from a small data s ubset, providing context like the construct codebook and r esearch question. The LLM identifies relevant text excerpts and proposes codes, providing justifications, confidence scores, and the source text. The researcher then critically reviews this structured output (e.g., spreadsheet), approves relevant codes, merges similar ones, discards irrelevant ones, refines definitions, and crucially, can adjus t LLM instructions or contextual information (e.g.,  exclusion criteria within the construct codebook) based on th e output quality. This cycle of LLM generation and human review/refinement continues with progressively wide r data samples (e.g., 10, 20, then 40 documents) until the researcher determines the initial code set adequate ly captures variations within the constructs across  the sample, potentially performing an optional final verificati on run. The sample size of at least 40 documents is recommended, based on prior research suggesting a saturation of meta-themes within a 20 to 40 interview range (Hagaman & Wutich, 2017). These codes represent the initial code set that wil l be applied across all documents. The primary product is a human-verified initial code set (code names, definitions,  examples) organized under their respective parent construct (or construct dimensions). In our illustrative study, this stage utilized the nine SCCT construct dimensions and a sample of 40 documents randomly selected.  An iterative widening approach was employed using o ur Python tool’s generate_initial_codes command. The LLM generated codes per construct dimension, pr oviding justifications and source excerpts. The human researcher reviewed these outputs, merged similar codes (n=8), identifi ed and filtered irrelevant ones (n=68), and subsequently updated exclusion criteria in the construct codebook to improve LLM focus. A verification run (generate_verify_codes) using the refined instructions and preliminary code set on the 40-document sample produced additional relevant codes (n=31), which were approved by the researcher. The resulting initial verified code set comprised 202 unique inductive codes, organized by construct (min=3, max=36, M=22.44, SD=10.88), and formatted as JSON with code definitions and exa mple extracts for Stage 3, with each linked to one of the nine parent SCCT construct dimension. Stage 3: Full dataset coding and code refinements Moving from initial code development of the previous stage, Stage 3 systematically applies this verified code set across the entire dataset, leveraging the LLM for comprehensive coverage while maintaining the hybrid deductiveinductive approach. This involves allowing the LLM to propose additional emergent codes if compelling data warrants them, provided they fit within the establi shed Stage 1 construct dimensions. The focus shifts  from code generation on a sample to exhaustive application gu ided by reflexive interpretation. Implementation be gins by providing the LLM with the initial code set, construct codebook, and full dataset. The model prompt instructs the LLM to identify relevant excerpts and apply existing codes, while also permitting the proposal of new codes with justifications where necessary within the defined c onstruct scope. Crucially, human researchers critic ally review the LLM's codi ng, particularly validating any newly proposed emer gent codes through approval, rejection, modification, or merging to ensure grounding in human interpretation and the theoretical framework. This review can occur iteratively. Finally, any validated emerg ent codes are integrated, and researchers may use L LM assistance (e.g., a merging tool) to consolidate si milar codes within the expanded set, further refini ng the codebook. Significant issues might necessitate revisiting Stage 2. The primary products are a fully coded dataset and a complete, refined set of codes.

Stage 4: Theme generation and review within constructs This stage utilizes the fully coded dataset from Stage 3 to develop initial themes by identifying patterns within the data. Distinct from thematic analysis approaches seeking a single set of overarching themes (e.g., Braun & Clarke, 2006), the HHitL-TA fra mework typically generates themes within each prede fined construct (or construct dimension), potentially further separated by subgroup for comparative analysis. This approach allows for nuanced exploration of patterns specific to each theoretica l aspect under investigation. The stage leverages t he LLM to propose potential themes based on code co-occurrence and meaning, drawing upon the complete construct codebook and full code set  provided by the researcher. Implementation involves  the researcher prompting the LLM per construct (and subgroup, if applicable), fo llowed by critical human review of the LLM-generated themes, definitions, and preliminary code-to-theme mappings. Researchers evaluate coherence, ens ure accurate representation of data patterns (checking theme prevalence in source texts), and verify relevance to the research question and theory, subsequently refining, merging, renaming, or discarding themes. Visualizations like thematic maps can aid this review, and LLM outputs (as JSON) facilitate this visualization and permit direct human revision to these thematic maps. The primary products are tentative themes with definitions and associated code mappings, organized by construct, which serve to highlight recurring patterns and potentially reveal analytical discrepancies requiring further investigation.

Application of this stage in the SCCT motivation study involved first optionally splitting the coded data by the five LCA profiles identified earlier. Using the generate_themes command, the LLM then proposed themes, definitions, and mappings for each SCCT dimension w ithin each class, based on the relevant codes and e xtracts provided. Human researchers subsequently reviewed t hese proposals for coherence, data representativene ss, and relevance to SCCT and the research question. The resulting tentative themes and mappings were output as JSON and could be visualized as thematic maps  using visualize_themes (for cross-construct maps) and visualize_codes to display thematic maps per construct dimension (see Figure 2 for Class A examples). This finding highlighted the presence of these factors, prompting further investigation in subsequent stages regarding their role and magnitude compared to the highly endorsed Subject Affinity (100% LCA probability). Stage 5: Within-case analysis and data triangulation Building upon the initial coding and thematic explo ration of previous stages, Stage 5 shifts focus tow ards developing a more nuanced understanding of particip ant narratives and triangulating multiple data sour ces. The goal is to move beyond preliminary patterns to buil d confidence in interpretations, identify and inves tigate discrepancies (e.g., between different data types l ike TA and LCA, or between LLM suggestions and human readings), and ultimately refine the narrative associated with individual cases or groups (such as the LCA-defined classes). This may involve synthesizing information from the quantitative code metrics (frequency, diversity from Stage 3), LLM-generated magnitude scores, and LLM-generated qualitative summaries, facilitated by mix edmethods displays (i.e., combining  quantizing [converting qualitative data to numerical ] and qualitizing [converting quantitative data into qualitatively analyzable data]; cf. Miles et al., 2013; Onwuegbuzie & Dickinson, 2008). The primary product is an integrated, triang ulated interpretation of the cases or classes under  study, often represented through enhanced displays and refined descriptive narratives. The implementation of this stage leverages the LLM primarily for quantizing the qualitative data (e.g., magnitude coding) and synthesizing qualitative find ings (accounting for patterns in code frequency/diversity/magnitude), always under humandirection and review. After co nducting initial checks on individual cases (e.g., generating personalized theme maps), the analysis may remain at the individual participant level or scale to the within-class level. Key LLM-assisted steps include: (a) Magnitude Coding: Researchers prompt the LLM (e.g., using generate_intensity_codes) to evaluate each coded extract and assign a numer ical score (e.g., 1-7) representing the intensity or magnitude with which the applied code is expressed in that specific text segment. The LLM receives the construct/code d efinitions and the coded extracts  for this task; and  (b) Qualitative Summary Generation: The LLM is prompted to synthesize findings for each theme (from Stage 4) within each case or class. The LLM is provided with relevant co ntext (e.g., constructs, codes, definitions, coded extracts for that theme/class, and research question) and tasked with identifying patt erns informed by code frequency, diversity, and magnitude, illustrating insights with specific participant extracts (using participant IDs), and relating findings back to the research question. Human researchers critically review the generated magnitude scores and qualitative summaries for accuracy, coherence, and representativeness, refining them as needed. Application of this stage in the SCCT motivation st udy began with generating theme graphs for individual cases to identify initial issues related  to LLM interpretation and data triangulation. The analysis then scaled to the within-class level for the five LCA profiles. We employed the LLM to generate magni tude scores (1-7) for all codes across the 2,622 extracts and to produce qualitative summaries  (generate_theme_summaries) for each theme within each of the nine SCCT dimensions for each LCA class. These newly generated data points were then triangulated by researchers with the exis ting LCA profiles and the code frequency/diversity metrics derived in Stage 3. This synthesis proved crucial for understanding nuances and resolving apparent discrepancies. For example, Class A showed near-zero LCA endorsement for Prior Experiences (0.01%) and very low endorsement for Social Influences (6.3%), contrasting with the moderate-to-high frequency of these themes in the overall thematic analysis dataset. For instanc e, under Social Influence, it coded specific adapta tion experiences (e.g., Student 170: "My cultural knowledge... will influence my assessment... differently than other p eople") or teamwork skills derived from non-academic contexts (e.g., Student 159: "influenced b y my past experiences in soccer"). Similarly, under Prior Experiences, TA noted informal tinkering (e.g., Student 193: practicing creativity even if "not exactly follow[ing] the... process") o r using school resources (e.g., Student 178: "made use of the programming and robotics facilities"). However, an LCA operating at the whole-document level might intentionally miss these nuances. Reflecting the me thodological sensitivities, the detailed thematic a nalysis captured any relevant extract (including  brief mentions of tinkering, adaptation, or non-academic teamwork), while the LCA was more likely to require higher the matic dominance or magnitude at the whole-document level or employed a less nuanced definition of the construct dimension to assign endorsement to a profile. Conversely, Class A's 100% LCA endorsement for Subject Affinity perfectly matched its qualitative centrality and quantitative dominance (high frequency/diversity/magnitude). Sim ilarly, the moderate LCA endorsement (43.8%; highes t among all classes) for Self-Efficacy in STEM reflected its qualitative importance and high quantitative magnitude but also its moderate frequency and very low divers ity, painting a picture of a strongly held but perhaps less universally or variably expressed belief within this group compared to thei r core subject interest. This within-class triangulation thus provided a deeper, contextualized narrative for each profile. Stage 6: Final synthesis and reporting Synthesizing the comprehensive findings from previo us stages is the central focus of Stage 6, culminat ing the HHitL-TA process in a coherent final report. The primary aim is to construct a compelling, evidence-based narrative that fully addresses the research question by weaving together the inductively derived thematic structure (codes, themes, and summaries) within the deductively informed constructs (e.g., from SCC T), contextualizing findings within existing knowledge. This stage is predominantly driven by human researchers undertaking critical synthesis and interpretation. Implementation involves meticulously connecting thematic findings (including Stage 5 magnitude data and qualitative summaries) with the overarching theoretical framework and, potentially, profile analyses to create integrated interpretations for e ach case or group. While researchers craft the main  analytic narrative supported by salient qualitative extracts  and triangulated evidence, LLMs can provide target ed support by generating cross-case comparative displays (e.g., tables, visualizat ions illustrating theme/code prevalence across groups) or identifying patterns across qualitative summaries and code metrics. The process concludes with researchers writing discussion sections comparing f indings to literature and theory, acknowledging lim itations, and including explicit reflexive commentary on the HHitL-TA process. The main product is the final research report or manuscript.

Applying this synthesis process to the SCCT motivat ion study involve s researchers integrating the detailed findings for each of the five motivational  profiles developed through Stage 5. ## Conclusions

Confronting the debated integration of large langua ge models (LLMs) into qualitative research, this pa per introduced and demonstrated the Hybrid Human-in-the-Loop Thematic Analysis (HHitL-TA) framework. This six-stage methodology offers a structured approach for conducting rigorous hybrid (deductive-inductive) thematic analysis, leveraging LLM capabilities while aiming to promote  human interpretation, decision-making, and reflexive engagement. Grounded in principles of int eroperability, human-LLM transactiv ity, scalability, and crucial transparency, HHitL-TA guides researchers in using LLMs as assistive tools—from defining theory-driven constructs (Stage 1) through inductive coding withi n those constructs (Stages 2-3), iterative theme generation (Stage 4), a nd nuanced triangulation using mixed data types lik e code metrics and magnitude scores (Stage 5), culminating in researcher-led synthesis and reporting (Stage 6). Our illustrative analysis of engineering students' SCCT-based motivations demonstrated the framework's capacity to manage large datasets efficiently while  facilitating, contextualized understanding. It sho wed how HHitL-TA can resolve discrepancies between different anal ytical lenses (e.g., LCA profiles vs. detailed TA findings) through structured triangulation, producing richer narratives than either approach alone might yield. By embedding human oversight and iterative refinement throughout, and demanding transparency from both the LLM (e.g., justifications, intermediate outputs) and th e researcher (e.g., clear reporting), HHitL-TA directly addresses concerns about "sloppy science" and the potential m arginalization of the human researcher. While chall enges remain, particularly in managing human verification of full datasets at sca le (Stage 3), and the ongoing need for critical vigilance against LLM limitations, this fr amework provides a viable pathway for augmenting qu alitative inquiry. It offers a means to harness LLM potential responsibly, ensuring technology serves, rather than supplants, the interpretive depth central to thematic analysis. Future development should focus on refining trans parent reporting guidelines and creating user-friendly tools that em bed these HHitL principles, safeguarding human cognitive reasoning as the cornerstone of qualitative research in an increasingly AI-influenced landscape.
