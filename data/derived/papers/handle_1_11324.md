# “Wow, I Can Actually Do This Now”: Girls’ Science Identity Development at Science Summer Camps

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Science identity research has grown exponentially in the past ten years and for good reason: researchers are recognizing how identity/identities affect science interest, career development, and peoples’ worldviews. Science identity comprises four aspects: competence, recognition, performance, and interest. I studied science identity development in girls aged 10-16 at a two-week science summer day camp. Drawing from pre-post camp interviews and observations, I examined three girls’ science identity development as they explored different science aspects (e.g. herpetology, marine biology) and communicated about science. Girls were categorized as having burgeoning, intermediate, and established science identities based on different levels of self-recognition, recognition by others, and performance, and competence. I show how recognition by others who represent the girls’ backgrounds and ideals was an important aspect of girls’ science identity as well as provide additional evidence for the importance of informal science learning contexts for science identity development.

## Background

Informal science education refers to learning that happens outside of formal schooling environments. Learning in informal spaces, which includes museums, summer camps, and science cafes accounts for 95% of science learning that happens in peoples’ lives (Falk & Dierking, 20 10). An especially robust amount of research has be en done around summer camps (e.g. Broder et al., 2023). The se informal science learning contexts are spaces wh ere underrepresented minorities may develop their scien ce identities, as traditional school settings  often do not provide adequate access to quality learning experie nces for them (Parsons, 2008). Science learning and  the development of science identities has been documented at summer camps, especially for girls (Roberts & Hughes, 2022; Morton & Smith-Mutegi, 2022). Outcomes of research conducted in th ese informal science education environments relates to how such learning can augment in-school learning (Riedinger, 2015), lead to more robust workforce development (Wilson & Sibthorp, 2018), and shape peoples’ identities over time (Ibourk et al., 2022). My research within the informal science education context of summer camps focuses on middle school girls’ expressions of science identity and builds on Milto n and colleagues (2023) findings regarding the deve lopment and fostering of middle school girls’ STEM identities during summer camps. Theoretical framing Science identity is defined as being competent (competence), able to perform science-based tasks (performance), and being recognized by others as a science person (recognition) (Carlone & Johnson, 2007). Science in terest is a related construct to science i dentity and can be defined as content-specific motivation composed of intrinsic feelings and value-related elements (Schiefele, 2011). Science identity is especially meaningful for girls or people from underrepresented groups, who have been histori cally excluded from science (King & Pringle, 2018; Avraamidou, 2020). Carlone and Johnson (2007) postu lated that recognition was important to science ide ntity because many scientists and thus potential role models, were old er, white males. This can be problematic when people who do not meet those criteria (i.e. underrepresented minorities) as they do not receive recognition, nor do they recognize themselves in such potential role mo dels. Atkins et al. (2020) added to this understand ing with their work on mentorship in the context of developi ng STEM leaders. Additionally, recognition by others, such as family and friends, influenced the ways that gir ls from underrepresented groups thought of themselv es as scientists (or not scientists) (Rodriguez et al., 2 017; Kuchynka et al., 2022). Carlone and colleagues  (2014) identified middle school as a particularly important time for girls’ science identity, as girls’ interest in science and science identities often lessen in middle school and continue to decline after middle school. Roberts and Hughes

(2022) found that when targeted strategies were used to encourage external recognition (e.g. having relationships

with female scientists as role models and mentors),  girls’ sense of recognition improved. I  built on this previous research by interviewing campers and taking observa tions during a two-week long summer camp for girls aged 10-16 who come from underrepresented groups (e.g. racial, ethnic, socioeconomic, gender). Thus, I ask: How are girls’ science identities expressed during an out-of-school science learning experience?

## Context, methods and data sources

Data were collected during the EcoLogik Summer Experience (henceforth EcoLogik), a free 2-week STEM summer day camp for underrepresented female and fem ale identifying youth, ages 10-16 (University IRB # Blinded for Review). EcoLogik camp instructors selected campers based on an application process, all girls who attended camp were enrolled in Title I schools and/or were underrepresented minorities. The camp fused ecology and technology to connect youth to the natural resources and science of a national monument in the western United States. During camp, participants explored the themes of biodiversity, conservation, herpetology, marine biology, and nature photography. They attended activities le d by scientists from underrepresented minorities, s uch as a herpetology nature walk, where a Black, female-identifying herpetology researcher taught campers h ow to identify local lizards and the techniques she used for ethically catching and handling lizards. Seventeen girls consented to research during the 20 24 EcoLogik Summer Experience. I collected data from campers through audio-recorded, semi-structured interviews with individual campers (pre-and post-camp) (Galletta & Cross, 2013) and video recordings of ca mpers during daily camp activities (Bauer & Gaskell, 2000). I was able to conduct pre-and post-interviews with seven of the seventeen campers, as some girls did not agree to be interviewed before camp, while others were ei ther absent on the day that post interviews occurre d or chose not to be interviewed after camp.  Three of the seven girls with pre-post interviews were chosen for this study: Maria, 11-year-old Latina girl, Kiki, an 11-year-old Black girl, and Jo, an 11-year-old White girl. Audio-recorded, semi-structured interviews included specific questions d eveloped from Chen and Wei’s (2020) validated instrument for measuring science identity. Questions included, “Tell me about your experiences in STEM outside of school” to measure science performance, “Tell me about a time when you felt you were really good at a science topic” to inquire about science competence, “Do you  think your family and friends recognize you as a s cience person? Why or why not?” to understand science reco gnition, and “What kinds of science activities do y ou like to do?” to determine science interest. Videos of th e campers were taken with a GoPro during regular ca mp activities. For instance, when the campers were des igning an experiment to determine how many species of bees were found in a certain section of the park, I joined their group with my GoPro and filmed as they discussed their ideas, such as assigning roles (data recorder, data  collector, etc.), where to set up a transect, and how to count bees. I observed Maria, Kiki, and Jo every day during the two-week long camp as well as interviewed them preand post-camp. I categorized the girls into one of three science i dentity types: burgeoning, intermediate, and established. I explain the categorizations in the results section below.

## Results

Maria had a slicked back ponytail, braces, and a huge smile. She was curious, kind, and excited to be outside at EcoLogik. I categorized her science identity as burgeoning,  as Maria’s pre-camp interview was filled with descriptions of non-recognition by self and others of being a science p erson. Maria said, “I don’t know [why I don’t identify as a science person], I just don ’t.” Additionally, when asked if her classmates saw her as a science person, she said no, “because they don’t really see  me doing stuff.” Lastly, she said her family did n ot recognize her as a science person, describing how her parents  would get her brothe r “STEM-related things” which he wouldn’t let her play with. Additionally, her paren ts didn’t buy her STEM-related toys. During camp, Maria started to recognize how she was able to perform science-related tasks, such as when the campers were exploring the tidepools at the national monument. At the tidepool s, Maria hopped from rock to rock, picking up lobst er shells and kelp and making observations about them,  such as “Ms. Nicole talked about kelp earlier, look at this one, it’s super soft and squishy. It also has a, like, bubble and tentacles at the end where it can wave b ack and forth on the sea floor.” Maria’s observation of kel p brought in the new science knowledge she learned about during camp. While she didn’t verbally say that she  felt competent at science, her observational skills, which are key to science competency, were evident. In her post-camp interview, Maria’s perceptions on herself as a science person changed, with her saying, “this camp has rea lly helped me be like, ‘wow, I’m actually really go od at science. Wow, I can actually do this now.’” Although my observations during camp showed that she made friends, during her post-camp interview she still did not necessarily think her friends saw her as a science person, saying, “I don’t know, I’ve never actually asked.” However,  Maria indicated that her family saw her as a scien ce person following camp, saying, “I think they do now that I ’ve been in this camp. I’ve been able to, like, impress my family more. Like, everyday I come home and say, like, ‘oh did you know this point, did you know that,’ and they didn’t know those things before.’” Maria’s end-of-camp recognition of herself as a science person as well as being able to “impress” her family and gain their recogni tion, while still working towards gaining recogniti on as a science person by her friends indicates her burgeoning science identity. Kiki, with her braided hair and a penchant for crac king jokes, started the camp as quiet and unsure, b ut by the end of camp, her carefree personality shined through. I categorized Kiki’s science identity as intermediate as she indicated some recognition by others, such as her teacher who said that she “was good at science” but she did not think of herself as a science person becaus e she didn’t like to do science-related things outside of school, instead she drew “designs for dresses and outfits” and liked to crochet. However, during camp, the unit on nature photography helped Kiki recognize that she could in tegrate her interests in art with science. Campers received macro lens for their phones, then they chose areas around the national monument to photograph. Kiki tried creative angles, such as laying down to steady her hands to take macro photographs of bees on plants, and excitedly showed other campers her photographs, saying, “look at tha t bee. I think it’s that Ultra Green Sweat Bee, and it’s o n California Buckwheat!”. Using specific species name s and effectively using a science tool shows two as pects of science identity: science competence and science pe rformance. In her post-camp interview, Kiki shared that her mom, “most definitely” saw her “as a scientist” aft er Kiki showed her mom how to use the macro lens to  look at “a random stain on the carpet.” She then said her mom told her that she was proud of how much she was learning. In her post-camp interview, Kiki also shared that she thought o f herself as a scientist because she liked “to buil d things, like engineering” and described multiple it ems she created, like a pencil  holder from a toilet paper roll, and a portable trash can from a shoebox. Kiki also spoke about how the scientists and speakers at camp, who were all women or non-binary, were “cool” and gave the campers “cool gifts” like a macro lens for their phones and a book called Women in Science: 50 Fearless Pioneers Who Changed the World. She also mentioned that a memorable experience during camp was making friends and that she “didn’t want to leave my friends” at the end of camp, but she was excited to volunteer for the camp when she turned 13. Kiki particularly singled out an activity about chemistry, in which a scientist with a backgr ound in oceanography talked the campers through oce an acidification and what the chemical building blocks  of coral were with a game. “I liked that game with  the dice” Kiki said in her post-interview. The afternoon after the game, during my observations, Kiki continuously talked about the scientist and the game she had them play.  This was clearly a memorable and interesting exper ience for Kiki, which might have been due to the novel activity and/or the scientist who shared her expertise with the campers. Thus, Kiki’s recognition of self and from others as a scientist, as well as her competence and performance during camp helped to characterize her as having an intermediate science identity. Lastly, Jo, an 11-year-old white girl with freckles, long, curly hair, and a love for humor was categorized as having an established science identity. During h er pre-camp interview, Jo shared how she knew she was a scientist, since she loved exploring outside, natur e journaling, and finding out new things. She also shared that her family saw her as a science person since she was always doing “a lot of science stuff at my house, like going out and taking little samples of plants in my backy ard.” During camp, Jo often took out her notebook t o sketch plants and animals she saw, labeling their parts and writing in queries, such as “is this a kind of ca ctus?” and an addition to this query, “(I found it in my guide-yes.)” (Figure 1).  Jo loved photography. Campers had the opportunity to meet a nature photographer and see t heir work. After the nature photographer’s presenta tion, campers went around the national monument  using microlens that they attached to their phones or iPhones the camp had. When her parent came to get her after the  nature photography lesson, Jo immediately took her  phone out and showed her parent the multiple pictures she  took of plants, sharing thei r common names and facts about them. Jo then spoke frequently during the rest of camp about this experience, saying that she was so excited to be able to show people plants and animals. Jo’s post-camp interview showed how her science identity only strengthened with camp, saying that she wanted to be a natu re photographer when she was older; an opportunity that she did not recognize as a possibility until Ecologik. Jo’s self-recognition, recognition by others, performance, and competence in science helped to categorize her as having an established science identity.

## Limitations

With a small target population that will be recruit ed from a group of campers who have already been ac cepted into the summer camp, there is a potential for selection bias. Additionally, this was a qualitative study. To alleviate some aspects that are often cited as issues with qu alitative work, I sought to ensure that data descri ptions were rich and thick (Creswell, 2014) through writing up data in a way that shows context and focuses on par ticipant quotes. Additionally, as the sole data collector, there wa s potential for me to miss out on some aspects of d ata during data collection.

## Discussion

With this paper, I hope to add to our ability to de scribe science identity aspects as they occur in sc ience-themed summer camps. Recognition, as identified by Carlone  and Johnson (2007) as the external validation from  others that one belongs in science and can be a scientist, was often missing for women who identified as scientists. They postulated that this was because most scientists and thus role models, were older, white males (Carlone & Johnson, 2007). Previous research on the development of scie nce identity within summer camps has shown that participating in summer camps can help girls feel r ecognized and build competencies in science (Robert s & Hughes, 2022). I built on this previous research by  interviewing campers and taking observations durin g a twoweek long summer camp for girls aged 10-16 who came from underrepresented groups (e.g. raci al, ethnic, socioeconomic, gender). The pre-post interviews with campers helped to show different categorizations of science identity that girls developed during camp.

For Maria and Kiki, who had burgeoning or intermedi ate science identities, I noticed a profound emphasis on the concept of recognition. Maria’s fam ily was beginning to recognize her science identity, but she still felt as if her friends did not. At the end of camp, Kiki knew her mom and family recognized her as a scientist, and that she had made friends at camp, who seemed t o share the same interests that she did. These reco gnitions speak to how EcoLogik reinforced science interests for girls who come in w ith established science identity but also acted as a place for other campers to foster t heir own science identities, as it provides some of  the external validation that students may not have received yet from their families or teachers (Milton et al., 202 3). In terms of equity and access to science opportunities, EcoL ogik also gave girls chances to explore their inter ests without being told to do science in a certain way, or that it looked a certain way, as is so often the case in  school settings (Parsons, 2008). The external forces—recognition of being a science person by others —clearly influenced girls’ science identities during EcoLogik.

## Conclusion

This study contributes to the ongoing conversation around who can be a scientist as well as who gets r ecognized as a scientist (Roberts & Hughes, 2022). My results  extend previous work showing that summer camps can  be places where science identity forms (e.g. Milton et al., 2023; Broder et al., 2022; Morton & Smith-Mutegi, 2022). My findings show that different types of science id entity can be developed during a two-week summer camp for girls, suggesting that there are different ways to reach young people who are building their science i dentities. Future work may be able to furth er detail at a wider scale the different types of s cience identities that develop at summer camps. Additionally, this work contributes w ays to encourage girls to be active transformative agents who can envision new solutions to global crises suc h as anthropogenic climate change, but first, they must be recognized as capable and competent as scientists.
