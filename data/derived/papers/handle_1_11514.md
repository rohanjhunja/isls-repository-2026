# Dignity-Affirming Care in the Context of Large-Scale Instructional Systems Change

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Through a case study of five colleges participating in a city-wide university system’s effort to bring computational and digital literacies into Schools of Education, this poster describes how relationships of dignity-affirming care (Riedy and Penuel, 2024) can support systems change efforts by encouraging: 1) faculty professional learning toward systems change goals, 2) buy-in to larger efforts, and 3) incorporation of new ideas and practices in courses/syllabi. Connections to related research in research-practice partnerships are shared.

## Introduction

Institutional change theories highlight how power d ynamics play an important role in systems change (e.g., described as status, privilege, and master-servant relationships via “ceremonial value” by Bus h, 1987; or issues of “ownership” by Coburn, 2003; etc.). Institutional relationships are often researched with a focus on who has more control and decision-making power to push forward a specific change vs. who has less flexibility to agree or disagree with mandates for change (Kingston & Ca ballero, 2009). Interestingly, a quick review of pe rsonal stories and perspectives ab out systems change from community organizations, fu nders, etc. (beyond research literature) do not focus on power, but instead emphasize relationships of caring, interpersonal understanding, and respect as key to systems change (e.g., freya, 2024; Milligan et al., 2022, etc.). In our own systems change effort within the City University of New York’s (CUNY) colleges, we have also observed how caring relationships between faculty within college teams shifted the perspective and work of many who were initially resistant to change. This Computing Integrated Teacher Education (CITE) effort involves 13 colleges’ Schools of Education across New York City, USA  to prepare future K12 public school teachers to infuse their work with computing and digital literacies. Through a review of three focal case colleges with different trajectories and progress toward our ultimate syste ms change goal, this poster shares ini tial findings to the following research question: What is the quality of interpersonal relationships that support systems change and how do such relationships ultimately impact change?  Findings describe how dignity-affirming care (Riedy & Penuel, 2024) was critical to systems change by: 1)  supporting faculty professional learning toward br oader systems change goals, 2) building buy-in, and 3) changing faculty teaching practices to a dopt systems change goals in courses and syllabi.

## Literature review

While Riedy and Penuel (2024) apply the concept of “dignity-affirming care” to research-practice partnerships specifically, we find their analysis generalizes to  other configurations of systems change efforts as well (e.g. partnerships in higher education between administra tors, faculty, and adjunct faculty). They define di gnityaffirming care as: “[S]peech, actions, and disposit ions that demonstrate concern for another and their  expressed needs and goals in a manner that affirms their essential worth or value. Care, or caring, is, as others have argued, an ethical stance that grows out of an understandin g that we are relational beings (Buber, 1965; Noddi ngs, 2012)…it recognizes the inherent worth or value of the other” (p. 260). Dignity-affirming care creates conditions by which all people are valued and honored at the “ highest rank possible” and in ways that cultivate p eoples’ greatest potential for growth, change, and self-realization (Espinoza et al., 2020, p. 3 & 327). Thus, relationships of care are not just part of a successful process, but also an important outcome reflecting how people have shifted and learned together (Riedy & Penuel, 2024). This i s exactly what we have begun witnessing  among diverse faculty teams in CUNY colleges participating in our systems change project as well.

## Methods

The larger context of this work involves a research-practice partnership between CUNY leadership and Telos Learning researchers, that seeks to understand how external and internal factors influence the decision-making of college faculty teams engaged in systems change ini tiatives aimed at integrating computational and dig ital literacies into teacher education programs. This po ster zooms in on interviews interrogating how decisions are made about what students should learn regarding computing and digital literacies  in the larger project. The interview protocol included questions about key influencers of each college’s decisions on student learning goals (e.g., What do you see as institutional factors impacting what you think students should learn in your progra ms? How are decisions made across different ideas surfa ced by your team? What is in your sphere of control  when it comes to making decisions? What external factors, if any, related to current issues in soc iety at tech impact your student learning goals? Etc.).  Interviewees included  17 strategy team  members across 5 colleges  (including department chairs, faculty, adjunct faculty, administrative staff, and /or coordinators, from early to late career). The research team initially used a data reduction approach to code interviews i nto broad categories such as personal roles in the systems change project, group values, team dynamics, institutional impacts, personal sphere of control, etc. Within each category, interviewees ’ quotes we re then reviewed in a dditional iterative rounds of coding using a grounded theory approach (Strauss & Corbin, 1997) to surface themes specific to the pro ject’s larger question about impacts on decision-making for systems change. Among various factors impacting decisionmaking, the theme of relationships became particularly prominent as an influencer of each college’s efforts.

## Findings

Across the colleges, faculty placed emphasis on the importance of caring relationships that elevated the humanity, value, and dignity of individuals across the teams. These caring relationships ultimately supported: 1) engagement in professional learning, 2) buy-in to larger systems change goals, and 3) incorpora tion of changes into faculty syllabi that moved systems change efforts forward.

Engagement in professional learning.  The larger systems change effort aimed to support f aculty to incorporate computational and digital literacies into their courses with teacher candidates, but for those with less technology experience, this was a completely new ar ea of professional learnin g that felt intimidating. However, one professor explained that while she initially fe lt hesitant about the initiative, her “sister” coll eague embraced her questions, valued her presence, and encouraged her participation so that “I don't feel uncomfortab le if we're in a discussion…we're modeling the kind of thinking  that helps us to grow and not to have a person lik e me feel incompetent as we're growing.” Similarly, at anothe r college, a professor described that supportive re lationships of dignity-affirming care allowed her to feel comfortable asking questio ns about ideas that others seemed to understand because she knew “that they wouldn't say, ‘Oh, you're a real dummy.’” In a context of supportive relationships, people could feel unafraid being vul nerable about what they do not understand which led  to the important professional learning necessary for building systems change. Buy-in to larger systems change goals. At a third college, an adjunct faculty member described how their team’s relationships involved “a lot of respect in terms of each other's values…there's a comfort level where we're able to be vulnerable and say what we really think. ” This was particularly important for her because, despite not being full-time faculty, she felt that her voice mattered and was welcome. This, in turn, encouraged her to join the systems change effort and believe in its value for her students and the college because she felt she could contribute meaningfully with her own background in computing e ducation, sharing ideas for technological tools the  other faculty could try using in their courses.

Incorporation of instructional innovations.  Finally, after buying-in to systems change efforts with the support of caring and dignity-affirming relationships, faculty felt encouraged by  these same relationships to take risks and incorporate new approaches to their teaching and syllabi. For example, one professor at another college explained that their relationships and team dynamic s gave them “the freedom to kind of share what we t hought, and I think that was big, and I like, you know, in terms of my decision making, having that kind of op enness as well as the freedom to kind of, you know, share ideas and create things.” As a result, she felt confident “doing the syllabi [in] creative ways” and “making sure that [the assignments] were more embedded” in the larger systems change effort. She did this in collaboration with a n adjunct faculty member, and together they were ab le to build both trust and experiment with their courses.

## Conclusion

These initial findings about how relationships affirming peoples’ dignity, humanity, and value can create a strong foundation for institutional change efforts demonstrate what it means to treat vulnerability with the care it deserves (Herrenkohl, Kawasaki, & Dewater; 2010) as college faculty engage in new content and skills  around computational and digital literacies. Similar to Riedy and Penuel’s (2024) findings regarding RPPs, college teams described how their familial relationships of care,  intentional efforts to be both respectful and vuln erable, and opportunities to feel seen and heard were critical to supporting deeper connection to their work on in corporating computational and digital literacies in teacher education programs. Attention to these relationships b uilt the foundation for systems change efforts to take shape across colleges.
