# Researchers’ Descriptions of Embodiment in Conjecture Mapping

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study systematically reviewed 117 articles that contained at least one of the four elements of embodiment (an articulation of the learning environment design) in the conjecture map. We found differences in the use of the four elements: Discursive practices were the least used. Our analysis using word clouds suggests that such limited use of discursive practices might result from the greater challenge of locating discursive practices than other elements in the embodiment.

## Introduction

Design-based research (DBR) is highly influential in the learning sciences and is a core content of many learning sciences programs in higher education (Sommerhoff et al., 2018). In particular, “conjecture mapping,” as proposed by Sandoval (2014), represents a recent advancement in DBR. Extensive studies have utilized conjecture maps to implement DBR. The original concept of conjecture mapping contribu tes to DBR by systematizing the investigation of design and theoretical conjectures.

However, the conjecture map presents challenges in structure and application. For example, Wu and Chen (2018) identified two structural issues: the need to link design principles and embodiment, and to locate mediating evidence to the original map. Herein, we highlight another challenge: the practical difficulty of using a conjecture map. Sandoval (2014) defined four components (high-level conjecture, embodiment, mediating process, and outcome) and presented examples of developing a conjecture map through teaching scientific argumentation in elementary sciences. However, developing a map for each practice can be difficult. Particularly, we recognize that filling out the embodiment, defined here as an articulation of the learning environment design (Sandoval, 2014), is one of the most challenging ta sks in using a conjecture map because it has four elements (tools and materials, task structures, participant structures, and discursive practices), but their differentiation is not clearly explained. Therefore, determining how researchers have used this map can provide suggestions for this challenge. The following two research questions (RQs) guide this study:

1. RQ1: To what extent are the four elements of the embodiment used in conjecture maps?

2. RQ2: What are the most frequently used words in the four elements of the embodiment in conjecture maps?

We believe that systematically reviewing previous papers that have used conjecture maps will provide answers to these questions, which will help researchers draw conjecture maps in future studies.

## Method

We conducted a systematic review using Google Schol ar (Figure 1). In September 2023, we searched for papers citing Sandoval (2014). More than 700 papers were initially identified; of which, we finally analyzed 117 papers (see Figure 1 for exclusion criteria). To address RQ1, we counted the number of occurrences of each element in the target paper s. To address RQ2, we input the collected descriptio ns and represented them in word cloud s, generated by MAXQDA 2022, to intuitively understand which words were frequently used in embodiment. ## Results and discussion

The numbers of occurrences of each element in the 117 papers were as follows: 110 for tools and materials, 109 for task structures, 96 for participant structures, and 71 for discursive practices. The first two appeared frequently, whereas the last appeared  the least frequently. Such large differences in number might have come fr om the characteristics of the definition and cases of discursive practices in  Sandoval (2014), where the discursive practices are defined just as a “way of talking” (p.22) and appeared only in the second example. Figure 2 shows the word clouds for each element and frequenc ies of the top five words. Words related to tools were often referred to as tools and materials. In task structures, tasks or activities related to learners (e.g., practice, design) frequently appeared. As participant structures, the word “group” or persons (i.e., teacher, student) were often mentioned. Discursive practice has three distinct features. First, the unique term “norm” was used relatively often. This may be because Sandoval (2014) used this term in his second example. Second, many other words used in this element were related to learners’ activities (e.g., discussion, reflection, shar e), rather than educators’ actions. Third, the remaining frequent words have already been used in the other elements. Therefore, discursive practices might be the least used because of the difficulty of distinguishing it from the other elements in the embodiment (e.g., task structures) and with the mediating process, since learners’ activities seem to be included in the mediating process as observable interactions. ## Conclusion

We found that discursive practices were the least used of the four elements in the conjecture map. Additionally, our analysis using word clouds suggests that discursive practices struggle to secure a unique position within embodiment. Future studies should investigate and identify what words in the discursive practices should be used if this element is to be considered in the conjecture map.
