# Making Science Ours: Inclusive Discourse, Legitimation, and the Formation of Learning Communities

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how teachers’ inclusive discourse, particularly the use of the pronoun we, shapes students’ participation, belonging, and identity development in science learning. Grounded in a sociocultural perspective, the analysis examines how language mediates legitimate access to the practices and norms of scientific inquiry. Drawing on c lassroom interactions from a two-week STEAM program with multilingual youth, we show that the teacher’s strategic use of we during moments of uncertainty, procedural error, and conceptual ambiguity reorganized epistemic authority, reframed mistakes as data, and positioned students as co-participants in scientific sense-making. Rather than treating we as inherently inclusive, the findings reveal its pow er as a responsive and situational discursive resource that opens participation when it is most at risk. This work highlights how teachers’ linguistic attunement can transform classroom talk into a form of apprenticeship that cultivates belonging, agency, and authentic engagement in science.

The discourse used by teachers plays a critical role in shaping the disciplinary learning environment and influencing students’ participation and engagement (Lemke, 1990). Research shows that teachers’ discursive moves are not merely a medium for deliverin g content; they are powerful tools that shape how students interact with knowledge, engage in scientific practices, and develop their identities as learners (Kahveci et al., 2025; Kayumova et al., 2025; Michaels & O’Connor, 2015). The ways teachers position students, frame questions, and provide feedback are critical in fostering a classroom culture that promotes deep understanding, critical thinking, and collaborative learning (Kayumova et al., 2025; Oliveira, 2010, 2011). In science education specifically, teachers’ discourse and interactions with students can either open or constrain students’ opportunities to engage in the practices of science, such as making observations, constructing explanations, and critiquing arguments (Berland et al., 2016; NGSS Lead States, 2013; Schwarz et al., 2021). While much research (e.g., Batlle, 2023;  Kayumova et al., 2025; Lee & Roth, 2004;  Roth et al., 1999; Wray & McDonald, 2025) has examined teacher discourse across a range of important dimensions, one small but telling linguistic resource remains under-examined, personal pronouns such as I, you, we, and they  (Brewer & Gardner, 1996; Orvell et al., 2022). These pronouns might function as linguistic cues that reveal not only how teachers position themselves and their students, but also the relational dimensions that connect teachers, students, and science (Erb, 2010; Johnston, 2004). This pattern echoes a broader historical tendency in scientific writing to efface the authorial self. From the seventeenth century onward, scientific prose shifted toward impersonal, passive constructions designed to present knowledge as objective and detached from the speaker (Bazerman, 1988; Hyland, 2002). Our study takes up these possibilities as a question rather than a given. We ask how the teacher’s use of we  might work as a discursive resource for participation, how, in particular moments of classroom interaction, it could open or constrain students’ legitimate access to scientific practice (Uzum, 2013). Drawing on Lave and Wenger’s (1991) Situated Learning framework, we inquire into how teachers’ linguistic choices mediate participation, how we might signal movement from peripheral observation toward fuller engagement, or, alternatively, reinforce boundaries of authority. From this perspective, the meaning of we  is not stable or prescriptive but emergent in the contingent flow of talk, shaped by when it appears, how it is taken up, and what it accomplishes in practice.

Lave and Wenger’s (1991) concept of legitimate peripheral participation reframes learning not as the acquisition of knowledge but as an evolving process of becoming within a community of practice. It invites us to ask how participation in science classroom s becomes possible and meaningful, what it takes for students to be recognized as legitimate contributors to the collective work of science. For newcomers, learning begins not with mastery but with access, with being positioned as someone entitled to engage (Varelas et al., 2024). This perspective turns attention from what teachers teach to how participation is opened, negotiated, and sustained in the moment-to-moment flow of interaction.

1622 Guided by this framework, our analysis investigates how the teacher’s use of we might signal or negotiate such moments of access. We do not assume that apprenticeship is already happening; rather, we explore whether and how language use resembles or moves toward the conditions of apprenticeship described by Lave and Wenger (1991). In this way, we become a lens for examining the micro dynamics through which legitimacy is constructed, resisted, or redefined in practice.

Our aim is to understand not whether the teacher apprentices’ students in a formal sense, but how her language participates in the social choreography of inclusion, how certain utterances open space for students to act as insiders to the scientific enterprise, while others may inadvertently close it.

## Method

The data for this paper comes from longitudinal design-based research that took place as a two-week STEAM program in the Northeastern U.S. The overarching research conjecture was to understand the relationship between teachers’ asset-based positioning of multilingual youth and their developing science identities. During the classroom observation of science teacher Debra and analysis of the classroom video, we noticed the teacher’s recurring use of we in moments of instruction and discussion. At first, this pattern caught our attention simply as an interesting feature of her talk, one that seemed to coincide with changes in student engagement. Only later, as the program developed, did we  become an intentional design feature, part of a broader effort to foreground collaboration and belonging in scientific inquiry. Before drawing any design implications, however, we sought to understand how we functioned in practice, what it did in interaction, when it appeared, and how students responded to it. Our data include approximately fifteen hours of classroom video and associated student artifacts from twenty-five sessions recorded during the 2021 program. We approached these materials using a socioculturally informed Interaction Analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995), attending to how participation in science was locally configured through moment-to-moment interaction rather than through pre-defined linguistic categories. Our goal was not to treat we  as a discrete linguistic variable or to count its occurrences, but to examine how it emerged within the teacher-student interactions and possibly shaped participation. For instance, we traced how, in moments of conceptual ambiguity, procedural difficulty, or epistemic uncertainty, the teacher’s shifts into inclusive language coincided with changes in participation structures, redistributing intellectual responsibility and inviting students into a shared inquiry space. Our analysis focused on the interactional consequences of these moments, such as, how authority, student positioning and roles, and epistemic access were reorganized through talk (Kelly, 2007, 2023; Lave & Wenger, 1991). Across the broader interactional patterning of the data, the teacher’s use of we did not appear as a blanket marker of friendliness or generic inclusion. Rather, it emerged with specificity, surfacing precisely at moments when participation was under threat, like following a procedural misstep, in the face of conceptual uncertainty, or when the inquiry began losing momentum and tilting toward checklist completion. These were the junctures where students’ engagement could easily narrow or fragment. In these contingent moments, we  functioned less as a pronoun and more as a pivot, an interactional maneuver that redistributed epistemic footing. It subtly, yet powerfully, reframed the unfolding problem not as an individual failure to be corrected but as a shared scientific inquiry to be explored. In doing so, it shifted students from passive recipients of instruction to active coconstructors of meaning and inquiry (Johnston, 2004). What made these moments consequential was not only the discursive shift itself but also what it accomplished socially and epistemically. The invocation of we signaled alignment with scientific ways of doing, such as naming and analyzing error, embracing uncertainty, and documenting process, thereby linking students’ immediate classroom actions with broader disciplinary norms. It conferred legitimacy on students’ participation and rendered their contributions as meaningful within the epistemic inquiry of science. In this sense, we did not merely soften directive talk or diffuse tension; it restructured the terms of engagement and stabilized access to inquiry when it was most fragile. To make visible how this discursive shift unfolds in practice, we now turn t o one interactional episode in which a potential site of blame becomes reworked into a shared scientific problem space.

At the first pivotal moment, a potential individual mistake, forgetting to remove paper from a slide, threatens to narrow participation and assign blame. Debora reframes the event with “I think we forgot to take the paper off this one,” shifting footing from individual responsibility to collective oversight. She couples this stance with concrete next steps “you are gonna write ‘Error’ and the list ‘Paper on slide’” so that moment becomes a shared procedural task rather than a punitive episode. By naming the incident as a “possible source of error,” she converts their misstep into an analyzable methodological variance. The immediate consequence is a reorientation from error as failure to error as data and chance to show scientific communication, preserving students’ epistemic agency and sustaining their participation in the work of observation and analysis. A second moment centers on conceptual ambiguity regarding whether the brine shrimp eggs “survived or not.” The teacher’s formulation, “maybe we can see if they survived or not,” legitimizes uncertainty as a scientifically productive state and invites students into co-investigation rather than passive reception. Here, “we” signals joint inquiry and opens space for hypothesis generation and reasoning, positioning doubt as a driver of method rather than an obstacle to understanding. This move authorizes students to look, test, and interpret alongside the teacher, thereby rebalancing epistemic authority and making room for students’ emerging ideas. A third moment bridges classroom practice to participation in a broader disciplinary community. Statements such as “That’s what scientists do, so this is a learning experience for us,” and “we call it a possible source of error… we let people know” connect  their actions, such as recording the error, anticipating its analytic implications, to recognizable scientific norms of transparency and reporting. By invoking an imagined audience and routine scientific practices, the teacher hails students as legitimate  participants in a wider community of practice. The move culminates in “It happens all the time when scientists [are] running the experiment… it was cool that it happened to us,” which repositions the class as scientists-in-training encountering ordinary contingencies of experimental work, not as novices who have merely “messed up.” This analysis reveals how the teacher's strategic use of inclusive language not only fosters a sense of belonging but also actively engages students in the scientific process, enhancing their learning experiences. Across these moments runs a meta-pattern of attunement. The teacher reads where participation is at risk, blame after a procedural slip, confusion in the face of uncertain outcomes, drift toward “just schoolwork”, and deploys an inclusive stance precisely in response to those contingencies. Crucially, the stance is never offered alone. It is coupled with procedural scaffolds (document the error), epistemic reframing (treat variation as analyzable), and identity positioning moves (align with what scientists do). This calibrated responsiveness stabilizes learners’ participation and renders subsequent inquiry plausible and legitimate, as evidenced by students’ embodied alignment and readiness to proceed. This analysis underscores the importance of teachers’ res ponsive language choices in shaping students' experiences and identities in science, particularly in fostering a sense of belonging and agency during inquiry-based learning.

Our central finding is that “we” functions here as a possibility-opening discursive resource, effective not because it is inherently inclusive but because it is deployed strategically and relationally at moments of instructional ambiguity, procedural error, or shared uncertainty. When tuned to these junctures, the move reorganizes epistemic relationships, distributes responsibility for sense-making, and links classroom actions to disciplinary norms. Its force is situational and interactional, it works when it is responsive to the specific trouble at hand and when it is paired with actionable scientific practices. This study yielded three interrelated claims about transformation. First, strategic matters, the same word does different work depending on when and how it is used, and here it opens participation precisely when students’ opportunities are narrowing. Second, the effects are multidimensional, each use simultaneously generates epistemic, social, identity, and participatory possibilities by reframing error as data, diffusing blame, inviting co-reasoning, and authorizing documentation and analysis. Third, the c hanges cascade, once legitimacy and shared 1624 ownership are established, subsequent interactions tend to carry forward the stance of collective inquiry, making active contribution, observation, and hypothesis-generation more likely. Taken together, the findings reframe apprenticeship as the opening of participation rather than the transmission of predefined skills (Lave & Wenger, 1991). The teacher’s discursive attunement, her capacity to recognize emerging tensions and to use language that makes scientific practice feel shared and situated, creates the conditions for students to take up roles as co-participants in scientific sense-making. For scholarly significance, the implication is that professional learning should cultivate teachers’ sensitivity to interactional risk, their ability to pair an inclusive stance with concrete scientific procedures, and their orientation toward transformation rather than mere inclusion.
