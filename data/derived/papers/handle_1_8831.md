# A Student’s Access to Practice-Linked Resources in an Elementary Unplugged-to-Plugged Computer Science Unit

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents a case study of a student’s participation in an expansively-framed unplugged computer science (CS) instructional unit that leverages activities and materials not typically associated with CS: playing an “unplugged” tabletop board game and designing a paper prototype of a new game level. We use the practice-linked identity framework as a lens to characterize resources available in our instructional design and how they afforded or hindered the student’s participation across the instructional activities. Our findings highlight the importance of relational resources in influencing participation, both positively and negatively, and provide implications for the design of future CS learning environments.

## Introduction

One approach to making computer science (CS) instru ction more accessible to those who are historically underrepresented in the field is to change the mate riality through which CS ideas are encountered. Thi s is an approach that has been used to some degree of success with craft materials, such as electronic textiles (Searle & Kafai, 2015) and paper circuitry (Lee & Recker, 2018). Presumably, this also is part of the appeal of “unplugged” approaches to CS instruction that do not involve an actual computer in order to enact. By moving CS away from the screen, it seems possible that the standard pathways for participation can be disrupted. New participants can gain entry when the barriers associated with traditional media are removed. Taking seriously the idea that encountering CS thr ough different media has promise in changing who can participate in and become proficient with compu ting, we have been engaging in iterative design-bas ed research that introduces CS through an unplugged medium but, in lieu of stopping once those ideas are initially encountered, attempts to facilitate transfer to the  more canonical computing medium (e.g., a screen-ba sed programming environment) (Lee et al., 2020). The specific form of unplugged computing from which we begin is the computing involved in board game play (Berland & Lee, 2011). Students then transition to using Scratch to play the same game and program new game levels.

The present study examines, by way of case study, t he student experience of participating in this unit. We sought to understand if and how this strategy of  beginning with unplugged materials and then shifti ng to plugged materials enabled participation and identif ication with CS over the course of the unit. To do this, we strategically identified a specific student success case from one iteration of the designed and enacted unit and then reviewed all video records of that student’s partic ipation. We found, and describe in this paper, that  materiality contributed to this youth’s self-reported positive identification with CS. However, the case was more complex than simply concluding that new materials increased access. There were also important social interactions that we identified as being likely contributors to the case  student’s positive identification with CS. Through  iterative review and coding, we found that viewing this case study as the product of a combination of material, relational, and ideational resource availability yielded the most insight for us. This case study was formed around the question of, how do material, relational, and ideational resourc es combine in cultivating a positive identification  with computer science in the context of an unplugged-to-plugged unit? Theoretical framing The focal case in this study is a fifth-grade student, Ella, who showed a positive change in her identification with CS after participating in a research-designed unplugged-to-plugged unit. Because the change we observed in Ella was connected to participation in an activity and involved developing positive identification with CS, we chose a theoretical lens that explains identity construction through activity, specifically practice-linked identity resources. Practice-linked identity resources refer to support s that were made available and accessed during participation in social activities. Through a study of how positive identities were constructed through participation in a track and field team, Nasir and Cooks (2009) i dentified three practice-linked resources found in learning settings: material resources, ideational resources, and relational resources. Material resources are physical artifacts in the settings and “the way in which the physical environment, its organization, and the artifacts in  it support one’s sense of connection to the practice” (Nasir & Cooks, 2009, p.47). Second, “relational resources refer to the ICLS2022 Proceedings 496 © ISLS positive relationships with others in the context t hat can increase connection to the practice” (Nasir  & Cooks, 2009, p.47). Finally, “ideational resources refer to the ideas about oneself and one’s relationship to and place in the practice and the world, as well as ideas about what is valued or good” (Nasir & Cooks, 2009, p.47). While Nasir and Cooks originated this theory from natural istic study of intact social practices, we believe that it has potential for looking at researcher-designed activities, such as a curriculum. Indeed, through its use, we noticed some unintended but influential relational resource s that were provided to our case student by members  of our research team. While these were not intentionally d esigned as part of the curricular unit as resources, they appeared to counteract some other interactions that restricted resource access for the case study stud ent. Before describing how that transpired, we first turn to a description of the designed unit. The designed elementary computer science unit With the potential of alternative media for providing different forms of access to CS in mind, we developed our unit based off of a commercial paper-based board ga me. Board games have been both studied and designed  as computationally rich environments; a number of comp utational ideas can be instantiated through game pl ay, whether or not the players are aware that what they are doing is computational. Some board games where this had been explored include one on disease spread simulation and another that represented a wolf-sheep predation model (Horn et al., 2012). However, there are now board games that explicitly present their game play as computing and their value to players as supporting learning of CS  (Poole et al., 2021). These games vary in structur e but can instantiate CS in the form of actions presented as written code, algorithmic sequences represented as player moves, and or solutions as being derived through application of Boolean logic. After review of several options, we created our designed unit with the commercial board game, //Code: On the Brink, published by ThinkFun, as the initial unplugged entry point. The board game is comprised of levels, or puzzles for players to solve. Each level has a path within a grid through which a player needs to ‘program’ an agent (a robot token) to move from a ‘Start’ square  to ‘Finish’ square. The Start and Finish are separ ated by colored spaces. Landing on a colored space means ex ecuting the commands associated with that color. Pl ayers need to figure out the procedures for each color su ch that the robot token can execute the commands fr om the colored starting space and then whichever colored spaces it lands on so that it will eventually, without any changes to the player-specified procedures, land on the ‘Finish.’ Players use move cards (‘Move Forward’, ‘Turn Right’, ‘Do Nothing’ etc.) that they place on a control panel to make procedures for each color (see Figure  1). For example, if a player puts a ‘Turn Right’ i n the first spot and ‘Move Forward’ in the second spot of the c ontrol panel for blue, each time the robot lands on a blue it will rotate right and then move forward one square.

The designed unit consists of seven weekly lessons that span the classroom and school library. This was done because the partnering schools were exploring the school library as a setting for implementing CS instruction. Our unit design was an effort to support both the c lassroom teachers and school librarians in providin g CS instruction. In separate work, we found that the us e of the board game and the unplugged-to-plugged st ructure served as a support for the teachers and school librarian to learn new ideas about CS (Stephens et al., 2021). Figure 1 Instructional unit sequence The general lesson structure involved a portion that began in the classroom (for up to 20 minutes) and was led by the classroom teacher. Then students went to their scheduled weekly library time and, for the next 30 minutes had CS instruction. At the library, the school librarian introduced and then helped facilitate student work in pairs to: play the physical board game; play a Scratch version of the same game; design their own level of the game on paper using colored paper pieces and then colored pencils; and then program their level in Scratch for their classmates to try. As this was a research endeavor, research team members were present to set up cameras and microphones. However, they provided assistance as needed with the unit, helping to distribute materials or answer questions as needed. As we will show later, this proved consequential for our case student’s participation and identification with CS over the course of the unit. ICLS2022 Proceedings 497 © ISLS

## Methods and data

This project was conducted with a rural elementary school in the western United States. Seventy-four fifth graders (girls = 35) across three classrooms participated i n this iteration of the project. Ten of those stude nts were randomly selected (girls = 5) to be focal students. While all students completed pre-post surveys, the focal students were interviewed before and after the unit. They were also videotaped throughout the unit. As we were interested in understanding if and how the unit worked as intended, we identified the focal student who started the unit with low affect and knowledge in programming, yet showed the largest growth, based on the pre-post surveys. This student was Ella (a p seudonym). We then reviewed all video records we ha d collected with Ella featured in them. This occasion ally included full class activity in the classroom,  but very minimally. The bulk of our analysis leveraged a total of two hours of video footage of her and her partner, Astor, in the library. This footage included the pair: playing the game, designing a level, and programming their level in Scratch. Analysis consisted of a combination of open and In Vivo coding. We coded interactions around her use of materials across the different activities and id entified themes around how the activities shaped he r access to identity resources. We then collapsed them into two  contrasting categories of support/access/affordanc es and constraints/limitations/barriers. We focus our analytic findings on how the constraints and affordances shaped her participation. Table 1 shows examples of the identity resources that were available to Ella across activities. In the design activity, resources were paper, color pencils, and the robot marker (see Figure 3). In the programming activity, resources were a Chromebook, a computer mouse, Scratch, and their paper design. ## Findings

As discussed earlier, our expectation was that the choice of materials can positively shape one’s participation in learning activities. Through analysis based on practice-linked identity resources, we found that the use of materials, one’s relationship to others, and one’s view of sel f were indeed all linked and shaped Ella’s particip ation in the unit and identification with CS. However, we found that these interacted in ways that both constrained or afforded participation. We describe these constraints and affordances below. Constraints to participation In this section, we offer examples of the constraints that hindered Ella’s participation in the instructional unit. We found that constraints were often related to relational resources such as negotiating access to materials, negotiating decisions around materials, or seeking information from peers. Negotiating access to materials In terms of negotiating access to materials, whethe r the activity was unplugged or plugged further shaped how Ella and her partner participated. As partners, Ell a and Astor shared control of materials between the m. Since students were working in pairs, a student could ass ume one of two roles: a driver or a navigator. The driver controlled the materials, whereas the navigator gave instructions to the driver. Students were encouraged to switch roles between them but the switching was not enforc ed. During the board game play, the driver physical ly controlled the movement of the robot on the puzzle.  In the design activity, the driver moved the color ed paper pieces on the grid to design their puzzle. In the Scratch programming, the driver controlled the computer mouse and keyboard to make changes in Scratch (see Figure 2). Ella had more control of the materials in proportion to Astor during the unplugged activities (game play and design) than in the plugged (computer-based) activities (see Table 2). During the plugged activity they continually fought for control of the mouse and keyboard. During one fight for control, Ella was using the computer mouse to work on the digital version of the game board in Scratch. Astor wanted to do something else, so he tried to get control by moving his finger on the touch pad of the laptop. Astor said in a loud voice, “why don’t you let me do anything?” Hearing this, the librarian walked over to them. Ella may have interpreted being questioned by Astor as him undermining her expertise and a potential barrier to her participation. Accessing a relational resource in this example led to her practicing her creative choice and personalizing the design of their boardgame. However, Ella did not always seek help and sometim es conceded to Astor. By  conceding and letting Astor put a random green squ are that served no game purpose on the board, Ella was also conceding to participation on Astor’s terms. Affordances to participation Participation in the unit involved students working in pairs, thus it is not surprising that relational resources shaped Ella’s participation in many ways. As we saw in the previous section, her interactions with her partner and peers often created constraints and barriers to participation. In this section, we focus on her interactions with researchers ICLS2022 Proceedings 500 © ISLS that shaped her participation and connection to practice in positive ways. In particular, we focus on the interactions that happened during the unplugged activity of designing their board game level. The structure of this activity led to researchers walking around the room and asking students about their design and their procedures. As we saw in Table 2, Ella had more control of the materials than Astor during this activity and seemed engaged in the design. She showed excitement upon someone else noticing the co mplexity of their design, recognizing what others v alue, which is a type of ideational resource.

When Ella was done with the board and coloring in the distractor squares, a researcher walked up to her: R: “Look at that beautiful board. Wow” E: “Yea it’s.. a hard board ” R: “Is it?” E: “Very hard [laughs]” (Later in the conversation) R: ”Oh, do you think this is gonna work?” E: “Yea, we tried it many times” In this example, Ella once again took pride in her ability to create a complex design. She also showed confidence in her knowledge of the activity when she said “Yea, we tried it many times” when questioned by the researcher on whether it was going to work or not.

These examples show that when someone took notice of Ella and Astor’s game’s design, Ella was the first one to respond. She showed excitement about o thers noticing their design and took pride in it. I n the interactions Ella had with her peers, they did not recognize her competence. In her interactions with researchers, however, she received recognition for it. In other words, Ella was accessing her ideational resources during her interactions with the researchers. Her interactions with the researchers appeared to make her feel good about her design. Her agreeing that her blocks were “crazy” and adding that her board was “hard” showed she knew what was valued and good in terms of the practice of designing the game level. This also could explain why she resisted Astor’s efforts to add a green block to their board because it took away from what was valued (e.g., being hard). We found that Ella’s participation in the unit changed her ideas around what programming was and what participating in it meant. In the post-interview, she told us that: “[this project]...got me more interested in programming. Because at first, when I thought about it, I thought this was gonna be the most boring project ever. Now I really want to do computer programming.” Changing the materials and activities in Ella’s com puting class changed how she participated. The activities afforded different identity resources which further shaped her participation. When asked about how this project compared to what she typically did in her c omputer class, she said: “You weren't just sitting on your computer the entire time; you were actually designi ng your game.” A common stereotype that can negativ ely affect girls’ interest in programming is that it in volves sitting at a computer all day (Pantic et al,  2018). Ella’s preference for the unplugged activity of game desig n illustrates the importance of changing the activi ties and materials associated with programming and or CS in order to broaden participation. It is important to note that Ella had a positive ex perience despite the barriers to participation she experienced. Another student in Ella’s shoes may not have persisted. While we candesign curricula that empowers participation for individuals, like in the case of Ella, we need to also ensure the classroom culture and environment enables all students to participate. Without making any causal claims about Ella, we assert that the design of media is not sufficient for enabling participation of everyone. Ella needed ‘others’ in the setting to access her practicelinked identity resources. In case of Ella, she sought recognition of her ability to create a complex design. She did not find this recognition from her peers, but she d id find it from the researchers present in the room. We assert ICLS2022 Proceedings 501 © ISLS that in order to further the goal of broadening par ticipation in CS, more effort needs to be put in en gendering values in learning settings that help students feel supported and recognized by others.

## Discussion and conclusion

The study explored how one student, Ella, participated in a CS unit that was specifically designed around activities and materials not typically associated with CS: playing board games and designing a game level on paper. Using Nasir and Cooks’ practice-linked identity resources framework as a lens, we discussed how the design of the unit influenced student participation. Our findings showed how others in the setting afforded as well as co nstrained Ella’s participation in the unit.

Relational resources are one’s relationship to others in the environment. Our findings showed how Ella’s partner and her peers constrained her participation  and restricted her access to material resources. T he findings also showed how this lack of support mediated access to ideational resources. Ideational resources constitute one’s relationship to the place, practice, and the world and one’s ideas about themselves (Nasir & Cooks, 2009). In one example, we saw how Ella’s peers refused to share t heir ‘shimmy’ code with her, but shared it with her  (boy) partner. By excluding Ella, her peers undermined her place and participation in the learning setting. Challenging someone’s place can also have an effect on their ideas about themselves and who they consider themselves to be. Research has shown that subtle cues that suggest students don’t belong can weaken motivation to participate and that women leave STEM because the social climate un dermined their motivation (Cheryan et al, 2009). On  the other hand, receiving public recognition for one’s competence from more knowledgeable others can lead girls to identify themselves as expert coders (Hughes et al., 2021). Our findings revealed that Ella actively s ought recognition from more knowledgeable others, e.g., researchers, on multiple occasions. In one example, she sought feedback from a researcher to resolve a conflict with Astor when he challenged Ella on creating a new move for a blue square. Ella also sought recognition for her  ability to create a complex design. She was elated  when a researcher commented on her “crazy blocks”. In another example, a researcher commented on how beautiful their board (board game design) was. Ella’s response was “Yea it’s a hard board,” indicating that she was se eking recognition despite the lack of recognition from he r peers. The recognition Ella received from the res earchers helped her view herself as a competent coder. Ella’s persistence in learning and taking part in activities as an equal participant is also evidenced by her refusal to give up control of the materials in the plugged activity. Despite the barriers she faced in the form of a lack of recognition from and restrictions from accessing material resources by her peers, Ella persisted. However, it is important to note that not all students will.

## Implications

Our findings have implications for researchers, des igners, and instructors of CS curricula. The first implication relates to the design of the CS unit. We assert tha t in order to appeal to a broader set of participan ts, the media used for learning should be designed to afford part icipation from a variety of learners. Consider the example during the plugged (computer-based) programming activity in which Ella asked her peers (boys) to teach her how they had programmed their sprite to do a shimmy and  they refused. Similarly, Ella and Astor continued to fight for the control of computer mouse and keyboard. One  possible explanation for Ella’s tension with them can be explained by the prevailing gendered cultures in CS  education. Studies have shown that boys are less l ikely to share control of the resources during coding activities (Gray & Leith, 2004), while girls are likely to have a lower sense of belonging in computing activities than boy s owing to perceived stereotypes (Master et al., 20 16). In contrast to plugged activities, unplugged activities of playing the board game and designing the new game levels provided students with tangible materials. Ella was much more in control of the materials during the u nplugged activities, while she struggled to keep control of the materials in the plugged tasks. We also argue t hat the sequencing of our activities encouraged Ella’s part icipation. In our unit, CS concepts were introduced  in unplugged media and then revisited in the plugged media. Ella appeared to have a high level of engagement during the unplugged activities. Her participation changed during the plugged tasks owing to Astor seizing more control of materials. In spite of that, Ella sustained a hi gh level of engagement and persisted to keep contro l of the materials. We thus see how the use of unplugged materials to introduce CS concepts, before transitioning to the use of plugged materials may have helped Ella to assert herself during the computing tasks, and to go against the stereotypes embedded in the discipline of CS (Master et al., 2016). The second set of implications of this study relate s to the role of others present in a learning setti ng. Studies suggest that girls perceive CS as a stereotypically male discipline (Master et al., 2016). Thus, in order to engage girls in CS learning, the ‘others’ in the learning setting should recognize expertise of girls. Recognition of one's expertise can transform one’s view of the field (Keune et al., 2019) and help girls become more confident in their abilities to code (Hughes et al., 2021). A s we saw in Ella’s example, ‘others’ meant research ers in the ICLS2022 Proceedings 502 © ISLS room, her partner, as well as other students in the  classroom. In a more naturalistic classroom, the c lass teacher should play a similar role as the researchers did for Ella. We thus highlight that educator should be aware of the role their recognition can play in encouraging girls to participate in CS classrooms. The second part of our assertion relates to creating environments where girls are also recognized by their peers for their expertise. Even though Ella received recognition she sought from the researchers, we saw examples in which Ella lacked support from her partner and her peers. In their study of three girls in a code camp, Hughes et al. (2021) found ou t that public recognition of girls by their teacher s in a CS classroom can lead other students to perceive the g irls that receive recognition as expert coders. In our study, however, Ella did not receive recognition from her peers. In sum, CS curriculum design should foreground how activity structures and materials shape who participates and how they participate. Further, the design of learning settings should include strategies that help a broa der array of students feel supported as well as rec ognized by others.
