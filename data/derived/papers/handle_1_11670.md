# Academic Productive Dialogue in Practice: Teacher Insights on Approach Benefits and Application Challenges

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite the well-documented benefits of academically productive classroom dialogue—and the central role teachers play in facilitating it—dialogic pedagogy remains relatively rare in practice. This study investigates teachers’ perspectives on the perceived benefits and challenges of implementing dialogic teaching. As part of a four-year research-practice partnership aimed at promoting dialogic instruction in 4th–6th-grade language arts classrooms, we conducted thematic analysis of 17 elementary teachers' interviews. Findings indicate that teachers perceive dialogic teaching as offering both academic and social-emotional benefits. However, they also report two key challenges: establishing discourse norms and managing the cognitive demands of facilitating rich classroom dialogue. Notably, the benefits teachers describe suggest that their use of dialogic teaching may differ from how researchers typically define or measure it. The challenges, in turn, underscore the significant demands placed on teachers. Together, these findings offer insights into the gap between research and classroom implementation of dialogic pedagogy.

## Theoretical framework

Dialogic teaching is an instructional approach characterized by the co-construction of meaning through classroom discourse between teachers and students. In this approach, participants engage in dialogue, articulating reasoned positions and challenging each other's perspectives. Dialogic teaching includes open-ended questions, reasoning, and responses to others' contributions. Scholars have found that when students participate in this type of discourse, their learning and development are positively impacted (e.g., Reznitskaya & Gregory, 2013; Tao & Chen, 2024). The literature in this field mainly focuses on learning and academic gains. However, despite numerous professional development initiatives to promote dialogic teaching, it remains uncommon in practice, and there is a gap between the research on this topic and the ed ucational field (Hardman, 2019; Kim & Wilkinson, 2019; Matsumura & Garnier, 2015; Michaels & O’Connor, 2015; Sedova et al., 2014). Existing literature on this topic primarily examine s how various professional development features influence teachers ’ facilitation behavior and student achievement  (e.g., Alexander, 2018; Chen et al., 2020; Sedova et al., 2016). This l iterature is based on the  assumption that current professional development is insufficiently effective and that enhancing teacher  learning frameworks would improve learning outcomes. Teachers' beliefs, understandings, and ideas about dialogic teaching can provide significant insights into improving professional development frameworks and materials (Calcagni & Lago, 2018; Clarke & Hollingsworth, 2002). However, despite t eachers’ central role and the limited success of professiona l development initiatives, studies examining teachers' perspectives and interpretations of dialogic teaching remain scarce. In this study, we focus on the perspectives of teac hers participating in a professional development program on dialogic teaching, examining the benefits and challenges they ascribe to this approach. We explore how these teachers perceive the benefits and challenges of di alogic teaching to understand better the documented  gap between the research about dialogic teaching and classroom practices. Our research questions are:

1. What benefits, goals, and aims of dialogic teaching do teachers describe based on their experience?

2. What challenges to dialogic teaching do teachers identify based on their classroom experiences?

## Context, participants, and data collection

The study was conducted within a four-year-long research-practice partnership with three municipalities to promote dialogic teaching in upper elementary school language arts classes. The program’s professional learning model was based on school-based teacher communities. The program was based on curriculum text  and emphasized academic benefits. In the program's fourth year, semi-structured interviews were conducted with all 17 teachers, comprising the entire membership of th ree school-based professional learning communities we accompanied (all female, Mage = 41). The interviewees’ program participation ranged from one to four years and varied in teaching seniority (M = 14 years). The questions aimed to uncover different aspects of the  teachers’ knowledge, experiences with and perceptions about dialogic teaching and included, among others: “What are the goals of a class discussion?” and “What is your definition of a high-quality discussion?”.

## Data analysis

The interview analysis procedure was based on Peel’s (2020) approach to thematic analysis. All intervi ews were recorded and transcribed. Recordings and transcripts were reviewed in detail to search for segments in which the teachers referred to dialogic teaching benefits or challenges. When a segment of the interview referred to the benefits or challenges of dialogic teaching, we defined it as an analysis unit. We then classified and analyzed these segments according to their content. Based on this analysis, we formulated themes and insights.

## Findings

Teachers’ perceived benefits of dialogic teaching Academic benefits All 17 teachers stated that dialogic teaching promotes students ’ academic attainment and skills in at least some way. The academic benefits they mentioned included vocabulary building, an improved understanding, improved recall of learning content, and stimulation of students ’ thinking. For instance, in the following excerpt, Y asmin explains that student participation in reasoned discussion leads to better retention of the learning content: “I cannot forget that I’m arguing with a friend about my answer. I disagree with him (…), so I remember it.” Social-emotional benefits Another recurrent theme in 13 teacher interviews was how classroom dialogue  addresses and solves  socialemotional issues. These included, among others, ins tilling values and behavioral norms  and discussing socialemotional issues introduced via the selected texts. Teachers also described addressing behavioral issues raised by the students, such as conflicts during recess. These issues were discussed through collective class discussion, including arguments and persuasion, through which  a collective and student-led decision  was reached. Teachers’ perceived challenges of dialogic teaching Instilling talk norms Sixteen teachers discussed the challenge of instilling norms that enable classroom discourse, including mu tual respect and listening. For instance, a ccording to Vardit, open discussion has the potenti al for disruptions and chaos, rendering talk norms necessary: “[I]f we talk too much, it opens up a room for disruption. And you kind of always need to bring them back to someplace where we can have a discussion.” Intellectual demand Thirteen teachers mentioned the intellectual demand in facilitating academic-oriented discussions. This demand manifests in different forms. For instance, teachers must balance maintaining an open discussion with gu iding it toward the lesson's objectives. As Revi states, “The challenge is that the discussion goes to a plac e you didn ’t want it to go because it always needs to return to that point, to that lesson ’s anchor, to serve its purpose. ” Yali describes the difficulty of formulating the right q uestion: “Sometimes I exaggerate a bit, asking a question tha t’s too challenging or tiring. You need to balance.” According to Shani, guiding a discussion is demanding and tiring: “It does require much concentration. After a dialogic lesson, I’m exhausted. I feel that it requires a lot.”

## Discussion and conclusions

Although the program focused on curriculum-based textual discussions and academic benefits, teachers expanded beyond the curriculum to social and personal issues  and emphasized social-emotional benefits in different types of discussions. These benefits suggest they use discussions and contexts researchers might fail to recognize. The challenges teachers discuss highlight the significant demands that dialogic teaching places on them and may also explain why they choose not to practice it. These findings offe r a potential explanation for the research-practice gap in this field since teachers might avoid conducting academic discussions due to their cognitive demands while conducting social-emotional discussions that researchers  may miss.  Thus, the rarity of dialogic teaching in schools is not necessarily due to insufficient professional  development, as often perceived, but may stem from  other reasons. The study demonstrates the value of teachers' voices in advancing research and practice.
