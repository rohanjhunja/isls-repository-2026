# Designing AI Partner-Embedded Learning Environments to Support AI Literacies

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As AI technologies become increasingly integrated into schools, questions arise about what students should learn about AI and how AI-powered tools can support collaborative learning. This symposium reports on the Institute of Student-AI Teaming (iSAT), which designs AI partners and curricular activity systems to promote AI literacy and collaboration. Unlike most classroom AI tools designed for 1:1 interactions, iSAT develops AI partners as social collaborators that both facilitate group learning and serve as objects of inquiry —helping students “look under the hood” of AI systems. Our multi-year co-design process across three U.S. states engages teachers, students, and parents to build integrated systems of curriculum, technology, and professional learning. The symposium’s six posters span AI design, infrastructure, curriculum, and teacher learning, illustrating how AI can be productively embedded within classroom collaboration. Together, they advance understanding of how AI systems can meaningfully support and transform collaborative learning. Objective of the session As AI technologies become increasingly embedded in education (Reich & Dukes, 2024), so do questions about what students should learn about AI systems (AI literacy) and how AI-powered tools may support (or hinder) student learning. While there is growing excitement about AI’s potential to transform education, decades of research highlight repeated failures of earlier innovations to do so. Scholars argue these failures often stem from underappreciating the infrastructural nature of technology and overestimating its power to drive change in classroom practice (Roschelle et al., 2010). First, technology is infrastructural in that its value lies not in its particular content but in its ability to generate new representations of disciplinary ideas and create opportunities for students to engage more deeply in those ideas (Hegedus & Kaput, 2003). Second, like previous technologies, today’s AI tools will not transform education on their own. Their success depends on how curriculum activity systems—teacher professional learning (PL), instructional materials, and technology —work together to reshape learning environments (Vahey et al., 2013). Their success also depends on how teachers and students work together within the system to support learning. This symposium reports on work from the Institute of Student-AI Teaming (iSAT), an institute that aims to design AI-powered tools and curricular activity systems that promote collaborative knowledge building and student learning around AI-literacy specifically (D’Mello et al., 2024, OECD, 2025). Our project intentionally combines both AI-literacy and AI as a tool for collaborative learning in ways that will enable the AI-partner to serve simultaneously as a collaboration support and an object of inquiry as students think critically about how the AI works. That is, our AI partners play a dual role of supporting student collaboration and as a tool for “looking under the hood” at how AI systems work. Supporting collaborative learning is a longstanding goal of both the ISLS and CSCL community, and is crucial because we know that learning is inherently a social process. Yet, the dominant approach to incorporating AI inside K-12 classrooms focuses on 1:1 interactions between students and AI systems. iSAT’s approach is radically different: we aim to develop AI as a social, collaborative partner that works alongside students and teachers to deepen their engagement with one another. This goal has required that our team engage in multi-year co-design processes to better understand the needs and desires of multiple users 556 and stakeholders (e.g. teachers, students, parents) as we work to develop and refine AI partners, curriculum materials, and professional development to support the development of AI literacies. Over the past five years, our work has involved working with stakeholders in districts across three U.S. states to conceptualize, develop, enact, and refine our curricular activity system. This session will focus on the design of iSAT’s curricular activity system to deepen the field’s conversation about the role of AI-systems in supporting collaborative learning. Across the posters, we will describe how our co-design practices, back-end models, classroom pilot studies, professional learning, and curricular routines and designs worked toward our emerging understanding of the utility of these tools for supporting collaborative learning. ## Perspectives

Here, I view stories about co-design activities as an evolving boundary object that guides how large-scale AI-Ed implementation teams take on co-design findings (Star & Griesemer, 1989). Boundary objects are conceptualized as “how artifacts can fulfill a specific function in bridging intersecting practices” (pg. 134, Akkerman & Bakker, 2011). As Star (2010) clarifies, a boundary object is characterized by interpretive flexibility across different contexts, allowing each context to tailor the object to its own needs locally.  Finally, boundary objects follow particular trajectories within organizational contexts; once created, boundary objects evolve through local tailoring, standardize over time, and, finally, fade away as the standardization practices begin to constrain possible local adaptations. In turn, new boundary objects are then created. Along with several other iSAT members, I co-led the development of three co-design workshops that engaged youth of color in envisioning expansive possibilities for AI-supported collaboration (Chang et al., 2024). During that time period, iSAT also collectively began to explore the findings from the workshops. Here, I study the presentations I (and also other iSAT members) created to share these findings, both internally and externally. Additionally, I examine the field notes from discussions held during design meetings to build the Community Builder (CoBi), a tool created by iSAT that was directly informed by prototypes developed by youth participants during our co-design sessions (Breideband et al., 2023). Across these data sources, I qualitatively coded fo r instances where meeting participants referenced the narrative about how CoBi was created. Initially, the CoBi co-design narrative described how youth participants in our co-design engagements visited a graduate student cooperative house, where they learned how members of that cooperative house collectively created agreements about house policies, which they were then held accountable for. This cooperative house then inspired a new AI Tool, CoBi, that supported collaboration by helping youth stay accountable to collaborative norms that they themselves  deemed to be important. Institute members repeatedly described how this narrative helped them understand the collective commitments and pedagogical values embodied in the youth’s envisionings of CoBi, beyond what could be conveyed simply through the youth participants' drawings of CoBi. Over time, this narrative about co-design became “standardized” through both internal and external facing presentations, as slides about this narrative were re-purposed and shortened. Concurrently, my own deeper analysis of the co-design data introduced new complexities about CoBi. For instance, CoBi had not only sprung from the cooperative house visit but also from the participants’ developing political identities; indeed, CoBi was exciting to the youth primarily because it could be envisioned to support their collective agency in creating better school-wide conditions.

## Significance

Large-scale interdisciplinary teams building AI-Ed tools now often make commitments to responsible innovation (Stilgoe et al., 2013). This work demonstrates how boundary objects from co-design spaces can both accelerate interdisciplinary collaboration and responsiveness to the co-design space, while simultaneously constraining the uptake of particular ideas, particularly as analysis of co-design efforts deepens and shifts. This work points to the need for constant iteration and collaboration in the use and revision of boundary objects. Poster 2: Jigsaw Interactive Agent (JIA): Pilot Study of JIA’s Real-time Support for Collaboration through Productive Uncertainty Interventions Mon-Lin Monica Ko, Chelsea Chandler, Sierra Rose, Brooklyn Clines, Emily Watts, John Weatherley, Peter W. Foltz 558 Objectives This study investigates how an AI partner can support small-group collaboration in a middle school classroom jigsaw activity. While jigsaws have long been used to structure collaborative learning, their success depends on high-quality facilitation. Teachers face challenges in providing real-time, tailored support across multiple groups of students. The preliminary designs of our AI partner were tested in the lab (see Doherty et al., 2025). We designed an AI partner to generate just-in-time interventions that foster productive collaboration, aiming to extend CSCL research on how artificial intelligence can scaffold multi-party learning interactions.

## Perspectives

Drawing on existing work on collaborative problem solving (Sun et al., 2020), we position AI not as a replacement for teachers, but as a partner that captures multiparty discourse and generates timely prompts to support social, cognitive, and metacognitive  processes in group learning. Drawing on scholarship on productive uncertainty (Manz & Suarez, 2018), we designed the partner to intervene in 4 ways:  provide social support, connect ideas, problematize assumptions, and stabilize group progress. This study  investigates both the accuracy and appropriateness of the AI partner interventions as well as students’ responses to them. We embedded the AI partner within a single lesson from a co-designed AI literacy curriculum implemented in two school districts with students aged 13-14 years old (N=64 groups). In this lesson, students engaged in a jigsaw task where they read about differ ent moderation approaches, generated criteria for evaluating these approaches, and then worked collaboratively to summarize the approaches and rank them based on their ability to foster inclusive online communities. In the context of this lesson, the AI partner, the Jigsaw Interactive Agent (JIA) analyzed student talk in real time using a multimodal pipeline and provided one-minute interval interventions via a classroom web interface. We tested three approaches to JIA (rule-based, LLM-based, hybrid). Data s ources included audio, video, log files, and 347 AI –student interaction transcripts. Human annotators evaluated (a) appropriateness of interventions, (b) student uptake, and (c) emotional responses. The LLM and hybrid models generated contextually appropriate interventions 82– 87% of the time, compared to 63% for the rule-based model. Most interventions focused on connecting (50%) and social support (26%). Although 61% of the AI partner interventions received no reaction from students, when students did  notice the intervention, 28% of students’ responses integrated the AI partner’s feedback into group reasoning, deepening or shifting the trajectory of their subsequent discussion. Emotional responses were mostly neutral or positive, with 78% of interactions judged as authentic through human annotations. Instances of genuine uptake included students' increased attention to criteria when ranking the approaches, weighing tradeoffs between the approaches, and incorporating aspects of the AI partner intervention in their subsequent conversations.

## Significance

This study demonstrates how AI partners might be able to extend teacher facilitation in CSCL contexts by supporting multiparty discourse and fostering productive uncertainty. The findings highlight design considerations for AI partners: the importance of integrating rule-based collaboration analytics with adaptive LLM feedback, calibrating the timing and framing of interventions, and exploring new embodiments to increase student uptake. This work also underscores that evaluating AI in collaborative learning requires attending to how feedback is noticed, integrated, and trusted by students. These insights advance the design of AI systems that authentically scaffold collaboration in real-world classrooms.

Foltz Objectives Collaborative discourse in classrooms represents a rich source of data for understanding and supporting learning. Yet despite advances in discourse analytics, current AI tools often fall short in interpreting the full context of student dialogue, or aligning feedback to pedagogically meaningful constructs (Graesser et al., 2018). Our objective is to design and evaluate AI partners that can analyze and respond to student collaboration in principled 559 ways anchored in established frameworks of collaboration and pedagogical practice. By grounding large language models (LLMs) in frameworks such as Productive Uncertainty (Manz & Suarez, 2018), Collaborative Problem Solving (CPS) (e.g., Sun et al., 2020), a nd Community Agreements (e.g., OpenSciEd), we aim to create AI partners capable of both assessing and scaffolding higher-order collaborative and metacognitive skills in real time.

## Perspectives

Prior research has shown that “traditional” Natural Language Processing (NLP) approaches-such as discriminative models trained to detect discourse moves or collaborative indicators-can achieve reasonable accuracy (e.g. Dowell et al., 2019). However, su ch models are typically limited to surface-level features, local utterances, and limited context. They struggle to capture the dynamic, multi-turn, and socially situated nature of collaborative learning. In contrast, LLMs offer the potential for deeper con textual understanding and generative, real-time feedback, enabling more adaptive and pedagogically aligned forms of support. The challenge lies in ensuring that these models’ responses are not just accurate in assessing collaborative states and fluent in their feedback, but also that they are instructionally principled. This work brings a dual perspective: (1) advancing AI’s capacity to understand collaborative learning as a social-cognitive process, and (2) ensuring that AI-generated interventions align with established theories of learning and assessment constructs rather thandata-driven heuristics.

We describe our experience in the design and evaluation of two distinct AI partners: CoBi (the Community Builder, based on CPS-mapped Community Agreements) and JIA (the Jigsaw Interactive Agent) - each implementing different levels of alignment between LLM capabilities and collaborative learning frameworks. Both partners process spoken discourse from small-group interactions to provide support. CoBi uses a fine-tuned RoBERTa model that is trained to identify discourse behavior related to Community Agreements (e.g., moving thinking forward, being respectful, and committed to the community). Ongoing work is supporting the translation of this model to a generative LLM that can bring in custom classroom agreements and enables more contextual feedback to students. JIA is an LLM-based partner that generates real-time feedback based on multi-turn context windows, and is guided by principles of Productive Uncertainty to encourage elaboration, sense-making, and collaboration among students participating in small-group work. JIA was developed using three unique approaches: (1) discriminative models with rule-based thresholds and hard-coded feedback, (2) prompting an LLM with descriptions of our framework and desired types of feedback, and (3) a hybrid model that uses both rule-based outputs and framework descriptions to allow an LLM to be grounded in both pedagogical heuristics and generative LLM capabilities. All agents were evaluated using classroom data combining quantitative and qualitative performance metrics.

Across agents, we observe clear trade-offs between interpretability, contextual depth, and pedagogical alignment depending on the dual use and roles of discriminative models and LLMs. Traditional fine-tuned models provide stable performance but limited adaptability to novel contexts. LLM-based agents achieve greater contextual sensitivity (identifying when students were reasoning well or exploring connecting ideas from a larger contextual window), but occasionally produce feedback that is not entirely pedagogically grounded. Early classroom trials indicate that framework-aligned prompting substantially improves feedback quality and relevance. For example, the hybrid JIA partner harnesses both rule-based knowledge and LLM-based contextual conversation information to intervene in a group that was evaluating gaming moderation systems by giving them support in problematizing, saying, “What makes you think the paid moderators approach is the most effective? Are there any potential downsides to this approach?”. In response, students elaborated on the pros and cons of choosing AI + paid moderators as the best approach.

## Significance

This work demonstrates both the promise and the necessity of theory-aligned AI for assessing and supporting collaborative learning. By embedding frameworks like Productive Uncertainty and CPS into model objectives, we move toward AI partners that not only recognize collaboration through defined constructs but that can also participate meaningfully in supporting it. Penuel, Tamara Sumner Objectives This study investigates how curriculum routines can mediate the purposeful integration of AI-Partner tools into secondary classrooms in ways that support collaborative learning, equity, and student agency. Specifically, we focus on understanding the interactions between routines and AI tools in the context of co-designed AI partner, CoBi (Chang et al. 2024, Breideband et al., 2023), developed to help students establish and revisit community agreements that govern classroom collaboration (Bush, et al., 2025).

## Perspectives

We draw on sociocultural theories that frame learning as a collective enterprise, where heterogeneity of ideas and participation is central to equity (Rosebery et al., 2010; Paris, 2012). At the same time, AI technologies are predisposed to convergent behaviors, reinforcing dominant patterns in their training data (Madaio et al., 2022). This creates tensions between equity goals and algorithmic bias. To address this, we developed curriculum routines, repeatable structures of teacher and student activity, with the intent to help students and teachers span boundaries in classrooms that help them engage with the AI in critical and agentic ways while avoiding the potential harms of AI’s convergence bias. We analyze how these routines act as boundary objects (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011) that can mediate student transitions to productive reflection on their collaboration (meta-collaboration) and agentic critique of AI’s accuracy.

The research draws on a multi-year partnership with teachers in a suburban/rural U.S. district. During the 20242025 school year, 11 teachers in various co-design and implementation focused PLCs implemented iSAT curriculum with CoBi. Using data from video of middle-grade (12-14 year old) classroom implementations, we employed thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006) to identify moments related to the research questions. We report on an instrumental case study (Stake, 1995) of Ms. Tessa’s (pseudonym) classroom, one of several case studies in subsequent research.

Teachers using CoBi predominantly enacted routines with high integrity, frequently adapting them in expansive ways aligned with goals. They often leveraged mistakes productively, incorporated student ideas, and reoriented routines toward new disciplinary aims. Though occasional disruptions occurred, overall enactments showed CoBi malleability for supporting collaboration and reflective learning. Despite this, opportunities for CoBi and the routines to support critical agency were rare; therefore, we present  an instrumental case study highlighting an instance where CoBi did support this critical agency.

The case vignette illustrates how curriculum routines could function as a boundary object, mediating between the AI partner’s algorithmic classifications and students’ lived experiences of collaboration. In Ms. Tessa’s classroom, the community agreement revisiting routine prompted students’ initial reflections about how they collaborated and student critique about the AI system’s judgements. By projecting CoBi’s AI-generated sentence cloud of student talk and associated confidence intervals (for ASR and sem antic classification), the teacher repositioned the AI’s classifications as provisional, framing them as “proto-judgements” open to critique. This encouraged students to exercise critical agency, weighing the AI’s labels against their own interpretations of respectful or productive talk. Rather than passively adopting system outputs, students engaged in meta-collaborative reflection, using the AI as a mirror to surface features of their teamwork that might otherwise remain invisible. The routine anchored dialogue while the tool generated new representations of collaboration that could be interrogated, sustaining an extended class conversation about norms, practices of equitable group work and students’ lived experiences of collaboration.

## Significance

This study shows how AI partners like CoBi can work with curriculum routines to present new representations of collaboration that anchor reflection and extend fleeting student observations into sustained dialogue. Embedded in curricular activity systems —pr ofessional learning, instructional materials, and classroom routines —CoBi supported high-integrity enactments, diverse adaptations, and productive use of mistakes and student ideas. Yet opportunities for critical agency were less frequent, requiring careful teacher framing. This research shows how CoBi’s outputs can become provisional “proto-judgements,” functioning as a boundary object that students could 561 question, fostering meta-collaborative reflection. While critical agency was rare, the case study demonstrates the possibilities for designers to support teachers as they work to 1) balance AI’s convergent tendencies with the need to value divergent contributions, and to 2) catalyze meta-collaborative reflections that sustain purposeful, collaborative learning.

## Perspectives

Teachers face a challenge: both they and their students have unlimited access to AI tools, yet neither group has had opportunities to develop AI literacy (Touretzky et al., 2022). Instructing this new topic and embedding it into the curriculum, without agreed-upon standards, is challenging. On iSAT, we designed curriculum-linked PL for secondary teachers across content areas to address a triple innovation challenge: teaching about AI, using AI-enabled tools, and adopting student-centered pedagogies. By leveraging teacher’s diverse disciplinary perspectives, we explored both the technical and ethical facets of AI literacy while creating a testbed for adaptations of the same unit (Ko et al., 2023). Our PL design supported teachers in addressing the challenge of implementing the Moderation Unit —a n AI-literacy unit that incorporates CoBi, an AI agent designed to facilitate classroom collaboration. Research shows effective PL in STEM focuses on high-quality instructional materials (Lynch et al., 2019). Integrating curriculum and AI tools created both opportunities and challenges, especially when the curriculum itself teaches AI Literacy (Tan et al., 2025). Seven secondary grade teachers from twoneighboring U.S. school districts participated in a week-long (32-hour) PL workshop in Summer 2024. They represented diverse disciplines, including history, English Language Arts (ELA), math, and design thinking.

For this analysis, we drew on three PL activities: teachers experiencing the unit in a “student hat,” interacting with CoBi in a “student hat,” and planning for adaptations. The first activity supported AI literacy development; the second familiarized teachers with CoBi; the third allowed them to apply what they learned. Together, these emphasized student-centered learning and integration of AI literacy and tools. Teachers engaged with the triple innovation challenge by adapting instructional materials, planning to integrate CoBi, and fostering student-centered approaches.

First, teachers shared adaptations they planned to make to the Moderation unit to foster critical perspectives on AI within their disciplines. A math teacher, for example, planned to teach the unit as written while connecting the section on AI confidence scores to mathematical concepts. A middle school ELA teacher went further, designing an additional lesson on generative AI to help students analyze bias in the news and media. These adaptations showed how teachers linked AI literacy to their own disciplinary expertise and curricular goals. Teachers also expressed enthusiasm for CoBi as a classroom tool. One noted it provided “100% positive reinforcement,” while another emphasized that because CoBi is built on agreements set by students, “kids can expect what is going to happen and be able to relax in the environment.” Such observations highlight CoBi’s potential to encourage constructive participation and strengthen classroom community. Finally, teachers demonstrated readiness to elevate student expertise in their implementation plans. For example, a high school ELA teacher described using driving question boards to give students more opportunities to generate their own questions, fostering authentic and student-centered conversations.

## Perspectives

We used a storyline model to center student learning on problems rooted in experience or community priorities (Penuel et al., 2024). Co-design teams—including youth— refined goals, selected problems, and built coherent lesson sequences. Iterative refinement addressed diverse teacher and student contexts, with varied sites reducing “hothouse” risks (Fishman & Krajcik, 2003) and professional learning supporting educator sensemaking. Our project followed a Design-Based Implementation Research (DBIR) approach (Fishman et al., 2013). Goals and focal problems were negotiated with students, teachers, and district leaders. We adapted storyline co-design approaches (Penuel et al., 2022) to balance AI literacy goals with connections to students’ lived experiences. Here, we provide a design narrative —a retrospective account of design choices informed by observations, student learning data, and teacher feedback (Gravemeijer & Cobb, 2013). Negotiating a focus for the unit We modified the co-design process to incorporate youth voice and to exercise more agency in defining learning goals. Previously, youth contributed only through surveys, but here we recruited young people —primarily youth of color—from partner districts to h elp select the anchoring problem and shape the storyline. Survey data still informed the team, but classroom teachers, afterschool educators, and youth negotiated the unit’s goals and design challenge.

Co-adaptation of the units Because we recruited teachers across disciplines to enact the units, including STEM, science, mathematics, and English language arts, we recognized that the units could not be implemented as is, but would need to be adapted. Therefore, we adapted the professional learning model for storyline units (see McNeill et al., 2022) to include opportunities for teachers to review and identify how the unit’s learning goals intersect with standards they typically address in their discipline. We also provided time for teachers to discuss and plan modifications to slides. Iterative refinement of the units Initial implementation in two 8th-grade classrooms (2023– 2024) showed partial success. The unit was too long and often taught after state tests, limiting discussions of AI ethics. Student work showed awareness of algorithmic bias, but depth varied. We revised the unit by shortening it, focusing on AI and human moderation in gaming spaces, and strengthening lessons on bias in AI model training. These revisions improved usability and increased some teachers’ confidence in implementing units.

## Significance

Our project highlights challenges in developing instructional materials for AI literacy: co-designers need sufficient literacy themselves, units must adapt to multiple subject areas, integrating AI tools into instruction requires support, and assessments must guide iteration.
