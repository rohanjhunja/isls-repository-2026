# Investigating Students’ Immersion, Motivation and Cultural Heritage Learning in Gamified and Non-gamified Virtual Reality Environments

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigated the effect of gamification on university students’ immersion, motivation, and cultural heritage learning in the context of a Virtual Reality (VR) learning environment. An experimental research design was adopted to analyze the experience of forty-six (n=46) undergraduate students who were randomly assigned to two conditions (Gamified / Non-gamified VR environment). Data were collected using pre-and post-learning assessments, immersion and motivation questionnaires. Analyses showed that students in the gamified VR learning environment experienced higher levels of attention and flow, reported greater perceived competence, and exhibited increased learning gains, in comparison to their counterparts who used the non-gamified VR environment. Moreover, higher and more positive correlations were identified between students’ immersion, motivation and learning in the gamified condition. The findings of this study can contribute to the principled design of VR environments to optimize students’ knowledge acquisition and learning experience.

## Introduction

Recently there is increasing interest in using Virtual Reality (VR) to support learning in K-16 educational contexts (Pellas et al., 2021; Radianti et al., 2020). Grounded on unique affordances such as mul timodal interaction, embodiment, and sense of presence, VR has been argued to facilitate experiential learning, resulting to enhanced learning experiences which lead to increased achievement and motivation  (Tilhou al., 202 0). Despite these arguments, empirical studies have often yielded contradictory findings when comparing the learning effectiveness of VR to traditional instruction. Several explanations have been proposed for th ese contradictory results. For instance, Hamilton et al. (2021) argued that the methods used in these studies were insufficient and weak.  Another explanation that has been gaining traction is that the immersive nature of VR environments may detract attention from the learning process (Parong & Mayer, 2021; Makransky & Petersen, 2021). There is an ongoing debate on whether immersion reflects a subjective psychologic al process or whether it is simply an objective concept reflecting the technical affordances and properties  of a VR system (Agrewal et al., 2020). We question the latter assumption that immersion is an inherent characteristic  of VR environments, in favor of the position that immersion is an individual psychological process which may not always result in increased sense of presence and flow (Georgiou & Kyza, 2017; Georgiou & Kyza, 2018). This definition of immersion, as a psychological process reflecting the gradated users’ cognitive and emotional involvement, which may or may not result in a high sense of flow and presence, may provide an alternate explanation of the learning effectiveness of VR environments. Implied in the argument that VR environments are inherently immersive is a claim that students are also inherently motivated to learn in virtual educational experiences; however, we also argue that students’ motivation to learn in VR environments should not been taken for granted. Despite their advantages, VR learning experiences also come with certain challenges. For instance, some students may feel overwhelmed by free exploration; in turn, this may negatively affect their motivation and their engagement level (Das et al., 2022), which are prerequisites for learning (Blumenfeld et al., 2006). Motivation, which includes both intrinsic and extrinsic factors that drive learners to engage with and persist in learning activities, plays an important role in determining the level of effort and commitment learners invest in their learning endeavours. Ensuring that students’ motivation remains at high levels is, therefore, important (Krapp, 1999; Tsirulnikov et al., 2023). Gamification, namely the process of applying game el ements and mechanics to non-game contexts to motivate people to achieve certain goals or complete specific tasks (Deterding et al., 2011), has been discussed as a possible contributor to enhancing students’ engagement, making learning more attractive and  more effective (Caponetto et al., 2014). Gamification involves taking advantage of the psych ological properties of games, such as competition, rewards, achievements, or social interaction, and incorporating them into var ious domains, such as education.  W hile the gamification of learning has a long histor y, significant budgetary and technological constraints have limited the use of gamification in  VR so far (Khaitova, 2021). This work investigates the effect of gamification on students’ learning, immersion and motivation  in VR environments for cultural heritage learning.

## Participants and sampling

An experimental design was adopted, which included two groups of university students (n=46) aged 19-23 years, recruited through convenience sampling. Each student was randomly assigned to one of two conditions (Condition 1: Gamified VR environment, Condition 2: Non-gamified VR environment), forming two groups of 23 students each, to investigate students’ immersion  and motivation between conditions, learning gains within and betwe en the two conditions, as well as the relation between all variables  (immersion, motivation and learning gains)  per condition. Condition 1 included 10 males and 13 females, whereas Condition 2 included 7 males and 16 females (as self-identified).

Learning intervention As part of this study, we developed a  VR learning environment to support  students’ cultural heritage learning (Nisiotis et al., 2023). The VR environment employs an inquiry-based learning scenario  according to which students assume the role of historians who investigate the dating of a 6th century AD ceiling mosaic at the church of Panagia Aggeloktisti  in Cyprus, through the collection of evidence. The mosaic, located over the sanctuary apse in a half dome wall structure, is the only sur viving wall mosaic in Cyprus to date, and is considered to be one of the most significant ceiling mosaics of early Christian art. When the user connects to the VR environment (via a head-held device), a dedicated learning companion, acting as a Non-Playing Character (NPC), engages with the user, delivering details about the learning environment through audio and captions. The VR experience is grounded on an inquiry-based learning approach; it involves exploring three learning stations within the church, collecting evidence and complet ing a quiz at each learning station before reaching  the fourth and final learning station. The NPC guides the user throughout, providing learning content, historical insights, instructions, and gui dance for each learning station. Each section features learning materials in various multimedia formats, focusing on  the mosaic's historical significance  and on information on how mosaics were made in different time periods. The gamified VR learning environment includes the following gamification elements: (a) scoring system, (b) badg es, and (c) time-limit to complete the inquiry (Souropetsis et al., 2023). The non-gamified version is exactly the same, without the three gamification elements.

## Data collection and analysis

We collected quantitative data on students’ learnin g, motivation, and immersion; post-intervention interviews were also conducted to  further explain the findings from the quantitative analyses.  More specifically, a pre-post questionnaire assessed students’ initial and post-intervention understanding; the questionnaire included eight multiple-choice items evaluating students’ factual knowledge, two open-ended questions evaluat ing students’ conceptual understanding, and two open-ended questions evaluating students’ reasoning, for a maximum score of

10. After the completion of the inquiry mission in the VR, students completed the Virtual R eality Immersion

(VRI) questionnaire, which was adapted from a  validated questionnaire developed to measure immersion in augmented reality settings (Georgiou & Kyza, 2017). The VRI included three scales: (a) The Engagement scale, consisting of two subscales (Interest and Usability), (b) The Engrossment scale, consisting of two subscales (Focus of attention and Emotional attachment), and (c) The Total Immersion scale, consisting of two subscales (Presence and Flow). Cronbach’s alpha for all scales and subscales ranged from 0.77-0.87. In addition, students responded to the Intrinsic Motivation Inventory (IMI) (Ryan, 1982). The IMI is a multidimensional questionnaire that aims to evaluate the subjective experience of participants involved in an activity. According to the guidelines provided by the authors of the instrument, fi ve subscales of the IMI (a total of 27 items) were used in this study: Interest/enjoyment, Perceived competence, Effort, Experienced pressure and tension, and Value/usefulness. Cronbach’s alpha for all scales and subscales range d from 0.72-0.88. The reliability test confirmed the already validated scale.

The questionnaire data were analyzed statistically using T-test for paired samples, T-Test for independent samples, as well as Pearson’s r coefficient for the correlations. The aforementioned statistical tests were conducted to investigate (a) whether there was a statistically significant diffe rence in students’ total immersion  and selfreported intrinsic motivation between the two conditions (T-Test for independent samples), (b) whether there was a statistically significant difference between the pre-and post-test learning scores within the two conditions (Ttest for paired samples) a s well as  between the two conditions  (T-Test for independent samples), and  (c) the correlations between immersion, motivation and lear ning scores within each condition  (Pearson’s r coefficient). The research design was approved by the National Bioethics Committee (ΕΕΒΚ ΕΠ 2023.01.128).

## Findings

Analyses showed statistically significant differences in immersion between the two conditions. Specifically, the focus of attention in the gamified condition (M=5.96, SD=.83) exceeded the focus of attention in the non-gamified condition (M=5.32, SD=.85), and this difference was statistically significant [t=2.57, p<.05]. Moreover, students’ sense of flow in the gamified condition was higher (M=5.72. SD=1.26) in comparison to the non-gamified condition (M=4.82, SD=1.03) and this difference was statistically significa nt [t=2.64, p<.05]. This indicates that students who participated in the gamified experience felt more immersed than the students who participated in the non-gamified experience.

No statistically significant differences were identifi ed in students’  total intrinsic motivation  scores. However, the analysis revealed a statistically sign ificant difference in students’ perceived competence  between the two conditions. More specifically, the perceived competence of th e students in the gamified condition (M=5.30, SD= 1.15) exceeded the perceived competence of studen ts in the non-gamified condition (M=4.49, SD=1.26) and this difference was statistically signif icant [t=2.28, p<.05]. This indicates that students who participated in the gamified experience felt more competent than the students who participated in the non-gamified experience.

No statistically significant differences were also found between the overall pre-test learning scores of the students in the gamified and non-gamified condition. However, students’ learning scores in the gamified condition (M=5.93, SD=1.00) exceeded the pre-test learning scores (M=3.21, SD=1.50) and this difference was statistically significant [t=-9.63, p<.001]. Likewise, in the non-gamified condition, the learning scores of the students (M=5.10, SD=1.33), exceeded the pre-test learning  scores (M=2.90, SD=1.37) and this difference was also statistically significant [t=-8.05, p<.001]. Furthermore, the overall post-test learning scores of students in the gamified condition (M=5.93 SD=1.00) exceeded the overall post-test learning scores of students in the non-gamified condition (M=5.10 SD=1.33) [t=2.40, p<.05] and this difference was statistically significant. These results suggest that gamification supported students’ immersion, mo tivation and learning. In the gamified condition we also identified a greater number of statistically significant and stronger positive correlations between all the components of motivation and the di mensions of immersion (except pressure/tension which was negative related to immersion) in comparison to the non-gamified conditions. Collectively, these findings indicate that students’ learning, experienced immersion and motivation in the  gamified condition not only increased but were also more positively correlated in comparison to the non-gamified condition.

## Conclusions and implications

This study employed the same inquiry-based VR environment with and without gamification elements to explore the influence of gamification on students’ immersion, motivation, and learning. This research went beyond a mere comparison of different media to understand if VR supports learning, examining different variables that can support learning. Our approach is aligned with Kozma’s  (1991) argument that “the capabilities (attributes) of a particular medium, in conjunction with methods that  take advantage of these capabilities, interact wit h and influence the ways learners represent and process information” (p. 179). According to our findings the integration of the in quiry-based approach in the VR environments supported students’ learning in both conditions; these findings agree with Tilhu et al. (2020) who stated that the pairing of inquiry learning with VR environments may result in enhanced learning experiences. At the same time, our findings indicate that enriching an inquiry-based VR environment with gamification elements may contribute to students’ increased learning gains, higher motivation in terms of perceived competence, and higher experienced immersion. These findings provide empirical substantiation for the value of gamification  in the d esign of VR learning environments, if appropriately designed. However, one of the  limitations of this work is that  we approached the impact of the gamification elements used in thi s study holistically rather than reporting on the potential impact of each of the different gamification elements. Next steps of this work are to analyze the interview data to investigate the participants’ statements ab out the gamification elements which may have influe nced the learning process, and students’ motivation, and experienced immersion.
