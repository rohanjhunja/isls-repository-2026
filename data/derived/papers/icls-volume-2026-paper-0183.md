# Co-Designing Hybrid Intelligent Learning Environments: One Educator’s Journey Navigating Relationships with AI

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have resulted in a “replacement framing” in education. Hybrid intelligence (HI) has been proposed to reframe the relationship between educators and AI as collaborative, positioning humans and AI as partners in learning. Empirically, however, little is known about the impact of the HI system on the complex ecologies of learning in K-12 classrooms. To foreground educators’ agency in shaping HI systems for learning, we partner with a group of secondary science teachers exploring the role of hybrid intelligent systems in science learning contexts. We present a case of one educator’s experience to illustrate how teachers navigated their relationship with AI during the process to co-design a hybrid learning environment based on the existing science learning simulation, HoloOrbits. Findings highlight teachers’ evolving trajectories and leadership in co-designing AI-augmented systems, which reveals how teachers negotiate forms of AI-teaming aligned with their pedagogical visions.

Artificial Intelligence (AI) tools are becoming increasingly ubiquitous across diverse learning contexts yet concerns about “replacement” continue to dominate the AI discourse in educational settings, which challenges educators’ roles and leadership in stewarding formal learning environments for meaningful learning and engagement. However, less is empirically kn own about how teachers navigate the relationship with AI in order to create such learning environments. Hybrid intelligence (HI) is an emerging conc ept with the potential to transform formal learning spaces (Cukurova, 2024; Järvelä et al., 2025). While many celebrate the humancentered values of the concept, little is known about how HI systems actually take shape within the complex ecologies of K-12 classrooms (Barab & Roth, 2006), spaces shaped by interdependent relationships among students, teachers, tools, tasks, and institutional norms. Specifically, HI further demands expansion of the role of educators: not just as facilitators, but as co-designers of learning environments who help shape and leverage such sociotechnical ensembles in classrooms.

As a response, we initiated a partnership between a team of university researchers and K-12 educators in the United States to reimagine what HI could look like in science classrooms. This paper focuses on a co-design workshop (Goldman et al., 2022) involvi ng seven secondary school science teachers. Together we aim at exploring the integration of AI-empowered pedagogical agents within an existing science learning simulation, HoloOrbits. Guided by a case study methodology (Stake, 2008), we ask in this study, How do teachers negotiate relationships with AI in co-designing hybrid intelligent learning environments? We share a case from one educator to illustrate how teachers navigated their relationships with AI through reflective discussion and co-design, shaping the pedagogical possibilities of HoloOrbits as a hybrid intelligent learning environment.

## Literature Review

Hybrid intelligence and teacher-AI partnerships in K-12 education The growing presence of AI in classrooms has prompted researchers to rethink teacher-technology relations through the lens of hybrid intelligence (HI) – a paradigm that emphasizes the augmentation, not replacement, of human pedagogical capacities (Järvelä et al., 2025). HI systems aim to support collaboration between human and artificial agents through mechanisms such as shared regulation of learning, joint orchestration, and co-adaptation (Edwards et al., 2025). In this framework, teachers are not passive users but active partners who co-direct learning experiences alongside AI (Holstein et al., 2020). However, in classroom contexts, this partnership is especially complex; for example, teachers must dynamically adapt to student needs while negotiating the a ffordances and constraints of AI systems (Cosentino et al., 2025). Meaningful teacher-AI partnership depends on systems that are transparent, trustworthy, and aligned with pedagogical goals.  Therefore, this study intends to build an empirical understanding of this partnership by focusing on teachers’ relationships with AI. Teachers in co-design of AI-augmented learning environments 1397 Teacher co-design (Goldman et al., 2022) has emerged as a promising approach for integrating AI into classrooms in ways that foreground teachers’ expertise and agency. Teachers engaged in co-design contribute pedagogical insights, critique AI behaviors, and help define how, when, and why AI should interact with students. This shift, from teacher-as-user to teacher-as-partner, enables more contextually grounded and socially responsive AI designs. While earlier co-design efforts primarily focused on curriculum or pedagogy (e.g., Ko et al., 2022; Penuel et al., 2022), recent studies have expanded this work to include teacher participation in shaping the logic and behavior of AI systems (e.g., Lawrence et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2024), which advance how we can support teachers’ instructional and design agency through this collaborative work. These studies have highlighted the complexities of hybrid orchestration, revealing key tensions around control, trust, and interpretability. At the same time, they have surfaced pedagogical and ethical considerations essential for developing AI tools that are meaningfully integrated into classroom practices. Building on this emerging work, our partnership between university researchers and K-12 educators in the United States  aims to collaboratively reimagine how hybrid intelligence could take shape in science classrooms.

## Context: HoloOrbits

HoloOrbits is a web-based collaborative science learning simulation developed in Unity and refined across multiple design iterations (see Figure 1). The simulation engages students in a narrative scenario where they act as crew members aboard a malfunctioning spaces hip. To resolve navigation issues, they investigate planetary motion. HoloOrbits integrates interactive orbital visualizations, data collection tools, and narrative-driven challenges to support conceptual understanding of planetary motion. Pedagogical agents (“Nova” and “Kepler”) currently provide pre-scripted prompts that encourage students to plan, share observations, validate ideas, and build consensus.

## Participants and procedure

This paper draws on a co-design workshop (Goldman et al., 2022) with seven secondary science teachers focused on reimagining how AI-empowered pedagogical agents might better support collaboration in HoloOrbits. Teachers with diverse science teaching backgrounds were invited to participate, and all seven teachers consented to the study. The two-hour workshop included (a) discussion about AI literacy and teachers’ values for AI in education, (b) exploration of the concept of hybrid intelligence (Dellermann et  al., 2019) and its application in classrooms, (c) reflection on teachers’ own strategies for supporting collaboration, and (d) a design challenge using Miro to prototype new agent behaviors for different student personas (e.g., a distracted student, a qui et but insightful student). Participants also discussed how AI agents could engage students in deeper discussion, adapt to learning needs, and promote equitable collaboration. Guided by a case study approach (Stake, 2008), we collected multiple data sources during the co-design workshop, including video recordings, co-design artifacts (Miro boards, sketches), and post-workshop surveys. To trace teachers’ relationship with AI over the course of the workshop and to examine how the design activity appeared to shape their vision for AI-supported collaboration, we developed cases for each participant, bounded by all available data sources. Our analysis focuses on teachers’ relationship with AI. We conceptualize this relationship beyond traditional notions of technology integration, which highlights connections between teachers’ beliefs and their use of technologies (e.g., Ertmer et al., 2012; Kim et al., 2013). Instead, we frame relati onships with AI as 1398 encompassing teachers’ interactions with, expectations of, and orientations toward AI systems that are critical for various levels of teacher-AI teaming based on interactivity and adaptability (Cukurova et al., 2025). We coded teachers’ perceived roles of AI to identify characteristics of such relationships. We then traced how each educator’s views and design ideas evolved across the workshop. In this paper, we present one illustrative case to highlight a possible trajectory of how teachers navigate and negotiate their relationship with AI. To understand what it means to invite and design HI agents for science classrooms, we partnered with secondary science educators in a co-design workshop. Teachers brought diverse prior experience using AI tools in their teaching, ranging from limited exper ience to lesson co-planning. Collectively, they shared a desire to navigate educators’ roles in designing AI systems in K-12 contexts. This section traces Teacher D’s journey of negotiating the relationship with AI throughout the co-design session that focuses on redesigning hybrid intelligent AI agents for a science learning game. We align the teacher’s discussion contributions, design decisions, and post-workshop reflections into three phases: separate, shifted, and distributed roles of AI in science teac hing. The separate role of AI At the beginning of the workshop, Teacher D described what we identify as a separated role of AI, a role situated outside the boundaries of classroom instructions– the role of a tutor. “What I've seen,” he said, “is more of an athome kind of tutor … that's kind of how I've utilized it” (Recording, 07/2025). For Teacher D, his school district had recently introduced Magic School AI, and he saw its value mainly in helping students independently revisit concepts after class. In particular, he explained, students often felt frustrated when working alone to learn chemistry: “... They do practice, and they go home and they don't get it… their notes don't make sense …I can set up like prompts for them … once they kind of get the feel of it, they cando it themselves.” (Recording, 07/2025).

In this framing, AI filled the space left by teachers’ absence. It was something students could turn to when confusion or frustration arose, especially for procedural topics like stoichiometry. Initially, teacher D viewed AI as useful for reinforcing material and easing affective barriers, but not as part of in-class teaching. His comments positioned a certain relationship with AI, an out-of-class tutor that extended instruction beyond the school day rather than sharing roles in classrooms.

Shifted role of AI Teacher D described a shifted role of AI as he engaged in the workshop discussion with other teacher participants on hybrid intelligence. Instead of seeing AI only as a tool for at-home learning, Teacher D pointed out its strength in playing a facilitator in the classroom, as he grappled with the idea of hybrid intelligence. For Teacher D, hybrid intelligence is a form of “co-intelligence” that allows for “a lot of back and forth, both with feedback and ideas” (Recording, 07/2025). He highlighted this particular affordance of AI in the following comment, “this is one of my things with AI that I do think that back and forth and struggle is a strength. And what makes it horrible is also what makes it great. And so I do think that I would not want to eliminate  that when integrating AI” (Recording, 07/2025). As Teacher D described how AI can support “back and forth” learning processes, his relationship with AI shifted. Teacher D further described the ways in which to invite AI for facilitation, as he commented: I was like, that’s cool, you know it’s you’re using an AI, where you’ve already kind of set up and cued it into what you want it to do with the students, and then the students will be working with it specifically, you know, and obviously check work. And then obviously in the classroom, where it's more facilitative, just like computers in general. Right, you know. Just give them a laptop and be like oh, good luck, you know, we monitor, facilitate, have specific times that we use these things. (Recording, 07/2025) To illustrate this facilitator role, Teacher D compared AI with other computer-based technology, for which teachers should decide when and how to use the technology in the classroom. While AI has the potential to support learning processes, the teacher would alsoneed to monitor the process through setting up prompts and making decisions on when AI tools can be used.

Distributed role of AI Alongside two other educators, Teacher D co-designed an AI agent named Capt. Tiberius, a commanding yet supportive figure who guides students through a narrative-driven science inquiry in HoloOrbits. Their prototype 1399 reflects a meaningful shift in Teacher D’s orientation toward AI: from viewing AI as a passive, separate “at-home tutor” to envisioning it as a co-orchestrator within the classroom. The design was catalyzed by a shared critique of the existing agent’s limitations. As Teacher D pointed out during the discussion, “it gave too much information at once … it was a lot of just kind of passive information.” This lack of interactivity and adaptability prompted the group to reimagine a more dynamic AI that scaffolds collaborative learning through structured routines, timely prompts, and playful urgency.

Teacher D invited AI to play the role of co-orchestrator as he worked on the Capt. Tiberius  prototype, particularly in students’ collaboration and reasoning. In the Capt. Tiberius  prototype, Teacher D described that AI can be a “co-intelligence” for teachers in assigning group roles and monitoring participation, for which he asked in the design session, “what would your agent say to spark conversation?” Furthermore, Teacher D envisioned AI to be able to support students’ reasoning, which is a role that teachers usually would take. He illustrated this in how AI can facilitate how students’ reason about Kepler’s laws: I think it tells you this is what you need to do. Like, it's like, hey, we believe in Kepler's law, right? Things don't go in a circle. Like, I feel like that's where we're going, right? Like, it's trying to figure out the planets are not rotating in a nice, perfect circle around the sun. I don't know if they're getting into the details about multiple f oci. You know, focus points. It's not the sun's in the center of the ellipses either. But one way or another, there's something there. So it's like, maybe asking the student to be like, cool. You think it's X, Y or Z? How would you prove it? You know and maybe prod back and forth. (Recording, 07/2025) In this quote, Teacher D’s notion of AI evolved to be a dialogic partner: “cool, you think it’s X, Y, Z? How would you prove it? … prod[ding] back and forth.” In this discussion, another teacher added, “That’s how a teacher would do it, right? If he had time to spend with every single student.” Through co-design, Teacher D embraced the potential of AI to share instructional work to prompt, redirect, and reinforce student thinking. The Capt. Tiberius  agent embodies a hybrid orchestration model where AI and t eacher roles are distributed, not replaced, and both contribute to responsive, collaborative science learning. This model resonates with Teacher D’s belief about learning in the hybrid intelligent learning environment, “students can leverage the strengths of AI while not surrendering their own critical thinking skills” (Survey, 07/2025). Aligned with this design vision, Teacher D further described his role as an educator in the AI-augmented classrooms as “creating the classroom culture and sense of expectations to achieve a common outcome” (Survey, 07/2025).

In this paper, we trace one teacher’s evolving relationship with AI during a co-design workshop aimed at reimagining a hybrid intelligent learning environment based on a collaborative science learning simulation, HoloOrbits. Findings from this case study extend existing frameworks on technology integration (e.g, Ertmer et al., 2012; Kim et al., 2013) by foregrounding the complexity of teachers’ relationships with emerging technologies. Over the course of the workshop, Teacher D’s conceptualization of AI shifted meaningfully, reflecting an evolving orientation toward teacher-AI teaming (Cukurova et al., 2025) and a clearer sense of the educator’s role in AI-augmented learning environments. Initially, he viewed AI as separate from classroom instruction, positioning it primarily as an “ at-home tutor”, a form of situational teaming that supports learning beyond classroom boundaries. As discussions deepened, he began to recognize AI as a facilitator  of students’ learning, such as checking work or scaffolding individual tasks within teacher-defined instructional goals, reflecting operational teaming as AI operates according to pedagogical parameters set by the teacher. Ultimately, through co-design, his perspective evolved toward seeing AI as a co-orchestrator in the learning environment – what he called “co-intelligence” for both teacher and students, representing a form of synergistic teaming where AI and teachers jointly shape learning processes through co-orchestration. Notably, this progression illustrates a shift from AI as a tool for discrete task support toward a more integrated and collaborative role in orchestrating learning processes in an AI-augmented learning environment. Teacher D articulated how teachers remain central to shaping classroom culture, settin g expectations and safeguards, deciding how and when AI tools should be deployed. This study shares an empirical account of how teachers develop partnerships with AI, helping to decenter the dominant “replacement” narrative often associated with AI in K-12 classrooms. Instead, it contributes to understanding how human pedagogical capacities (Järvelä et al., 2025) can be augmented through reimagining hybrid intelligent learning environments for their classrooms. This work also highlights the role of teachers ’ pedagogical beliefs (e.g., Kim et al., 2013) in shaping their evolving relationships with AI, which further inform how we might support teacher agency and unique leadership in co-designing AI-augmented systems that align with their instructional goals and values.
