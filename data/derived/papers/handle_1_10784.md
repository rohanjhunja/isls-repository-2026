# Designing for Collaborative Teacher Learning Through Mixed-Reality Teaching Simulations

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
How do novice teachers learn to lead controversial issue discussions in justice-oriented ways that simultaneously attend to today’s contentious political climate and the pedagogical complexity of the task? This paper analyzes teacher learning using mixed-reality simulation technology to approximate a controversial discussion about Dobbs v. Jackson (2022) and abortion jurisprudence. This paper explores the design, affordances, and limitations of teacher learning via collective pedagogical reasoning when novice teachers grapple with a polarized, emotionally-laden, and fraught topic: abortion. We frame our work as engaging with and challenging practice-based teacher education models to focus on the power of culturally situated and collaborative pedagogical reasoning within the shared experience of the mixed-reality simulation to shape teacher learning.

## Introduction

Scholarship has long held controversial issue discussions (CIDs) are key to democratic school-based civic education (Parker, 1996; Hess, 2009), a pedagogy which is also challenging for teachers to navigate in contexts of political polarization and contentiousness (Swalwell & Schweber, 2016). Teachers have reported wariness about engaging controversial issues for numerous reasons, a fear that has only increased with the recent “conflict campaign” of politically-motivated attacks intended to restrict classroom considerations of all controversial issues, especially anything related to race and racism (Pollock et al., 2022). In light of current precarious conditions for teaching and learning (Rogers et al, 2019), ensuring teachers learn effectively to facilitate CIDs within hostile political climates is vitally important to support democratic, justice-oriented classroom discussions. However, little is known about how such teacher learning happens. ## Theoretical framework

This fear has only increased with the recent “conflict campaign” of politically motivated attacks intended to restrict classroom considerations of all controversial issues, especially anything related to race and racism (Pollock et al., 2022). In light of the current precarious conditions for teaching and learning (Rogers et al., 2019), ensuring teachers learn effectively to facilitate CIDs within hostile political climates is vitally important to support democratic, justice-oriented classroom discussions. However, extremely little is known about how such teacher learning happens. We present findings from a project (Geller et al., 2022) grounded in design-based research (Sandoval & Bell, 2004) and practice-based teacher education (PBTE) (Grossman, Compton, et al., 2009) exploring teacher learning of CID facilitation using Mursion mixedreality simulation (MRS) technology. The MRS technology uses digital avatars of students operating in real time, operated by a skilled actor trained in improvisation (a “simulation specialist”) who can see and hear the novice teachers participating in the simulation. As a result, the avatars function as digital puppets that can respond genuinely to participants but are fully under the control of the simulation specialist. In our study, the simulation specialist was an integral member of the research team who was fully incorporated into the simulation design for research and teacher education purposes.

## Methods and data

This paper addresses the following research question: what were the relative constraints and affordances for teacher learning of using mixed-reality simulation technology to support the development of the skills, knowledge, and dispositions associated with CID facilitation around Dobbs v. Jackson and abortion jurisprudence? Data were collected at a large southern research university within a social studies curriculum course for preservice teachers Author2 taught. 22 students enrolled in the course participated in the study, which analyzed a mini-unit on teaching controversial issues oriented around pedagogies of practice (Grossman et al., 2009). First, the preservice teachers participated in a representation of practice as if they were the high school students themselves. They completed a homework assignment acquainting them with relevant legal principles, the precedents of Roe v. Wade (1973) and Planned Parenthood v. Casey (1992), and the facts of the case in Dobbs. Next, we analyzed language in the 14th Amendment together, and they worked collaboratively to analyze four amicus briefs filed with the Supreme Court in Dobbs which served as the grounding texts for the MRS discussion. Author2 selected these briefs, modified them in student-friendly language, and provided graphic organizers. Then the preservice teachers shifted to a teacher role and, guided by previous course readings (Pace, 2021; Reisman, 2022), worked in small groups to team-plan a CID in which the MRS student avatars would be positioned as Supreme Court justices deciding Dobbs on its merits, initially by arguing which of the amicus briefs was most persuasive. Finally, the preservice teachers participated in the simulated discussion. Half of them were simulation teachers responsible for leading a portion of the discussion with the avatars, while the other half were coaches responsible for using a notice and wonder protocol (van Es, 2011) to observe closely, give feedback, and drive shared pedagogical reasoning. This paper draws on analyses of transcriptions of the MRS Zoom sessions, the author’s contemporaneous field notes and analytic memos, students’ written preparation work, and written reflections collected via individual submission, Google Jamboard, and written discussions. The data were uploaded to Dedoose and coded collaboratively in multiple cycles. First, we reviewed the data corpus to take stock of the breadth of the data, taking note of particularly interesting or notable data and themes through rough structural and descriptive coding (Saldaña, 2016). With this initial code list as a guide, we reread the corpus with greater granular attention and conducted open coding using a constant comparative method (Saldaña, 2016). As we coded across individual data points and aggregated, we constructed codes that became more abstract and conceptual as patterns, contradictions, and central themes emerged. Finally, we code-mapped (Saldaña, 2016) to establish a more comprehensive, conceptual understanding of the corpus and its implications. All names are pseudonyms.

## Results

Because of limited space, we offer an abbreviated summary of the findings. We analyze episodes of pedagogical reasoning (Horn, 2015) which illuminate our design choices and the relative affordances and constraints for teacher learning through collective pedagogical reasoning. Importantly, where our team’s previous work on this project (Geller et al., 2022) showed a robust use of teacher time-outs, and group debriefs for grappling with highlevel problems of practice, much of the debriefs and teacher time-outs in this iteration were centered on the particular challenges associated with teaching about abortion, indicating the topic selection–while related to an incredibly important and timely contemporary public problem–may have constrained their ability to see through to those bigger picture pedagogical questions.

The PSTs in this iteration focused their problem-solving on such questions as how to control for students’ emotions or to redirect students’ opinions to the grounding texts rather than exploring their thinking more deeply. For example, at one point, two of the digital avatars began to disagree about their answer to the guiding question. Avatar Ava said, “Like, Dev, why would you honestly pick document C? Did you misspeak?!” Cristina, the participating teacher, immediately paused the simulation. She explained, “Ava wasn’t really talking in the most productive way. She was kinda making it sound like Dev was stupid. And I don’t know, I stopped it before it went out of control.” Cristina’s hesitancy to allow the simulated lesson to get “out of control” meant that while she was able to take a teacher time out to get support from her group, that support was immediately about how to rein in Ava’s emotion with a reminder of norms. Therefore, we could not use this “sticky moment” in the discussion to draw out a deeper understanding of how a teacher might hold Ava’s strong emotions while also eliciting academic disagreement through other students’ opinions. Cristina’s concern about losing control of a simulated discussion with young people limited the extent to which she–and the rest of the group–could take full advantage of the affordances of the MRS.

One such important affordance of the simulation as a mechanism for learning collective pedagogical reasoning is its role in helping the preservice teachers move from theoretical and abstract teaching principles into specific teacher actions. This affordance played out most specifically concerning teacher political disclosure or teachers’ sharing their political beliefs and opinions with students (Journell, 2016). The preservice teachers in this course had often spoken about the importance of sharing their opinions as one of many political opinions and encouraging students to disagree to model political thinking and to allow students to understand their teachers’ biases (what Kelly (1986) called committed impartiality). However, when opportunities presented themselves for them to disclose their opinions about abortion in the course of the simulated discussion, those same preservice teachers dodged and avoided them because they felt unsure of what exactly to say. It was one thing for them to hold particular perspectives and teaching philosophies about what constitutes an ethical approach to political disclosure; learning to facilitate this CID through the MRS provided us with space to make sense collectively of various pedagogical choices and their consequences. Hence while the approximation of practice did not facilitate repetitive practice with disclosure, it created a powerful discussion point around which our whole group could reason.

## Significance

In this paper, we present an argument for conceptualizing PBTE from a situated learning perspective. Based on our grounding in sociocultural theories of learning, we argue for employing pedagogies of practice as mechanisms to create common learning experiences that will prompt shared reasoning and sensemaking. We understand that facilitating CIDs in politically contested contexts is not a discrete skill with a single correct method that can be rehearsed to the point of fluidity. Instead, we see CIDs as important products of “the social, cultural, political, and situated dimensions of teachers’ practices” with the power to “reproduce, challenge, and/or transform systems and hierarchies of power in classrooms and society” (Philip et al., 2019, p. 259). As a result, learning CID facilitation cannot be accomplished only through repeated skill rehearsal but must include opportunities to reckon with these hierarchies of power and their consequences for such discussions. While the MRS is certainly not the only mechanism to do so, we see the technology as an effective tool to create concrete “sticky moments” around which sensemaking and collective pedagogical reasoning can occur. In this paper, we frame this form of approximation of practice–a collaborative, shared experience with the MRS–as a central text around which participants co-construct learning.

These findings and the accompanying argument have implications for how teacher education activities are structured to support novice teacher learning. PBTE can more effectively prepare teachers to engage in complex pedagogical tasks such as CID facilitation when the pedagogies of practice are designed and framed to facilitate collaborative pedagogical reasoning amongst a community of practice. Such designs enable teachers not only to learn about some of the harder concepts within the pedagogy itself (e.g., teacher political disclosure (Journell, 2016)) but also to begin to build habits of pedagogical reasoning (Horn & Little, 2010). We argue that the teachers’ learning to navigate the intensely complicated, politically fraught political climates in schools across the U.S. at this writing matters tremendously for the kinds of classroom experiences marginalized and minoritized youth have as they and their classmates grapple with the big social questions of our time–especially those related to race and racism. The AERA call quotes Kelley (2002): what type of society do you want to live in? We assert that the teacher learning we explore in this project not only facilitates the development of a society in which discussions of controversial issues–including abortion or racial injustice–can be held across lines of disagreement while explicitly centering justice and equity but also that this teacher learning supports teachers to explore this question with the young people in their classrooms.
