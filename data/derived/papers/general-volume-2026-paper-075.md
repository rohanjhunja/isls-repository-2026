# Co-designing and Implementing Reflective Tools and Routines with Children in Cooperative Inquiry

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Reflection is a valuable component of learning and design processes, yet motivating children to reflect in informal learning contexts can be challenging. My dissertation examines this issue in a university-based Cooperative Inquiry co-design team where children and adults collaborate as equal design partners. Leveraging a DBR approach, I conduct two studies. The first investigates how children co-design reflection tools and processes that align with their values and mental models. The second explores how they use these systems over time and how this shapes their developing self-concept as designers. Through this research, I will contribute an adaptable infrastructure for authentically integrating reflection into child co-design, insights into how DBR can meaningfully include children as co-design stakeholders, and a deeper understanding of how children think, learn, and develop their identities through co-design. Motivation and background The learning sciences have long documented the importance of reflection for a range of learning processes (Dewey, 1986; Moon, 2004; Schön, 2017), with classrooms traditionally serving as important spaces for developing children’s reflective learning. Though less structured and systematic, informal learning contexts can also offer rich environments for reflection and identity development, but it can be challenging to motivate children to pause and reflect in the absence of standard classroom norms and enforcing structures (Gardner et al., 2006). As a doctoral student at the University of Maryland with an interest in how children learn and develop their identities through co-design, I have experienced some of these challenges firsthand through my participation in UMD KidsTeam—an intergenerational co-design team where university faculty and students collaborate with children between the ages of 7 and 12 to design new child-facing technologies. Guided by the Cooperative Inquiry (CI) co-design framework (Druin, 1999; Guha et al., 2013), which aims to involve children as equal collaborators with adults across the entire design cycle, our team employs a variety of established CI ‘design techniques’ aimed at making co-design accessible, engaging, equitable, and empowering for children. One such technique that has been mentioned in the literature, but which has received considerably less attention, is ‘journaling’, which has been described as a tool for “design partners to reflect on their experiences by writing or drawing” (Guha et al., 2013). Despite this directive, CI scholars and practitioners have observed child design partners’ lack of intrinsic motivation to engage in reflective journaling and have reported challenges in communicating the goals and value of this technique in a manner that resonates with children’s personal interests and values (Blinder et al., 2025). Issues with implementing reflective journaling in CI are further complicated by logistical challenges (e.g., time constraints, the need for adult writing support), ideological challenges (e.g., adult discomfort with the solitary school-like nature of written reflection) and adult and child design partners’ conflicting conceptualizations of journaling (with children prioritizing documenting and remembering design ideas and adults prioritizing process-and identity-oriented reflection) (Blinder et al., 2025). These challenges indicate an opportunity to explore additional approaches toward fostering purposeful sustained reflection within CI and other child co-design contexts. In my dissertation, I capitalize on this opportunity by engaging UMD KidsTeam children in contemplating the role reflection can play in their lives and identities as designers through co-designing new developmentally accessible and personally meaningful reflection tools and routines to be used in our co-design context. I then examine how children’s engagement with these reflection approaches and their self-perceptions as designers evolve over time.

I draw on Design-based Research (DBR) (Wang & Hannafin, 2005), CI co-design (Druin, 1999; Guha et al., 2013), and case study methodologies (Stake, 1995) to investigate the following research questions: RQ1: How can tools and routines for documented reflection be integrated into co-design in a manner that resonates with children’s values and mental models? RQ2: How do children interact with co-designed reflective tools and routines over time? RQ3: How do children’s perceptions of themselves as designers evolve as they interact with codesigned reflective tools and routines over time? I answer these questions through two phases of qualitative research. In Study 1, I use CI to investigate RQ1 by facilitating a series of co-design sessions with UMD KidsTeam to prototype reflection tools and routines to be implemented in our KidsTeam context. In Study 2, I use case study research to investigate RQ2 and RQ3 over a period of 4-6 months to understand how children interact with the reflection tools and routines developed during Study 1 and how their self-perceptions as designers concurrently evolve. The data I am collecting for these qualitative studies include co-design artifacts, children’s documented reflections, my observational field notes and reflective memos, and audio and video recordings of co-design sessions, children’s individual and group interviews, and adults’ session debrief discussions.

I am analyzing the data from Study 1 using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2019). Across Study 2, I will engage in continuous cycles of data collection and analysis (Stake, 1995) by iteratively coding my collected data to identify patterns and themes related to children’s reflection content, modalities, and behaviors. Though I will primarily use inductive coding for these analyses, I may apply deductive codes derived from established leveled reflection frameworks (Fleck & Fitzpatrick, 2010; Moon, 2004) to answer RQ2. ## Collected data and preliminary results

I have conducted four ideation and prototyping co-design sessions with KidsTeam, which resulted in two prototypes that we user-tested and refined during a 1-week KidsTeam summer camp. Design 1 is an immersive environment called “Reflection Islands”, through which children generate multimodal accounts of their design ideas, each of which is represented as a customizable ‘island’ on a virtual map. Children grow and develop their islands by selecting from a menu of prompts (or creating their own) to reflect about the decisions and processes that informed each of their designs. They can also visit and create pathways across their own and teammates’ islands by reflecting on overarching patterns they observe across this broader landscape. Design 2 is a noncompetitive card game called “Memory String” that uses predetermined prompts to elicit participants’ accounts of their design experiences and learning, while encouraging players to understand and discuss their shared and divergent perspectives as designers. The game’s color-coded cards represent different categories of KidsTeamrelevant reflection, as determined by child design partners, including: “favorite memory of processes”; “sad/emotional”, “funniest”, and “lightbulb” moments; and “random [creative] challenges”. Expected contributions Through this research, I aim to make the following design, methodological, and empirical contributions: (1) a refined and tested adaptable infrastructure (consisting of tools, routines, implementation guidelines, etc.) that can be used to promote a more authentic and developmentally appropriate integration of reflection into co-design with children in a manner demonstrated to support their growth and development as designers; (2) a process model for a how DBR—which often involves co-design with adult practitioners (e.g., teachers) and testing in authentic classroom contexts with K-12 students—can incorporate co-design with young learners as a primary stakeholder group by positioning them as full design partners rather than as mere testers, users, and/or informants; and (3) an enhanced understanding of how children think, learn, and develop their identities through co-design.
