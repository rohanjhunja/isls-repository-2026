# Transformative Learning through Situated Practices: Understanding In-Service Teachers’ Critical Engagement with AI Tools in Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how in-service teachers engage with generative AI tools, Claude 3.0, to enhance critical thinking and design AI-infused lesson plans. Using the dual frameworks of Situated Learning Theory (SLT) and Transformative Learning Theory (TLT), the research investigates how teachers develop expertise in AI integration through legitimate peripheral participation (LPP) and experience cognitive transformation in their instructional practices. Data from 250 teacher-AI interaction episodes, lesson plans, reflection papers and interviews were analyzed to identify engagement patterns, prompting strategies, and critical thinking practices. Findings reveal a progression from intermittent to continuous engagement, reflecting a developmental trajectory of expertise. Critical prompting strategies such as clarification, ethical awareness, and reflective adaptation highlighted the transformative potential of AI tools in fostering teachers’ pedagogical and ethical literacy. This paper concludes with recommendations for professional development programs that scaffold AI literacy through situated and transformative learning experiences.

## Introduction

The rise of generative AI tools has opened new possibilities for instructional design, content personalization, and critical thinking in education. However, teachers often feel overwhelmed by the pace of techn ological change (Adiguzel et al., 2023), and both teachers and students risk uncritically adopting AI outputs without understanding the limitations such as hallucinations, bias, or ethical implicatio ns (Ji et al., 2023; Walter, 2024). Concerns also exist about students offloading cognitive work  (Ng et al., 2023). This study contributes to the growing literature on AI in teacher professional development, focusing  specifically on how interaction patterns and criti cal prompting evolve through situated practice.

In response to these challenges, a growing body of research emphasizes the importance of AI literacy and effective prompt engineering as critical skills  for both teachers and learners (Kohnke et al., 202 3; Long & Magerko, 2020). Prompting is increasingly seen as a  pedagogical act requiring critical thinking  (Knoth et al., 2024). Studies have shown that only well-constructed prompts consistently generate high-quality AI outputs, including feedback that rivals or even exceeds human experts in speed and specificity (Jacobsen & Weber, 2023). This highlights the critical role of prompt quality  in leveraging AI effectively for learning.  While prompt types (Eager & Brunton, 2023) and styles are studied (Wang et al., 2024), less is known about how non-expert in-service teachers develop prompting strategies in practice. Novices often struggle without iterative practice a nd support (Oppenlaunder et al., 2023) suggesting a need for scaffolding to craft effective critical prompts (Jang et al., 2024). Mollick and Mollick (2023) emphasize that prompting is a complex, pedagogically driven practice tied to distinct instructional roles such as mentor, coach, and tuto r. Without structured support, it can lead to misuse or over-reliance. Understanding how teachers refine their prompts ove r time can inform professional development that empowers educators to use AI critically and pedagogically.

To address this gap, the present study explores how in-service teachers engage in prompt engineering as both a technical and reflective practice. Drawing on Situated Learning Theory (SLT, Lave & Wenger, 1991) and Transformative Learning Theory (TLT, Mezirow, 1997), this research examines how teachers ’ AI interactions unfold within a learning community and foster pedagogical growth. SLT highlights progression toward expertise via legitimate peripheral participation (LPP) where novice teachers start with uncritical uses of AI, and over time, develop more reflective and sophisticated practices. The disorienting dilemmas (e.g., flawed AI responses) are framed in TLT, which triggers reflection and transformation. This study investigates:

1.  What characterizes initial interactions and reasons for conversation cessation  between teachers and AI

in the early stage of legitimate peripheral participation?

2. What critical prompting strategies do teachers use to enhance critical engagement with AI?

3. How do teachers’ interactions with AI reflect their  progression toward expertise in AI integration as

situated in their professional learning community?

Situated, transformative learning and prompt critical thinking with AI Situated Learning Theory (Lave & Wenger,1991), posits that  learning occurs via  participation in authentic activities within a community.  This applies to teachers using AI tools for real instructional design tasks within a course cohort, where knowledge is co-constructed. Through situated practice, teachers achieve perspective shifts and AI integrated teaching practices that is well informed by Transformative Learning Theory (Mezirow,1991, 1997), which focuses on critical reflection leading to shifts in beliefs. When teachers assess AI outpu ts, they engage in perspective transformation, questioning a ssumptions, and  adapting pedagogy. Critical prompting supports this process. The Prompt Critical Thinking Framework (Spector & Ma, 2019) reinforces these processes by o utlining seven interrelated elements (e.g., clarification, interpretation, evaluation, ethical awareness, integration, reflection, and application) to support meaningful engagement with AI.

## Methods

This qualitative case study explores how 27 in-service K-12 teachers engaged in a six-week graduate course, Introduction to AI in Education in the US. Teachers used Claude 3.0 for course assignments, primarily designing AI-infused lesson plans,  prompting authentic interactions situated within th e learning community.  The dataset includes all 48 available chat logs generated by the participating teachers for coursew ork, comprising 250 total interaction episodes. An episode is a single utterance (teacher prompt or AI response).  A chat log is one teacher’s full conversation thread with the AI for a specific task/session (identified by ID number in Table 2).  The unit of analysis was the prompt-response pair. Besides chat logs, AI-infused lesson plans, reflection papers and interviews are also included for triangulating analysis.

Analysis involved three phases:

1. Short interactions (<6 turns) were coded inductively to identify emergent patterns of initial use and

cessation. Open coding (Merriam, 2009) was chosen because it allowed for an inductive appr oach to capture the unstructured and exploratory nature of novice engagement.

2. Longer interactions were analyzed  deductively using Spector and Ma’s (2019) critical Thinking

framework aligning with TLT framework to identify deeper critical thinking prompting strategies.

3. Patterns across interactions were comparted qualita tively to trace developmental trajectories, linking

engagement types (intermittent/sustained) to prompting strategies and transformative learning moments. Teacher’s reflection paper, lesson plan, and interview transcript are triangulated using thematic analysis and applied initial and axial coding to trace shifts in AI-related beliefs, instructional strategies, and ethical considerations. Teachers reported encountering disorienting dilemmas including time management issues, ethical concerns, uncertainty about school policies, and misuse of AI to avoid thinking by students. These disruptions became critical learning moments  as teachers began to reflect, adapt their approaches, and reframe their understanding of AI ’s role in education.  Due to space limitations, this paper focuses on three illustrative cases from the cohort. We report on three teachers’ experience through three data sources: three AI-infused lesson plan, three reflection papers, and three interview transcripts. To maintain anonymity, all teacher names used in this paper are pseudonyms. This case study highlights the transformative shifts in teacher s’ actions and beliefs about AI, from initial uncertai nty to more confident, critical use.

Transformative learning through situated AI prompting TLT can help illuminate how pedagogical growth can emerge throug h authentic, situated tasks such as lesson planning, crafting a classroom policy on AI, and sc affolding responsible AI use. In this section, we showcase three teachers  (to preserve anonymity, we refer to them using pseudo nyms): Ms. Torres (a high school social studies teacher), Mr. Chen (a middle school math teacher)  and Ms. Reed (a sixth grade  STEM teacher), whose evolving practices illustrate transformative and si tuated learning through their authentic engagement with AI. A disorienting dilemma (Mezirow, 1991) arose when Ms. Torres recognizing potential inaccuracies in AI-simulated historical dialogues (using Perplexity AI); Mr. Chen rethinking the role of AI beyond efficiency, center ing more on student ownership; and Ms. Reed encountering students’ unauthorized AI use. This catalyzed teachers’ critical reflection on the affordances and limitations of AI tools, leading to cognitive and practical shifts. While designing a lesson that  used Perplexity AI to simulate conversations with nationalist leaders, Ms. Torres emphasized the importance of source validation and critical thinking, “the evaluation piece there is to be able to look at... whether that’s interviews or primary sources that explain some of those perspectives... can you go back to that speec h and make sure that it confirms the language, usag e, or the perspective?” This statement shows a shift from viewing AI as a mere content gen erator to a partner requiring scrutiny. The lesson plan reflected this deepened understandi ng. The “Speed DAIting” project required students to analyze AI responses for  “reliability, bias, and corroborate source informat ion,” demonstrating a shift toward critical consciousness (TLT). R eflection on ethics further deepened:  “investigate and critically analyze any potential bias” and addressing privacy: “we’re required to use things that don’t collect their personal information.” Over time, their perspective evolved: “this class r eally helped me to see that… there’s ways to apply the AI, but then [students are] still doing the work too.”  This shift reflect ed a transformation from a cautious  novice to reflective practitioner committed to ethical and inquiry-driven AI integration. While designing an AI introductory lesson used worksheet to facilitate a reflective discussio n on AI’s affordances and limitations, Mr. Chen moved from focusing on AI efficiency to fostering s tudent inquiry and ethical awareness. The teacher reflected “I didn’t want to learn how to use AI for myself. I wanted to introduce it to my students too.” This marked a critical transformation from personal utility to student-centered empowerment. The lesson plan reflected student ownership through  a collaborative classroom policy creating activity  where students helped define acceptable AI practices. “We talked about whether it’s okay to use AI for checking answers, and what counts as ‘doing your own work.’” This demonstrated his evolving view of AI as a pedagogical partner rather than a shortcut.

When designing a lesson on thermal insulation used Canva AI to visually generate student-created coolers, Ms. Reed  emphasized the importance of research-based design and accountable use of AI. In the interview, she described how students were required to conduct res earch on insulators first and then justify their choices before being allowed to use Canva AI: “If y ou can’t prove it to me, I don’t believe it... back  up your information, find something that proves that.” This statement marks a pedagogical shift from AI  as a potential shortcut to a tool that supports critical thinking and evidence-based reasoning. The lesson plan embodied this shift. Students explored insulators through video, discussion, and research, then used Canva AI to design a cooler only after meeting a research-based checklist and teacher approval. This shows how the teacher scaffolded ethical, purposeful AI use.

## Conclusion

Teachers’ engagement with AI revealed a transformative learning process (TLT) situated within their professional practice (SLT). Initial challenges prompted  critical reflection  and ethical consideration, leading to redesign ed lessons fostering student critique of AI outputs. Iterative prompting strategies (e.g., simulating bias, generating counterarguments) evolved as teachers saw prompting as a pedagogical skill. They moved from cautious users to reflective practitioners using AI to cultivate crit ical thinking. This study highlights the value of s ituated, community-based learning for developing AI literacy.  Professional development should scaffold AI literac y through similar authentic, reflective experiences.  To encourage sustained and meaningful engagement wi th generative AI in education, future  professional development program should focus on clarifying AI’s role in the classroom, developing AI tools as course-specific assistants using uploaded syllabi and mate rials, and training teachers in effective prompting strategies such as zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought, and instr uctional prompting. Supporting iterative prompting, enabling AI to offer clarifications or follow-ups, and establishing clear academic honesty policies will further empower teachers to confidently integrate AI into their instructional design.
