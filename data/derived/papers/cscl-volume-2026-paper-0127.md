# Comparing High School Students’ Collaborative Discourse in Idea-Centered and Procedure-Based Technology-Supported Scientific PBL

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This quasi-experimental study compared collaborative discourse between idea-centered and procedure-based science PBL among 61 Taiwanese high school students over 12-week. Using a hierarchical coding framework, student contributions were coded into three levels—information sharing, knowledge construction, and knowledge building—at individual and group levels. Results showed idea-centered PBL significantly increased knowledge-building discourse (73.6% vs. 34%) and promoted idea diversification and synthesis. These findings suggest that epistemological framing embedded in platform design shapes students' collaborative discourse.

Collaborative discourse, through which high school students exchange and build on each other's ideas to advance shared understanding, is fundamental to science project-based learning (PBL) (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014; Hmelo‐Silver, 2004). Through such discourse in science PBL, students have the opportunities tonegotiate meaning and co-construct explanation, mirroring authentic scientific practice (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014). However, traditionally, the implementation of science PBL has been largely procedure-based, with students following structured steps rather than engaging in authentic collaborative inquiry, thereby limiting students’ collaborative discourse as participants in a scientific community (Pedaste et al., 2015). In recent years, Knowledge Building (KB) theory, an idea-centered approach reframing students as members of a knowledge-building community and highlighting the importance of agency and collaborative discourse, has been proposed (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014). To promote high school students’ collaborative discourse, KB is promising for designing and implementing science PBL. However, little empirical research has been conducted with this perspective. Therefore, the current study aims to address this research gap by comparing two groups of high school students’ collaborative discourse during two different science PBLs, idea-centered and procedure-based. The research questions of this study are: RQ1: What types of collaborative discourse do students have in idea-centered and procedure-based science PBLs? RQ2: Are there significant differences in the distribution of collaborative discourse types between the two groups of students having idea-centered and procedure-based science PBLs?

With a quasi-experimental design, this study was conducted with two classes of second-year students (N = 61) at a high school in southern Taiwan. One class was assigned to the Idea-centered Group (n = 30), in which an ideacentered science PBL was implemen ted, while the other group was assigned to the Procedure-based PBL Group (n = 3 1), in which a conventional procedure-based science PBL was implemented. The two groups of students were asked to conduct science projects in groups (four or five students per group) for 12 weeks, a 2-period-perweek investigation into “maximum load capacity.” The science PBL task required students to collaboratively design and test solutions to determine the maximum load capacity of a given structure, involving hypothesis generation, data collection, and iterative refinement of explanations. To promote students’ collaborative discourse, an idea-centered platform, in which students could generate and refine their ideas, for example, students in the idea-centered condition were prompted with openended questions such as “What ideas can explain this phenomenon?” and “Can you build on or challenge a peer’s idea?”, whereas students in the procedure-based group followed procedure-based instructions such as “Record your observations” and “Complete the worksheet.” The platform guided students  without explicitly requiring specific discourse types were developed in this study. Critically, neither platform explicitly instructed students to produce knowledge-building discourse; they differed in whether they afforded open-ended idea exploration or step-by-step task completion —reflecting the distinction between problematizing and structuring functions of scaffolding (Reiser, 2004).

Mann-Whitney U tests comparing the two groups (n = 8 classes each) revealed significant group differences in idea-diversification (Idea-centered Group: M = 26.25, S.D. = 9.00 vs. Procedure-based Group: M = 11.38, S.D. = 3.50; U = 60.5, p =.003, r = 0.74) and idea-synthesis (Idea-centered Group: M = 2.00, S.D. = 1.93 vs. Procedurebased Group: M = 0.25, S.D. = 0.71; U = 49.5, p =.039, r = 0.51), with large and medium effect sizes respectively. Idea-elaboration showed no significant difference (Idea-centered Group: M = 5.25, S.D. = 5.23 vs. Procedure-based Group: M = 5.38, S.D. = 3.29; U = 28.5, p =.751, r = 0.08). Particularly, the idea-centered group showed considerably higher variability in idea-diversification (S.D. = 9.00) compared to the Procedure-based group (S.D. = 3.50).

Idea-centered scientific PBL was associated with substantially higher levels of  knowledge-building discourse (73.6% vs. 34%), suggesting that idea-centered platform design is associated with more generative forms of student participation (Tong & Chan, 2023; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006; Stahl & Hakkarainen, 2021). When pedagogies foreground ideas rather than procedural-based tasks, students shift from reporting information to building knowledge —consistent with KB theory's emphasis on idea agency and community knowledge advancement (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014).The subcategory analysis further indicates that idea diversification and synthesis may emerge within a relatively short time under idea-centered conditions, whereas idea elaboration appears to require longer-term engagement  (Zhang et al., 2011). The higher variability in idea-diversification within the idea-centered group suggests that group dynamics may play a key role in shaping how idea-centered platform affordances  translate into collaborative discourse.  This study has several limitations. The quasi-experimental design and modest sample size limit generalizability. The 12-week duration might not be enough to capture the development of deeper epistemic processes such as elaboration. Furthermore, the findings are based on discourse patterns rat her thandirect measures of learning outcomes. Future studies should explore the connection between student discourse and their actual understanding. Specifically, researchers might adjust platform features to see exactly how they affect the results. Overall, these findings suggest that the way a platform frames learning—as idea improvement versus task completion—directly shapes what students say and how they say it.
