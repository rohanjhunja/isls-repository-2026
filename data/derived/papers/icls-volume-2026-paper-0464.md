# Emotional Equity in Education: Rethinking Social-Emotional Learning Across Cultures

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Social-emotional learning (SEL) is widely presented as a universal framework for student development. However, emotional learning is shaped by cultural values, social norms, and family practices. This conceptual paper introduces the notion of emotional equity, defined as the recognition of culturally diverse emotional norms in education. Drawing on literature from SEL, cultural psychology, and comparative education, the paper argues that educational equity must include emotional equity and that SEL should be understood as a culturally situated process.

Social-emotional learning (SEL) has become a central focus of contemporary education and is widely promoted as essential for students’ well-being, academic achievement, and social development (CASEL, 2020; Durlak et al., 2011). Across educational systems, SEL is often presented as a universal framework designed to support all learners through the development of competencies such as self-awareness, self-regulation, and relationship skills. However, emotional learning is not culturally neutral. Research in cultural psychology and the learning sciences demonstrates that emotions are shaped by social interaction, cultural values, and moral expectations (Markus & Kitayama, 1991; Mesquita, 2001; Zembylas, 2019). Children learn not only what to feel, but how to express, regulate, and interpret emotions in ways that reflect their cultural environments. These processes are embedded in family practices, language, and broader social norms. In culturally diverse educational settings, students bring different emotional norms into shared learning environments. When one model of emotional development is treated as standard, other ways of experiencing and expressing emotion may be overlooked or misinterpreted. This raises an important question: what does educational equity mean when emotional norms themselves differ across cultural contexts? Emotional equity Emotional equity highlights the need to recognize that emotional development is culturally shaped and that multiple emotional norms should be valued within educational systems. While SEL frameworks have contributed significantly to student learning and wel l-being, they are largely grounded in Western psychological traditions that emphasize self-expression, autonomy, and individual voice (Jagers et al., 2019; Durlak et al., 2011). These assumptions influence how emotional competence is defined and assessed in classrooms. Students are often expected to verbalize their emotions, engage in reflective dialogue, and demonstrate confidence through self-expression. However, these expectations reflect culturally specific norms rather than universal standards. Research in cultural psychology suggests that emotions are not simply internal states but socially organized practices shaped through interaction and moral expectations (Markus & Kitayama, 1991; Mesquita, 2001). Studies of classroom interaction further show that emotional meaning is constructed through participation, relationships, and communicative norms rather than individual expression alone (Cekaite, 2019). From the perspective of transformative SEL, emotional development must also be understood in relation to equity and cultural identity. Jagers et al. (2019) emphasize that SEL should support not only individual competencies but also students’ cultural identities and lived experiences. Building on this perspective, emotional equity underscores that different emotional repertoires should be recognized as legitimate forms of participation rather thandeviations from a dominant norm. Without such recognition, educational systems risk interpreting culturally different emotional behaviors as deficits rather than as alternative forms of competence. Across cultural contexts Differences in emotional learning become particularly visible in everyday classroom practices and broader processes of socialization. In many U.S. educational settings, emotional development is supported through structured activities such as reflection, discussion, and emotional check-ins. Within this framework, students are encouraged to name their feelings, articulate their perspectives, and advocate for their needs, reflecting broader cultural values of individual voice and self-expression. Although social-emotional learning as a formal framework is not widely developed in Central Asian research, related concepts can be found in studies of moral upbringing and socialization. In many of these contexts, 2821 emotional development is closely connected to broader processes of tarbiya, which emphasize respect, relational responsibility, and moral conduct. Research on education in post-Soviet and Central Asian contexts highlights the importance of family, community, and cultural traditions in shaping children’s behavior and social participation. Within these frameworks, emotional regulation is not understood primarily as individual self-expression, but as a relational and ethical practice embedded in social life. In many Central Asian contexts, emotional and social development is not framed as a separate domain such as social-emotional learning, nor is it typically formalized within school-based policies. Instead, it is embedded within broader educational and cultural practices, including moral education, classroom expectations, and family-based upbringing. Concepts such as tarbiya  reflect this integrated approach, where emotional development is inseparable from moral, social, and relational learning. Within such frameworks, emotional learning is less focused on verbal expression and more oriented toward relational awareness, respect, and the maintenance of social harmony. Children are socialized to understand how their emotions affect others and to regulate their behavior accordingly. Emotional competence is often demonstrated through attentiveness, patience, and responsiveness to social expectations rather than through explicit self-expression. These differences illustrate that emotional learning is not simply a matter of individual development but is shaped by culturally specific understandings of social relationships, communication, and moral conduct. When educational systems rely on a single model of emotional competence, they risk overlooking alternative ways in which students engage and participate in learning.

## Implications for education

Understanding emotional learning as culturally situated has important implications for educational practice and policy. When SEL is implemented as a standardized and explicit framework, it reflects particular cultural assumptions about how emotional development should be taught, expressed, and assessed. However, as seen in many Central Asian contexts, emotional learning is often not organized as a separate domain but is embedded within broader processes of moral upbringing and social participation. This contrast highlights a key tension: while SEL frameworks emphasize explicit instruction, reflection, and verbal expression, other traditions rely on implicit learning through observation, relational expectations, and participation in community life. As a result, students socialized in these contexts may demonstrate emotional competence in ways that are not immediately recognized within dominant SEL models. In classroom settings, this can shape how educators interpret behavior. For example, quiet participation, indirect communication, or restraint may be misinterpreted as disengagement, lack of confidence, or limited emotional awareness. In reality, these behaviors may reflect culturally valued forms of attentiveness, respect, and self-regulation. Research on transformative and culturally responsive approaches to SEL emphasizes the importance of aligning emotional learning with students’ cultural identities and lived experiences (Jagers et al., 2019; McCallops et al., 2019). These perspectives highlight that SEL should not be limited to a universal set of competencies, but should be adapted to reflect diverse cultural norms and practices. A culturally responsive approach to emotional learning therefore requires expanding how emotional competence is understood and recognized. This includes acknowledging that emotional development may be taught explicitly in some contexts and implicitly in others, and that both forms represent valid and meaningful ways of learning. Rather than replacing one model with another, educational systems can move toward greater flexibility by recognizing multiple pathways of emotional development and by creating environments where different forms of participation are understood within their cultural contexts. Emotional learning does not follow a single universal model. It is shaped by cultural values, social expectations, and forms of participation that vary across contexts.

The comparison between explicit SEL frameworks and culturally embedded approaches such as tarbiya illustrates that emotional development can be organized in fundamentally different ways: as a structured set of skills in some systems, and as an integrated moral and relational process in others. Recognizing these differences shifts the focus from standardizing emotional competencies to understanding how they are culturally defined and expressed. Educational equity, therefore, must include emotional equity: the recognition that diverse emotional practices are not deficits, but legitimate forms of learning and participation. Such a perspective allows for more inclusive and context-sensitive approaches to education, where emotional development is understood not only as an individual process, but as a culturally grounded and socially embedded one.
