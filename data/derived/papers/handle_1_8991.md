# Multiplicity Matters: The Development of Interest Profiles During Dynamic Institutional Transitions

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Students pursue multiple interests within their daily lives. The current study aims to describe the development of interest profiles (the total composition of all interests one pursues) in terms of the amount of interests, their domain divergence and developmental stages. Using Latent Growth Models on interest data of 309 students over the course of three years (10 timepoints), we will specifically explore interest profile development, also in the context of institutional transitions and COVID-19 measures.

## Introduction

Students pursue multiple interests within their daily lives (Akkerman & Bakker, 2019; Hofer, 2010). Given limited time and resources in daily life, interests develop relative to one another and students may experience competition between interests. For example, a student may have an interest in both playing tennis and hockey, but may only be able to pursue one of these sports due to the costs involved and the required time investment. This may prompt unconscious or deliberate weighing of tennis versus hockey, forcing a choice to continue pursuing only one or the other. The weighing of interests against each other may also be more subtle, not forced by external factors but by internal prioritization (Draijer et al., 2022). For  example, if a student is interested in both painti ng and drawing because engaging in these interests allows them to express their emotions creatively, they may over time choose to pursue only one of these, allowing them to still express their creativity but simultaneously pursue a new interest serving a different purpose, for example going out with friends in order to satisfy their social needs. Studying the development of interest profiles (i.e., the total composition of all interests one pursues at a certain point in time) is key in understanding the continuation of a single interest. Whether an interest is continued over time may depend on the amount of parallel interests one pursues, the developmental stage of these interests (i.e., how novel or developed the interests are), and their domain divergence, i.e. the degree to which the interest object is of similar or different domain from parallel interests. The amount of interests one actively pursues may decrease as one grows older due to specialization, possibly enhanced by changing life-wide circumstanc es. Previous interest studies theorize that, as an inte rest develops over time, the object of interest bec omes more valued and the individual desires to become more skilled or knowledgeable (Hidi & Renninger, 2006), prompting them to spend more time engaging with it. This also  relates to identity formation: as a student grows older they increasingly realize who they are and what is important to them, including which interests they want to continue pursuing (Hofer, 2010; Krapp, 2002). This process of specialization may result in a decreasing amount of parallel interests as the growing interest takes up time and  resources previously spent on other interests. Dom ain divergence may also decrease when the objects of in terest that remain are similar in content (e.g., so meone studying in IT and engaging in various computer games in their spare time). On the other hand, individuals may also drop interests that concern similar domains, s o as to retain variety (as in the above example abo ut painting and drawing). Changing life-wide circumstances, suc h as transferring to tertiary education, likely con tribute to interest profile development as the new environment offers new opportunities or takes others away (Hofer, 2010). The research question we aim to answer is: How do interest profiles develop over time in terms of amount, domain variety and predominant developmental stages? More knowledge about intrapersonal interest dynamics can enrich theory on interest development: studying  an interest in relation to the student’s whole lif e yields a broader understanding of the value and development of the interest than studying it in isolation. For example, parallel competing interests can aid richer understanding of declining STEM interest (Potvin & Hasni, 2014).

## Participants

Participants of this study concerned 309 students a ged 14-30 (M = 19), with 70% female. These students were enrolled in diverse educational tracks in the Nethe rlands, including (pre-)vocational education, gener al (professional) and (pre-) university education (in Dutch: mavo, havo, vwo, mbo, hbo, wo). All students were sampled in the penultimate year of their current study program. ICLS2022 Proceedings 1886 © ISLS Materials In this study, an ESM (Experience Sampling Method) smartphone application called inTin was used to capture (changes in) daily-life interests of the participat ing students. Students used this application to log  their existing and novel (incidental) interests that they experien ced within a week. When adding an object of interes t to the application, participants filled out six scales about the characteristics of this interest (e.g., how novel the interest is to them). Participants were free to give any nam e to their objects of interest instead of choosing from preexisting categories, doing justice to the idiosyncratic nature of interest (Akkerman & Bakker, 2019).

## Procedure

Students used inTin for ten waves (consisting of one week) total, spread out equally across three years. For most participants, the transition to another program, gap year or job took place between wave 5 and 6. Before wave 8 the COVID-19 crisis started, which meant that both wave 8 and wave 10 (second COVID-19 wave) were spen t in lockdown, e.g., with schools being closed and restrictions on inside sports. All participants signed an informed consent form and for participants who were younger than 16, a parent or caregiver signed an additional permission form. Ethical approval for this study was given by the departmental Ethics Committee. Analysis In order to map the development of students’ interest profiles, each object of interest is to be coded into one of 21 domains (sports, social, music, et cetera). We will  run several Growth mixture modeling (GMM) models f or parallel processes in MPlus (Muthén & Muthén, 1998-2017). This allows us to test growth trajectories i n terms of amount of interests, developmental stages and domain divergence, also allowing for different types of growth trajectories for different (groups of) students. Li near and quadratic effects are compared for this pu rpose. To describe domain divergence, we use the amount of do mains in someone’s interest profile as outcome vari able, and for developmental stages we use the ratio of le ss-developed interests versus well-developed intere sts (indicating that, for example, someone has relatively many novel interests in wave 1, and fewer novel interests at wave 10). If applicable, follow-up analyses are conducted, for example to get more information on which domains are increasing or decreasing.

## Anticipated results and discussion

We anticipate that overall the amount of interests will decrease over time, and that the domain divergence either remains similar or decreases as well. When both the  amount of interests in a profile and the domain di vergence decrease, this will confirm that processes of speci alization take place in interest development over t ime. Alternatively, when the amount of interests decreas es and the domain divergence remains on a similar l evel throughout time, this will indicate that individual s strive to retain a degree of variation in their d aily interestrelated pursuits. Results may indicate individual variety in these growth patterns. In addition, resul ts may show that a longstanding interest can indeed fall away d uring times of transition, but is likely to be repl aced with an object of similar domain and purpose. Results are likely influenced by COVID-19 measures, we will discuss the suspected impact of these on interest profile development.
