# Analyzing and Designing for Sustained Organizational Change

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
When designing, implementing, and studying changes in education, learning scientists and our partners are frequently aiming for sustained impact. However, achieving this is notoriously difficult. Research on organizational change and institutionalization most commonly looks to the past, retrospectively explaining why practices have—or have not—been sustained over time. However, the field is in need of an approach that can give clues about future sustainability in real time and aid in collaborative strategizing and design for long-term change. We argue that the MoRe Institutional framework can complement current design and improvement approaches to serve this purpose, by focusing attention on specific structures likely to aid or hinder durability. We illustrate with two examples of empirical projects drawing on this framework, demonstrating its potential to structure partnered and participatory change efforts, and integrate attention to long-term processes of institutionalization and de-institutionalization into the timescales of iterative design.

## Introduction

As learning scientists, we work to ensure that all learners have access to high quality, equitable learning environments. In many cases —particularly in traditions of design-based and partnered research —this means designing for ambitious changes to practice, which we then hope will be sustained over time. In doing so, however, we come face to face with a host of common obstacles to the long term efficacy of educational improvement efforts: new approaches quickly fall out of fashion (Coburn,  2004); policies are formally adopted but decoupled from practice or do not meaningfully affect the central issue  (Meyer & Rowan, 197 7); new practices meant to replace ineffective or inequitable practices are in stead enacted alongside existing approaches  (Spillane, 2009); new practices are sustained only as long as a strong advocate is in place (Datnow, 2005); new practices reconfigure rather than remove the inequities they are meant to address (McCambly & Colyvas, 2022). Educational scholars have documented some features that support the maintenan ce of high quality pedagogical practices over time, including strong a nd deep social networks (Coburn et al., 2012), routines that explicitly couple daily work with formal policies (Spillane et al., 2011), alignment between reform logic and perspectives of local implementers (Yonezawa & Stringfield, 2000), and local context fe atures such as positive past experience with reform and high implementer capacity (Datnow, 2005). And yet, initiatives often fail to match their lofty goals, as long-standing practices that are ineffective or inequitable, often prove remarkably “sticky,” despite multiple attempt s to dismantle them (e.g. Zeller, 2022b), while new approaches designed with greater thoughtfulness all too often fail to “stick.” Institutional theorizing, both within the education al arena, and from the organizational sociology and management traditions, also has a great deal to say about both change and persi stence (e.g. Burch & Crowson, 2020; Rowan, 2006; Scott, 2013). The construct of institutionalization, shed s light on both what allows new practices to become durable, as well as what prevents old practices from being replaced. Social structures that are institutionalized are characterized by taken-for-granted-ness, and a sense of importance beyond their techni cal value (Scott, 2013). This creates feedback loops in which  established practices persist through processes of continuous social reproduction. Institutional chang e, or de-institutionalization, occurs when these reproductive processes are re-organized, or dismantled (Anderson & Colyvas, 2021). Empirical studies of institutionalization and sustainability typically draw on retrospective methods in order to capture evidence of shifts or continuity over multiple years  in the past  (e.g. Century & Levy, 2002; Colyvas & Powell, 2007; Datnow, 2005). This approach is appropriate for analytic work and has yielded important insights. However, this strategy is highly limited in what it can offe r for processes of partnered and participatory design research, which, by their nature, are forward looking. Scholarship in the learning sciences spans multiple approaches that incorporate attention to  contextual and institutional dynamics into design and improvement efforts, including design-based implementation research (Penuel et al., 2011), change laboratory methods (Virkkunen & Newnham, 2013) and improvement science (Bryk et al., 2011). Yet d esigning explicitly for durability within such approaches requires a lens  that illuminates indicators of future sustainability that are measurable and malleable over relatively short cycles of data collection, analysis, and iterative (re)design. We argue that the MoRe Institutional framework —an approach highlighting concrete loci of institutionalization and de-institutionalization dynamics in educational settin gs—can provide conceptual and analytic support for exactly these processes. Framework: Modes of reproduction The MoRe Institutional framework  (Anderson & Colyvas, 2021)  is rooted in neo-institutional theorizing originating in sociology and management scholarship (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Meyer & Rowan, 1977). More specifically, it builds on a line  of institutional scholarship that emphasizes the de fining characteristic of institutionalization as integration into self-activating processes of  reproduction. Jepperson (1991)  defined institutions as “social patterns” that are maintain ed by “ relatively self-activating social processes” or “routine reproductive procedures” (p. 145). The implications of this definition were extended by Colyvas & Jonsson (2011) who emphasized the distinction between supports tha t are s elf-activating, and thus indicative of institutionalization—termed modes of reproduction—and processes dependent on special mobilization or action, which cannot alone make new practices stick. For example, while short-term funding or the extraordinary efforts of a few individuals can be highly impactful for br inging about the adoption of a new practice, such supports are unlikely to sustain practices for the long term. By contrast, structures of support that are permanent, and/or create positive feedback lo ops whereby attention and resources are directed increa singly towards the same outcome, create conditions under which patterns or practices are more likely to continue over time. Anderson and Colyvas (2021) introduced the MoRe Institutional framework as a theoretical tool for the application of these insights to the analysis and design of educational change efforts. Drawing on prior theoretical and empirical literature, t hey identified concrete loci of common reproductive processes in the education sector including formal polic ies, interest-group priorities, routines, metrics, professional norms, student id entities, and resonant frames or logics. The heart of the approach is the insight that structures like these, which can operate and exert influence across multiple levels, sustain lon gstanding educational outcomes with little further action required—and at times even despite efforts to the contrary. Highly persistent educational patterns and practices are typically integrated into several of these stru ctures. This means that if strongly institutionalized educational patterns and practices are to be eliminated, it will require severing their connections to the modes of reproduction that sustain them. If new, more effective, and equitable practices are to take hold in a susta inable way, it will require their integration intonew or existing modes of reproduction. Drawing on both published and ongoing work, w e further illustrate this model by presenting two cases of empirical learning sciences research projects utilizing the MoRe framework. Both make use of partnered and/or participatory methods and apply the MoRe framework  in varying ways to focus attention on d ynamics of longterm change and stability. We close with discussion of the framework’s particular affordance s and contributions to research in the field.

Case 1: Analytical action research informing future design In this study, the MoRe framework was utilized as both a conceptua l and analytical framework to examine how classroom-level practices of representing science within a n interdisciplinary science  course for pre-service teachers were supported through institutional structures and systems. The goal of the study was to understand how and why more antiquated practices of representing s cience as facts were sustained within a course that  was intended to align more with currently valued ways o f representing science  that integrate knowledge, practices, and conceptual understanding of science. In addition, the framework provided a lens for exam ining practice that shifted away from thinking of the persistence of mo re and less powerful pedagogical practices as a reflection of the success or failure of individuals. Rather, b y focusing on structures and systems that  served as modes of reproduction for particular classroom practices, th e results of the study served as a powerful tool for fostering conversations about change that did not feel like an evaluation of the practitioners involved. Project overview This study was an action research case study conducted by the first author within an undergraduate physics, Earth, and space sciences course for undergraduate, pre-service elementary teachers (Zeller, 2022a; Zeller, 2022b). The course instructional team included myself, a former high school physics teacher and, at the time, a graduate student in learning sciences; an Earth and space sciences  professor; and two teaching assistants who were selected for their interest in education.  As an action research er, I was positioned as both a practitioner and a researcher. I participated in discussions about the course the year prior to b eing an instructor and strove to leverage my experience and expertise of K-12 science standards and teaching practices to enri ch the experience of students within the course.

The MoRe framework guided the study both theoretica lly and methodologically, bringing focus to how classroom practices were sustained—for better and for worse. The research questions for the study were, (1) what practices of representing science occur within this course, and (2) what are the modes of reproduction that sustain these practices? Within and following the research, I engaged in conversations with the  fourth author, who was the physics instructor  for the course  in prior and subsequent years. These discussions re sulted in further design changes to the course the following year s, reflecting shifts away from longstanding practice s towards new approaches that more fully centered students’ learning. ## MoRe data collection, analysis, and findings

Strategies for both data collection and analysis in this study were developed by integrating the MoRe framework with the framework selected to characterize the pra ctices being examined (Lederman & Lederman, 2020). The data for th e study (shown in Figure 2 in yellow) included course materials (lecture, lab, and assessments) and semi-structured instructional team interviews and reflections. Course materials were used to capture classroom practices along a continuum, ranging from represent ing science as isolated scientific facts or knowledge to representing science as a combination of scientific knowledge, scientific inquiry, and of the nature of scientific knowledge (Lederman & Lederman, 2020). Interviews and reflections from instructional team members were used to elicit structures at multiple levels that shaped design and instruction, including modes of reproduction. During the  qualitative analysis  process (shown in blue), first the course materials were coded using deductive codes capturing representations of scienc e, drawn from Lederman & Lederman, 2020, while the interviews and reflections were deductively coded using the seven modes of reproduction identified by Anderson and Colyvas  (2021). Iterative analysis then involved open sub-coding and cross-coding the data sets and constructing connections between the patterns of pr actices observed and modes of reproduction  that supported these practices.

Figure 2 Data, analysis/findings, and action/changes within Case 1 study. A key finding regarding classroom practices,  was that representing the nature of scientific knowledge was much less common than representing scientific knowledge and scientific inquiry. Most interestingly, there was a substantial difference between the Earth and space units and the physics units. The  physics units had consistent practices of representing knowledge and inquiry without including much, if any, representation of the nature of scientific knowledge, while the Earth and space units showed patterns of representin g science in a way that included more of the nature of scientific knowledge, along with knowledge and inquiry. Analysis of the modes of reproduction sustaining th is divide in practice s between disciplines revealed multiple sources of support across multiple levels. These included  deep seated, discipline-level modes of reproduction as well as instructor-level teacher training.  The lack of representing the nature of scientific knowledge in physics was supported by the professional norms of pedagogical traditions within introductory physics courses. This includes the content as well as the traditional algorithmic, fact and answer focus for learning physics. These norms privilege practices of representing science as isolated knowledge and although efforts have continually been made to update and push classes to meet more inquiry-based models, this and many introductory physics clas ses are still based in the default form of traditio n. The general lack of representing the nature of scientific knowledge in physics units was also supported by my own professional norms, which were focused on the integration of scientific knowledge and inquiry. These norms were established in my training in Modeling Instruction (Wells et al., 1995) for high school physics instruction. Overall, combining the MoRe framework with a practice-focused framework allowed for capturing a wide breadth of possible modes of reproduction whil e having targeted analysis of specific practices. This combination of frameworks moved the study beyond si mply reporting what was happening in the course to building an  understanding of how and why specific patterns of p ractice were occurring  from an institutional perspective. The analysis was further enriched through the actio n research approach. The intimate kn owledge afforded by combining practitioner and researcher k nowledge (Roth, 2001) was instrumental to the conne ctions constructed between patterns of practice and modes of reproduction. It also built trust between myself and the instructional team that was instrumental in enacting changes based on the results of the study. (Re)design and strategizing The rich, explanatory findings of practices coupled  with corresponding modes of reproduction spoke not  only to what was happening in the course, but also how and why these practices were sustained as patterns. As such, they also facilitated productive and strategic conversat ions about possibilities for future iterations of c ourse design (shown in aqua). As an instructor and action researcher, I leveraged the findings to discuss the implications with the instructional team, including the fourth author. These discussions created a space for problematizing patterns of practice that seemed counter to the goals of the course and implementing change. The following semester the fourth author returned to the course and implemented various chan ges inspired by the first author’s  findings. The focus of the analysis on structures, rather than in dividual choices, avoided singling out individuals or introducing a deficit mindset of the instructional team. Instea d, it welcomed wonderings of how modes of reproduction associated with patterns of instruction, specifically long-held assumptions and norms, may be hindering student learning. The major  resulting shifts were associated with problematizing the content priorities and sequence that were based in canonical traditions for physics courses—a powerful mode of reproduction surfaced by the study. Together, we came up with a new sequence for the course that created more emphasis on how the physics and Earth and space content are interconnected and reduc ed the focus on rote use of equations to solve for answers. The study also impacted another introductory cour se within the department through conversations between the fourth author and other professors in the department who implemented similar sequencing changes to increase student access to deeper sensemaking. Case 2: Partnered organizational change In this ongoing project, the second and third authors have been working in collaboration with internal researchers and other staff at an informal learning organization, “Local Learning Zone” (LLZ). This  prominent organization serves thousands of families in the region annually, and, recognizing its disproportionately White leadership and audience, has made significant investments in equity. We are using the MoRe framework to support LLZ staff in designing organizational supports to strengthen, protect, and sustain current equity-focused initiatives. The framework has provided us with ways to structure data analysis, ongoing iterative feedback, and co-strategizing processes, as well as offering new process-focused ways LLZ staff can think about their equity work. Project overview LLZ recently embarked on a multi-year initiative to  (re)design the pedagogical  practices used across the organization, with the goal of fostering a greater sense of belonging among both the families served by LLZ and staff themselves, with a particular focus on Black and brown, LGBTQ+,  and disabled interest groups. This work is being led by LLZ’s internal research team and engages both staff and local learners. Our cross-institutional collaboration is intended to complement this work by supporting  the LLZ staff in aligning their newly designed practices to key sources of inequity, and ensuring the shifts they create are durable. We seek to answer the question: H ow do modes of reproduction  operate as obstacles and/or levers for durable, rac ially equitable organizational change?

The roles of our internal and external research tea ms continue to evolve, unfolding in phases corresponding with the phases of the initiative, as well as dynamic shifts in organizati onal priorities and needs. Broadly, the internal research team has designed, c onvened, and facilitated activities for staff and f amilies, and collected data capturing participants’ perceptions and reflections related to those activities and a broader sense of belonging. The external team has serve d as participant observers  of i nitiative activities, interviewed staff participants, and offered introductory and interim workshops to present the MoRe framework and our preliminary findings to interested staff and organizational leaders.

## MoRe data collection, analysis, and findings

Figure 3 represents a schematic overview of our pro cess as external research partners  (adapted from McCambly & Anderson, 2020). We have currently completed our first round of data collection (shown in yellow) and a preliminary round of data analysis (shown in blue). Data collected included semi-structured interviews with staff across roles and departments, observation notes of working group meetings, and artifacts  including policy documents and working group products. Using those data, we  worked to name the key educational and organizational patterns and practices that LLZ staff are interested in remediating, and the inequities embedded in those patterns. We started with patterns directly r elating to family and staff belonging, the focus of the initiative. We left room for other themes to arise as well, noting additional challenges that staff expressed interest in targeting as well as visions of what LLZ could offer that arose during working group meetings and individual interviews. We also sought to highlight current promising patte rns that could be extended or sustained, as a way t o acknowledge and affirm the good work that is already being done, and strategize to maintain it, despite the overall change process.

Next, we looked to understand the processes through which the status quo persists, including both modes of reproduction maintaining overall patterns, and p rocesses exacerbating the issue for marginalized gr oups. Our preliminary data analysis process involved highlighting structures identified by Anderson and Colyvas (2021) as mentioned within staff interviews, and memoing about their relationships to key outc omes. We found  evidence of structures at multiple levels reproducing the or ganizational status quo, inclu ding frames regarding organizational purpose, cultural norms within the w orkplace and the field, staff professional and personal identities, internal policies, and routines around meetings and communication channels. As we systematize this into a formal coding scheme in the next phases of the work, we will also open code to identify additional modes of reproduction that emerge from the data. Then, following Zeller (2022b), we will produce a matrix to investigate links between the structures identified and the outcomes they may  (or may not) be reproducing, including the potential for new structures.  In addition to looking for structures that are upholding the outcomes that LLZ staff no longer want to reproduce, we also seek to highlight existing structu res that are promising which may be places to engage in order to sustain and deepen positive outcomes. Figure 3 Use of the MoRe Institutional framework within Case 2 Study (Re)design & strategizing Ultimately, the purpose of our work is to support strategic action within our partner organization, towards the goal of long-term equitable practice. The MoRe framework, with its emphasis on sites of reproduction, helps us support LLZ staff and leadership in making decisions about where to focus their atte ntion in order to maximize the durability and effectiveness of their equity-focused change efforts. Our participation in this stage of the work (represented in aqua) is just beginning. Our plan is to embed within staff-led design projects to develop change strategies related to the current initiative, and conduct iterative rounds of data analysis, workshops, and data collec tion, as relevant. These analytic processes will fo cus specific attention on the viability of strategies to disrupt existing modes of reproduction, particularly those responsible for inequitable outcomes —or extend those supporting promising patterns.  We will develop a set of logic models representing how each current or potential strategy might influence the relevant self-sustaining structures and their corresponding outcomes, to present to our LLZ partners as they continue to develop their work. At the same time, we have already seen some evidence of our collaboration’s impact. For example, based on our first round of data collection and analysis, it became clear that persistent patterns related to belonging were influenced by modes of reproduction operating acros s organizational levels and departments. Parallelin g the findings in the science course discussed above, preliminary analyses suggest these structures operate at levels ranging from the local context of this specific institution, to the informal learning field, to society as a whole. The internal research team was able to use this insight  in their planning for phase two, which  is now structured intentionally to include staff representing a broad range of roles and positions. More broadly, our internal research partners report that the introduction of the framework has supported a new way of thinking for both them and their colle agues that is process-focused and emphasizes sustainability, for this particular project and widely across the organization’s work. They are invested in the framework as a comprehensible approach to systems-focused change, that provides an entry point to working towards equity at a feasible scale: Neither as complicated and overwhelming as loftier goals of societal change, nor the normal level of project development and evaluation activities that do not sustain or exist past their completion point. Initiatives in informal learning organizations (both in this particular organization and the field  at large) are often supported by short grant cycles that support work for a brief time period, focused on creating or designing a particular object or program, and then ends once creation is complete. LLZ staff are drawn to the framework due to their desire to create lasting change, and for its ability to ident ify levers that specific individuals can push on wi thin their own work, as well as acknowledge the pressures that all staff are experiencing across different levels.

## Cross-study discussion and implications

As seen across these t wo cases, the use of the MoRe framework can guide multiple aspects of a participatory or partnered research process, from planning, to data collection, to data analysis, to the strategic  design of shifts to practice, to foci for iteration. The framework’s flexibility, along with its groundi ng in specific mechanisms that promote or inhibit sustainability, make it a powerful tool, especially well-suited to supporting shifts in arenas that prior efforts have demonstrated to be particularly resistant to change. As such it can be utilized independently, or in combination with existing methodological and domain-specific tools. The cases presented here  differ along multiple dimensions, including learning envir onment (formal vs informal), scope (classroom practice  vs organization-wide), and method (action research vs partnered design cycles). Yet in each setting, the MoRe framework offers a systematic approach for looking across levels to identify the processes reproducing patterns of interest. For  example, Case 1 identified both local, instructor-level norms and discipline-level norms shaping practices of representing science in t he classroom. In Case 2, preliminary analysis suggests a strong influence of departmenta l, organizational, field-wide, and society-wide impacts on experiences of belonging at LLZ. Similarly, t he MoRe framework’s deep roots in theoretical and e mpirical evidence about loci of institutionalization direct analytic attention towards aspects of contex t that are likely to represent high-leverage intervention points; in other words, it offers insight into likely long term outcomes  of particular courses of action (o r inaction), without having to wait and see what happens. For example, in Case 2, our work with LLZ design teams will allow us to pro vide analysis relevant to their ongoing design proc esses in real time, supporting the alignment and durability of current projects, rather than standing by as they sink or swim and providing a post-mortem after the fact. In addition, the MoRe framework’s focus on structures and systems as opposed to implying or placing blame on individu als creates a positive environment for co llaborating for change. The authors in both studies were able to wo rk collaboratively between researchers and practitioners to strategize on how the contextual factors of their environments and their day-to-day routines contributed to barriers to transformation. Fostering collaborative problem solving in this way can help nurture progress. We see this approach as a powerful complement to a number of research paradigms prominent in the learning sciences. Like design-based, participatory, and partnered research modali ties broadly, it takes seriously the importance of attending to problems of practice (Gutierrez & Penuel, 2014), while offering special relevance to issues of stagnation and/or churn. Like change l aboratory (Virkkunen & Newnham, 2013) and improveme nt science methods (Bryk et al,, 2010), the MoRe framework calls for careful attention to the multiple, contextually specific causes of patterns targeted for change; it further extends special consideration to self-activating modes of reproduction as the sources of the most stable and persistent outcomes. And, like DBIR—and particularly attention to infrastructuring  (Penuel, 2019) —the MoRe framework highlights the organizational dy namics of implementation and improvement efforts and the stru ctures needed for their success; it provides additional principled guidance for design elements and i mplementation strategies most likely to lead to ins titutionalization (and de-institutionalization).

Offering principles for conceptualization, analysis, and design, we have argued that the MoRe Institutional framework can be used to examine and intervene in active systems: targeting modes of reproduction that are sustaining harmful practices and/or strategiz ing around  sustaining new practices. It is a tool that has facilitated change and can be used by learning scie ntists and our partners to support the durability of effective, equitable education practices across learning contexts, as part of our collective efforts for a more just world.
