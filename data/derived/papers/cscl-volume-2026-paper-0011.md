# Structural Signals and Role Archetypes: The Emergence of Roles in a Distributed Epistemic Game

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Roles in collaborative learning are often scripted or assigned; in distributed epistemic games (DEGs), however, they emerge through interaction over time. This study analyzes the first Polymath Project, a large-scale mathematical collaboration, to show how roles crystallize in practice. Using canonicalized epistemic move sequences, temporal distance metrics, and cross-method clustering, I identify two converging empirical signals. First, participant distance matrices display visible “plaid” structures—evide nce that contributors gravitated into role-like repertoires even before clustering. Second, across five analytic pipelines, three participant groupings (‘consistent companionships’) persisted: Proof Builders, Paired Refiners, and Process Integrators. Their recurrence across methods supports viewing them as durable role archetypes. Conceptually, the findings show that roles in DEGs are emergent and self-organizing; methodologically, they demonstrate how structural and reflexive analysis (via AIRR) can jointly surface roles-in-practice, advancing methods for studying collective intelligence and informing the design of systems that support dynamic role ecologies.

## Related work

In most CSCL studies, roles are carefully scripted to support productive collaboration—for example, by assigning learners to act as summarizers, explainers, or critics (Kollar et al., 2006; Strijbos & De Laat, 2010; Strijbos & Weinberger, 2010; Saqr & López-Pernas, 2022). Such scaffolds make participation visible and equitable, but they do not capture how roles evolve once interaction becomes open-ended. By contrast, large-scale collectives such as Wikipedia or citizen science projects show that recognizabl e roles can emerge spontaneously from repeated contribution patterns (Arazy, Ortega, Nov, Yeo, & Balila, 2016; Masters et al., 2016; Watson & Floridi, 2016). Participants differentiate themselves not through assignment but through how they work, how often they engage, and the epistemic moves they repeat or combine (De Wever, Keer, Schellens, & Valcke, 2010; Dowell, Lin, Godfrey, & Brooks, 2020; Hecker & Taddicken, 2022). Distributed epistemic games extend this dynamic: contributors take on characteristic re pertoires of action that can be traced across time rather than predefined categories.

Recent research in learning analytics has emphasized the temporal dimension of participation —how sequences of discourse moves relate to coordination and learning (Chen et al., 2018; Chiu, 2018; Dowell et al., 2020; Rosé et al., 2008). Building on this line  of work, I analyze ordered move sequences from Polymath participants to uncover evolving repertoires of epistemic practice. I employ both edit distance, which captures 15 local substitution and insertion patterns (Levenshtein, 1966), and Dynamic Time Warping (DTW), which aligns sequences even when their pacing differs (Berndt & Clifford, 1994). Together, these measures show similarity in rhythm and structure across participants, exposing the latent architecture of collective reasoning. These perspectives intersect with studies of role ecologies and collective intelligence, where large-scale collaborations are described as layered networks of complementary functions —coordinators, challengers, integrators, synthesizers (Arazy et al., 2016;  Watson & Floridi, 2016; Wenger, 1998). My contribution is to demonstrate that such interdependent roles can be detected empirically: their traces appear as stable structures within distance matrices and recur across independent clustering methods. This mi rrors insights on distributed expertise in knowledge-building communities (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 1994; Sawyer, 2005). This framing positions the Polymath Project as an ideal case for empirical analysis. Because every contribution was timestamped and textually available, it offers a uniquely transparent record of how epistemic moves accumulate into patterned repertoires (G owers, 2009; Cranshaw & Kittur, 2011). To trace those patterns, I adopted a mixed computational and interpretive strategy: refining data collaboratively through AI Reliability through Refinement (AIRR) and analyzing participants’ move sequences using both temporal and structural perspectives (Matthews et al., 2024). The following section outlines how the dataset was prepared, canonicalized, and analyzed to identify the structural signals and consistent companionships that define emergent role archetypes. My methodological goal was to make the emergence of roles empirically visible without reducing them to prelabeled categories. To do so, I combined computational sequence analysis with a reflexive reliability framework (AIRR), in which human –AI interpretation is treated as a structured, traceable analytic process. The analysis proceeded in two complementary strands. First, a quantitative pipeline identified recurring patterns of epistemic activity through canonicalized move sequences, distance metrics, and clustering. Second, a qualitative layer, conducted through the AIRR workflow, documented how analytic decisions evolved, and how disagreements between human and AI readings were resolved. This dual approach provided both the reproducibility of computation and the transparency of interpretive traceability (Chen et al., 2018; Holstein, Aleven, & Rummel, 2020; Woolf, Lane, Chaudhri, & Kolodner, 2013).

The data The analysis draws on the Polymath Project (2009), an open, blog-based mathematical collaboration initiated as an experiment in “massively collaborative mathematics” (Gowers, 2009; Cranshaw & Kittur, 2011). Designed for public contribution in threaded comments, the project left a rich digital record spanning exploration to solution. For this study, I constructed a canonicalized dataset comprising approximately 118 distinct participants and their posts, each annotated with author identity, timestamp, thread context, and coded epistemic moves: ● TAP — Think-Aloud Problem-solving or working in progress ● Evaluate — Testing, checking, or verification ● Idea — Proposal, conjecture, or heuristic introduction ● Meta — Coordination and process management ● Clarify — Communication or clarification moves This representation treats discourse as sequences of epistemic actions rather than isolated messages, allowing temporal and structural analysis of how reasoning evolved. Ethical considerations All data analyzed are publicly available. To preserve privacy and scholarly integrity, findings are reported at the level of role archetypes and aggregated participation patterns. Nonew identifying information is introduced beyond what is public and essential for reproducibility. Data preparation Preparing the dataset required reconciling the openness of Polymath’s participation with the precision needed for temporal and structural analysis. Two refinements were essential: consolidating participant identities and canonicalizing epistemic moves. Because contributors used a mixture of personal names and online handles, I merged clear duplicates (e.g., “Tao” and “Terence Tao”) while retaining uncertain cases as distinct to avoid false conflation. Each post was coded by its primary epistemic function using the five canonical move types above. Multi-word codes (e.g., Meta/Coordination) were remapped into single tokens (Meta) to prevent artificial inflation of n-grams during sequence extraction. These choices align with calls for transparent analytic decis ions in 16 temporal analytics (Chen et al., 2018) and with reflexive, hybrid adaptivity in human– AI workflows (Holstein et al., 2020).

The preparation process exemplified AIRR: early discrepancies between human and AI tokenization revealed hidden boundary rules (e.g., compound labels preserved as single epistemic units). Rather than treating mismatches as errors, they were documented and debated, refining the canonical map through iterative human – AI negotiation (Matthews et al., 2024). With identities consolidated and moves canonicalized, the dataset was ready for sequence-level analysis.

Analytic pipeline Each participant’s contributions were represented as an ordered sequence of canonicalized move codes —TAP, Evaluate, Idea, Meta, Clarify—preserving temporal flow. To quantify similarity between trajectories, I employed two complementary distance measures: Levenshtein edit distance for local substitution/insert/delete sensitivity (Levenshtein, 1966) and Dynamic Time Warping for tempo-invariant alignment (Berndt & Clifford, 1994). Pairwise distance matrices were visualized as heatmaps. The resulting plaid stru ctures—interwoven bands of similarity—offered a first visual signal that participants gravitated into role-like repertoires without predefinition. To test robustness, I conducted five independent clustering analyses with varying parameters, inclusion thresholds, and linkage methods (Müllner, 2011), then compared stability across runs to identify consistent companionships—groupings that persisted across all analytic variations. At each stage, computational outputs were reviewed through the AIRR lens, pairing algorithmic sensitivity with human interpretive judgment (Chen et al., 2018; Holstein et al., 2020). Stability across methods served as the strongest evidence of role robustness.

Reliability and reflexivity Traditional inter-rater reliability assumes that agreement among coders signals validity; that model underrepresents the interpretive complexity of evolving, hybrid analyses. AIRR reframes reliability as iterative, documented negotiation between human and AI interpretations (Matthews et al., 2024). Divergence is treated as data: disagreements (e.g., whether to collapse repeated moves) triggered refinement cycles logged with reasoning and outcomes. Artifacts—comparison tables, refinement logs, decision memos —form an audit trail, aligning with calls for interpretive accountability in temporal analytics (Chen et al., 2018) and hybrid adaptivity (Holstein et al., 2020; Woolf et al., 2013).

All key AIRR stages generated material artifacts: comparison tables, refinement logs, and decision memos documenting both human and AI rationales. These artifacts serve as an audit trail, allowing readers to trace how specific analytical judgments were made and how refinement shaped the final dataset. In this sense, AIRR transforms reliability from a static metric into a reflexive workflow that captures the epistemic life of the This approach also leverages the distinct strengths of human and AI reasoning. The AI offers scalability and pattern sensitivity across vast textual data, while the human analyst provides theoretical grounding and contextual understanding. Reliability thus emerges not from replication but from reconciliation —the point at which machine regularity and human interpretation converge through iterative dialogue. Through AIRR, reliability in this study became evidence of process transparency rather than coder uniformity. It provides a traceable record of how analytic categories were refined, how disagreements were resolved, and how collective understanding was inc rementally stabilized—a reflexive stance consistent with the study’s broader interest in how coherence itself emerges within distributed systems. Taken together, these methodological choices established a framework for observing role emergence as both a computational and epistemic phenomenon. The combination of sequence modeling, distance-based similarity measures, and cross-method clustering made it possible to detect durable structural signals within the Polymath collaboration. The AIRR framework added a reflexive dimension, ensuring that analytic reliability emerges through transparent negotiation rather than static agreement. What follows turns from process to pattern—showing how these methods revealed the plaid structures that mark participants’ converging repertoires and the consistent companionships that define enduring role archetypes within the distributed epistemic game. The results of this analysis reveal two complementary forms of evidence for emergent roles in distributed epistemic games. The first is structural, visible in the patterned geometry of participant distance matrices—visual “plaids” that capture how contribu tors gravitated toward distinct repertoires of epistemic action. The second is robustness-based, demonstrated through the recurrence of specific participant groupings across five independent clustering methods. Together, these patterns provide converging evidence that roles in the Polymath Project 17 emerged organically from sequences of interaction, leaving measurable traces of coherence in both structure and stability.

Figure 1 Plaid Patterns in Distance Matrices Structural signals: Plaid patterns in distance matrices The earliest signal of emergent roles appeared not in the clustering results but within the raw distance matrices themselves as shown in figure 1 above. When participant trajectories were compared pairwise using both Levenshtein and Dynamic Time Warping (D TW) distances, the resulting visualizations revealed a striking plaid structure—a cross-hatched pattern of light and dark bands indicating recurrent pockets of similarity among contributors.

The lighter diagonal blocks correspond to groups of participants whose move sequences followed similar epistemic repertoires, while the darker cross-bands mark sharp divergences between clusters. Rather than a smooth gradient or random dispersion, the matrix displayed alternating ribbons of coherence and contrast, suggesting that contributors naturally coalesced into semi-stable groups with shared tactical rhythms. This emergent structure was consistent across both distance measures, indicating that it was  not an artifact of a particular alignment method but a robust signal of underlying organization. The two distance metrics offered complementary views of these dynamics. Levenshtein distance, sensitive to local substitutions and deletions, emphasized micro-level distinctiveness in participants’ reasoning styles—where small shifts in epistemic sequence created sharp contrasts between groups. In contrast, DTW allowed flexible temporal alignment, revealing broader rhythmic affinities even among contributors who participated at different tempos or durations. The persistence of the plaid structure across both measures confirmed that role differentiation was a structural property of the collaboration, not a byproduct of timing or scale. The plaid pattern represents a structural signal of roles-in-practice—the first indication that roles in the Polymath collaboration crystallized from temporal interaction sequences rather than from external assignment. It captures the collective’s latent organization: how participants gravitated into recognizable repertoires of epistemic action that stabilized the discourse. In this sense, the plaid structure serves as a visual metaphor for distributed cognition—revealing how coherence emerges through patt erned diversity.

## Consistent companionships: Stable archetypes across clustering methods

While the plaid structures revealed role differentiation as a structural pattern, the clustering analyses provided stability evidence—showing that certain participant groupings reappeared across multiple analytic approaches. Across five independent cluster ing runs —including proportional (Role Arc), temporal (DTW), integrated (Levenshtein–DTW fusion), replication (Jupyter reconstruction), and significance-filtered (Python subset) analyses—three participant clusters remained intact under every variation. These groupings, which I call consistent companionships, represent enduring role archetypes within the Polymath collaboration. Remarkably, their stability exceeded that of the project’s founding hosts: while Gowers 18 and Tao anchored several clusters, their membership boundaries shifted across methods, whereas the companionships below persisted unchanged.

Proof builders — This group (Jason Dyer, jozsef, and Michael Peake) formed the collaboration’s proof engine. Their sequences were dominated by TAP → Evaluate cycles, reflecting sustained work on formal proofs punctuated by verification. Quantitatively, the y produced over 100 posts per member —far above the project average—and sustained participation over 500+ days. Textually, their posts were rich in formal notation and structural argumentation. In the network, they occupied central, bridging positions across multiple threads, serving as connective tissue between problem subspaces.

Paired refiners — Comprising Kristal Cantwell and Ryan O’Donnell, this dyad enacted a refinement role built on iterative evaluation and idea injection. Their contributions displayed dense Evaluate → Evaluate loops punctuated by Idea → TAP transitions, suggesting a reciprocal rhythm of testing and extending conjectures. They were the most central participants in the bipartite participant– thread network (degree ≈.48, betweenness ≈.06), reflecting their influence in stabilizing ongoing reasoning and anchoring key problem threads. Process integrators — Christian Elsholtz and Kevin O’Bryant exemplified process anchoring and oversight. Their lower posting volume (≈15 posts each) contrasted with long participation spans (over a year) and distinctive Eval–Meta–TAP cycles, embedding evaluation within coordination and meta-level reflection. Their textual contributions often linked Polymath discussions to external literature (e.g., Behrend’s construction), situating local reasoning within established mathematical frameworks. Together, these companionships highlight a layered role ecology within distributed collaboration. Proof Builders maintained progress through dense proof cycles, Paired Refiners sustained epistemic stability through evaluative iteration, and Process Integrators maintained coherence through reflective oversight. Each archetype exhibited persistence, internal convergence, and structural differentiation—evidence that emergent roles can be empirically recovered from the temporal traces of epistemic activity. The convergence of these companionships across independent clustering runs strengthens the claim that emergent roles in distributed epistemic games are both detectable and durable. They are not analytic artifacts but recurring social–cognitive formations—patterns of contribution that stabilize collective inquiry. In this sense, the Polymath Project demonstrates how role archetypes can emerge naturally within distributed epistemic systems, providing structural scaffolds for sustained knowledge-building. Robustness and ecology of roles The three archetypes demonstrate structural robustness and layered interdependence (Arazy et al., 2016; Masters et al., 2016; Watson & Floridi, 2016). Proof builders, paired refiners, and process integrators collectively balance innovation and stability, echoing broader accounts of collective intelligence (He et al., 2019; Baltzersen, 2022). Together, these archetypes form a dynamic ecology that balances innovation and stability—mirroring how collective intelligence systems maintain adaptability without fragmentation. Such role ecologies illustrate the concept of epistemic elasticity across human-only collaborations: the capacity to stretch toward novelty while returning to coherence.

Methodological contributions: Reflexive reliability through AIRR AIRR reconceptualizes reliability as a traceable refinement process in hybrid analytics, aligning with calls for transparency and human–AI accountability in the design of human–AI systems (Amershi et al., 2019; Chen et al., 19 2018; Holstein et al., 2020; Woolf et al., 2013). In the pipeline, potential inconsistencies surfaced by computation became resources for conceptual clarification through documented negotiation (Matthews et al., 2024). AIRR treats human–AI interaction not as a supporting tool but as a designed analytic process whose structure shapes interpretive outcomes (Amershi et al., 2019).

Operationally, AIRR allowed computational methods to surface possible inconsistencies (e.g., tokenization artifacts, sequence distortions), which were then debated and resolved through documented refinement. This reflexive loop transformed potential analytic ambiguity into a resource for conceptual clarification. In doing so, AIRR aligns with emerging calls in CSCL for interpretive accountability in hybrid analytic workflows—making the reasoning process itself a traceable object of study. Methodologically, AIRR bridges quantitative and qualitative traditions. It pairs the scalability of computation (n-grams, clustering, distance metrics) with the interpretive discipline of qualitative analysis (reflexive memoing, decision logging). The res ult is an analytic framework that is both reproducible and narratively transparent—a model for future work at the intersection of data-intensive and theory-driven research.

## Implications and future directions

The results open several avenues for further research. First, they invite replication across other distributed epistemic games and large-scale collaborations —scientific, educational, or creative—to test whether similar archetypes and structural signals app ear. If such plaid structures and consistent companionships recur across contexts, they may represent a generalizable grammar of distributed reasoning. Second, the findings suggest design implications for systems that support collaborative knowledge-building. Tools could be developed to visualize emergent role patterns in real time, making structural feedback accessible to participants and facilitators a like. Such affordances might encourage reflective awareness of collective dynamics without imposing top-down scripts.

Finally, AIRR’s reflexive approach to reliability can extend beyond this study. Future research might formalize refinement cycles, test their generalizability across analytic teams, and integrate them into reproducibility standards for hybrid (human–AI) research. Synthesis Coherence in DEGs is not imposed but emerges through recurrent patterns of epistemic action. Plaid structures and consistent companionships show that open, asynchronous collectives develop stable, interlocking repertoires sustaining progress over time. AIRR makes that emergence traceable—not only in the data but in the interpretive process itself.

In this sense, the Polymath Project demonstrates that large-scale collaboration is both a social and epistemic achievement: coherence without script, reliability through refinement. This study demonstrates that roles in distributed epistemic games emerge through patterned interplay of epistemic moves rather than external assignment. Temporal sequence analysis plus reflexive human–AI refinement revealed two complementary signatures in Polymath: plaid structures visualizing latent coherence and consistent companionships defining durable role archetypes. Large-scale, open collaborations can self-organize into stable ecologies of contribution—sustaining collective intelligence through diversity, not uniformity. Methodologically, AIRR advances transparent, iterative validation in hybrid workflows. Viewed together, these strands point to epistemic elasticity: the capacity of a system to stretch toward novelty, absorb interpretive difference, and return to coherence (He et al., 2019; Baltzersen, 2022). Understanding and supporting that elasticity may be key to designing the next generation of collaborative knowledge systems.
