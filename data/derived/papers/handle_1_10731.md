# Issues-Based Systems Thinking: Understandings of Complex Systems in Service of Civic Engagement

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Understanding complex systems is vital for navigating the complexities of a globalized society. Although there is an extensive body of research aimed at supporting learners in understanding complex systems, much of this work is geared towards developing scientific knowledge, rather than explicitly preparing learners to address real-world challenges. This paper begins with an introduction to issues-based science education as an instructional context for preparing learners to make sense of complex systems in ways that support civic engagement. Next, trends in existing literature on how people learn about complex systems are reviewed, identifying a need for better alignment between how complex systems are taught in these contexts, and the overarching goal for developing knowledge that empowers learners beyond the classroom. This paper concludes by naming three principles which can be used to guide instructional design in service of these aims.

## Introduction

Societal challenges, such as environmental racism, ecosystem collapse, and the uncontrolled spread of disease, involve complex systems with multiple interacting components. These interactions give rise to emergent behavior which cannot be accounted for by studying features of a system in isolation (Jacobson & Wilensky, 2022; Yoon, 2018; Yoon et al., 2018).  Understanding these systems is crucial for addressi ng contemporary global challenges (Yoon, 2018). Researchers in the learning sciences,  particularly those who specialize in science education, have invested significant resources in understanding how  people come to engage in systems thinking —the skills enabling individuals to engage with and change comp lex systems. This has yielded  an abundance of research as well as dedicated chapters in major learning sciences handbooks (Jacobson & Wilensky, 2022; Yoon, 2018). For understandings of complex systems to matter, th ey must be usable to learners in situations they ar e likely to encounter. While this systems thinking research mostly studies scientific and technological applications of systems thinking, it is also true that most learners will not enter a STEM profession (Rudolph, 2023). Aligned with the theme of "Learning as a cornerstone of hea ling, resilience, and community," this conceptual paper explores how systems thinking education can benefit  a broader population in everyday contexts,  (e.g., civic engagement, personal decision-making) through issues-based science education. Issues-based science education grounds science learning in real-world problems often referred to as socioscientific  issues (e.g., climate change, food deserts, personal and public health concerns, genetic engineering, etc.). These issues are comple x, contentious, and are often used as contexts for complex systems instruction and research. This paper begins with an introduction to  issues-based science education  as a context for  preparing learners to practice systems thinking as civic participants. Trends in literature on systems thinking in issues-based contexts are briefly discussed, identifying a need for better alignment between how complex sys tems are taught in these contexts, and the overarching aims of issues-based instruction. This paper concludes with three principles which define issues-based systems thinking, as well as r ecommendations for designing lessons on complex systems for issues-based science education.

Issues-based science education Issues-based instruction is grounded in the assumption that knowledge is situated within material and sociocultural contexts. As such, issues-based instruction features learning experiences which align with democratic participation so that students are prepared to apply science in ways that are both meaningful and responsible (Sadler, 2009). While issues-based instruction covers the same science content as more traditional  science education methods, it diverges in how scientific knowledge is encountered and operationalized. By engaging with the complexities of the real world, learners are challenged to cons ider how science may be used to inform consequential personal and political decisions. Societal concerns are not only interesting examples of how science can be used, but are the phenomena of in terest themselves (Zeidler & Sadler, 2023). Issues-based science education offers a platform for learning which enables the decision making and sociopolitical praxis neede d to make meaningful, equitable, and sustainable differences in these issues. Developing learners’ capacity to use science as moral, civic agents is a commitment which defines issues-based science education (Zeidler & Sadler, 2023).

Issues-based science education and systems thinking Systems thinking is an essential aspect of understanding and addressing socioscientific issues  due to their complexity. Recently, Zeidler and Sadler (2023) have extended a call for a “more detailed [analysis ] of systems thinking as a practice relative to [issues-based] teaching and learning” (p. 913). Indeed, much of complex systems education literature features socioscientific issues such as water use, climate change, or disease spread (see Yoon et al., 2018 for a comprehensive list of topics).

Most research focuses on how learners engage in systems thinking to make sense of material systems without an explicit focus on how  human or societal systems are implicated in learner s’ thinking. The little work which does study how learners understand  the intersections between human and natural systems  suggests that learning experiences must be intentional about how they support learners in unde rstanding the societal systems implicated in these issues. Scholars have observed students failing to integrate humans into their understandings of material systems (e.g., Assaraf & Orpaz, 2010; Herman et al., 2019; Keynan et al., 2014). Overlooking the societal systems implicated in these issues risks developing incomplete, reductive, or essentialized explanation s of the phenomenon of interest, impeding the develop ment of sustainable, equitable solutions  (Amemiya et al., 2022). To be consistent with the aims of issues-based instruction, issues-based systems thinking should prioritize holistic understandings of an issue that enable civ ic praxis, rather than understandings of material s ystems in isolation from the sociopolitical structures with which they are entangled. Principles for supporting issues-based systems thinking The following sections articulate principles of an issues-based systems thinking which distinguish it from systems thinking performed  by professional scientists or  solely for  the purpose of learning scientific content. T hese principles were derived by considering the ways that complex systems are implicated in the axiological commitments of issues-based instruction. The first principle is that the end goal of issues-based systems thinking is not understanding, but action. As such, systems thinking instruction must incorporate opportunities for learners to practice applying their knowledge of complex systems in ways that prepare them to act as moral, civic agents. The second principle is that issues are states of existing systems, and learning to create just, sustainable solutions to these issues must involve addressing both the immediate issue, a nd the underlying structures which gave rise to it. The third principle is that issues-based systems thinking is inherently interdisciplin ary, requiring scientific practices to be modified to account for multiple domains of knowledge and/or the incorporation of practices which originate beyond professional science. The three principles named here are not a comprehensive list, and I invite others to extend this conversation and refine how issues-based systems thinking is conceptualized. Principle 1:  Issues-based systems thinking builds towards action As a form of situated learning, issues-based science education seeks to help students lear n through legitimate participation in practices used in authentic contexts (Sadler, 2009). While this may entail directly engaging within one’s local  community this may also  occur within a science classroom by taking part in experiences which resemble civic participation. Issues-based education often sees students participating i n lessons which are designed to replicate sociopolitical engagement like designing policy proposals or constructing arguments in favor of certain solutions. Learning experiences designed to support learners’ systems t hinking ought to build towards opportunities for learners to practice the skills needed to enact changes they wish to see in the world. If the purpose of issues-based systems thinking is to leverage knowledge of complex systems through civic participation, culminating activities should be flexible enough to  reflect the ways the learners see themselves apply ing their knowledge to make  a difference, be it through official political chan nels, technoscientific advances, or through grassroots organization and direct action. Activities that do not do so risk limiting who systems thinking education is relevant for. Morales-Doyle (2017) illustrates how this might look as his students confront the issue of environmental racism through soil science. Some examples include devising plans for using sunf lowers to address heavy metal contamination through phytoremediation in partnership with local gardeners, devoting senior capstone projects to raising community awareness of the relationship bet ween environmental contaminants released by a local power plant and their disproportionate impact on low inco me families and people o f color, and translating scientific findings for community members who may not have acc ess to formal educational resources. Although MoralesDoyle does not formally position this work as complex-systems education, his students demonstrate issues-based systems thinking as they leverage their knowledge of ecosocial and community systems to strategically address a systemic issue impacting their personal lives. Designing systems thinking lessons which culminate in authentic, personally meaningful application tasks can help ensure that learners develop understandings of complex systems which are useful to them as engaged citizens.

Principle 2:  Issues are states of existing systems How an issue is conceptualized holds implications for how it is addressed. Often, issues are described as complex systems. However, this description is imprecise; ecosystems and climate systems are not issues, but ecosystem collapse and climate change are. As such, it is important to be intentional in our ontological framing of issues as states which emerge across existing systems.

System states are essential foci of those working t o address large-scale, systemic issues (National Research Council, 2009; Yoon et al., 2018). A state-based understanding of issues creates an opportunity for learners to develop systems thinking practices that address issues across multiple scales, making it possible to consider larger questions related to equity, justice, and what truly constitutes a sustainable solution. Framing an issue as a system rather than a state risks developing a myopic focus which conflates remedies of symptoms for cures of the larger structural problems from which those symptoms arise. Viewing an issue as a state of existing systems invites critique of those systems, opening the door for discussions about how these issues reflect pervasive challenges such as racism or the prioritization of corporate interests over the common good. Preparing learners to recognize issues as states of systems  – systems which have pre-existed and will outlast the issue in question  – may help draw attention to the need to address acut e challenges brought on by the issue of interest, while also considering changes to the ways society is structured in service of a more sustainable, just future. Principle 3: Issues-based systems thinking is interdisciplinary Yoon and colleagues (2018) note that most systems thinking in science education research has focused on learning about biological or ecological systems. Because of this, there is a need to diversify complex systems education research to other knowledge domains. The authors  name scientific domains such as chemistry, physics,  and astronomy in their critique. However, the interdisciplinary nature of issues-based science education invites this call to be extended beyond the boundaries of scienc e and to explore other domains which better  capture the intersections between material and societal systems. Scientific knowledge and practices are invaluable. However, they do not afford a holistic understanding of a so cioscientific issue alone. Leveraging multiple disc iplinary practices to understand an issue opens the door for  appreciating the diversity of perspectives and exp eriences of others entangled within the issue (Zeidler & Sadler, 2023). Research on issues-based science education has a history of exploring how scientific practices may be modified to better account for interdisciplinary knowledge. For example, Ke and colleagues (2021) argue that systems modeling has a particularly important place in issu es-based science education, however they go on to note that traditional scientific  models may not sufficiently explain socioscientific phenomena. The authors contend that it is necessary to develop models which draw on other knowledge domains (e.g., economics, personal experience, cultural funds of knowledge) which allow learners to visualize, represent, and think about the systems implicated in these issues  in a holistic way.  There is emerging research aimed at understanding  how modeling activities may leverage interdisciplinary representations of complex systems (e.g., Lesnefsky et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2022), however, the extent to which these experiences develop lea rners’ understandings of complex systems and/or empower them to engage in systems thinking as citizens is an area of ongoing research. At the same time, it is important to consider the value of practices which are not central to the domain of science (Zeidler & Sadler, 2023). Although there is relatively little research explor ing the ways practices from outside the field of science may influence systems thinking in issues-based educational contexts, practices already considered central to issues-based reasoning such as perspective taking (e.g.,  Kirk & Sadler, 2023; Kahn & Zeidler, 2019) may provide productive starting poin ts. In addition, there are extensive bodies of scho larship and repositories of resources focused on the tools and practices use d by scholars and practitioners to construct knowledge of complex issues (e.g., Brouwer et al., 2016; Pruitt & Thomas, 2007). Issues-based instruction could stand to benefit from these resources, diversifying  the practices and tools learners use to think about systems which may support their civic engagement beyond the classroom.

## Conclusion

For systems thinking education to support  healing, resilience, and community,  it is important to ensure its relevance for learners navigating society’s challenges. This pape r has elaborated on how issues-based science education may serve as a platform for doing so.  It names issues-based systems thinking as a practice which may be used by learners in their everyday life, offering principles and recommendations for supporting its development. By attending to these principles, we stand to develop robust, meaningful understandings of complex systems which support learners in navigating and addressing  socioscientific issues in a holistic, impactful, and ethical manner.
