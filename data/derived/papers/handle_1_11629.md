## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Open-source software development is a longstanding form of collaboration in the technology sector. As high school teachers, we explore the open-source model as a pedagogical framework, building on our work in a graduate-level Knowledge Building course at the Ontario Institute for Studies in Education. We suggest that framing computer science education around the open-source model can support pervasive and authentic knowledge building discourses, by producing explicit artifacts made accessible to the local community and beyond to support continual idea improvement.

Open-Source Software Development (OSSD) Open-source software development is “the process of systematica lly harnessing open development and decentralized peer review to lower costs and improv e software quality” (Raymond, 1999, p. xi). It involves developers, often from across the world, pooling their efforts into a common proj ect. Linux, Android, Firefox, Chromium, WordPress, and VLC Media Player are examp les of open-source projects  with household name recognition. Also notable are the open-source frameworks with which artificial intelligence is commonly built. The term open source was coined by Peterson (2018) in 1998 as part of a movement pushing back against proprietary software and its obfuscated architecture. In 1999, Raymond likened the open-source model to a bazaar which—unlike the orderly and centralized building of a  cathedral—constitutes a cacophonous meeting “of differing agendas and approaches” in which a “coherent and stable system” (pp. 21–22) is nonetheless reached. The open-source model has implications beyond software development. Already in 2004, Coleman noted that “free and open source software, which is by no w entrenched in the technology sector, has recently  traveled far beyond this sphere in the form of artifacts, li cences, and a broader icon for openness and collabo ration” (p. 507). Levine and Prietual  (2014) have since expounded the wider concept of open collaboration, of which Wikipedia is a prime example with its vast encyclopedic content being produced and maintained by volunteers. OSSD is greatly facilitated by the Internet and the specialized tools which allow collaborators from across the world to communicate with each other, maintain version control, track issues, and deploy their projects. The most prominent such tool is GitHub, which has already been shown to have a positive effect on student collaboration and learning in undergraduate software engineering (Patani et al., 2023). OSSD as a whole is made even more relevant to the computer science classroo m by  the fact that  many programming languages are themselves open-source projects, such as Python, Java, Scratch, and R. Interestingly, while it uses the open-source version control system Git, GitHub itself is not open-source. Knowledge Building (KB) Knowledge Building is centred on “the creation and improvement of knowledge of value to one’s communit y,” (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2010, p. 8) within a community of communities framework. KB is social-constructivist in nature, engaging students as epistemic agents in  intentionally and collectively taking responsibili ty for high-level knowledge processes such as planning, goal se tting, monitoring progress, refining plans, and other aspects of knowledge work typically handled exclusively by the teacher. KB is described in twelve KB principles (Scardamalia, 2002; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2003, 2014). Discussions on KB are typically and historically in terwoven with the topic of educational technology. KB technology is “theoretically driven and principl e-based.” (Chen & Hong, 2016, p. 273). Students contribute ideas and artifacts to the community, with those co ntributions collaboratively refined, thus advancing  both individual achievement and community knowledge. The  web-based Knowledge Forum (KF) is the current iteration of the most promine nt example of such a theory-driven platform. It builds on software first introd uced in 1983 and is described by its creators as an environment, rather than a tool (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014). While KB has been principally developed in the context of schooling, it is intended to have far-reaching applications, namely by framing knowledge and ideas through multimodal artifacts: “Knowledge building is not confined to particular occasions or subjects but pe rvades mental life—in and out of school” (Scardamalia, 2002, p. 11). KB technology is meant to support such Pervasive Knowledge Building, one of the twelve KB principles. Practitioner AEvaluation of OSSD and KB in Computer Science Education Over the span of a graduate-level Knowledge Building course, the authors engaged in an exploratory practitioner evaluation of OSSD and GitHub in high school computer science education—a context they were not designed for—through the conceptual framework of KB. The authors systematically mapped the twelve principles of KB onto the OSSD model, as embodied by GitHub. In effect, two analyses occurred: first, comparing KB and OSSD as conceptual frameworks, and second, comparing the web platforms of KF and GitHub as operationalization of these respective frameworks. Notably, the course itself was facilitated by the KF platform, allowing the authors to reflect meaningfully on both the theory of KB and technical implications of its classroom implementation. Both authors had previously taught high school computer science and the second author was concurrently teaching the subject, allowing for  an authentic cycle of experimentation and discussion. Two key observations emerged from this initial exploration of KB in the computer science  classroom. First, constructive student discourses and iterative code improvements occurred readily and represent an easy entry point into KB. Second, having a range of coding skills in groups facilitated better learning, mirroring the KB principle of  Collective Responsibility. These and other observations made during this  initial experimentation fuelled our subsequent efforts to conceptualize OSSD as a form of KB that could support students in the computer science classroom. We argue that t here are conceptual overlaps between OSSD and KB.  Both are centred  on meaningful and authentic collaboration between peers, who share their diverse ideas and skill sets in a decentralized structure. Both also revolve heavily around purpose-made technologies which are themselves evolving alongside their user base. Both arguably also foster community and a collective sense of responsibility towards a shared goal. In our evaluation, we found that t he OSSD model overlaps specifically with the KB principle of  Real Ideas, Authentic Problems in the computer science classroom. Through the cycle of pulling, building, and pushing code, students can collaborate amongst themselves and contribute their work back into the public realm, creating explicit artifacts that others can build upon further. Likewise, OSSD supports the Knowledge Building Discourse principle, since contributors continually seek and accept technical feedback in search of better solutions. We found that this pull-build-push cycle supports different KB principles synergistically and in ways that might be difficult to summarize neatly.

Perhaps counterintuitively, we found that OSSD does not lend itself well to the principle of Improvable Ideas, which calls for multidisciplinary idea generation and unifying explanations. While ideas will be generated, tested, and improved upon under OSSD, these are lik ely to be technical in nature and meant to address narrow issues or request s. Likewise, we found that the Constructive Use of Authoritative Sources  may not occur seamlessly; copying code without proper attribution  is an outstanding problem in computer science, and  AI-generated code further complicates questions of authorship, trusted sources, and broader knowledge. Key features of GitHub were also evaluated for clas sroom use. First was Issues, which can be assigned to specific users, linked to subsequent pull  requests, and help keep track of both divergent and  convergent discussions. Second was the practice of editing code in place, effectively iterating over the same artifact, with GitHub keeping track of overlaid versions. Finally, as an outward-facing platform, knowledge advanced in any given GitHub project is accessible to a massive audience. This introduces a responsibility to maintain clean code and limits the need to reinvent the wheel for explored problems. Notably, KF also presented some key features that we found substantially more suited to classrooms. For instance, we found that the analytics tools in KF were more fleshed out and readily available than GitHub, where the commercial nature of the latter is made apparent. Future Research and Considerations This paper do es not point to any single path forward regarding the adoption of KB in the computer science classroom, nor regarding the development of softwar e which supports KB. The OSSD model  and GitHub could be used in educational context s t o support many facets of authentic KB. Still, the KF platform does hold advantages over the commercially driven GitHub plat form, even in a computer science classroom. We invite further discussion on the tension between industry standard versus educational software in the classroom. We note that the GitHub platform is actually blocked by certain school boards  and that other barriers to implementation need to be explored further. We also invite further inquiry into how KB principles align or contrast with those of the problem-solving framework of computational thinking, found in many computer science curricula. This paper emerged from an authentic graduate-level KB community, where we assumed the roles of learners, KF users, and co-designers. In the spirit of advancing community kno wledge, we plan on proposing changes to the KF platform itself, based on our computer science context. Contributions made back to KF could be utilized by users in other disciplines and contexts, and in unanticipated and novel ways.  Under this light, exploring OSSD as a model for classroom KB has impl ications beyond computer science.  We look forward to interfacing with researchers exploring KB in different spaces, such as science education (Soliman & Feng, 2024).
