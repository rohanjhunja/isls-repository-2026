# Partnering with Multispecies Discomfort: Fear-facing as Multispecies Sensemaking in a STEAM Summer Program

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores multispecies discomfort as a mediator of socioecological sensemaking for youth participating in an arts-integrated STEAM program focused on resource ecology and cartographic art. We share early insights from an observation-based activity with live insects. The program, Cartographic Creatures, engaged youth in modeling field ecology practices, having participants observe insects specimens both inside and outside. Our video-based analysis traces one youth, Gabriel, somatically shifting his body from an affect of fear to fear-facing while meeting a live cockroach. In an additional ethnographic analysis, we also bring in the perspective of the facilitator and the multispecies perspective of the cockroach, drawing attention to their own fear-facing and roles in the collective experience of sensemaking across species. These analyses aim to expand the way we understand the discomfort and fortitude youth, educators, and the more-than-humandemonstrate everyday while inhabiting shared spaces of multispecies relation.

In socioecological learning, unexpected, uneasy, and affectively charged partnerships can emerge as learners negotiate relationships with the more-than-human–not only through care or curiosity but also through hesitation, avoidance, and fear. This means fi guring out how our bodies fit —or misfit— within the spaces, rhythms, and movements of other, often-overlooked beings (Bang et al. 2007; Ma & Munter, 2014). Multispecies sensemaking is not just about noticing other bodies but about coming into contact with the unfamiliar, where affective responses such as disgust, fear, and fascination become shape ecological knowledge.  STEM education often overlooks the ethical and affective entanglement of science learning (Learning in Places, 2018). How we move with, regulate, and respond to other species —even insects —carries sociopolitical consequences that reverberate across human and more-than-human lives. In an increasingly urbanized world, most early multispecies encounters are mediated through schooled settings, where norms of control, hygiene, and safety shape how young people learn to relate to other species. In this study, we examine multispecies discomfort and fear as productive mediators of socioecological sensemaking. Rather than framing discomfort as a barrier to learning, we attend to how the physical and emotional entanglements that emerge in moments of fear-facing become sites where young people practice new forms of multispecies relation.

Multispecies discomfort as critical multispecies sensemaking This work contributes to learning sciences scholarship that seeks to design for more just and life-affirming futures across human and more-than-human communities (Bang, 2016). If we wish to understand and live cohesively with the more-than-human, we must critically engage with the multispecies assemblages that constitute our daily lives. We refer to this process as multispecies sensemaking—the ongoing practice of interacting with, interpreting, and giving meaning to our webs of interconnection with the more-than-human. Multispecies sensemaking involves feeling, moving, and engaging within the social, cultural, and material dimensions of human–more-thanhuman relations (Saba Fisher, Kelton et al.,  in press). Attending to youth’s multispecies sensemaking allows us to notice how they practice living with change. It reveals what matters to them, what they are willing to protect or avoid, and how they negotiate their place within ecological systems. However, these processes are not always comfortable. Encounters with insects, decay, death, or unfamiliar animal bodies often provoke fear, disgust, or hesitation. Rather than treating these reactions as obstacles, we position them as critical sites of ecological sensemaking. Partnering with discomfort —sitting with unea se, negotiating proximity, and recalibrating one’s response to another being— opens up space for youth to recognize theirs’ and others' embeddedness and vulnerability within multispecies webs. We theorize this discomfort as not a purely personal or psychological experience; fear-facing happens between more than one body, environment, or interaction. It becomes meaningful through entanglements with sensemaking and situated practices in social activity (Vea, 2020). Additionally, discomfort is inextricable from our collective interactions with the natural world; it is part of a body’s somatic experience. When experienced in safe contexts, discomfort can mediate positive experience (Rozin et al., 2013) 1842 and scaffold sensemaking about future discomfort. Such moments create opportunities to recognize interdependence not as an abstract ecological concept but as a lived, felt negotiation of risk, care, avoidance, and curiosity. Through these relational frictions, youth begin to sense that living well with others—insects included — requires ongoing recalibration rather than mastery or control. By framing multispecies sensemaking through the lens of discomfort, we argue that multispecies sensemaking is not only about acquiring knowledge but about learning to stay with complex, affectively charged relationships. We co-developed and facilitated Cartographic Creatures, a twenty-hour curriculum integrating ecology, animal behavior, and cartographic art in partnership with an affordable housing community of Latina/o/x families in the inland Northwest. These families navigate structural precarity shaped by intersecting fo rces such as the COVID19 pandemic, the climate crisis, and systemic inequities in schooling, mobility (Marrun & Rodriguez-Campo, 2023), and health infrastructures (Martínez et al., 2021). Rather than p ositioning their lives through crisis, we focus on everyday forms of navigating discomfort as meaningful knowledge practices —forms of sensemaking often erased by dominant, solution-oriented framings of science education (Medin & Bang, 2014; Oziewicz, 2022). Participants included youth ages 5-14. Research design, data collection, and analysis were informed by grounded theory (Corbin & Strauss, 2015), interaction analysis (Erickson, 1992; 1996; 2004; Jordan & Henderson, 1995), design-based research (Cobb et al., 2003; Collins et al., 2004), arts-based visual research (Leavy, 2017), and ethnography (Emerson et al., 2011). We collected data on youth activity as it occurred in context for three weeks in July 2024, and asked: How do youth engage in multispecies sensemaking in the context of cartographic art activities?

Emerging analysis: Fear-facing Our analysis centers around multispecies fear-facing in individual and collective experiences. We understand multispecies fear-facing as making sense of multispecies connections through expressions of alertness, anxiety, nervousness, defensiveness, discomfort, and avoidance toward the more-than-human. Fear-facing itself emerged as an in vivo code from one youth who described touching a live insect as “facing my fear right now” (field notes, 22 July 2024). Tensions and affects are omnipresent in human interaction with the natural world. Providing youth the scaffolding to engage in this sensemaking helps them notice and observe the more-than-human, a key practice in ecological learning. Youth can begin to articulate how they and others relate to other bodies, situate themselves in relation to living systems, and understand the interdependence of ecological webs. The following provide three analyses focusing on three different actors within a scene of fear-facing. The youth The second week of Cartographic Creatures  centered on “things that move” within ecological systems, with particular attention to insects as key actors in these networks. Early in the unit, we introduced a collection of livedemonstration or “educator” insects for open-ended exploration. Facilitators and youth  arranged themselves with the educator insects in a loose circle, engaging through practices of looking, speaking to, questioning, touching, and at times holding the insects. During this initial encoun ter, one youth, Gabriel, positioned himself close to a facilitator for an extended period, navigating the activity through careful bodily positioning and proximity. The following field notes illustrate this moment: Gabriel was really nervous at first, and so he wanted, he kind of sat behind me, and he didn't want anyone near him or any bugs near him. Heaven put a bug in his face and he didn't really like it and kept moving behind the other facilitator [entomology graduate student]. But over the course of the last 30 minutes he started touching them more and wanting to have them on his paper and on his art. And he invited Irene [Gabriel’s sister] over to take a look. Not, I think, out of protection, but as a “look how cool this is. Come over.” (field notes, 24 July 2022) As Gabriel sat in a place he deemed safe, he observed from a distance the insects around the community center, he could take them in—observing and noticing how they move and the features of their varying bodies. Through these practices, Gabriel made sense of how he related to a more-than-human body smaller than him. These practices also helped him reach towards further sensemaking about insects. This was true for many youth with the educator insects; while at first wary, they became actively engaged in bein g with the  educator insects in ways facilitators had not yet seen. Their attention and focus expanded in these activities; many youth shared different sides of themselves in interaction–they became calm when they were usually rambunctious, focused and engaged when they usually chose to opt-out.

These components operate together as tools for making sense: Gabriel’s affective responses—hesitation, cautious approach, and engagement —mediated his learning and understanding in concert with his bodily positioning, illustrating how fear-facing is both an embodied and affective process. In particular, Gabriel began to position himself as an expert, wanting to share knowledge about the cockroach, advise others how to engage, and direct the roach’s body as well. This step from physical distance to taking initiative reflects how Gabriel’s bodily and affective practices coalesced into emergent expertise and relational sensemaking. By moving from hesitancy to active participation, he not only deepened his own understanding of the cockroach’s behaviors and body, but also shaped how others in the circle engaged with the insect, illustrating how fear-facing can serve as a generative process for learning through both affect and embodied coll aboration. The facilitator The majority of the time, insect education is informal and revolves around facilitating experiences with insect bodies for people of all ages and stages (Pautzke et al., 2025). The goal is to generate an interaction that can foster 1844 curiosity, respect, or affection for insects. This requires an attunement to the affective condition of the learner you are engaging with –being able to respond to their physical and mental reactions as they move through the experience. A majority of people possess some configuration of fear-disgust when viewing or touching an insect (Lockwood, 2013). In the moment, me (the facilitator), you (the learner), and her (the insect) exist in a dynamic multispecies association. Even if this assemblage is not consciously felt as a partnership by those involved, the facilitator intentionally builds a space in which discomfort can be encountered (Rozin et al., 2013) and provides an association that may lessen discomfort in the future (Rozin, 2008) with the more-than-human. As the facilitator in this designed activity during Cartographic Creatures, I intended to curate a predictable, safe environment through a number of physical and emotional choices for myself, the youth, and the insect. First, I use my body as the initial filter for the interaction: holding the cockroach, modeling how to pet her, displaying different features of her body. Being very intentional about my own behavior and language, maintaining a calm and curious state, provides a reference to the learners for how they can behave themselves. Second, I provide multiple options for how the youth might engage with the cockroach. At all stages of interaction with the cockroach, I guide youth to consider sensations: What colors does the cockroach have? What does she feel like— soft or smooth, furry or slimy? Third, I take care to acknowledge all of the youth’s observations and answer all of their questions honestly and scientifically. Demonstrating that both their observations and my own scientific expertise are equall y valued in the setting is important for their sensemaking. My position as the facilitator is as an integrated body in the learning space and a part of the coproduction of activity. My body and experience is a mediator for the interaction between multiple bodies and multiple species. The cockroach The Madagascar hissing cockroach, Gromphadorhina portentosa, is a large (2-4 inches long), wingless, slow-moving, charismatic (depending on who you ask) cockroach species native to the subtropical island of Madagascar (Mulder & Shufran, 2016). They are nocturnal, plant-eaters, and can hiss (Mulder & Shufran, 2016). These approachable, cat-like characteristics make them a wonderful outreach partner. However, we take this moment to consider, do they enjoy participating–or even want to–in outreach? One of the most iconic features of G. portentosa is their hissing noises. They are able to make multiple different kinds of hisses, with a subset used for defense and disturbance warnings (Shotton, 2014). The cockroach who participated in this activity hissed and pinched (Figure 1C) when we first touched her, indicating a disturbance (or perhaps discomfort) response (Shotton, 2014). Over the course of the activity, she no longer produced a hiss even when prodded. Instead, she would sit calmly (not running or pinching) while held (Figure 1F). Her antennae moved slowly along the hands of the people holding her, evidencing an active, calm attention to her surroundings (Shotton, 2014). As one species trying to make sense of another, we might project that this pr ogression from initially hissing to calmly sitting represents the cockroach’s version of fear-facing (Davis & Heslop, 2004). She, too, is making sense of multispecies connections through expressions of alertness, defensiveness, discomfort, and avoidance toward an other, and then realized there is no threat necessitating defensive behaviors. Importantly, both the initial fear-facing behavior of the roach and her eventual shift into calmness are what made youth interaction possible in the first place. Fear-facing, then, is not a one-sided human endeavor but a mutual negotiation of safety and risk across species.

Further explorations Across the activity, multiple species expressed a range of affective orientations —hesitation, avoidance, expertise—prompting us to ask how fear-facing shifts across time, somatic proximity, and collective participation. In the excerpt shared, Gabriel’s sequence of movements and affective recalibrations reorganized not only his own stance, but also the responses of peers, facilitators, and the cockroach itself. Fear-facing operates as a distributed process towards multispecies connections. We also consider how the conditions of encounter shape these processes. For instance, local insects are often situated within youths’ everyday ecological familiarity and thus dismissed, swatted at, or overlooked. Educator insects, by contrast, arrive as marked others —named,  held, and introduced through careful facilitation—which alters how youth calibrate fear, curiosity, and responsibility. These distinctions matter: they reveal how context, framing, and human curation structure which multispecies relationships are permitte d, valued, or made available for learning. Including the cockroach’s own disturbance and multispecies engagement in analysis requires us to confront the limits and partiality of our interpretations. What appears to us as alertness, resistance, or calm may reflect an entirely different affective world. Attending to these uncertainties is an ethical practice of multispecies sensemaking—it demands that we remain accountable to our own affective positionings as designers, facilitators and analysts. In this way,  multispecies sensemaking 1845 becomes not only an object of study but a practice of ongoing attunement to the beings with whom we learn and partner with in discomfort.
