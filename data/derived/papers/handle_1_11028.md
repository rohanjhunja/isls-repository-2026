# Using Technology-Mediated Lesson Study with Rural Teachers to Improve Three-Dimensional Science Instruction

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Rural teachers have fewer collaborative and professional learning opportunities. Technology-Mediated Lesson Study bridges the isolation of rural teachers by connecting them to colleagues. This research aimed to build teachers’ capacity to understand and implement three-dimensional (3D) science principles. After one year, interviews reported rural teachers’ growth with 3D science knowledge and implementation and increased value for 3D science.

## Introduction

Current best practices in science education require instruction designed around three major dimensions, known as three-dimensional (3D) science: science and engineering p ractices, crosscutting concepts, and disciplinary c ore ideas (SEPs, CCCs, and DCIs; National Research Council, 2012). A central principle is to make sense of science in a way that combines the 3Ds for more authentic learning experiences. Professional development in 3D science has been effective in helping teachers improve their knowledge and skills (Christian et al., 2021). However, many rural teachers have received little training. Isola tion is a significant problem for many rural teache rs as they are often the only teacher of a particular subject in a school. As a result, they have fewer opportunities for meaningful collaboration, even though collaboration is a crucial char acteristic of effective professional learning (Sven dsen, 2020). Technology-Mediated Lesson Study (TMLS) allows teachers to view and create lessons and learn together when not co-located (Hudson et al., 2024). Thus, supporting rural science teachers in TMLS to improve their 3D science skills led to the following research questi on: How do teachers in a professional learning prog ram build their capacity with 3D science using TMLS?

## Methodology

This case study focused on the impacts of TMLS on 3D science teacher knowledge and implementation to explore 3D science understanding change over a year. Twelve rural science teachers particip ated in an in-person professional development on the principles of 3D sc ience and the TMLS process.  G roups of four teachers developed high school biology lessons aligned with 3D science. All teachers were professionally isolated because they were separated from colleagues by geography or  field of expertise. Semi-structured interviews determine d teachers’ understanding and implementation  of 3D lessons before and after the program. Relevant questions fo r this analysis included: “What do you know about 3D science?” and “Can you describe a science lesson you have implemented in the past?” Qualitative content analysis classified texts by identifying themes.

## Results

Analysis of t he teachers’ post-interviews reported increased knowledge and understanding of 3D science. Teachers used 3D science vocabulary more fluently a nd expressed increased confidence in  their classroom implementation. Some who started the program with minimal knowledge of 3D science could now explain and describe 3D lessons. Even teachers who had prior knowledge  of 3D science  had growth. We examined teacher growth in one TMLS team whose members had various levels of prior 3D science knowledge and experience. What do you know about 3D science?

The teachers were asked to explain 3D science and give an example of a lesson they had taught. Ronan, a secondyear teacher, initially defined 3D science as “trying to see [science] from all angles,” showing a lack of understanding, further demonstrated by his failure to share an example of a 3D lesson. The second teacher, Rich, had been teaching science for eight years. When asked what he knew about 3D science, he said he tries to “correct and gear towards more of the skill sides of [science].” While he does not use any specific 3D terminology, he shows some comprehension of the ideas. Rich’s exampl e lesson did not include any 3D elements, but he did mention some general 3D principles  by emphasizing science content. The third team member, Wayne, a fifteenyear science educator, connected the DCI to a SEP and shared his knowledge with a lesson on 3D elements. Post-interviews demonstrated that all three grew in understanding and comprehension of 3D science after writing four 3D lessons through TMLS. Ronan —the least knowledgeable at the start —still struggled but recognized his knowledge increased. He said, “Having a phenomenon at the base of your lesson is the foundation,” and correctly identified the CCC as the “tool” between different scientific concepts. Ronan picked a project lesson and said, “When you can efficiently incorporate all [3D elements] into a lesson, it really does make a difference.” Rich had had a “significant paradigm shift” and now defined 3D science as when “ those three things [build] on the other…. 3D science provides a framework so [any] question c an be answered.” He elaborated on how he incorporates 3D elements in his teaching: “It was like I had a classroom mind and a lab mind. Now I [see] those are the same thing. There’s no classroom. There’s no lab. It’s just the science.” Even Wayne, who had prior knowledge of 3D science, explained how his teaching and understanding were still evolving. “I’m changing, and the assessment is catching up to that where it’s not so much about regurgitati on; it’s more about how you apply [it to] different scenarios.” He helps students recognize what CCC they can use to ask themselves questions such as, “What pattern can I recognize [to determine] that this solution might be the best way to approach the situation?” How has TMLS changed science teachers’ perceptions of 3D science? Teachers found value in collaborating. One said, “The group that I’ve worked with here, I’ve talked more with about instruction than I’ve had with anyone in the past eighteen years.” They discussed how much they had grown as educators: “Creating lessons specifically tailored to these aspects [of 3D] has increased my comfort level.” One teacher expressed that his outlook changed  and working in a group helped, saying, “If it were just up to me, I would teach a lesson, but I wouldn’t go back and change it. But having some accountability, knowing that others will teach the lesson, [makes it] better.”  Teachers frequently discussed the value for students. One explained that these lessons have “fostered an atmosphere where [students] feel more comfortable being curious and open,” and another sa id it was “probably the first time that [students had] been challenged with problems they had to solve without me giving them the solution.” Another described the potential for students, saying, “They recognize that they will have a new confidence in something scientifically. The three dimensions empower [students].”

## Discussion and conclusion

Previous research shows that one barrier to implementing 3D science is the compl exity of professional development needed for 3D instruction. An initial s tep to successful implementation is for teachers to know and understand what 3D science is and what it looks like  in practice. Our research found that using a TMLS format could be a way to overcome this difficulty. Looking at the research question, we can conclude that teachers’ knowledge, ability to implement, and perceived value of 3D science increased over one year of involvement in a TMLS program. Teachers who began with various levels of comprehen sion about 3D science grew in understanding and capability. Sources of increased knowledge can be attributed to several factors. 1). Teachers participated in repeated professional development workshops that provided in struction and practice with 3D science. 2) Teachers gained experience developing lessons as groups and observing others teach the same lesson. 3). T eachers focused on evaluating each lesson through a  3D science lens. Thus, using the TMLS process to provide professional development for isolated teachers can help them gain knowledge and skills.
