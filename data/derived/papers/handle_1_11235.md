# Prompts or Protocol: Helping Teacher Candidates See Students’ Strengths

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Science education has shifted from a focus on canonical correctness toward students and sensemaking. In response, teacher educators have explored video analysis as an activity for helping teacher candidates notice students’ strengths. Framing influences what teacher candidates learn through video analysis. In this paper, we analyze a protocol as a support for teacher candidates’ noticing of students’ strengths. We find the protocol supported strengths-based noticing and positioned the facilitator as a collaborator. These findings offer implications for teacher educators aiming to support equitable science teaching.

Objectives Science education has shifted from a focus on canonical correctness toward students and sensemaking (Campbell et al., 2016; Tekkumru-Kisa et al., 2023). This shift requires teacher edu cators to help teacher candidates (TCs) recognize and respond to students’ resources (Haver ly et al., 2020; Nasir & Hand, 2006). However, rese arch demonstrates that TCs often focus on teacher actions (Roth McDuffie et al., 2014) and on classroom management (Mitchell & Marin, 2015) rather than on students’ resources. This focus can prevent TCs from noticing  students and their strengths (Feiman-Nemser, 2012). In response, teacher educators have explored video analysis for supporting TCs to notice students and their thinking (Barnhart & van Es, 2020; Santagata, 2009; van Es & Sherin, 2008). Specifically, they have explored a variety o f approaches for focusing noticing on students’ str engths, including facilitator prompts (Barnhart & van Es, 2020), tools like graphic organizers (Johnson & Mawyer, 2019), and discussion protocols with targeted sentence fra mes (Jilk, 2016). While structured approaches may s caffold TCs’ noticing, they may also limit learning by cons training discourse. To explore these tradeoffs, in this paper, we compare two approaches for supporting TCs’ noticing of students’ strengths.

## Theoretical framework

In science education, there is broad agreement that  equity is important; yet, multiple conceptions of equity exist (Grapin et al., 2023). Drawing on these conceptions, we created a  conceptual framework that situates forms of noticing within approaches to equity. We conceptualize transformative approaches to equity as expansive perspectives that challenge Eurocentric ways of kno wing and doing, amplifying multiple epistemologies (e.g., Warren et al., 2020). Such approaches frame student s’ contributions as generative. In contrast, dominant approaches narrowly reify disciplinary norms (a “se ttled” [Bang et al., 2013] image of science). D ominant approaches have the potential to harm minoritized students (e.g., by focusing on “achievement gaps” that compare minoritized students to white peers), particularly by encouraging evaluative stances toward students. At the same time, dominant approaches to equity also have the p otential to support minoritized students to engage with disciplinary concepts and practices. We situate for ms of noticing along this continuum, focusing on ho w TCs notice students. We place deficit noticing (e.g., noticing student contributions, int erpreting them as incorrect) on the left of the spectrum. This form of noticing tak es a dominant approach to equity, measuring student s’ contributions against disciplinary norms, and it is harmful to minoritized students. We place asset-based noticing in the middle of the spectrum. This form of noticin g also draws on a dominant approach, recognizing students’ assets for achieving disciplinary norms. At the sam e time, it has the potential to support minoritized  students by helping them see themselves as capable of achievement in disciplinary spaces. We place strengths-based noticing toward the right side of the spectrum. By strengths-based noticing, we refer to teachers attending to s tudents’ contributions and interpreting them as strengths fo r asking and answering questions about phenomena,  whether or not these strengths align with disciplinary norms. As Russ (2018) explains, teachers’ attention sends messages to students about what is valued. Strengths-based noticing has the potential to implicitly or e xplicitly challenge what counts as learning, knowing, and doing in scie nce education; therefore, we place it closer to the transformative end. However, simply attending to students’ contributions and interpreting them as strengths does not necessarily challenge disciplinary norms; a transformative response is alsoneeded. We thus place these forms of noticing to the left of transformative approaches.

## Methods

Thus, rather th an taking a cognitive approach to teacher learning by aiming to demonstrate a conclusive shif t from dominant approaches toward strengths-based approaches within individuals, we instead take a sociocultural approach that considers the affordances of specific tools for focusing collective discourse on students ’ strengths. We used data from a study which took place at two u niversities (one small private university in the southeastern US, and one large public university in  the midwestern US). Each site used a similar eleme ntary science methods curriculum to introduce video clubs, and each offered opportunities for TCs to partici pate in video clubs in the semesters following their methods courses. Data sources include video recordings of the video clubs. Our analysis considers one video club an exploratory case (Yin, 2014) of the influence of framing on video club. We analyze the only video club in which we piloted our adaptation of the Jilk (2016) protocol. We analyzed TCs’ noticing, specifically related to students’ contributions and their interpretation of students’ understanding and participation. We conceptualized noticing using the Framing-Attending-InterpretingResponding (FAIR) framework  (Louie et al., 2021). For framing, we analyzed both explicit framing (e.g., prompting) and implicit framing (e.g., ideological narratives). For noticing, we analyzed what TCs attended to. When TCs attended to students, we considered how th ey interpreted students’ actions and interactions (e.g., genius, silly). When applicable, we also analyzed t heir imagined responses—what they might do differently, or how they might follow up. Each of the first two aut hors analyzed the video independently, then met to discuss their application of the FAIR framework and resolve discrepancies. We then identified themes, such as how often TCs attended to students (compared to teachers, cla ssroom management, etc.), as well as how TCs charac terized students’ participation. Focusing on TCs’ noticing of students allowed us to consider the implications  of the protocol for supporting TCs to recognize and value students’ resources.

## Findings

We present a case that illustrates how framing influenced discourse in video club. This video club took place in the semester after the methods course. Two TCs (Bet sy and Boophis) and one facilitator (Heather) participated. At the beginning of their meeting, Heather introduced the protocol. Betsy shared a video in which she conducted a student thinking interview with Lela, a kindergar tener she was babysitting. Betsy used interview questions that she developed during her methods course. Betsy firs t showed Lela a video of a disappearing puddle in o rder to “get at evaporation.” Betsy noted that she “never a ctually taught her evaporation, because this was ju st eliciting her initial ideas about why the puddle disappeared. ” She explained that she struggled to “eli cit” in this case because “Lela never mentioned the sun, so it was a little harder for me to elicit thinking, because she never got to that point.” From this framing, Betsy implied that “eliciting” student ideas is more valuable when it aligns with the teacher’s goal. Yet, while Betsy initially attended to  this conceptual goal, during the video club, parti cipants challenged this narrow focus.

First, participants engaged in the “science task.” Due to time constraints, the group brainstormed around the question Betsy asked Lela: why does a puddle di sappear? Boophis and Heather introduced a range of ideas including: the sun dries it up, someone splashes in it, an animal drinks it, and it sinks “into the ground.” Then, the group watched the whole video without any interruption, asked clarifying questions, and discussed student work. Boophis immediately attended to Lela, commenting on  her intelligence in response to the student work. Betsy reported that although Lela did not yet know how to write numbers, she represented time by drawing vertical lines of decreasing length. Boophis described this practi ce as “genius.”  Next, the group discussed the entire video, attending to Lela’s understanding. Starting from a strengths-based approach, Boophis attended to a moment after Betsy asked a question about hot sand. Lela paused,  then made a connection to the beach, which Boophis interpreted as complex. Betsy also attended to Lela’s thinking—a moment when Lela noted that a bigger puddle takes “longer to disappear.” Then, Betsy reflected,  “but maybe that’s because it was correct so that’s  why I am seeing it.” Betsy thus pushed back on her own frami ng about what counts as “understanding.” She also p ointed out that Lela “didn’t give strong reasoning for it,” evaluating Lela’s response normatively even while challenging this framing. Here, Betsy grappled with dominant narratives of “understanding.” Heather elaborated, emphasizing that Lela was consistent in her “bigger puddle take s longer to disappear” explanation throughout the c lip, interpreting coherence as another strength in Lela’s understanding. For the second step (student understanding), the group watched the first two minutes of the video again. Betsy noticed Lela’s attention to the different contexts across the two videos they watched (puddles disappearing on pavement and in the desert). She interpreted Lela’s focus on environmental factors as complex and nuanced, a strengths-based interpretation. Boophis then noticed that Lel a seemed to believe “hot” is important, repeating, “The desert is hot. Sand is hot.” Boophis explained  that Lela might not have known why this is relevan t. Yet, rather than focusing on what Lela did not understand, she interpreted Lela’s connection as a valuable resource for later explaining evaporation. Heather and Boophis noted additional understandings: puddle s come from rain and sink into the ground.

For the third step (participation), Heather suggested thinking about participation norms both with respect to Lela and classroom implications. The group watch ed the next two minutes of the video. Betsy attende d to a moment when, after being asked why a puddle bubbled  in the desert, Lela took a long  pause before responding. Betsy interpreted this pause as evidence that Lela understood the norm of “when your teacher asks you a question, you have to provide an answer.” Betsy noticed that Lela was also very responsive when Betsy asked her to “pick up your pencil,” explaining, “she clearly is conditioned to do that.” Boophis challenged Betsy’s interpretation by saying “What you said about “she took time to think about it” is also important, because the norm that her thought process is important, and it’s not the first thing that came to her head—like clearly she is comfortable just letting you sit in silence…. She doesn’t feel like it has to be immediate. So I think that’s a norm that is go ing to support intellectual participation instead of just any-answer participation.” Here, Boophis reframed Betsy’s account of compliance by focusing on the pause rather than the response, describing Lela as knowing and acting on the value of her sensemaking. Heather then identified Lela’s generative connections and raised classroom implications. Fourth, the group watched the last two minutes of t he video in which Lela created her drawing, then discussed “smartness.” Before addressing the question in the protocol, Boophis attended to Betsy’s teaching. She praised Betsy for “letting thinking come from the student” rather than “feeding [answers] to her.” Yet, the protocol helped maintain a focus on the student rather than teaching—after responding with “thank you,” Betsy attended to Lela’s contributions and listed multiple reasons  they were “smart.” First, Bet sy interpreted Lela’s puddle progression in her illustration as smart. Betsy att ended to the way Lela coordinated (a) the illustrat ion of the puddle getting smaller (b) a verbal description of the puddle getting smaller (“largest, medium, small est”), and

(c) the passage of time, repre sented with vertical lines of decreasing length. Heather noted that Lela represented

the puddle getting smaller with a hand gesture as w ell. Boophis attended to another representational c hoice: she interpreted drawing the pavement as a square as sma rt because it helped “distinguish” the pavement from the puddle. She also attended to how Lela included bubbles in her drawing, which she interpreted as evidence of Lela incorporating new ideas from the conversation into her drawing. Then, the group considered how teachers might encourage students to use representations to share their thinking in other contexts. In contrast to steps 2-4, step 5 (takeaways) enabled teachers to consider pedagogical implications rather than focus on student contributions. Drawing on the  “participation” and “smartness” discussions, Booph is considered how drawing could allow a teacher to see  how each student is thinking about the phenomenon.  The quality of this teacher-focused discussion was different from our first cas e, because these pedagogical actions were grounded in Boophis’ noticing of Lela’s representational strengths. Betsy considered instructional next steps, again grounded in Lela’s strengths. Building on Lela’s connection to heat, Betsy considered ways to help students see the role of the sun in evaporation. Boophis and  Betsy exchanged ideas for enabling students to obs erve the role of heat in evaporation. Boophis then reflected  that there were many ideas that Lela shared that s he would want to hear more about, implicitly framing Lela’s thinking as valuable. Betsy agreed she was too narr owly focused on listening for evaporation “I think I was picking out the things that were closest to what I considered to be ‘right thinking’ about it and so I didn’t follow up on the things that came out of left field as much. But if I did it again, I think I would probe her thinking on tho se thin gs as well. ” With this statement, Betsy agreed with Boophis’s framing of Lela’s ideas as intrinsically valuable. In response, she hoped in the future to listen carefully to all of her students’ ideas, not only those aligned with her canonical understanding of a concept.

## Discussion

In this study, we analyze how a protocol influenced participants’ noticing. The protocol encouraged TCs to notice students’ strengths early and often, and it encoura ged discussion of how they could be leveraged in in struction. We found that framing also affected facilitation: the protocol enabled more collaborative discussion. In the video club, a dominant lens was initially used for noticing. Betsy began the discussion by saying her goal was to show Lela a disappearing puddle in order to “get at evaporation” (a narrow canonical idea). Using settled disciplinary frames can limit teachers’ res ponsiveness. Though these frames could help teachers a nalyze students’ disciplinary understanding (Robertson et al., 2016), they could also “locate students, parti cularly those from nondominant communities, in untenable epistemo logical positions that work against engagement in meaningful science learning” (Bang et al., 2013, p.  302). If teacher educators aim to help TCs to valu e students’ cultural and social worlds as central to sensemakin g (Bang & Medin, 2010; Nasir & Hand, 2006), then we  need to provide TC experiences that disrupt this limiting disciplinary lens. In Betsy’s video club, the protocol supported TCs to more quickly and frequently notice the richn ess of Lela’s ideas. The steps within the protocol provided language and structure for participants to entertain different interpretations of the same moment and to center their noticing on student strengths. With the protocol, w e see robust evidence of TCs disrupting dominant na rratives, creating opportunities for discourse about what students understand. Moreover, when the protocol was used, some of the facilitator’s framing responsibilities were offloaded. As a result, Heather’s participation looked more collaborative; she more often validated and elaborated what other participants noticed. Because the protocol provided  a frame for attending to student’s contributions a nd interpreting them as evidence of understanding, par ticipation, and smartness, Boophis and Betsy centered Le la and her resources in their noticing. This allowed t he discussion to focus on interpretation and respon se (rather than negotiating what to attend to), pushing participants toward a more sophisticated analysis of student thinking (Barnhart & van Es, 2015) and challenging normative  conceptions of what counts as “understanding” or “smartness.” As teacher educators work to embed equity-oriented practices in their courses, it is importan t to consider the impacts of having a facilitator (or th e instructor) enforce a student-strengths framing. The shift in Heather’s role toward a collaborator (rather than facilitator) was also promising, because it suggests that, using a protocol, TCs might be able to facilitate their own fruitful video club discussions.
