# Shared Epistemic Agency in K-12 AI-Infused Lesson Planning: Exploring Human-AI Collaboration in Designing Pedagogically Meaningful Learning Experiences

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how K-12 teachers engage in shared epistemic agency with generative AI tools during the co-construction of AI-integrated lesson plans. Using a mixedcourse. Data sources included 36 lesson plans, and 332 teacher-AI interaction episodes across 68 chatlogs. Rubric-based evaluation scored instructional quality, GenAI integration, and epistemic agency, revealing strong positive correlations among these dimensions. Findings show that teachers demonstrated strong epistemic agency in AI collaboration, actively framing tasks, evaluating AI outputs, and maintaining human control through the guidance of epistemic aims, ideals, dispositions and epistemic metacognitive strategies. Shared epistemic agency emerged when teachers and students co-regulated inquiry and epistemic control was distributed, regulated, and reflected upon across human-AI turns. Qualitative analysis guided by the Epistemic Conjecture Framework (Barzilai  & Chinn, 2024; Sandoval, 2014) showed that lessons integrating AI through purposeful tools, activity structures, and discourse supported reliable epistemic processes  demonstrating that well-designed AI use can cultivate epistemic growth.

Although there is growing interest in preparing teachers for AI integration, significant gaps remain in how this process is understood and supported, especially as teachers express ambivalence about its pedagogical and ethical implications (Bae et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2025). Existing reviews focus on student-centered AI curricula (CasalOtero et al., 2023; Sperling et al., 2024), leaving a deeper question for learning sciences: How do teachers exercise and share epistemic control when designing AI-infused classes and how does that control become visible in the lessons students experience? The vital point of AI integration is not technical fluency but epistemic agency, teachers’ capacity to set epistemic aims, apply standards of knowing, and regulate inquiry toward pedagogically meaningful epistemic performance.

Lesson planning with AI is conceptualize d by shared epistemic agency in teacher-AI collaboration as a dynamic interplay of distribution (who frames problems, evaluates warrants), regulation (how the teacher monitors, critiques, and steers AI), and reflexivity (how the teacher maintains awareness of AI’s limits and preserves professional judgment). This shared agency is not a surrender of authority; it is governance teachers orchestrate the partnership so that AI serves the lesson’s epistemic purposes rather than the other way around. Epistemic agency must be exerted in the design of the class  to promote  students’ epistemic performance, where students encounter tasks that require them to inquire posing questions, to investigate alternatives, to justify with evidence and criteria, to model and explain through constructing representations, as well as reflect on the limits of claims, sources, or tools. The value of human-AI collaboration should be creating instructional sequences, scaffolds, discourse moves, and assessments that invite students to do substantive epistemic work. This study reframes teacher AI literacy as epistemic practice, emphasizing how teachers reason with AI rather than merely reason through AI. It extends the notion of shared epistemic agency to human-AI partnerships, specifying distribution-regulation-reflexivity as core dynamics of epistemic governance in design. The study links process to product by showing how particular epistemic moves in teacher-AI chatlogs correspond to features of lesson quality, using a transparent rubric that emphasizes instructional rigor, the quality and ethics of GenAI integration, and the cultivation of student and epistemic agency. With these aims, the research is conducted to answer the following questions:

(1) How do teachers’ interactions with AI reflect their epistemic agency in governing human-AI

collaboration?

(2) In what ways does shared epistemic agency emerge in AI-integrated lesson design?

(3) What characterizes a pedagogically valuable and meaningful AI-enhanced lesson plan?

1008 Shared epistemic agency in human-AI collaboration Epistemic aims, ideals, and reliable epistemic process in human-AI collaboration Across decades of learning-sciences research, agency links learning, identity, and participation (Engle & Conant, 2002; Scardamalia, 2002). Epistemic agency refers to the capacity of individuals or groups to take ownership of their knowledge-building processes, posing questions, evaluating claims, and reshaping ideas in pursuit of deeper understanding (Scardamalia, 2002; Miller et al., 2018). It emphasizes active engagement with inquiry, critiqu es of assumptions, and the transformation of knowledge practices (Tao & Zhang, 2021).  As characterized in AIR (Chinn et al., 2014, p. 91) framework, epistemic thinking involves three interdependent components: epistemic aim, epistemic ideal, and reliable epistemic processes.  First, the process of lesson planning, teachers’ epistemic aims involve developing a representation of the world, such as models, theories, explanations, and beliefs of the world. While traditionally considered a human capacity, studies suggest that GenAI systems may also function as epistemic agents, challenging conventional notions of how knowledge is produced, validated, and shared (Ponti et al., 2024). The rise of GenAI tools has prompted a reconceptualization of epistemic agency as shared between human and non-human actors, including the underlying algorithms, the data used to train the model, the hardware running the software, and the interface or modality through which the interaction takes place (Wu et al., 2025). While GenAI can simulate inquiry and generate novel content, it lacks self-correction mechanisms, critical reflection, and ethical reasoning (Harari, 2024). Therefore, teachers remain the epistemic leaders, responsible for guiding the interaction and directing cooperative pedagogical  practice (Walton et al., 2025). Prior AI-literacy work emphasizes technical and ethical dimensions (Ng et al., 2021; Long & Magerko, 2020) but often overlooks this epistemic dimension: how teachers make sense of AI’s contributions as knowledge claims.  In teacher-AI collaborative lesson design process, the value of epistemic aims can affect if and how teachers make efforts to initiate their initial prompting with the AI-driven Chatbot. It is the beginning point where AI chatbot comes as a design partner to help test teachers’ design conjectures. Second, epistemic ideals are the standards or criteria that teachers use to evaluate whether their epistemic aims have been achieved and the quality of the resulting presentations. Epistemic ideals are of significance in evaluating AI proposals and critiquing if AI generated suggestions align with the standards and goals. Reliable epistemic processes are the individual and collective strategies or methods that teachers use to try to achieve their epistemic aims in light of the associated ideals. Teacher lesson design with AI: conjecture testing and mediating process Empirical reviews show teachers use AI to brainstorm, generate materials, or simulate student questions (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Sperling et al., 2024), however, much of that work emphasizes efficiency and productivity, not the epistemic reasoning by which teachers think with AI-testing conceptual boundaries, evaluating explanations, and refining pedagogical conjectures. Lesson planning is a process that “gets specific about how it is expected to operate within a particular context” (Sandoval, 2014, p. 22). In lesson design, teachers exert epistemic agency to set epistemic aims, apply standards of knowing, and regulate inquiry as they decide what counts as evidence, which explanations are warranted, and what intellectual moves students should make (Barzilai & Zohar, 2012; Chinn et al., 2011). To improve teachers’ capacity to produce innovative learning environments, generative-AI tools provide a means for teachers to construct knowledge about how designs function in the settings for which they are intended through ongoing experimentation and fe edback (Park et al., 2025). AI-driven Chatbot as a thinking partner, can facilitate teachers to test their design conjectures such as “tools and materials, task structures, participant structures, and discursive practices” (Sandoval, 2014, p. 22) and to examine how these theoretica lly salient features of learning-environment design interact to produce desired instructional outcomes. This study adopts Sandoval’s (2014) conjecture mapping to model teacher-AI planning (see Figure 1). Conjecture mapping makes a high-level conjecture explicit, which is a principled hypothesis about how learning will be supported. Through cycles of design, enactment, analysis, and revision, teachers and AI jointly pursue both the practical improvement of lesson plans and the theoretical refinement of pedagogical understanding. As Sandoval (2014, p.23) emphasizes, “the embodied elements of designs are typically socially and temporally distributed,” and the AI-supported design process exemplifies this princ iple by epistemic commitments-joint pursuit of practical improvement and theoretical refinement through cycles of design-enactment-analysis-revision. Design conjectures as hypotheses about reliable epistemic processes Sandoval’s (2014) conjecture mapping framework conceptualizes learning design as a process of embodying and testing conjectures, if learners engage in specific activities with certain tools, then particular mediating processes should emerge, which in turn lead to desired learning outcomes. These mediating processes represent the cognitive and social mechanisms through which designs are expected to function. However, not all mediating processes are equally epistemically reliable. Barzilai and Zohar (2012) argue that epistemic reliability depends on the 1009 sophistication of participants’ epistemic thinking: how they conceptualize knowledge, evaluate sources, and regulate strategies for knowing. In this sense, a “reliable epistemic process” (Barzilai & Chinn, 2019) is one that fosters justified, evidence-based, and context-sensitive knowledge construction rather than rote acceptance or arbitrary selection. Integrating these perspectives allows us to reinterpret design conjectures through an epistemic lens: teachers’ design choices can be understood as hypothes es about which epistemic processes their students will engage in justification, evaluation, or integration of evidence and how these can be supported through specific tools, task structures, and discourse practices.

In this study, reliable epistemic processes are characterized by three interrelated forms of reasoning. First, justification and evaluation occur when teachers or students explain and warrant their claims based on evidence, reflecting adherence to epistemic ideals of evidence-based reasoning. Second, integration and verification involve combining multiple sources or perspectives, demonstrating epistemic metacognitive knowledge about strategies (EMKS). Finally, critical reflection entails recognizing bias, limitations, or uncertainty in knowledge sources, an aspect linked to epistemic metacognitive knowledge about persons (EMKP). By linking Sandoval’s mediating-process layer with these epistemic constructs, we can trace how AI-integrated lesson designs not only predict learning behaviors but also promote trustworthy epistemic engagement. Teachers’ epistemic agency, therefore, governs which mediating processes become epistemically reliable and which remain superficial or mechanistic.

To address the three research questions, this study employed a multi-layered qualitative analysis integrating interaction analysis, design artifact analysis, and epistemic coding. Teacher-AI chat logs and corresponding AI-infused lesson plans were treated as complementary data sources that reveal both discursive and design-level expressions of epistemic agency. To answer RQ1, teachers’ prompts and follow-up questions were coded for indicators of epistemic aims, epistemic ideals, epistemic dispositions, and epistemic metacognitive knowledge about persons (EMKP) and knowledge about strategies and tasks (EMKS). For RQ2 a pattern analysis was applied to capture how epistemic control was distributed, regulated, and reflected upon across human-AI turns, guided by the “distribution-regulation-reflexivity” pattern. To address RQ3, the analysis was guided by the Epistemic Conjecture Framework which integrates the AIR model (Barzilai & Chinn, 2024) with Sandoval’s

(2014) conjecture mapping. Each teacher’s AI-integrated lesson plan was treated as a design embodiment that

communicates epistemic aims and ideals through specific mediating processes. Using this combined framework, the study examined how lesson features tools and materials, activity structures, and discursive practices supported reliable epistemic processes. These analytic dimensions were cross-referenced with the three-domain rubric to assess whether design conjectures translated into epistemically reliable pedagogical practices. The research team includes former K-12 educators and learning sciences researchers with experience in instructional design, teacher education, and AI literacy. Several authors were involved in the design and facilitation of the graduate course in which the  data were collected, positioning them as both instructors and researchers. To address potential bias, analytic decisions were guided by established epistemic frameworks, and rubric scoring procedures included interrater reliability checks. Reflexive memoing was employed throughout qualitative analysis to surface assumptions about AI use, teacher agency, and pedagogical quality. The team approached generative AI neither as a neutral tool nor as a threat to professional judgment, but as a sociotechnical system requiring careful epistemic governance.

Teachers’ epistemic agency governing teacher-AI collaboration The teacher-AI chatlogs  (Table 1) reveals that teachers actively governed the interaction through distinct manifestations of epistemic agency, in other words, their ability to define epistemic aims, apply evaluative ideals, adopt reflective dispositions, and deploy metacognitive strategies that ma intained human control over meaning-making. Across most interactions, the teachers’ dialogue with AI demonstrates that they positioned the system not as a cognitive replacement but as a responsive instrument for constructing, evaluating, and translating knowledge. Their conversational moves consistently reasserted human judgment, situational awareness, and professional ethics which are indicators that human intelligence remained at  the top of the pyramid of authority. Epistemic aims: Framing the scope of inquiry Teachers’ epistemic aims shaped how knowledge was pursued and construct within the conversation. Rather than asking AI for answers in a passive manner, teachers initiated purposeful, goal-oriented prompts that clearly framed the task as human-led creation. Teacher 3 opening, “I would like to create an 8th-grade math activity for reviewing equations of lines,” teachers’ linguistic framing performed a subtle epistemic move, retaining ownership over the task purpose and boundaries even as AI generated extensive text. This pattern suggests that epistemic aims are not only cognitive goals but also acts of control that determine who holds authorship in the collaboration.  After AI generated activity suggestions, Teacher 3 asked, “Can you include examples that connect to real-world applications, like slope in architecture or design,” a move that redirected AI from generic correctness toward conceptual relevance and epistemic depth.

Epistemic ideals: Demanding credibility and accountability The teachers’ epistemic ideals further reinforced their control by embedding standards for quality and credibility into the dialogue. Teacher 5 further inquiries “Where did you get the information to write this?” which exemplifies a demand for provenance accountability, compelling the AI to expose its epistemic basis rather than assuming neutrality. The AI’s reply, explaining that its information was synthesized from historical sources rather than 1011 drawn from a single citation, shifted the conversation toward transparency and evaluation rather thandeference. Similarly, Teacher 7’s request for a review of her annotated lesson plan invited the AI to respond within her professional standards: alignment with the Digital Promise AI Literacy Framework and APA ethical guidelines. Teachers governed collaboration by requiring AI to answer to disciplinary and ethical ideals, reframing the AI’s outputs as inputs into a human evaluative system.

Epistemic dispositions: Balancing openness and control Teachers’ epistemic dispositions, their habitual stances toward knowledge also reveal a balance between openness and control. Teacher 7 exemplified a coordination mindset when she noted, “I used the AI chatbot to list tools, then did my own research and selected the ones I preferred.” It demonstrates the evaluativist stance: recognizing AI’s generative capacity while asserting her own authority to filter, compare, and validate. In contrast, Teacher 5’s acceptance of the AI-generated “three-paragraph essay o n the American Revolution” without critique illustrated a brief episode of quick closure, where epistemic perseverance was suspended. However, such moments clarify the contours of agency: they show how easily control could shift if human users disengage from reflective scrutiny. Most teachers  demonstrated a critical-inquiry orientation, probing AI reasoning such as “How do you define self-awareness or emotions?” or identifying its limitations such as “You don’t have livedata?” These conversational acts functioned as epistemic guardian, protecting human reasoning from being eroded by AI. Execising epistemic metacognition: Awareness of persons and strategies Through epistemic metacognitive knowledge about persons (EMKP), teachers articulated explicit awareness of the AI’s epistemic status. In multiple exchanges, AI self-disclosure such as “I don’t have personal opinions,” “I can’t access livedata” provided op portunities for teachers to reaffirm human uniqueness in emotional, ethical, and contextual judgment. Teacher 2 repeatedly questioned “If you were to have a personal opinion, how would you formulate it?” which provoked AI to describe the difference between evidence-based reasoning and personal belief, effectively positioning the teacher as a meta-analyst of AI epistemology. Teachers exhibited epistemic metacognitive knowledge about strategies and tasks (EMKS) by regulating how to work with AI efficiently and ethically. They demonstrated strategic prompting by narrowing questions “AI tools for administrative tasks,” “AI tools f or diagnosis” and evaluation strategies through structured rubrics “evaluate tools using the APA checklist”. Teacher 2’s meta-conversation about procrastination, where AI suggested micro-goals and environment adjustments which illustrates how AI can scaffold self-regulated epistemic action without overriding autonomy, and the teacher remained the decision-maker implementing or rejecting suggestions. Across cases, teachers blended balance and reflexivity, alternating between skepticism “I will double-check that” and pragmatic use “I’ll adapt this example for my context”. These patterns show that epistemic agency operates not only at the level of ideas but also at the level of workflow governance, teachers decide how, when, and under what conditions AI contributes. The conversation analysis reveals that teachers’ epistemic agency governs AI collaboration through cycles of framing, probing, filtering, and reflecting. Human users articulate epistemic aims that constrain AI’s role, apply evaluative ideals to judge its output, sustain critical dispositions that resist automation bias, and employ metacognitive strategies that ensure intellectual autonomy. The AI, when properly guided, supports rather than replace these human capacities by making reasoning explicit, offering drafts for critique, or exposing its own limitations. Thus, the human-AI dialogue becomes a site of epistemic co-regulation in which teachers deliberately shape AI participation to amplify human intelligence.

While the degree of agency distribution varied by developmental level and disciplinary context, lessons demonstrated how human actors, teachers and learners, govern the epistemic process, and position AI as a supportive collaborator rather than an autonomous knower. In essence, the epistemic center of gravity remained human, but the means of inquiry expanded through AI mediation. Distribution of epistemic roles In multiple lesson plans, AI was tasked with initiating or producing content while human participants assumed responsibility for evaluation, adaptation, and sensemaking. For example, in the 11th Grade “AI in Journalism” lesson, students compared AI-generated news summaries (via ChatGPT and Gemini) with human-written ones. AI proposed knowledge claims, but students were required to judge, detect bias, omissions, or tone inconsistencies, and discussing how deviations shape public perception. The AI acted as an epistemic provocation that surfaced the need for humandiscernment and enables students to exercise evaluative epistemic agency. Regulation of inquiry: human-guided monitoring of AI interaction and learning processes Shared epistemic agency also appeared through co-regulation where humans and AI jointly monitor and refine learning progress. In the 9th Grade Algebra Regents Review lesson, the diagnostic AI system (IXL) generated individualized data on student weaknesses. However, the teacher and students retained regulatory authority: they analyzed AI feedback, selected which part to visit, and developed personal improvement plans. The AI’s role was analytic (identifying patterns), but human actors interpreted, contextualized, and operationalized that data towards meaningful learning goals.

A similar regulatory dynamic appeared in the professional education webinar “Responsible Use of AI in Mental Health”, where therapists used APA’s Evaluating AI Tools Checklist to assess Blueprint.ai, Greenspace, Rosebud, and MindDoc. The facilitator struct ured reflection activities where participants cross-examined AI-generated features against ethical criteria such as privacy, empathy, and clinical validity. This case exemplifies collective epistemic regulation: the AI generated data and simulated interact ions, but the participants collaboratively determined what constituted trustworthy, ethical knowledge. Across grade levels, epistemic regulation depended on teacher scaffolding that framed how AI should be used. Teachers designed checkpoints (e.g., reflect ion sheets, polls, KWL charts) to ensure that learners’ interpretations, not AI responses guided sensemaking. This human-mediated oversight maintained epistemic integrity by transforming AI outputs into objects of critique rather than unquestioned authority. Reflexivity and ethical awareness: recognizing AI’s limits and reasserting human judgment Epistemic reflexivity refers to the ability to reflect on AI’s role, reliability, and limitations, which is a unifying thread across lessons. In the Middle School “Eclipses” lesson, students created a checklist to evaluate AI chatbot explanations (ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot). They then rated AI responses for accuracy and scientific vocabulary, explicitly identifying strengths, errors, and omissions. This activity transformed students from AI consumers into epistemic evaluators, cultivating critical awareness of how knowledge is generated and verified. In the 9th Grade Earth Science “AI-Generated Infiltration and Runoff” lesson, reflexivity emerged when students critiqued AI-generated images from Adobe Firefly for accuracy and bias. They questioned whether visuals aligned with scientific reality or exaggerated environmental conditions. This meta-conversation about AI representation “Could anything be misleading?” invited students to problematize the trustworthiness of generative systems and the epistemic ethics of visualization.

Finally, in the 1st Grade Alexa lesson, reflexivity was simplified but symbolically powerful: students discussed what it means that “Alexa is a helper, not a human.” This ethical framing introduced the foundational distinction between human understanding and machine output, a key epistemic stance underlying AI literacy. Shared epistemic agency became reflexive when learners questioned AI’s epistemic boundaries and reaffirmed the role of human sensemaking, empathy, and judgment.

Shared epistemic agency unfolded as a dynamic co-enactment of inquiry in which humans and AI complemented one another’s cognitive and epistemic capacities. AI tools contributed speed, visualization, and adaptive feedback, while learners and teachers preserved int entionality, contextual interpretation, and ethical reasoning. Through carefully designed prompts, structured reflection, and guided dialogue, teachers ensured that students actively intervened in, rather than submitted to, AI systems, maintaining human interpretive authority. This relational balance resonates with Barzilai and Zohar’s (2012) conception of epistemic agency as learners’ capacity to set epistemic goals and regulate inquiry, and with Sandoval’s (2014) notion of design conjectures, where learning arises from iterative testing and reflection within knowledge-building environments. Collectively, 1013 these AI-integrated lesson designs illustrate that technology does not diminish epistemic agency; rather, when coupled with purposeful scaffolding for reflexivity, transparency, and critical judgment, it expands learners’ ability to participate in complex,  shared meaning-making processes that strengthen both epistemic and pedagogical integrity.

Pedagogically meaningful AI-infused lesson design When teachers approach AI as designer intentionally using its capabilities to support inquiry and creativity, the value of their own agency is not only preserved but strengthened. This design mindset fosters epistemic ownership and keeps teachers at the helm of meaning-making. As Krawec (2022) reminds us, “How can we unforget the past?”, which is a call to remember teachers’ central role in shaping knowledge traditions, even as we innovate with AI.

Designing for epistemic growth: how AI-enhanced lessons support meaningful learning The epistemic conjecture analysis revealed that pedagogically meaningful AI-infused lessons are characterized by their capacity to align epistemic aims (disciplinary understanding), epistemic ideals (justification, coherence, ethical reflection), and relia ble mediating processes (AI-supported reasoning and structured social validation). Rather than positioning AI as an autonomous authority, effective lesson plans treat AI as an epistemic collaborator which is a mediating tool that amplifies students’ inquir y, reasoning, and reflexivity. The presence of epistemic growth was linked to how the lesson’s tools, activity structures, and discourse patterns were designed to elicit evidence-based reasoning, promote justification, and sustain collective sensemaking. AI as an epistemic mediator In AI in Journalism, Natural Language Processing (NLP) tools such as ChatGPT and Google Gemini mediated epistemic reflection by generating news summaries that students compared with human-written texts. The lesson’s discursive design: “Which one do you trust more? Why?” e licited demands for justification and reflection on epistemic norms such as credibility and bias. AI served as a reliable epistemic mediator: it provided data for demonstrated how AI-facilitated inquiry environments can support epistemic reliability through data-supported explanation. By guiding students to use AI to simulate solar eclipse conditions and test predictions about light and shadow, the teacher enacted a conjecture that AI modeling tools enhance conceptual reasoning when paired with demands for explanation. The discursive norms emphasized “how do you know” and what evidence supports your claim? It encourages students to articulate warrants between observation and inference, an indicator of epistemic growth.

AI for adaptive reflection and self-regulation In the Regents Algebra I Review, AI was used diagnostically to identify conceptual weaknesses and generate individualized feedback. While less dialogic than the journalism lesson, this design supported epistemic regulation where students interpreted AI-generated performance analytics to determine focus areas and self-evaluate their learning progress. The teacher’s scaffolding shifted from direct instruction to meta-epistemic guidance: helping 1014 students interpret AI data critically rather than accepting it unreflectively. This corresponds to the AIR framework (Barzilai & Chinn, 2024) component of epistemic ideals, as students engaged in reflection about reliability, evidence, and self-efficacy. The meaningfulness arose not from AI’s adaptivity alone but from the teacher’s orchestration of epistemic agency, turning machine feedback into human-driven inquiry. Scaffolded epistemic awareness in early learners The Alexa Story Sequencing lesson demonstrated how even young learners in a 12:1:1 special education setting can engage in emergent epistemic agency when AI is embedded in storytelling. By asking Alexa to “read the story” and then sequencing events, students practiced listening comprehension as an epistemic process: distinguishing narrative order, cause-effect, and relevance. Although heavily scaffolded, the activity fostered foundational epistemic dispositions: curiosity, verification, and reflection on how technological “helpers” convey knowledge. The teacher explicitly framed AI as a tool, not an authority, cultivating early epistemic awareness aligned with the ethical dimension of epistemic ideals. Across all analyzed cases, three interrelated design features characterized pedagogically meaningful AIenhanced lessons that foster epistemic growth. First, effective lessons positioned AI as an epistemic partner rather than a source of truth, inviting students to critique, validate, and interpret AI outputs through human reasoning. Second, these lessons established discursive structures that demanded justification and reflection, transforming AI use into a process of epistemic negotiation, such as  in the Eclipse Investigation where students explained and justified their AI-assisted predictions. Finally, teacher mediation ensured epistemic reliability, with educators orchestrating inquiry environments that balanced AI affordances with ethical reflection and disciplinary norms. Shared epistemic agency in K-12 AI-infused lesson planning This study explored how K-12 teachers enact shared epistemic agency in AI-infused lesson design, revealing that teachers do not simply adapt to generative AI tools but actively govern them through deliberate epistemic reasoning. Analysis of 332 teacher-AI interaction episodes and 36 lesson plans demonstrated that teachers framed epistemic aims, applied evaluative ideals, and maintained reflective dispositions that positioned AI as a collaborator rather than an autonomous authority. Through purposeful prompt ing, critical questioning, and metacognitive regulation, teachers preserved intellectual authorship while leveraging AI for idea generation, content refinement, and inquiry design. These findings highlight that epistemic agency is not diminished by AI assistance but reconfigured through human-AI co-regulation, in which teachers orchestrate AI’s participation to sustain meaning-making and ethical judgment.  Shared epistemic agency unfolded through the intertwined processes of distribution, regulation, and reflexivity. Distribution occurred when epistemic roles were shared among teachers, students, and AI, with humans retaining evaluative authority. Regulation was visible in teachers’ strategic monitoring and adaptation of AI-generated outputs to align with curricular and ethical standards. Reflexivity emerged when both teachers and students questioned AI’s reliability, bias, and epistemic boundaries, transforming AI outputs into subjects of critique rather than sources of truth. Theoretical and pedagogical contributions This study extends Barzilai and Zohar’s (2012) and Barzilai and Chinn’s (2024) frameworks by operationalizing epistemic agency as a design conjecture, a hypothesis about how human-AI collaboration can yield reliable epistemic processes. The proposed Epistemic Conjecture Framework integrates the AIR model and Sandoval’s

(2014) conjecture mapping to show how AI can mediate justification, evidence-based reasoning, and ethical

reflection. Pedagogically, the study reframes teacher AI literacy as epistemic practice, learning to reason with AI through inquiry, critique, and reflective design.

Limitation and future research The analysis focused on lesson artifacts and teacher –AI planning interactions rather than enacted classroom practice. Although rubric scoring showed strong reliability, interpretations of epistemic agency remain partly subjective; future work should incorporate classroom enactments and student learning data to strengthen epistemic validity. For research, future studies should examine how teachers’ epistemic agency evolves longitudinally as they iteratively design and enact AI-supported lessons, and how disciplinary contexts shape the forms of shared agency that emerge.
