# Mentorship Approaches for Establishing Communities of Practice in Constructionist Learning Spaces

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Constructionist spaces encourage learners to create artifacts and, in the process, engage with others who hold diverse knowledge, skills, and expertise, thus creating a community of practice. This can lead to the development of and need for mentorship – often taking the form of peer and near-peer mentorship. In this structured poster symposium, we present a collection of implementations and studies within diverse contexts of constructionist learning spaces to discuss how mentorship practices support the development of communities of practice. Drawing from a variety of contexts and approaches, we seek to understand how to implement mentorship, what it means, its benefits, and the different forms it can take.

Drawing from a variety of contexts and approaches, we seek to understand how to implement mentorship, what it means, its benefits, and the different forms it can take. Learners in constructionist environments are encouraged to create public artifacts that are personally meaningful and relevant to their communities (Papert & Harel, 1991). This leads to students working on unique projects that often require not only a variety of technical expertise, but also mentorship and guidance in the process of ideating and refining their project ideas and creations (Bawankule & Tissenbaum, 2025). Mentorship in constructionist spaces is key for learners to continue growing their skills and make meaningful projects that push their boundaries. Papert’s (1980) early ideas of constructionist learning were inspired by his Samba school observations, where people with different experiences and levels of expertise learned with and from each other. Flash forward to the present day, constructionist makerspaces, clubs, and museums bring people with different expertise together to learn and share knowledge and skills towards creating meaningful artifacts. These spaces often move away from “instructionist” teaching approaches which view teachers as the  sole source of knowledge (Tutiyaphuengprasert, 2020), towards models that support broader notions of communities of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Sheridan, 2020), where educators and learners work together towards authentic exploratory 2296 learning. Mentorship in these spaces comes in various forms, whether it is hands-on help in makerspaces, museum facilitation, engineering classrooms, coaches in robotics teams, or peer mentors. These mentors also tend to take on multiple roles, including co-constructing, and learning, often leading to their own growth (Kafai et al., 2008). Providing these varied approaches to mentorship can be beneficial, especially when looking to support students who have previously lacked role models, relatable mentors, and like-minded peers that they feel comfortable learning with (Allen, 2024; Trujillo et al., 2015; Rosales et al., 2024). Fostering relationships where mentors are also relatable and caring can help fill this gap of community, while also providing mentees and mentors with opportunities to grow and develop their skills, confidence, and identities in STEM and com puting (Braswell et al., 2024; Trujillo et al., 2015).  Further, when the mentoring relationship is of the peer-or near-peer nature, it can be particularly effective towards establishing learners’ sense of belonging (Nadelson et al., 2024) and self-efficacy (Sun & Clarke-Midura, 2022; Garcia et al., 2021) within the community of practice. Objectives In this structured poster session, we bring together scholars and practitioners exploring and understanding the role that mentorship plays within constructionist learning environments, to better facilitate such relationships and models in practice. Our col lection of seven posters explores mentorship in diverse settings like informal maker camps, museums, and across institutions like universities and community organizations. Posters in this session look to understand how to support and scaffold mentorship pr actices and develop sustainable communities of practice. Authors in this session also advance diverse approaches such as peer feedback and artifact construction as constituting and supporting mentorship. They explore distributed mentorship across various levels of learners, and within the context of game-based learning. Our collection of posters is interdisciplinary, ranging from arts and creative writing to game-based learning, robotics, and maker camps. ## Implications

Through this poster session, we will engage scholars and practitioners in discussion about the nature of peer and near peer mentorship within constructionist learning environments. We seek to understand what constitutes mentorship within these hands-on learning environments, and how the members within the community of practice

– including the mentors and mentees – can benefit from such relationships. Through participation in this session,

attendees will further their understanding of how learning and community are shaped by these models of mentorship, and how we as scholars and practitioners within the field can contribute to better facilitating and establishing such communities of practice. We also anticipate drawing from feedback and participant discussions during the session to submit a special issue on peer and near-peer mentorship to the Journal of the Learning Sciences.

Mentors’ computational action experiences in a Connected Spaces summer camp Ashita Bawankule, Mike Tissenbaum, Santiago Ospina-Tabares, David Hopping, Blake Danzig, Emmy Semprun, J.K. Voorhis, Nathan Holbert Computational action (Tissenbaum et al., 2019) suggests that computing experiences for students should enable them to develop their identities associated with computing and put these identities into action in ways that are meaningful and relevant to them and their communities (Bawankule & Tissenbaum, 2023) rather than solely focusing on technical skill development. Near-peer mentorship can have positive influences on students’ identities and sense of belonging, especially for students from communities that are underrepresented (Rosales et al., 2024; Trujillo et al., 2015). Additionally, near-peer mentors in STEM fields report positive impacts on their confidence and persistence (Garcia et al., 2021, Ju et al., 2023). As part of the Connected Spaces 2025 summer camp, we implemented a model in which high school students (some who had attended previous camp years) served as near-peer mentors for middle school students. 2297 They supported students through the two weeks of camp, to develop meaningful and relevant mobile apps. The near peer mentors participated in a day-long training session to prepare for their role and engaged in a group debrief session with the primary facilitators at the end of each day. In this poster, we will discuss the experiences of our mentors in the camp, specifically investigating how their computational action – computational identity, digital empowerment, and computational design thinking – was influenced. Each of our mentors experienced the mentorship process differently. For example, those who had attended our camps previously as students took a different approach compared to those who were meeting us for the first time. Some mentors designed apps alongside the students, and some mentors worked directly with mentees who were closer in age, taking on the role of a close friend. Some mentors took on responsibilities like taking photos of the group to post on sharing platforms, while others suggested displaying previous year posts on the platforms to further establish a continuous community of practice. As part of this session, we will explore how these varying intersectional factors played a role in the mentors’ experiences and interactions within the camp, both as mentors and as learners.

We will draw from video and audio analysis of the mentors’ training day in conjunction with data from the camp to capture their experiences through narrative anecdotes. We will also utilize post-camp one-on-one and focus group mentor interviews, and pre-and post-camp surveys to refine our understanding of those experiences. As participants in this poster session, we anticipate facilitating discussion in and around which factors have the potential to influence mentors’ experiences within a computational action driven maker camp. These discussions will further our collective understandings on how to design and foster these experiences and environments. Lessons from Connecting Dots: Constructionist mentorship and communitybuilding at scale Francesca Fiore, David M Zikovitz, Alberto Montresor Constructionist spaces thrive when learners can create personally meaningful projects while engaging in supportive communities of practice. Yet sustaining these communities, particularly on a larger scale, requires more than providing tools or workshops; it demands structures for mentorship that allow knowledge, practices, and identities to circulate across levels of expertise (Papert, 1991; Meyerhoff and Strycharz, 2013; Lave & Wenger, 1991).

Our work with the Connecting Dots Learning Lab offers one such model. Originally emerging from the UniTrento FabLab, the initiative has expanded into a regional hub that integrates university research, nonprofit organizations, and schools. Within this distributed ecosystem, mentorship plays a central role: university students, recent graduates, and teachers act as near-peer mentors who guide younger learners while simultaneously refining their own pedagogical identities (Fiore et al., 2025).

The model rests on three pillars:

1. Distributed mentorship across roles  – Students, early-career educators, and experienced teachers all

participate as both learners and mentors, creating a layered system of guidance and reciprocity.

2. Partnership with nonprofits for reach and adaptability  – Organizations such as Glow and Verona

Fablab extend activities into underserved schools and communities, ensuring that mentorship opportunities are accessible and contextually relevant.

3. Institutional anchoring for sustainability – The university provides formal teacher training, systematic

evaluation, and integration with national policies, embedding constructionist practices in structures that outlast short-term projects.

Scaling this model has already yielded tangible outcomes: in the 2024-25 academic year, the collaboration delivered over 8,000 hours of student labs and 2,500 hours of teacher professional development. These activities not only introduced learners to coding, making, and STEAM practices, but also cultivated mentorship dynamics where teachers became multipliers, and young educators transitioned from mentees to mentors. For replication, two design principles are especially important. First, mentorship should be conceived as a reciprocal process —mentors learn and grow alongside mentees, reinforcing a culture of shared ownership. Second, sustainability depends on multi-actor collaboration, where universities, nonprofits, and schools each play complementary roles. By combining grassroots flexibility with institutional legitimacy, constructionist mentorship can take root at scale without losing its participatory spirit. This poster invites discussion on how constructionist labs and learning spaces can adapt this layered mentorship model to their own contexts, and how cross-sector partnerships can help replicate not only the activities, but also the community structures that sustain them. 2298 Distributed mentorship in a constructionist critical machine learning gamebased learning environment Golnaz Arastoopour Irgens, Omer Zahid, Atefeh Behboudi In constructionist learning environments, learners play, tinker, and build computational objects in ways that matter to them and externalize their learning (Papert, 1980). These actions lead to multiple and diverse pathways of exploration. These pathways a re even more expansive when the design of the learning environment embraces multiple epistemologies and heterogeneity of culture (Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003). How do facilitators help and mentor students so that they reach their goals and also reach disciplinary content goals? One approach is to consider distributed mentoring (Nash, 2017), which suggests that young people learn and grow through interactions not just with an individual mentor but with a mentoring system. No one mentor is key; guidance and feedback come from different people in various contexts, such as instructors, coaches, and peers, as well as through knowledge-building and information communication tools. Thus, mentorship is distributed among people, places, and tools.

Drawing on the concept of distributed mentorship, SPOT is a hybrid physical/digital role-playing game in which students role-play as agents for a top-secret organization called Solving Problem Of Tomorrow (SPOT). Through SPOT, elementary-school students immerse themselves in a story to explore algorithm bias and program their own machine learning robots. In the game, students travel to the future to investigate and solve the problems of the technology they use and bring back information and knowledge they acquire to solve the problems of the present day. Students follow a progressive level, in which they, as agents, complete each level to earn a badge and a power-up that takes them to their next mission in the next level. Throughout the game, the child agent  is introduced to other SPOT digital agents/characters as mentors, and experiences a series of training to understand the concepts of algorithms and machine learning technologies. By the end of playing SPOT, students will be able to program their machine learning based robots for social good (Adisa et al., 2023). Through iterative design, students externalize both their computational and critical knowledge as they create a computational object that reflects their identities, cultures, and histories, while exploring the tensions when the tool or algorithm limits how they can express these values (Arastoopour Irgens et al., 2022). In SPOT, distributed mentoring happens in multiple forms: multiple adult facilitators, peers, ethnically and gender diverse characters in the game, feedback from training and testing their machine learning models, and feedback from the block-based programming that requires them to debug their code and their thinking. Drawing on a recent implementation of SPOT with over 200 students, we explain the distributed mentoring system in SPOT and how this mentoring system facilitated students’ critical computing engagement and learning. Examples include how students interacted with the instructor and researchers to find a variety of pictures for their image classification to have a fair model, how students reacted positively to diverse characters in the game, and how they discussed with their peers about the issues they see in their community that could be addressed with a machine learning robot.

Peer feedback on creative work as mentorship Naomi Thompson, Mary DiCioccio, Gabrielle Graham, Jay Barber In an interdisciplinary out-of-school workshop, we designed an experience to support students’ engagement at the intersection of creative writing and the science of carbon. Such intersections of the arts and the sciences have potential to support deeper and more meaningful learning (e.g., Peppler & Wohlwend, 2018; Mejias et al., 2021). This writing workshop was built around the perspective that writing/rewriting and drafting/redrafting are tinkering processes wherein writers negotiate and renegotiate with w ords, narrative, tone, character, and more. Although writing is less often discussed through the lens of constructionism, we view the workshop as a constructionist learning environment due to this tinkering (e.g., Turkle & Papert, 1990; Thompson, 2023). In  particular, peer feedback sessions stood out as uniquely interesting for further exploration. In this poster, we will dive more deeply into the peer feedback data from this workshop to better understand whether and how peer feedback on creative work can serve as peer mentorship in a constructionist environment.

For this poster, we will pull out moments of data that centered around peer feedback. This includes moments of video recorded small group interactions, video recorded whole-group feedback roundtables, and participant reflections on peer feedback from interviews. The workshop took place over eight sessions with eleven participants ages 14-18. All sessions were video recorded and additional data included pre-and post-interviews 2299 and images of the original artifacts produced by the participants. A fuller account of this study can be found elsewhere (Thompson et al., 2025).

Here, we give a brief preview of findings from across multiple data sources. Interview insights During the post-interviews, we asked participants to reflect upon their experiences giving and receiving feedback on their writing with peers. Several of the youth expressed that they were nervous to give feedback to others, feeling unsure that they had useful insight to offer. Along these lines, some youth expressed disappointment in the lack of critique that they got. However, many appreciated getting feedback on their projects from peers, with some saying that the feedback helped them make their stories better, giving them new ideas and ensuring that their ideas were conveyed as intended. While this may suggest that some of the youth were reluctant to act as mentors to one another, it was an overall positive experience for those both providing and receiving feedback.

Video recordings The recorded interactions show us that while peer feedback was meaningful, we could more intentionally scaffold how to give feedback and how to build on others’ ideas. In one episode, we can see that while participants were given explicit advice on providing feedback, some still struggled to produce useful ideas.

This work shows that while peer feedback on writing is a valuable form of mentorship in interdisciplinary spaces, more explicit scaffolding could help youth feel better prepared to both provide and receive feedback. Our poster will also discuss future design iterations meant to implement these findings. Artifacts as a fulcrum for relational connection with mentors Colby Tofel-Grehl, Sarah Bradenl, Edgar Diaz, Gulnar Ozbekbayeva Close relationships with mentors can support young people in developing their own connection and identification with learning (Nasir & Cooks, 2009). Notably, near-peer mentors offer a particularly compelling opportunity for connection that showcases the possibilities for the young person as reflected in the success of the near-peer mentor. This uniquely influential relationship can be especially important within STEM disciplines, where a “chilly climate” is often reported for those who find themselves historically underrepresented across disciplines. But how are these relationships formed and fostered? In what ways do constructionist fundamentals scaffold or hinder the development of these relationships?

Constructionism argues that humans learn best when engaged in the design and building of tangible objects. Not only do learners enjoy learning more when engaged in constructionist learning activities (Tep & Tanprasertkul, 2022), but they also learn more core classroom content than peers who engaged in business-asusual learning (Tofel-Grehl et al., 2023). Prior research reports the critical importance of artifacts and their nature as permanent objects in building meaningful connections outside the classroom (Howell et al. 2016) and bridging contexts within it (Ball et al., 2020). It is precisely the tangible nature of constructionist learning and artifacts that frames this poster. By engaging in the hands-on process of construction, we believe that mentors a nd learners actually co-construct both the artifact of focus and the relationship between themselves. Through the construction of the artifact, space is made to construct, negotiate, and develop the relationship between the mentee and the mentor. Thus, constructionism anchors learners and gives durability to the liminal spaces. With this understanding of the importance of constructionist artifacts for improving learning outcomes and building community, we hypothesize that such artifacts would also support and foster emerging relationships between youth and mentors. Over the course of 4 years, we engaged over 20 mentors in working with hundreds of high school students. Mentors were college students at the undergraduate and graduate levels who reported an interest or focus on engineering and identified as Latinx.  We share findings from this work in supporting asset-based teaching trained mentors (Wilson Lopez & Acosta-Feliz, 2021) to engage with Latinx high school students to respond to multiple engineering design challenges. We share findings about the ways in which the constructionist nature of the engineering design cycle created opportunities for the development of rich, meaningful, and durable mentoring relationships. Findings indicate that constructionism is important because of the durability of the object and the ability of those objects to foster spaces of discourse, connection, and community.

Materials as co-mentor for arts-integrated robotics education Anna Keune, Harper Staples, Miriam Voß Recent perspectives reimagine mentorship as a reciprocal, context-dependent partnership (McGee, 2016), paralleling developments in arts-based education that emphasize creativity, collaboration, and socio-emotional learning (Eisner, 2003). In STEM contexts,  integrating the arts and introducing novel material forms candeepen 2300 engagement and inclusion (Peppler et al., 2021). Socio-material approaches in the learning sciences further suggest that tangible materials do not merely support learning but actively shape what can be learned and how, positioning them as participants in t he learning process (e.g., Seitama-Hakkarainen et al., 2023). Yet, little is known about how materials may function as co-mentors within STEM learning environments. Addressing this gap, this study examines how materials actively shape and participate in mentorship and collaborative practices within arts-integrated robotics education.

Building on socio-material approaches merged with constructionist perspective (e.g., Keune & Peppler, 2019), which advanced the idea of materials-to-develop-with, highlighting h ow project, spaces, and materials mutually co-develop to foster powerful educational future pathways, this poster examines work with the project manager (Author 3) at an  education lab of the Deutsches Museum in Germany to understand how materials constitute mentorship relationships. Building on constructionist perspectives, especially focusing on youth-led design aligned with core robotics ideas, we collaboratively designed an arts-based robotics project to integrate into the lab’s curriculum. We focused on supporting young people’s understanding and experiences of belonging and inclusion in such spaces, and to which all individuals involved contributed specific expertise (i.e., facilitation skills, knowledge of the space, technological know-how). To analyze the role materials played in shaping the comentoring relationship, we drew on field notes and recordings of online exchanges and in-person meetings. The mentoring was examined through a posthumanist lens, guided by the idea of learning as a process of change that expands the possible tangible arrangements of processes, techniques, and components that make up the conceptual performance under investigation (Keune, 2024). This led us to focus on material-related ruptures to human intentions and routines and how that fostered mentoring. A key outcome of this project is the pivotal role that tangible objects played in driving the co-mentor relationship. This dynamic emerged through a process of designing and implementing an activity in which young people envisioned inclusive robotics learning spaces by making tangible models and augmenting them with generative AI. First, we conducted an inventory of the robotic lab’s existing materials and a series of conversations to identify typical materials that served as references for the robotics lab and as drivers to expand existing robotics activities at the lab through arts-based approaches. We collaboratively selected Lego as the material for designing an activ ity for young people to construct inclusive robotics spaces. By intentionally removing Lego from its routine uses in robotics activities and introducing less conventional Lego pieces, we invited participants to reimagine what could be built and who could belong in robotics. Here, materials created opportunities for our mentorship team to critically reflect on how they are used and how they shape social meaning and participation. For one, the student-created artefacts (i.e., envisioned inclusive robotics learning environments) inspired other education lab activities, functioning as materials-to-develop-with (Keune & Peppler, 2019), where learning activities were co-developed using these  materials in ways that were novel in the lab. The co-development expanded beyond the robotics-lab activity. The fact that the artifacts were physically present in the space prompted conversations with others and inspired new program ideas that the lab implemented. Working closely with materials also made the institutional red tape surrounding access to resources visible. For example, when we attempted to purchase a high-end Lego set, well-suited for both exploratory and curricular use, administrative rules around budget allocations prevented a timely purchase. This revealed how institutional systems shape what learning is materially possible, directly impacting young people. Materials became institutional actors, leading us to explore collectively how to make material possibilities viable within institutional administration. Overall, the research suggests that integrating novel, arts-based materials into learning contexts not only benefits students’ socio-emotional developmen t (Halverson et al., 2023) but also supports professional relationships among adult co-mentors. The research also extends the theoretical idea of materials-to-develop-with by examining it from an administrative perspective, showing that material realities and needs also shape orientations toward policies and regulations. Using Legos in new ways called us to question how materials are allowed to exist in mentorship relationships across university and museum settings, and to envision new ways that would foster learning at the intersection of the arts, robotics, and civic learning.

This work was supported by the TUM Institute for Advanced Study (TUM-IAS) and the European Union’s Horizon Europe program through The Cultural Literacies’ Value in Europe (CLiViE) project No. 101132285. 2301 Deconstructing the Samba School: Relatability as a designed mechanism for near-peer mentorship Jody Clarke-Midura, Lingna Xu Papert’s (1980) vision of the Samba School has long served as the constructionist ideal for a community of practice: an organic community where learning is authentic and purposeful, where mentorship is not a formal role but an organic and spontaneous interaction between novices and experts, all engaged in a shared, meaningful activity. Papert used this metaphor to critique the dissociated learning of traditional schools. Yet, while we often invoke this ideal, the specific mechanisms, particularly regarding mentorship, that make such communities effective often remain assumed. How, then, can we move from this inspiring metaphor to deliberate, evidencebased practices for fostering such learning communities? We addressed this question by isolating and empirically testing a key component of the Samba School model: the power of the near-peer relationship. We designed a summer camp where middle school youth learn to make personally meaningful mobile apps, mentored by high school youth who were themselves near-novices. This structure allowed us to investigate the social dynamics that underpin an effective mentor-mentee relationship, focusing specifically on how a mentor’s relatability shaped a mentee’s self-efficacy. We define relatability as the social-emotional bond that forms from a mentee’s perception of shared experience and struggles of making apps with their mentor (Sun & Clarke-Midura, 2022), and we argue it is this connection that makes role modeling effective  in a mentorship. Testing this concept, our analysis of pre-post survey data (n=185) found that mentor relatability was a significant predictor of mentee post-self-efficacy (controlling for pre-self-efficacy), the core belief that "I cando this” (β =.71,  Β =.68, p <.001). Our qualitative data revealed how mentors built this connection: by sharing stories of their own coding struggles while making apps (highlighting shared experience and making success seem attainable) and making connections by sharing apps designed around personal interests (emotional availability) during the app-making process. Our finding that designed relational practices can foster relatability highlights a critical tension. Papert’s Samba School is an emergent culture that "was not made. It happened” (Papert, 1980).  In contrast, our camp is a structured, replicable intervent ion, an "artificially maintained oasis" (Papert, 1980).  Our mentors were trained using a pedagogical framework that operationalized sources of self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997) into specific relational practices. This presents a challenge: How do we design learning environments that retain the spirit of the Samba School without becoming overly prescriptive?

Based on our findings, we propose three design principles for fostering relatability: (1) Anchor Relational Work in Authentic, Shared Struggle: Design constructionist tasks where challenges and debugging are not seen as obstacles, but as primary sites for mentorship. The shared struggle to solve a problem is the context where mentors can share their own vulnerability and build connections. (2) Train for Relational Practices, Not Just Content Expertise: Mentor training should focus on pedagogical practices that build connection, such as sharing personal stories of failure and success, active listening, and finding common ground outside the immediate task.

(3) Design for Relatability: Actively structure the environment to create the conditions for relatability  to emerge.

This includes intentional near-peer matching, creating low-stakes social time, and explicitly encouraging mentors to share their own learning journeys, not just their knowledge. We argue the path forward lies not in attempting to replicate an organic culture, but in the intentional design of its social architecture. Our work contributes to this session by offering an empirically grounded framework for understanding and designing for relatability. Our findings suggest that relatability is a foundational social mechanism that enables other forms of mentorship to succeed. To create authentic, modern "Samba Schools," we must move beyond simply placing novices and experts together and i nstead adopt a theoretically grounded, evidence-based approach to fostering the powerful social relationships, which are essential for building resilient communities of practice.
