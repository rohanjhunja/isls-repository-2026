# Diving into Joyful Disciplinary Investigation as a Practice of Resistance and Healing

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Experiences of joy can foster learning, connection, and a sense of hope in unjust and dehumanizing environments. Schooling is increasingly organized toward different ends, prioritizing “accountability” and standardization over choice, creativity, and joy. This paper shares our emerging analysis, examining the work of two justice-focused teachers in an alternative school environment, where trends toward checklists and pressure to “earn” credits are particularly pervasive. Drawing from a spiritual and political articulation of joy as involving not only fun but also empathy, empowerment, and engagement with the world, we investigate how these teachers’ repeated choice to dive into disciplinary engagement functioned to bring these multiple dimensions of joy into their work, thus becoming a practice of healing and resistance.

## Introduction

Recent scholarship has drawn attention to the role of joy in supporting learning and in maintaining a sense of hope and possibility in an unjust world. The experience of joy in a learning environment can foster deeper engagement with academic content as we ll as a greater sense of connection and belonging (Parks, 2020; Quigley & Herro, 2016). Additionally, fostering joy can help nourish teachers’ spirits in the exhausting work of trying to transform unjust systems while working within them. The need for joy can feel particularly acute in the current climate, where trends toward greater “accountability” and st andardization in schools have contributed to a grow ing atmosphere of pressure and anxiety for teachers and  for young people (Horn, 2018; Nichols & Berliner, 2008). Instead of turning toward joy, systems of schooling often respond to evidence of student or community struggles by intensifying pressure, for instance by increasing oversight or emphasizing a small number of narrow measures of success for schools, teachers, and students (Ana gnostopoulous, 2003; Darling-Hammond, 2004). These dynamics affect the experiences of human beings in schools, as pressure to perform increases and space and time for joy, play, or rest decrease, with the cost of these dynamics often falling most heavily on  urban communities of color. This paper is motivated by a deep commitment to attending to experiences of joy in schools, both because we believe that each human being has a fundamental right to meaningful experiences, and because these experiences matter for students’ and teachers’ learning and wellness (Ladson-Billings, 2021).

In this paper, we examine the work of two teachers in an urban alternative school who choose, over and over, to prioritize joy for themselves and their students. Against the backdrop of highly constrained options commonplace in today’s schools, we see this practice of repeatedly choosing to bring joy into their work as an act of both healing and resistance. We explore the questio n: How do teachers bring joy into their work togeth er to support their own healing and to resist dehumanizing conditions of schooling?

## Background

Working to support wellness for both teachers and y oung people while working within dehumanizing syste ms can be a lonely and exhausting task for educators. While increasing attention has been paid in recent decades to the importance of supporting students’  holistic wellness in schools, for instance through support for Social and Emotional Learning, much less attention has been paid to such efforts for teachers (Garcia, 2019). Without space and support for the ir wellness and healing, teachers often experience psychological challenges and are less able to be fully present for their students (hooks, 2003). These issues are particularly acute for teachers  who love and support students in communities where extreme loss and trauma are prevalent (Garcia, 2019). Recent scholarship has  focused on different aspects of supporting  wellness and healing for teachers, including training in mindfulness practices (Flook et al., 2013), creating spaces of solidarity and connection (Martinez et al., 2016; Pizarro, 2017), and fosteri ng time and space for vulnerability and grief (Garc ia, 2019). While this research has helped to elaborate conditi ons that support teacher wellness, less attention has been paid to how teachers collectively work to support their own  wellness and healing in their interactions. In this paper, we investigate ways in which teachers  in their interactions with each other, attend to th eir own healing as they resist dehumanizing conditions of schooling. In par ticular, we focus on how teachers  bring joy into their work together as a practice of both healing and resistance.

Parks’ (2020) article about bringing joy into elementary mathematics classrooms quotes the Dalai Lama and Desmond Tutu’s The Book of Joy, “Being joyful is not just about having more fun. We ’re talking about a more empathetic, more empowered, even more spiritua l state of mind that is totally engaged with the wo rld” (2016, p.  63). In this paper, we lean on these two political and spiritual wor ld leaders as we investigate how teachers can bring joyfulness into their work throu gh (1) fun; (2) empathy, or connection with other humans; (3) empowerment in their work; and (4) engagement, or personal connection, with the larger world.

## Preliminary analytic methods

We read through field notes for the 12 conversations Evie observed and recorded, looking for moments in which Jill and Zeze’s conversations shifted from absence of joy to its presence. Borrowing from The Book of Joy,  we considered joy to be present when the first of the following elements was evident alo ng with one or more of the others: (1) fun, as indicated through facial expres sions, body language, and expressions such as laugh ter; (2) empathy, evidenced by talk suggesting connection wi th other humans; (3) empowerment, evidenced by teachers engaging in creative work; and (4) engagement with the world, evidenced by teachers’ talk suggesting connection with aspects of the world outside of the teachers and their immediate environment. We analyzed interactions that accompanied these turns toward joy, identifying eac h of these four elements and considering how the te achers introduced them and how they were related to each other.

## Emerging findings

We identified multiple conversations between Jill and Zeze in which evidence of joy was at first missing and then the conversation turned toward joy. We noticed a few ways in which Jill and Zeze made this turn, one of which we investigate further in this paper:   choosing to engage together in a disciplinary inves tigation. We use one conversation that took place on October 7 as an exa mple of how engaging in disciplinary investigation brought joy into their work.

As the meeting began, Jill explained to Evie, “I’m feeling so sad... The last 3 days have been so fric kin rough. I’m just feeling… yeah, just feeling that. Just feeling that a lot.” She and Zeze went on to talk about feeling disappointed and let down by their administration’s  lack of action to support a safe environment for s tudents. They also talked about significant personal challen ges their students were facing and said that these emotional challenges impaired their ability to feel okay and to engage productively in their work. Jill said, “That’s a lot, and trying to hold all that at the same time that we’re in curriculum design? They’re not at odds, but it’s just like a lot of energy. It’s a lot of energy.” At another moment, she named an interaction she had with a student as “heartbreaking” and continued, “ So my heart is just like… breaking for them. It’s a  lot.” Zeze shared that the frustrations related to interacting with administrators “amplified [his] nervous system” so much that he needed to leave class to walk around to “just release it.” During this talk, sadness and exhaustion were palpa ble. Beyond the absence of fun, other elements of joy were also missing. Jill and Zeze’s talk suggests a lack of empathy or connection with school administrators, with so much resulting frustration that Zeze needed  to leave class to “release” it. Their descriptions of administrative decisions and “heartbreaking” studen t experiences about which they had no control  indicate a missing sense of empowerment. There was nothing in this early part of the conversation that suggests a  sense of engagement or personal connection with the world.

About 30 minutes into the conversation, Jill reques ted a change of focus, deliberately shifting the conversation toward something more generative.

Jill: But, I’d like to shift gears because it will make me feel better. Zeze: Let’s do it.

Jill: (to Evie) Because after our time yesterday… we had our very  exciting, ‘oh my god we can build melatonin’ moment. That was fun. (long laugh) From here the pair, along with Evie, shifted into 7 0 minutes of what Jill later referred to as “geekin g out.” They explored ideas related to the chemical m akeup of melatonin and other human hormones, theori es tracing the origin of the four elements that make up all human hormones (hydrogen, nitrogen, carbon, and oxygen) to exploding stars, and ways in which models of DNA might be constructed from these four elements. During this extended period of disciplinary investigation, Jill, Zeze, and Evie were animated and visually engaged, laughing and pantomiming the processes the y were talking about (such as clapping hands togeth er to pantomime fusion, or the “smooshing” together of two hydrogens in an exp loding star to form helium). Their vocal tone and body language ma de clear the presence of fun, and their exploration of the chemical and astronomical origins of human life suggests deep engagement with the world. Their exploration of these ideas was interspersed w ith talk about the kinds of experiences they might create for students to engage with this content, in dicating the presence of empathy with their student s. At one point, Jill described her own “utterly uninterestin g” experiences learning about similar Chemistry con tent in school and went on to contrast this with the kind o f experience that might be available in a task they  were brainstorming that involved building models of elem ents and combining them to mo del processes theorized to have taken place in exploding stars.

Jill: But I can imagine that if I made a bunch of hydrogens and then sat there and blobbed them together and then was like ‘Oh crap, I made helium,’ Zeze: I just made helium, yeah.

Jill: and then I took those things and smooshed them together and now I’ve made frickin DNA. That’s pretty cool.

Zeze: I agree. Yes.

Jill: That’s pretty fun.

Through imagining the experiences students might ha ve in this nascent modeling task, Jill empathized with them as learners, considering how they  might experience fun and engagement with the world outside of themselves. Throughout this talk, Jill and Zeze wer e engaged in the creative work of curriculum design, evidencing a sense of empowerment that was absent earlier in the conversation. Our analysis of this extended conversation  highlights the deeply intertwined nature of the elements of joy. As an example of this, we share a brief exchan ge in which Jill and Zeze brought together these el ements as they considered how their student Austin might expe rience this learning. Jill held up a picture of an exploding star and said, “Right, because ultimately this is w here you came from.” With a facial expression of am azement, she continued, “I mean what?” She gestured with her hands an explosion coming from her head and made a sound of an explosion before saying, “Austin’s mind is going to be blown.” Zeze laughed with her, responding “I came from this exploding star.” In this brief exchange, Jill and Zeze were having fun at the same time they  were empathizing with Austin, imagining the fun, or sense of wonder, that he might experience through deep engagement with the world. Their joyful interchange  was part of their empowered, creative work of desi gning learning experiences for students.

## Discussion

As we investigated how Jill and Zeze brought joy in to their conversations when it was missing, a few p atterns emerged, one of which we investigated in this paper: the repeated choice to engage together in discipl inary investigations. In the conversation examined here, the teachers began their time together by expressing significant emotional challenges of their work. After expressing these challenges to Evie, Jill requested to “shift gears because it will make me feel better.” The pair shifted into  a prolonged disciplinary investigation, which, our analysis reveals, ushered in multiple dimensions of joyfulne ss, leading to a much more generative conversation. As we continue our work, we plan to investigate how Jill and Zeze repeatedly made the choice to engage in jo yful disciplinary investigation, and how making this  choice again and again functioned as a practice that supported their own healing and furthered their resistance to dehumanizing aspects of schooling. We also plan to investigate systematically other choices these teachers made in their work to shift toward joy in their interactions. For instance, we noticed that telling stories about their students’ ingenuity, creativity, and particular ways of being, which they did frequently, seemed to represent another move toward joy, brin ging in increased expressions of fun, empathy, empowerment,  and engagement. We look forward to investigating t hese patterns more fully.

We hope that our investigations into these teachers ’ joyful practices can contribute to building  our collective knowledge about the interactional work teachers engage in to foster  healing and resistance. Additionally, we hope this work can eventually inform the design of systems and structures that nurture teachers’ joy as an important aspect of creating possibilities for healing and resistance.
