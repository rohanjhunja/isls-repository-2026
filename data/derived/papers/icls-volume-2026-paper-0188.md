# “I was so confused about the gases unit until now”: Mapping the Dynamics of Epistemic Affects and Cognitive Processing in Learning Gas Laws on YouTube

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
YouTube has emerged as a key space for informal science learning, where learners actively engage with complex ideas outside formal instruction. This exploratory study examines how epistemic affects and cognitive processing interact during science learning on YouTube, focusing on the topic of Gas Laws. Using search terms from Ambitious Science Teaching and the Next Generation Science Standards, 131 videos and 18,290 viewer comments were analyzed through a human-in-the-loop, AI-assisted coding process based on epistemic affect s and Bloom’s taxonomy. Preliminary findings indicate that curiosity, vexation, and joy often co-occurred with higher-order cognitive processes such as analyzing and evaluating, suggesting that both positive and negative emotions can stimulate deeper reasoning and conceptual understanding. The study underscores the importance of recognizing learners’ affective expressions as integral to their cognitive engagement and offers new insights for designing emotionally and intellectually supportive environments in informal digital science learning.

Science learners are increasingly using YouTube videos for learning in both formal and informal settings (Breslyn & Green, 2022). Learners can select, pause, rewind, or skip content at their own pace, creating a dynamic, self-directed learning ecosystem.  This self-directed engagement with educational content creators provides a unique window into the authentic learning process, one that is often hidden in formal academic settings. Understanding how this informal learning unfolds is critical to appreciating  the role that digital media plays in modern science literacy.

The journey of learning, especially in a self-directed space like YouTube, is not merely a cognitive exercise but is deeply intertwined with emotional experiences, particularly epistemic affects —the feelings associated with knowing and understanding (Jaber & Hammer, 2016). Understanding the dynamics of epistemic affects and cognitive processing in these informal learning environments is crucial for comprehending how students make sense of complex scientific concepts.  Epistemic affects such as confusion, curiosity, frustration, and the excitement  of breakthrough moments are not simply byproducts of learning but are integral forces that shape the cognitive processing of new information (Agustian et al., 2025; Pekrun, 2006). Research demonstrates that both emotions and cognition, affecting one another, play integral roles in scientific sensemaking (Vilhunen et al., 2022). The intertwined nature of emotional and cognitive processes during learning suggests that mapping these dynamics can reveal critical insights into how students move from states of confusion to clarity when engaging with challenging scientific content on YouTube. By examining the epistemic emotional trajectories students experience while learning gas laws through online videos, we can better understand the affordances and constraints of informal digital learning spaces and develop more effective pedagogical approaches that support both cognitive understanding and positive emotional engagement with science. To this end, this proposal aims to answer one research question:  What patterns of epistemic affects and cognitive processing are reflected during science learning on YouTube?

## Literature review

Epistemic affects in science learning The process of learning is often viewed through a predominantly  cognitive lens, focusing on the mechanisms of information processing, memory, and problem-solving (Eylon & Linn, 1988). However, a growing body of research highlights the integral role of emotions, particularly epistemic affects, in shaping these cognitive activities (e.g., Agustian et al., 2025; Jaber & Hammer, 2016; Muis et al., 2015). Epistemic affects  are emotions that relate directly to the process of learning and knowing, such as curiosity, confusion, surprise, and joy  (Muis et al., 2015). These epistemic affects are not peripheral to learning but are deeply embedded within the process of 1422 inquiry and knowledge construction. In science education, where learners grapple with complex, abstract, and often counterintuitive concepts, these emotions are particularly salient. Empirical studies have demonstrated how epistemic affects operate as both indicators and drivers of engagement in science learning. For example, Agustian et al. (2025) found that students experience a wide range of epistemic affects (e.g., curiosity, frustration, joy, confidence, pride, and humility) during chemistry laboratory sessions, and these affects act as key drivers of cognitive engagement in experimental work. Critically, while students frequently experience “negative” affects (e.g., anxiety and frustration) as a result of failure and mistakes, they are more motivated to learn and experiment if provided with appropriate teacher support. Similarly, Jaber and Hammer (2016) observed that curiosity and surprise can trigger productive exploration and persistence, while confusion, if supported through scaffolding, can promote deeper conceptual change rather thandisengagement. Classroom-level research also emphasizes the importance of fostering supportive affective climates. Tobin et al.

(2013) showed that  when classroom emotions were characterized by humor and joy, teacher –student dialogue

became more interactive and inquiry-oriented. Together, these findings illustrate that epistemic affects are not ancillary to cognition but fundamental to how learners engage, persist, and construct understanding in science education.

Cognitive processing Cognitive processing refers to the mental activities involved in acquiring, organizing, and applying knowledge, encompassing attention, comprehension, reasoning, and metacognitive regulation (Krathwohl, 2002). Bloom’s taxonomy provides a foundational framework for categorizing cognitive processing into hierarchical levels—from lower-order processes like remembering and understanding to higher-order processes such as applying, analyzing, evaluating, and creating (Bloom et al., 1956; Krathwohl, 2002). Within this hierarchy, learners move from reproducing information to transforming and generating new knowledge, reflecting increasing cognitive complexity and autonomy. Extensive research in formal educational contexts has applied Bloom’s taxonomy to assess the depth of student learning, instructional design, and assessment practices (Adams, 2015). However, cognitive processing is not only a function of instructional design but also of learners’ self-regulation and engagement strategies. Active processes such as self-explanation, elaboration, and reflection have been found to enhance conceptual understanding by linking new information to existing knowledge structures. In informal learning spaces such as YouTube, understanding cognitive processing is particularly important because learners independently regulate their engagement without structured guidance. Unlike formal classrooms, YouTube learning environments are self-paced, user-directed, and multimodal, allowing learners to control their exposure, attention, and depth of processing. Viewers may engage in lower-level cognitive activities, such as recalling definitions or memorizing formulas, or higher-level processes, such as analyzing relationships, evaluating explanations, or applying concepts tonew problems (Dubovi & Tabak, 2021). The interactive affordances of the platform—such as pausing, rewinding, commenting, or seeking alternative explanations —offer opportunities for metacognitive regulation, but they also rely heavily on learners’ motivation and self-awareness. Prior research on video-based learning indicates that engagement behaviors (e.g., pausing and note-taking) often correspond with deeper levels of processing, whereas passive viewing tends to limit conceptual change  (Yoon et al., 2021). Thus, applying Bloom’s taxonomy to YouTube learning provides a lens for understanding not only what learners know but also how they think and process information in informal, technology-mediated contexts. This exploratory study aims to advance understanding of the entanglement of epistemic affects and cognitive processing in learning science on YouTube. Using keywords drawn from Ambitious Science Teaching and Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS Lead States, 2013), the authors conducted a comprehensive search on YouTube to build the dataset. The videos were further screened to exclude those with no or few comments (fewer than 10), Shorts, non-English, or children-generated content. This screening process yielded a total of 131 videos, with 20,921 comments. The comments were cleaned to remove comments that only contain emojis or hyperlinks. The final comments included were 18,290.

For data analysis, we employed a  human-in-the-loop, AI-assisted analytic approach. Specifically, we used the Gemini 2.5 Flash API  to generate initial automated codes based on a predefined coding schema  on epistemic affects and cognitive processing. Four researchers collectively reviewed the results to assess accuracy, refine the prompt, and ensure conceptual alignment with the coding framework. Discrepancies and ambiguous cases were discussed until a consensus was reached, allowing for the iterative refinement of the analytic procedure. At the preliminary stage of the analysis, we report results from a random sample of 200 comments. 1423 The characteristics of the epistemic affects As shown in Table 1, neutral statements, those conveying factual understanding or conceptual explanation without an explicit emotional tone, accounted for half of the data (n = 101, 50.5%). These comments often involved clarifications of equations or definitions. Among the affective expressions, joy and curiosity were the most frequently observed, each comprising 11.5% of the data (n = 23 respectively). Learners expressing joy often described satisfaction or progress in their understanding, while curiosity appeared in questions probing deeper conceptual meanings.

Other positive or mixed epistemic affects appeared with lower frequencies. Humor (10.0%) often emerged in playful or self-referential comments about the learning context, while excitement (3.5%) reflected moments of high engagement in the learning process.  Conversely, several negative epistemic affects were also evident. Anxiety (7.0%) and frustration (4.0%) appeared in comments reflecting self-doubt or dissatisfaction with explanations, while vexation (4.0%) captured moments of cognitive conflict or confusion between competing concepts. Surprise (3.5%) was expressed in reaction to unexpected historical or conceptual information, and boredom was rare, accounting for only 1% of the dataset. Collectively, these patterns suggest that YouTube science learning el icits a wide emotional spectrum —ranging from curiosity and humor to anxiety and frustration—indicating that epistemic affects are deeply embedded in learners’ meaning-making processes. Rather than serving as byproducts of cognition, the results suggest that emotions such as curiosity, vexation, and joy act as epistemic regulators that shape learners’ movement across cognitive stages, from remembering and questioning to analyzing and evaluating. This supports emerging views that emotions not only signal the state of learning but also modulate epistemic agency, guiding learners through complex science learning processes (Agustian et al., 2025). The frequent co-occurrence of vexation with higherorder cognitive processing reflects the productive tension described by D’Mello and Graesser (2014), wherein confusion can catalyze conceptual restructuring if learners perceive it as resolvable. Similarly, the alignment of joy with understanding reinforces the idea that affective satisfaction may mark successful resolution of conceptual uncertainty, a key transition from exploration to consolidation in learning (Muis et al., 2018). These findings also illuminate the potential of YouTube as a dynamic space for science learning. Unlike traditional classroom environments, YouTube allows learners to engage voluntarily and dialogically through comments, using emotion both tonegotiate understanding and to build social connections. The presence of humor, excitement, and even frustration in these interactions underscores the social and participatory nature of epistemic affect in digital learning spaces. Importantly, the results highlight tha t productive emotional engagement, both positive or negative, can sustain learners’ inquiry and reflection in the absence of direct instructional scaffolding. Future research could further examine how these cognitive-affective dynamics unfold over time and how platform design or community norms might support more intentional affective-cognitive integration in informal science learning.

## Implications

This study underscores the importance of educators and science communicators recognizing and interpreting students’ epistemic affects as integral components of learning, rather than treating them as incidental or external to cognitive engagement  (Zhang et al., 2025). In informal learning contexts like YouTube, learners’ emotions, such as curiosity, confusion, or joy, serve as valuable indicators of their sense-making processes. By attuning to these affective cues, educators and content creators can better u nderstand how students navigate uncertainty, regulate motivation, and sustain cognitive effort in self-directed learning. Practitioners can use insights from affective patterns to design more responsive and supportive learning environments that acknowledge and validate the emotional dimensions of scientific inquiry.

From a research perspective, the human –AI collaborative analytic approach demonstrated in this study provides a promising pathway for examining large-scale affective and cognitive data in digital learning environments. By combining automated coding with human interpretive refinement, researchers can capture both the breadth and nuance of learners’ cognitive–affective dynamics. Future studies might expand this line of work by modeling temporal patterns of epistemic affects or exploring how community interaction structures (e.g., replies, likes, or creator responses) influence the depth of cognitive processing. Together, these insights advance understanding of how affect and cognition co-occur in participatory online spaces, offering new directions for designing emotionally and intellectually engaging science learning experiences. 1425 Adams, N.
