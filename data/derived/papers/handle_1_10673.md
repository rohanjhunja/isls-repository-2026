# The Role of Instruction in Shaping Reasoning about Bodies of Evidence: An Experimental Comparison

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The public often falls prey to skewed narratives on social media, such as when these narratives are built on cherry-picked evidence, overlooking larger bodies of evidence. We compared three instructional conditions (control with complementary information only, invention task and cued questions, and invention task and text-based instruction in evidence evaluation) to develop Japanese undergraduate students’ epistemic reasoning to discern and lean towards claims backed by a larger body of research as well as detailed descriptions of its evidence sources. Results showed that students with an invention task and text-based instruction more critically evaluated the strength of evidence and trustworthiness who presented cherry-picked evidence than those in the other conditions, which indicates the effect of instruction.

## Introduction

When citizens probe issues like vaccination safety, they are met with divergent claims from various sources (Lewandowsky et al., 2017). Though these sources ci te evidence, they frequently fall short of presenting the full breadth of research —often highlighting a couple of studies out of poten tially hundreds. These studies are often cherry picked, presenting one or two studies as sho wing what is accurate when a large majority of stud ies show something different. As such, individuals should prioritize seeking and understanding the consensus of larger bodies of evidence and be cautious of selective study citation (Duncan et al., 2018). Concomitantly, authors who cherry pick evidence should be regarded as less tru stworthy. There is a pressing need to develop instructional methods that equip students to better align their beliefs with larger bodies of evid ence and to recognize sources that may skew facts by cherry picking studies.

In a previous study (Oura et al., 2023), we designed and assessed an on-demand session in which students were required to  learn with materials containing varied evidence supportin g conflicting claims within a week. This instruction was grounded in the Preparation for Future Learning (PFL) approach (Schwartz & Martin, 2004): Students first attempted to generate criteria for the strength of evidence based on examining conflicting documents with different amounts of evidence. After this, stu dents received text-based instruction  on the significance of considering the bodies of evidence when reaching judgments about which positions to support and which authors to trust. We found that students receiving this instruction reasoned better about ne w topics than students not receiving instruction. Howeve r, there  is still a question regarding why this instruction was effective. Was i t because they received instruction, or because they had been encouraged to think about the amount of ev idence available (separate from any instruction)? To answer this question, we modified instruction in a new study, adding a new condition that provided us with a better understand ing of what kind of instruction is needed to suppor t students in appreciating the value of larger bodies of evidence.

## Method

A total of one hundred fifty-nine undergraduate students participated as part of an online introductory education course at a public university in Japan. The student s engaged in all procedures individually. The desig n was experimental with three conditions (control, invention-and-cues, and  invention-and-instruction), and students were randomly assigned to one  of the conditions. The experiment consisted of two instruction  phases and one assessment phase (see Table 1 for a summary). The first instruction phase aimed to develop students’ competence in basing epistemic judgments on larger bodies of e vidence instead of one or two pieces of evidence. Students read three documents (A, B, and C) on the topic of wheth er a drug (called “IFC”) has a positive effect on c uring a fictitious disease called equatorial hepatitis, which has symptoms like those of Hepatitis E. Document A supported a pro-IFC claim, and Document B supported the same pro claim, each based on one or two studies. In contrast, Document C supported an anti-IFC claim based on a meta-analysis. Students in the control condition read and summarized each of these three documents, and then read additional information about the background of the disease. In the third, invention-and-instruction condition, students first engaged in the same invention task that was described in the previous condition. They were subsequently given text-based instruction, in which a student and a professor discussed how to weigh the strength of evidence from different st udies and why it matters for laypeople to attend to bodies of evidence, rather than results from a few studies. The second instruction set aimed to develop students’ evaluation skills on the trustworthiness of authors who cited larger amounts of evidence to support their claims. We developed four tweets (A, B, C, and D) by four different authors on the topic of whether a fictitious artificial sweetener (AS), called sucratame, increases the risk of cancer. Tweet A supported an anti-AS claim based on “one large-scale study.” Conversely, Tweet B supported a pro-AS claim based on “most of the studies” with no specific studies cited. In contras t, Tweet C supported the anti-AS claim based on 25 studies in which 72% of them show ed some increase of cancer risk, 16% show ed no increase, and 12% showed that their results were no t conclusive. Lastly, Tweet D support ed the pro-AS claim based on “a study conducted in 2008;” the author al so stated that he “read various studies.”  As with the first instruction set, students in the control condition read and summarized major points of the f our tweets and then read unrelated background information on the sweetener. Students in the invention-and-cues condition engaged in the same ranking and criteria invention for the strength of evidence, followed by the same cue questions on the present topic. Likewise, students in the invention-and-instruction condition engaged in the same ranking and criteria invention tasks, followed by text-based instruction in which the same student and the professor discussed how to evaluate the trustworthiness of authors based on the extent to which they were familiar with all the evidence on a topic and could thus base their judgments on m ost or all the evidence. Specifically, the professor explained that some authors may introduce their evidence as “ typical” or “representative” even though it is ofte n not clear whether the authors really know all or most availab le evidence in the topic; moreover, it takes a grea t deal of specialized expertise to know all the evidence on a topic and t o evaluate and interpret it appropriately. The professor encouraged the student to pay attention to how specifically authors of the tweets present or express their evidence, emphasizing that they might not know all the available evidence on its topic, especially when their evidence description is less specific.

The post-instruction assessment was conducted one week later. It examined whether students could make judgments about claims based on larger bodies of evidence (i.e., all the studies available) rather than one or a few studies, which might be cherry picked. It also had them rate the trustworthiness of authors who varied in the amount of evidence they discussed. For the assessme nt, we developed three documents (A, B, and C) maki ng conflicting claims on the relationship between rewa rds and work performance. Document A supported a proreward claim based on two studies whose evidence supported their  claim. In contrast, Document B supported an anti-reward claim based on a meta-analysis analyzing 91 studies; this document was the sole document based on a large body of evidence. Document C  also supported the anti-rewards claim but based this conclusion on just one study. Thus, among these three documents, Document B (anti-rewards) presented the strongest evidence. Prior to reading the three documents, students answered two pre-survey items to measure their prior positions on both pro-rewards claim that rewards increase work performance (pre pro-rewards position) and the anti-rewards claim that rewards decrease work performance (pre anti-rewards position). After this pre-survey, students read the three documents and answered seven post-survey items to measure their post-instruction positions on both claims (pro-and anti-rewards positions), the strength of evidence on both positions (pro-and anti-rewards strength of evidence), and the author trustworthiness on each document (Trust in Doc A, Doc B, and Doc C). All the responses were on 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree and 7 = s trongly agree). They also were asked to explain reasons for each of their ratings. In the analysis, one-way ANOVA was conducted for the nine variables as well as the written explanations were analyzed qualitatively.

## Results

Results are shown in Table 2. Prior to reading the documents, s tudents in all three conditions strongly endorsed the pro-rewards position (MC1 = 5.96, MC2 = 6.11, MC3 = 6.15) and were strongly against the anti-rewards position (MC1 = 2.70, MC2 = 2.19, MC3 = 2.37), and the differences among the means across the three conditions were small on both measures.

On post-reading measures after reading the documents, significant shifts in the pro-rewards position and strength of evidence rating were observed (see left and middle line plots in Figure 1). Results of Tukey’s multiple comparisons showed that students in the invention-and-instruction condition (C3) rated the pro-rewards condition lower than the other two conditions. The students in C3 also rated the pro-reward strength of evidence lower than the students in the control condition (C1) and marginally lower (p  =.053) than students in the invention-and-cues condition (C2). Significant shifts were also observed on each of the measures of trust in Documents A, B, and C. Results of Tukey’s multiple comparisons show ed that students in the invention-and-instruction condition (C3) rated Doc A lower and Doc B higher than students in the other conditions (see right line plots in Figure 1).

## Discussion

The present study compared reasoning based on weigh ing the amount of evidence among the control (C1), the invention-and-cues (C2), and the invention-and-instruction (C3) conditions. Results show that, on the post-survey, students with th e instruction showed greater awareness of the importa nce of larger amounts of evidence along several dimensions. First, they gave lower ratings for the  pro-reward position supported by less evidence, in comparison to the control group. In comparison to both of the other conditions, they rated the evidence as weaker for the pro-rewards position, and rated the author who cited th e larger amount of evidence  (Doc B)  as more trustworthy. Analyses of the students’ written explanations for their ratings showed that the students in C3 were more likely to give reasons related to the number o f studies or to express concerns about cherry picked evidence than those in the other two conditions. In contrast, the students in the invention-and-cues condition, who received no instruction that explained why larger bodies of evidence are important to consider, did not outperf orm the control condition on any measure. The results also show that their directions for the change between the pro-and anti-positions are asymmetrical. Students in the invention-and-instruction condition showed strong effects of the amount of  evidence in lowering ratings related to the pro-rewards position (which most students had initially supported) but not in most cases in raising ratings related to the anti-rewards position.
