# Evaluating for Ideas: A Method for Scoring Fine-Grained Analyses in Chemistry Assessment

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Understanding how students organize their ideas is a central concern in chemistry education research. Chemistry students coordinate abstract ideas from multiple levels of behavior to explain how a complex system of particles results in observable behavior. While fine-grained constructivist perspectives offer an analytic framework for identifying organizing knowledge structures, researchers sometimes abandon this perspective and adopt more deficit-oriented frameworks when interested in comparing the quality of student responses. The current work presents a method for assigning a composite quality score based on numbers of unique, scientifically valid ideas expressed and the connections drawn between them. This approach uses a syntax-based method to unitize distinct ideas as well as identify and quantify links between them. Preliminary analyses demonstrate that this approach captured the richness, organization, and scoring of students’ reasoning. These findings have implications for how researchers and practitioners assess conceptual understanding and track the reorganization of ideas over time.

## Theoretical background

Fine-grained constructivist epistemological perspectives are a class of theories that describe knowledge as a complex system of elemental yet pedagogically interpretable ideas. These ideas are context-sensitive and are formalized and abstracted from everyday experience and actions  (diSessa, 1993; diSessa & Sherin, 1998). Understanding emerges when a learner has organized relevant ideas into a coherent network, making it available to interpret a given prompt (diSessa et al., 2016). These perspectives are typically used  by learning scientists to inform studies of how ideas influence in-the-moment thinking (diSessa, 1993), how ideas are reorganized during learning (Parnafes, 2007), and how changes in knowledge structures endure over time (Rosenberg et al., 2006). The KI framework has informed a rigorously tested measurement tool that assigns numerical values to open-response items based on the number of and links among unique, scientifically normative, and relevant ideas students present in response to a prompt (Lee et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2008). While the current literature allows for flexible definitions of several constructs due to the unexpected nature of students’ responses to assessment items (Liu et al., 2008), we found it necessary to operationalize three specific constructs: (1) ideas, (2) relevance and

(3) linkages, to accommodate a systematic analysis of our students’ responses. Thus, we have extend ed the KI

scoring approach by operationalizing these constructs from the framework to score our large dataset. Additionally, we broaden scoring requirements to create a general approach to scoring students’ responses that does not impose an a priori set of expected ideas and links.  This flexibility allows an asset-based perspective towards students’ responses, building a scoring scheme based on the ideas they present, rather than ideas imposed by an expert or instructor. Lastly, we report preliminary analyses to highlight that this method retains many affordances of a finegrained constructivist analytic tool, including  reporting insights into the categories and organization of the ideas students generated in response to our assessment.

## Methodology & Study Context

The study was conducted in a first-semester introductory undergraduate chemistry course. The research team analyzed responses from  N = 139 students.

1197 The assessment was designed following a protocol that emphasizes causal and mechanistic explanations of observable chemistry phenomena by separating primary questions into prompts for students to illustrate, describe, and explain their responses (Ralph et al., 2022). The illustrate and describe prompts  provide space for students to draw and write about particle-level representations of the problem space. The explain prompt is designed to elicit a written causal and mechanistic report ing of the phenomena. Among science education communities (including course instructors) causal and mechanistic explanations of observable behavior are valued because they allow students to interpret the cause of emergent behavior based on the structure of the system (Russ et al., 2008).

Analyses First, we used an inductive coding process to inform our fine-grained analyses of the scientific ideas in students’ responses to justify their conclusions on the assessment. Our analyses focused on the written reports to focus our scope of work around the same type of data;  future work may extend the present method to include illustrations. Students’ responses are codified by first identifying all ideas regardless of their accuracy or relevance; next, the scientifically accurate and relevant ideas are segmented and separated for use in the KI framework construct map. Two raters developed a preliminary codebook, categorizing ideas presented by students from a first random 10% portion of the dataset, after which the codebook was refined using an additional 10%  of the data. The resulting coding scheme yielded 20 codes.

We determined the boundaries around ideas by transcribing and segmenting students’ responses (Chi, 1997). Segmentation was based on written clauses using a syntax-based approach (Reed et al., 2018). Inter-rater reliability was established for a 20% sub-set randomized at the student level. While orthogonality is ideal for categorical ascription of data, there were still instances in which multiple codes could be assigned to some segments. Thus, we calculated an average Cohen’s Kappa of all codes; 𝜅𝜅  =.81, indicating very high agreement. This process yielded a fine-grained description of the ideas  students reported in response to each subproblem. It does not yet provide quality comparisons among the responses, nor does it offer interpretations of the connections among the ideas.

The first step for quantifying the quality of students’ responses using the KI construct map included identifying which ideas were scientifically relevant and accurate. Pellegrino et al. (2001) use the assessment triangle to highlight the interplay among the assessor’s beliefs of learning, their observation of that learning, and its subsequent interpretation. Recognizing the deeply contextualized interpretability of the triangle, relevance and accuracy are operationalized as they relate to the assessment's goals. For this study, codes for scientific relevance and accuracy were applied if students accurately described the problem space at a particle level or accurately discussed the particle-level mechanism that describes the change in state. Ostensibly, this emphasis can  vary among researchers and practitioners depending on the decisions made regarding the assessment triangle. In line with the syntax-based segmentation scheme, researchers identified links between scientifically relevant and accurate ideas using “syntactic markers” defined by Halliday and Hasan (2014) for each sub-problem. Scores are only assigned based on the number of unique, scientifically relevant, and accurate ideas students present, as well as the number of unique, scientifically relevant, and accurate links among them. If more than two ideas were linked in a single cluster, it was assumed that  students could link all ideas equally. Therefore, we calculated links in a cluster as i  −1 where i is the number of unique ideas. If a second cluster occurred in a response, the links in the other cluster were only included if at least one idea was not included in the previous cluster. Lastly, we extended the scoring scheme to allow for higher scores to identify more links (Figure 1). Since undergraduate-level chemistry is an abstract and interdisciplinary subject requiring students to use many different ideas to interpret phenomena (Talanquer, 2011), responses containing many ideas and links were expected.  The first coder identified links in the entire dataset while a second coder identified links for a random  20% sub-set, the identities of which were unknown to the first rater. Inter-rater reliability was established for this random subset. Cohen’s Kappa was calculated using the number of links identified as a nominal code in each sub-problem; 𝜅𝜅 =.80, indicating very high agreement.

An inductive analysis yielded twenty codes that described all ideas students provided in their worksheet responses (listed in Table 1). The causal and mechanistic framework values students’ ability to interpret observable phenomena with causal explanations that include how the constituent parts of the system give rise to the mechanisms (Russ et al., 2008). Students’ ideas are summarized at three hierarchical levels of interpretation. First-level codes categorize abstract, formalized ideas that describe the system and relationships among constituent parts and yielded three sub-levels: (a) representations of the problem space in purely symbolic form that discuss relationships among variables as components of an equation; (b) reasoning that described the variables 1198 in the equations themselves as causal agents when explaining the mechanism behind the observed change in pressure; and (c) descriptions of the state of the system using formalisms at the macroscopic/observable level, discussing attributes of the system using formal descriptions (e.g., temperature). For example, some students presented a formula as a causal agent for their explanation by reporting, for example, the reduction in  n (the number of molecules) caused the change in p (pressure). The second-level codes described the problem in terms of emergent properties of particle models. These did not  explicitly address how individual particles contribute to these properties. For example, some students described particles as exerting a greater net force on the tire, while others referred to particle density within the tire. At the third-level, students introduced ideas of individual particle models to describe how individual particles contribute to emergent-level behavior. For example, some discussed the change in the number of particles as the causal agent in the change in pressure. (e.g., the notion that adding more molecules in a confined space results in a densely packed container). Others discussed particle collisions with each other and with the tire walls while others introduced the idea of collisions without elaborating on the colliding entities. The fine-grained codes allowed the  identification of how students are organizing their ideas, as exemplified by the frequently invoked individual-level particle models to explain observed changes in pressure. However, it is important to note that the current scoring system was not fully represented in this dataset. We do find it necessary to retain these scores in the construct map for future analyses and comparisons to a different sample that has not yet formally learned the target concept in undergraduate instruction. Nor does the scoring system  yet attempt to fully account for the content of a student’s  ideas or the linkages between them beyond their accuracy, nor does it penalize non-normative ideas. Because the methodology used a syntax-based segmentation approach, higher scores tended to be assigned to responses that were more verbose. The fact that some students were able to explain that changing the number of particles inside the tire affects the frequency of particle collisions with the container wall, resulting in a change in pressure using fewer linked ideas than others should not yield a lower score. Thus, this method tries to control for this by determining the number of unique scientifically valid and relevant ideas and unique links among them. Nonetheless, researchers or instructors might subjectively assign greater weight to ideas more central to their learning goals. As the research team continues to refine this assessment, the psychometric propert ies of this approach for scoring constructed-response items must also be evaluated. As such, we take preliminary caution in interpreting the scale, particularly the assumption that a one-unit change in score is equivalent to a one-unit change at a different point on the scale. Finally, we speculate that a well-developed coding scheme could capture whether a unique idea might originate from a particular learning experience. While we have not yet empirically tested the use of this method to track changes in students’ reasoning over time, we believe it offers a promising foundation for doing so.
