# Understanding the Peer Mentoring Experiences of High School Students in an Out-of-School STEM Camp: An Exploratory Study

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The purpose of this study is to understand the experiences of four high-school students while mentoring younger middle-and high-school students, in an out-of-school STEM camp. The longitudinal nature of this study generated rich qualitative data comprising multiple semi-structured interviews with these participants conducted at different points in their journey as mentors. We conducted cross-case and within-case analyses using a grounded theory approach for coding, to answer the research question “What are the experiences of high-school students of being peer mentors to younger middle-and high-school students?” The results include a brief description of the mentoring experiences of one participant case and a brief description of the themes from the cross-case analysis of the experiences of all participants.

## Introduction

Mentorship involves a relationship of collaborative learning that is based on trust, intentionality, and interactions between mentors and mentees and can provide career and psychosocial support to both groups. Mentorship  has played an important role in developing professional s in the STEMM (science, technology, engineering, mathematics, and medicine) domains (National Academ ies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2019). The study by Clarke-Midura et al. (2018) defines near-peer mentors in the context of computer programming skills as mentors who are closer in age to their mentees and possess skills that are more than their mentees but lesser than an expert. The near-peer mentorship model demonstrated by Tenenbaum et al. (2014) has been helpful in facilitating personal, professional, and educationa l growth of their near-peer mentors who were undergraduates mentoring middle and high school students.  So, many research studies focus on understanding the im pact of mentoring by having undergraduates as peer mentors to younger peers. Our research team works on a project that situates high-school students as peer mentors for younger middle-and high-school students (mentees). This study, which is a part of this project, focuses on understanding the experiences of high-school youth as peer mentors to younger peers in an out-of-school STEM camp. These high-school students (mentors) have been associated with the STEM camp for multiple years and are proximal in age to their mentees. Thus, the longitudinal nature of this study has generated rich qualitative data about the experiences of the high-school students as peer mentors.

## Literature review

The near-peer mentoring model is an effective strategy for s cience and integrated STEM education and benefits the near-peer mentors (post-baccalaureates and undergraduate s) personally, professionally, and educationally (Tenenbaum et al., 2014). Also, youth mentoring activities take place in the contex t of social and interpersonal relations between peer mentors and their mentees (DuBois & Karcher, 2014). Another retrospective study (Anderson et al., 2019) from 2004 to 2015 investigated the career trajectories of 40 undergra duates and recent post-baccalaureates who mentored younger middle-and/or high-school students. This study indicated that the mentoring experiences of these undergraduates contributed to them building p ositive attitudes towards sciences, strengthening their persistence in the sciences, and gaining high commitment to continue mentoring younger peers to develop an interest in and appreciation for scie nce (Anderson et al., 2019). Similarly, Nelson et al. (2017) reported a study where undergraduate mentors’ reflections on their experiences of mentoring K-8 youth in STEM subjects in after-school settings, demonstrated multiple benefits like  better STEM content knowledge, improvement in organization and ability to teach and explain science, and improved problem-solving abilities. Our analysis of these research studies indicates that peer mentoring experiences offer various benefits to the personal and professional lives of the mentors who are undergraduates mentoring middle and high school students. In our study, the peer mentors were high-school students who first acquaint ed themselves with the technology they wanted to teach and then mentor ed younger middle-and high-school students. Unlike the prior research, as the peer mentors are high-school students, there is a smaller age gap between them and their mentees, which can facilitate smooth communication and build easy trust. However, this smaller age gap may also burden these high-school mentors with the responsibility of teaching STEM-related concepts to mentees who are not too young. Thus, through our study, we are interested in understanding these experiences of high-school mentors.

## Methods

In this exploratory study, through the research question  “What are the experiences of high-school students of being peer mentors to younger middle-and high-school students?” we are trying to understand the influence of the peer mentoring experiences of high-school student mentors on their lives, comprehend their journey as teachers to younger peers, and also understand their perceptions about themselves as peer mentors or teachers.

## Research context

This study is a part of the Smart Greenhouse Project which focuses on engaging high and middle-school youth in learning block-based coding, plant science, and other technologies (laser-cutting, 3D printing, etc.) during camps. After attending the camps multiple times, the campers become near-peer mentors (“leaders”) for the new campers. These leaders get further experienced to become “leader-of-leaders”, who mentor both the new leaders in their teaching process as well as the campers in learning  the technologies. This study focuses on the near-peer mentoring experiences of these leader-of-leaders. Our study’s context  is from three out-of-school (in-person) camps: (1) A two-week Summer Camp in August 2022 (SC22); (2) A one-week Winter Vacation Camp in February 2023 (WC23), and (3) A two-week Summer Camp in August 2023 (SC23). In the first week  of both summer camps, as “revise and recall” sessions, the high-school leaders learned to build a tabletop automated smart greenhouse, install environmental sensors in it, program these sensors using a micro:bit to collect environmental data. They also discussed and developed strategies to teach this technology to middle-school campers. In the second week, these high-school leaders, with the help of a high-school teacher, taught about the smart greenhouse to the new campers. The Winter Vacation Camp focused on the same greenhouse-related content where the leaders got acquainted with the technology and planned their mentoring strategies on the first day. On the remaining four days, they mentored the first-timer campers about the project.

## Data collection and analysis

For this study, we collected qualitative data which included multiple semi-structured interviews with the four participants that were conducted at different points in their journey. We conducted one set of interviews with all four participants after they were leaders at least once in the project, right before SC23. We then conducted the second set of interviews after the completion of SC23, with all four participants after they experienced the role of leader-of-leaders for the first time. Our data set also consists of one more interview with Penny conducted at the beginning of the WC23 to understand her initial experiences of attending SC22 for the first time. In this study, we conducted a within-case analysis (Miles et al., 2019) of one participa nt to understand, describe, and explain the experiences of mentoring in one bounded “case” (Miles et al., 2019). We also conducted a cross-case analysis (Miles et al., 2019) of the peer ment oring experiences of the four participants. The cro sscase analysis was conducted to increase the transfe rability and generalizability of the emerging themes of the context of one participant to other participants’ contexts and to obtain deepened understanding of the phenomenon of the peer mentoring experiences for all the participants (Miles et al., 2019). We used a grounded theory approach (Charmaz, 2006) for coding the semi-structured interviews to conduct the within-case and cross-case analyses, to best capture and understand the experiences of the participants as peer mentors.

## Results

In this section, we briefly describe the case of one participant in detail highlighting the factors that contributed to her mentoring experience and her self-perceptions about being a leader. Additionally, we describe in short, two major themes that emerged from the cross-case analysis of the data about the participants’ mentoring experiences. Brief description of Penny’s journey of being a leader-of-leaders Penny participated in the Smart Greenhouse Project  for the first time in the S C22 when she was to start Grade 10, then she was a leader in the WC23 followed by being a leader-of-leaders in the SC23. Penny’s repeated participation in these camps was mainly because she liked working with the various technologies at the camp, she got to spend time with her friends, and the freedom she got at the camps-freedom to learn whatever interests her and freedom of being a leader and mentor to other s tudents. Penny’s self-perceptions evolved from her first interview through her third one. Initially she perceived herself as not being very good with technology but slowly getting more comfortable (and even proficient) in working w ith technologies like laser-cutting and 3D printing and finally she also felt confident about teaching these technologies to younger peers. Although being nervous while teaching for the first time, she developed perceptions like a teacher about her middle-school students by mentioning “The kids were really  nice. And they were smart as well. They were like, understanding stuff really fast.” She felt th at overcoming shyness and being confident were the most important characteristics of being a leader. But sh e also mentioned that she has social anxiety and so  ha d difficulties in “speaking up” in a group. So, she p referred working with students in smaller groups. P enny mentioned that she liked being a leader because that gave her the opportunity of “helping others” which made her very happy. Also, in other settings, Penny usually found herself following others’ orders and not getting a chance to be heard or to change things around her, but she  aspired to do those things while being a leader. As a leader and a leader-of-leaders, Penny normalized failure and emphasized to her students that it was fine to make mistakes by telling them that “it is a part of growing”. Also, she ensured her students that the parts of the technology of the greenhouse were not breakable so that they could be less appre hensive about using technology. She valued that all the middle-school students were learning, growing, being creative, experimenting, and having fun at the camp. As a leader, Penny tried to sit with the students t o help them understand the concepts and resolve the ir difficulties. Also, she insisted that she would kee p repeating things for her students and would make sure they understood what she was teaching. She mentioned tha t she felt sad if they did not understand her expla nation. In her third interview, Penny shared “I have probably gotten better at being a leader.” From the analysis of all three Penny’s interviews, we interpret that the main underlying factors that steer Penny to being a leader are the ability to get herself heard  while being a leader, trying to create an environment where others can be heard, helping others, communicating with others confidently, and lastly, the freedom while learning and teaching abo ut technology about which she was extremely fascinated.

Brief description of the cross-cutting themes Theme 1: Experiences and self-perceptions of mentors while mentoring Through their own mentoring experiences, the peer m entors (as leaders and leader-of-leaders) described various roles and responsibilities they fulfilled as a leader in the camp as well as they spoke about general characteristics a leader or a teacher should possess. Some of these responsibilities, as they described, were helping other leaders and/or campers with whatever help they need ed, teaching technology, being flexible about teachin g strategies especially while working with middle-school students versus high-school leaders, and helping students understand the mechanism behind the functioning of various cod es and technologies. Some of the general characteristics of being a leader/teacher were saying things in an appropriate manner, making sure that all students listen to you and stay on their tasks, and acting in such a way that others develop respect for you as a leader. Additionally, these mentors experienced various str uggles while working with younger peers. Jack mentioned that he was always cautious about communicating in the correct way to avoid misinterpretation. Penny and Megan mentioned about “social anxiety” while speaking in public. However, Jack, Penny, and Megan learned to deal with their challenges to felt more confident with more experience. Edward did not mention about specific challenges as he felt confident about dealing with the challenges  that would arise while mentoring due to more experience with the camps. While understanding the self-perceptions of these participants as mentors, we observed that Jack and Edward perceived themselves as being confident/comfortable leaders. On the othe r hand, Penny felt nervous while teaching for the first tim e, had social anxiety due to which she preferred wo rking in small groups but finally she felt that she was improving as a leader. Although Megandid not specifically mention about her self-perceptions as a mentor, she seemed to have a simil ar trajectory to Penny. Thus, these mentors developed ideas of their own responsibilities from their experiences of being leaders and leader-of-leaders. It seemed that, with every new experience, they were t hinking more like teachers than mentors and their s ense of major responsibility was to provide enriching experiences to their “students”. Theme 2: Influence of mentoring experiences on the personal lives of the mentors The peer mentoring experiences helped the mentors t o develop a range of life-skills and these mentors also experienced a range of emotions while working with younger peers. The emotions experienced, as reported by the four mentors were, excitement, happiness, sadne ss, frustration, hope, fascination, enjoyment, and anxiety or nervousness. The range of life-skills that the mentors felt they developed were communication, confidence, being prepared and organized to teach a class, comfort wi th community engagement, patience, flexibility and adaptability while teaching, being open to feedback and improving based on feedback, and problem-solving skills.

## Concluding remarks

Our findings show that the peer mentoring experiences have demon strated to be beneficial to the participants, both personally and professionally (Tenenbaum et al., 2014). They developed various life skills like c onfidence, communication, patience, etc., that shaped their personalities and they also de veloped skills like behaving like a teacher, being adaptive as a mentor which will help  them professionally. Also, development of problem-solving skills, organization, preparedness to teach etc. and development of proficiency to work with technology to feel confident (in varying proportions) of teaching these technologies to middle-school students, aligned with the study reported by Nelson et al. (2017). These high-school mentors enjoyed the camp and were committed to continue working with younger peers which was similarly noticed in Anderson et al. (2019).  The cases showed that this kind of technology-aided youth-led informal STEM education project can provide multiple “hooks” for different high-school student mentors to demonstrate various leadership experiences and different ways in which they could connect with the project.  We are fully aware that there are some limitations in this study. For instance, the data analysis of the semi-structured interviews was carried out only by the first author. In the future, it will be helpful to triangulate the findings by using and analyzing the data from the focus group discussions that we have conducted with these participants at the end of SC23 and/or have another researcher carry out the data analysis.
