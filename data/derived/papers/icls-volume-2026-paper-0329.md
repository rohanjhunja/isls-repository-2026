# Brief Community-Building Interventions in an Undergraduate Synchronous Online Course

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
A team of six teaching faculty members implemented a community-building intervention in a synchronous online social-sciences course required for all first-year students at a small North American university serving a diverse, international student body. Results from surveys and online platform data suggest that these brief, regular interventions, taking only about five minutes per ninety-minute class period, may facilitate student belonging, psychological safety, and equitable participation. We discuss the nature of the intervention and initial findings, which may be of interest to other instructors, in any discipline, who wish to cultivate community in their classes. Pedagogical objectives While higher-education faculty tend to focus on the content that we teach, fundamentally, we are teaching people. Increasingly, scholars and faculty are recognizing the importance of student belonging in the university classroom (Brady & Gopalan, 2026; Freeman et al., 2007; Healey & Stroman, 2021), which may be defined as “the extent to which students feel personally accepted, respected, included, and supported by others” (Goodenow, 1993; Jeng et al., 2023). While building an inclusive classroom community with belonging helps all students, it especially supports the success of students from under-represented groups (Easterbrook & Hadden, 2021; Elliott et al., 2016; Pedler et al., 2022; Walton & Cohen, 2011). In online classes, in particular, a sense of social presence, belonging, and community may be more challenging to cultivate (Gulati, 2022; Rovai, 2002). We discuss a simple, brief, regular intervention in a first-year course at Minerva University designed to help build community and a sense of belonging. As community emerges from the instructor and students getting to know each other, the intervention aimed to increase the sense of community through spending about five minutes of every class on activities to help the class get to know each other. The goal was to help our diverse students feel more comfortable bringing their authentic selves to class, to feel that they knew each other’s authentic selves, to feel supported and accepted by each other, and to improve the equity of students’ participation and listening. The intervention’s design was based on Odera’s practical experience of integrating community-building time in his classes over the previous five years, including how devoting a small amount of time in every single class meeting helped students to build a habit of checking in on each other. The first three authors are teaching faculty at Minerva University. Morgan and Odera both teach the target course and participated in designing and implementing the intervention, and Sealfon is a collaborator who taught two other first-year courses, Empirical Analyses (EA) and Formal Analyses (FA), that did not implement the intervention. Karmazin is a student who participated in the intervention and helped analyze the data afterwards as a research assistant. Course description and context At Minerva University, all first-year undergraduates take the same four required courses. All courses are facilitated online via a proprietary video-conferencing software. The software is designed to facilitate student engagement via video, chat, and the use of emojis that appear as an overlay over the student’s video feed. The classes are taught live and the video feed of the instructor and students a ppear on screen for the entire class. All classes at Minerva University utilize a flipped-classroom model a nd the classes focus on discussion of topics rather than transmitting information from the instructor to students. The result is a culture of active participation by students in all classes. Each first-year cohort is about 100-200 students, and class sizes are capped at around 20 students, so there are multiple sections of each course. Different sections of the same course are taught by different faculty members using the same curriculum, assignments, and lesson plans. The required first-year social sciences course, Complex Systems (CX), introduces the social sciences through a complexity science lens, including basic psychology, levels of analysis, emergent properties, power dynamics, and complex causality. The 195 students in the fall 2023 cohort represented 55 countries across all continents, with about 50% from Asia, 23% from Europe, and 14% from Africa. Most students were of typical undergraduate age with a median age of 19. Gender demographics were 55% female, 44% male, and 1% other. 2137 Intervention The community-building intervention was implemented in the fall of 2023 in the CX course and spanned the entire fall semester (14 weeks total, 25 classes of 90 minutes each). The other three first-year courses, in natural sciences, computational sciences, and multimodal communication, taken by the same cohort of students and meeting for the same length of time, did not receive the intervention. The intervention consisted of setting aside time to deliberately build connections among students and between students and the instructor in every class. In all but six classes (19/25 classes), five minutes at the start of class was dedicated to conversations intended to help students get to know each other better and build a sense of community. In the six class periods without five minutes of dedicated community-building time at the start of class, community discussions had already been integrated into the lesson plans to complement course content. The specific format of discussion was left up to the instructor's discretion. Instructors were given a few suggested community-building activities at the start of the semester, but were encouraged to experiment with how they used the community-building time. Suggested discussion prompts included questions such as: “What color best describes how you are feeling today?” “Share a word or two about how you feel about your residence hall.” “What is your breakfast food?” “What is one thing you have learned about yourself since the start of the semester?” “On a scale of 1 to 5, how are you doing today? (Choose ‘No response’ if you do not want to answer the question)” with descriptions of what 1-5 mean, and an invitation for students to share more. Other examples included asking two students in each class to bring an item that is important to them to briefly present to the class, called a “me library” or “me museum”, or sending all students into breakout rooms for three minutes to tell each other a joke. The six CX instructors, including Morgan and Odera, would meet after every week of classes and share tips on how to best facilitate community-building activities in ways that would feel authentic to each instructor. However, instructors did not receive any formal training on how to facilitate the community-building interventions. Data sources Data were collected from two sources for this preliminary analysis. In the fall of 2023, students were surveyed during the 4th week of the semester and after the semester finished right before the start of the spring semester. Data were also collected from Minerva’s online teaching platform. Online platform data from the fall of 2021, 2022, and 2023 were included in the analysis. Survey data The two survey waves collected data on students’ subjective experiences. Students were asked six subjective questions related to their feelings about belonging, psychological safety, and inclusion in each of their four first-year classes. Morgan and Odera designed the survey questions based on relevance to the Minerva University context. Responses were recorded on a 1-5 Likert scale and included a free response option for short explanations or elaborations. Students were not asked anything specifically about the intervention in CX to avoid biasing results. Additional data were collected on objective outcomes that attempted to measure the connectivity of students outside of the classroom. Both waves of the survey included identical questions. Students were not required to fill out the survey, so the researchers offered incentives to encourage participation. Students were entered into a raffle to win clothing with our university’s logo if they completed one wave of the survey and a gift card to a popular doughnut restaurant if they completed both waves. In total, 66 students participated in the first wave and 69 students participated in the second wave. 49 students completed both waves out of a cohort of 195 students, so the response rate of students who completed both waves of the survey was around 25%. We based our analysis on the 49 students who completed both survey waves. Online platform data Data were downloaded from the online platform from fall semesters in 2021, 2022, and 2023. The variables used in this analysis were the talk time (measured in seconds) of each student, the number of emojis used, and chat usage for each student in each class across all first-year courses. The data were cleaned to remove data from students who did not complete the semester or who transferred between sections due to difficulty tracking the student data between both sections. The top 5th 2138 percentile of observations for all variables were also dropped due to bugs on the online platform leading to unrealistic observations in the data. Analysis and preliminary results Figure 1 presents the results from both waves for the six subjective assessments included in the survey. Results for all four required first-year courses, taken by the same students, are presented. The course names are abbreviated as follows: Complex Syste ms (CX), Empirical Analyses (EA), Formal Analyses (FA), and Multimodal Communications (MC). This analysis of the survey data focuses on the changes in subjective assessments between the first and second waves. The community-building intervention spanned the entire semester so the prominent effects of the intervention should be reflected in trends across the semester. Furthermore, the four first-year courses vary significantly in terms of content and thus the types of discussion in class. Analyzing the changes between waves controls for these fixed differences between the four courses. In all six subjective questions, the difference in average response between waves in CX compared favorably or as well as changes in average responses in EA and FA. The questions for which CX compared favorably to EA and FA assessed students' feelings of being comfortable being their authentic selves, feeling that they were supported by their classmates, feeling comfortable participating in class discussions, and feelings that they know their classmates' authentic selves. Similar trends were observed in the questions that focused on feeling accepted by classmates and feeling that their classmates listened to their contributions in class. The changes observed between waves in CX and MC were far more similar thandifferent. MC is a course that teaches students tools of self-expression and communication so it is expected that class discussion could facilitate the same feeling of belonging, ps ychological safety, and inclusion that the CX community-building intervention aimed to foster. Indeed, although students felt that their classmates were not listening to their comments as much in the second wave in CX, the opposite was observed in MC. Students provided positive free-response comments regarding the intervention, expressing appreciation for the chance to get to know their classmates better in CX, even though students were not prompted to comment specifically on the community-building intervention. For example, one student shared that, in CX, “we discussed personal values and experiences which gave me a deeper insight into my peers.” Another noted that, across the first-year courses, “In class, most conversation revolves around the subject topics and there was little time for 2139 getting to know each other personally. I highly appreciated the me library to get to know each other in CX.” One student explicitly made the connection between the intervention in CX and their sense of belonging: “Since my prof would have a debrief and ask about our day and what we were feeling that day, it becomes easier to discuss and be myself during classes.” Overall, it appears that classroom dynamics in CX and MC either promoted or protected feelings of belonging, psychological safety, and inclusion compared to the EA and FA courses. But, in the case of CX, it is not clear if this is due to discussions that w ould occur naturally without the community-building intervention or if the intervention contributed to the trends observed in the survey data. A comparison with CX courses before the intervention was implemented can help determine if the intervention actually changed classroom dynamics in CX. Minerva’s online platform records the talk time for each individual student and for the instructor, through recording the amount of time that they are speaking during each class. Figure 2 shows the standard deviation in individual students’ talk time for each class session over the entire semester for 2021, 2022, and 2023. A trend line is fitted to the raw da ta to illustrate the trends across the semester. In the two years without a community-building intervention in CX (2021 and 2022), there is a similar increasing trend in the standard deviation of talk time over the semester. This indicates a growing inequa lity of class participation over time. However, in 2023 with the intervention, there is no clear trend in the standard deviation of talk time over the semester. This suggests that the intervention may have helped foster more equitable student participation in class, which may serve as an indication of classroom equity (Tanner, 2013). Figure 2 Standard Deviation of Talk Time in CX, 2021-2023 Conclusions and relevance Taking the survey and online platform data analysis together, we conclude that there is suggestive evidence that such community-building interventions do affect classroom dynamics and may either promote or protect feelings of belonging, psychological safety, and inclusion in the course. Such brief, regular interventions may affect overall classroom culture through helping all students feel valued and validated, which may also improve equitable participation. Future work will further analyze the online platform data in conjunction with the survey data to investigate potential proxy indicators of community and belonging in individual class sections in the automatically-collected online platform data, such as through building on work in belonging analytics (Lim et al., 2023) and equity analytics (Reinholz & Shah, 2018). As this intervention uses minimal class time as well as no extra time for instructor preparation or training, it may offer an effective and versatile approach to improve community in a wide variety of online and potentially in-person classes. At Minerva Un iversity, professors of the other first-year courses, especially EA and FA, have expressed interest in integrating this community-building intervention into their courses. In Morgan’s experience, some instructors were initially resistant to taking class time away from course content to invest in community building, but all instructors became enthusiastic supporters of the intervention after experiencing its effect on their students and their classes.
