# Immersive Learning in Professional Wildlife Training: The Role of VR in Supporting Novice and Expert Interaction

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This research delves into the role of Virtual Reality (VR) in wildlife training, specifically examining its influence on novice and expert interactions in animal husbandry. Rooted in sociocultural theories, the research unfolds within an ongoing co-design initiative at an Environmental Center, capturing real-time work practices through 360-degree videos later showcased in VR. Two episodes illustrate the strategic integration of VR within an informal workplace setting, showcasing its adaptability and impact on knowledge construction. The findings suggest that VR offers nuanced and responsive affordances for joint attention on artifacts by novices and experts, leading to more personalized opportunities for learning and practice.

## Introduction

Virtual Reality (VR) technologies provide several l earning affordances, however, their use must be und erstood within the cultural context and pedagogical objecti ves of a learning environment (Roman & Racek, 2019). This study is embedded within a larger co-design (McKercher, 2020) endeavor at an Environment al Center over an eighteen-month period. This ongoing project seeks to understand the authentic evolution of VR use and practices for workplace learning. In collaboration with the c enter’s Wildlife Educato r staff, our focus has been on the production of 360-degree videos capturing real-time work practices in the center’s aviary and herp etarium. We used Oculus headsets and screen casting  to develop cost-effective and tailored VR training experiences for the specific needs of the center and its instructional staff. In this study, we ask the following research question: How does VR technology afford opportunities for experts and novices to engage in knowledge sharing and sensemaking around the practices of animal husbandry?

Theoretical framing Grounded in sociocultural theories of learning, par ticularly the concept of legitimate peripheral part icipation (Lave & Wenger, 1991), this study explores a workplace learning setting. Participants include student newcomers and expert animal caretakers who learn together  about animal husbandry practices, via active participation in contextualized activities with tools, people, and a nimals. Legitimate peripheral participation emphasi zes the dynamic interplay between more knowledgeable and le ss knowledgeable members, highlighting the importan ce of both structured and informal knowledge-sharing practices. VR is conceptualized as an artif act offering a tangible approximation of expert practice, serving as a reflective and meditative tool for practitione rs (Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Trausan-Matu & Slotta, 2021). Expert knowledge is not confi ned to individual minds but is situated in the material world interactions among c ommunity members, thus shifting the focus from individual cognitive activity to scrutinizing social interactions within communities of practice. In this paper, we focus on the use of language within the socio-cultural setting and use interaction analysis (Jord an & Henderson, 1995) to explore our research questions.

In integrating VR, we consider its affordances as highlighted by Yang et al. (2021), which include promoting motivation, autonomous exploration, and e xperiential learning. But more broadly, we draw on the multiple representation view of learning (Ainsworth, 2018), emphasizing the centrality of representations in joint tasks. Representations mediate communication, drive expl anations, and function as cultural tools, and this view aligns with the integration of sociocultural and co gnitive approaches to enhance representational lear ning in digitally simulated experiences, such as VR (Ainsworth, 2018).

## Context and materials

In this paper we focus on expert-novice interactions in the Aviary Husbandry module.  The module focuses on tasks such as food preparation as well as more nuan ced practices of observing and sensing bird behavio r and observable phenomena in animal training. Training m aterials include discussions, videos, reading mater ials, hands-on activities, and worksheets. Training also includes experiential tasks in the Aviary enclosures, including learning how to clean an empty enclosure, and cleaning with raptors in the enclosures while observed. By the staff, for the staff: VR enhanced video in practice Throughout the co-design process, the center staff and researcher used GoPro Max cameras to record several 360VR videos of their work practices in action for over a year. The researcher and the staff reflected on which videos had unique contributions that their current curricu lum modalities could not highlight. The VR video ch osen by the staff for this module came from a work session in which staff took the camera into an aviary enclo sure and captured key aspects of what they wanted newcomers to learn. The video captures a staff member’s clean ing interactions with barn owls, highlighting stress behav iors and moments of pause to ensure the animals' co mfort. The staff employs an array of tools, including brus hes, buckets, and a rake. Because of the immersiven ess of the Meta Quest 2 used to display the VR videos, the sou nds of rakes and brushes, and other environmental s ounds, like a hawk’s cry, during the recording, are accent uated and the animal’s reactions become more explic it. The video captures frequent pauses by the staff member to observe barn owl behaviors, which manifest stress indicators throughout the cleaning session. The sta ff member conscientiously waits, paying meticulous attention to the owls' comfort levels, before resuming the cleaning tasks. The VR video has been used to train all new 7 stude nts in this program cohort. Since the program is situated alongside real work duties, staff make on-the-spot decisions to introduce the order or type of mo dule materials as appropriate. For this research, we present episodes showcasing the main ways in which VR is helping students and experts practice animal husbandry. Episode 1 explores the experiences of Sam, a student in the later stages of the module, who reviews material and watches the VR video before engaging in live cleaning with barn owls. Episode 2 involves a student, Lois, who exper iences VR at the beginning of the module, and practices cleaning an empty enclosure with staff afterwards.

## Methodology

An overall ethnographic approach (Creswell, 2012) was adopted for data collection with the first author being a frequent participant-observer (Rossman & Rallis, 2017) and collaborator in the activities of the center. Every academic semester, the center welcomes new student-apprentices who complete a 10-Module program before continuing on to expert duties in subsequent semest ers. For one cohort  of students, data were recorded over the course of 3 months, resulting in 156 hours of obser vations. Data was collected in the form of videos, artifacts, pictures, and researcher field notes. For this pape r, we focus on two artifacts from our video data and use  two episodes to illustrate how VR representations affor d dynamic engagement and joint focus on important i deas (Ludvigsen et al., 2015). The two episodes were selected to represent a type of maximal variation sampling based on volunteer experience. Video data was reviewed by  the primary author and then selected segments of v ideo matching our research question were viewed and analyzed by both authors. Member checking was conducted with both the facilitator and students in the case studies to ensure trustworthiness of interpretations and data. ## Discussion

Affordances of VR in expert-decision to guide novices:  Both cases highlight the flexibility inherent in th e program's implementation of VR, with videos introduced at different time points based on the experts' on-the-spot decisions. This acknowledges the practical constrai nts of integrating education alongside real work du ties. Both cases reveal challenges students face in developing  an intuitive understanding of bird behavior and st ress recognition. Specifically, VR afforded opportunities for expert and novice to engage in discourse around a shared representational focus, in essence functioning as an object with meaning potential in collaboration (e.g, Ludvigsen et al., 2015). For example, when Sam fixed his gaze on a specific aspect of the virtual reality video, Hannah used those sustained gazes as an opportunity to describe  and expand on the representations. The VR also all owed flexibility in addressing each novice’s specific ne eds. In addition, the decision-making flexibility demonstrated by the experts in introducing materials and VR comp onents at different points in the students' trainin g reflects a responsive approach to the contextual demands of th e program and the use of the VR to collaboratively make sense of important directions for learning. Thus, i t can be not ed that VR allows additional adaptability to guide novices.

Collaborative Learning and Practical Application:  Both cases reveal instances of collaborative reflections, with discussions extending beyond imme diate tasks. Interactions between students, facilit ators, and additional staff members create a dynamic learning environment where insights, observations, and quest ions are shared and leads to a broad affordance space (Gibson, 1979)  offering a range of action possibilities.  In episode 1, both Bart and Hannah support Sam’s engagement with the practice in different ways. The practical application of VR learning, coupled with discussions and extern al observations, prepares students for the transiti on from virtual to real-world scenarios, as evidenced by both volunteers no ting greater comfort when entering the aviary enclosure and the staff members choosing to use this artifact as a new tool to extend the range of expertise shared with volunteers. The shared experience of "coming b ack to reality" indicates the immersive nature of V R and its potential to create a bridge between simulated experiences and the actual responsibilities of animal caretaking.

## Conclusion

The illustrated episodes  provide valuable insights into the dynamic interact ions during VR-enhanced animal husbandry training.  By facilitating an immersive, interactive learning environment, VR technology enables a nuanced exchange of knowledge between novices and experts, enhancing the overall educational experience. Our study demonstrates how VR can tailor learning exper iences to individual needs, making explicit the tacit knowledge of experienced practitioners, and prepari ng learners more effectively for their workplace. T he integration of VR into wildlife training programs n ot only augments traditional teaching methods but a lso highlights VR technology's potential to flexibly bridge theoretical knowledge and practical skills.
