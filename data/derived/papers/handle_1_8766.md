# Measuring Belonging for Neurodiverse Computer Science Students

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Neurodivergent students can thrive in undergraduate Computer Science programs with support, but research on effective learning and teaching practices is limited. The neurodiversity movement advocates an approach to research and design that examines social (i.e., external) barriers to student success and seeks to remove or mitigate them. We suggest that this research must begin with productive measures of student success. This poster presents a survey instrument to measure neurodivergent CS students’ sense of belonging. Keywords: belonging, computer science, neurodiversity, research, intersectionality

## Introduction

Neurodivergent students (e.g., with autism spectrum disorder and other neurological differences) experience the world differently and often possess highly valued skills, such as creativity and systematic thinking (Baron-Cohen et al., 2009). These are especially valued skills in Computer Science (CS), such that major international employers like Microsoft now actively recruit neurodivergent people. But, with neurodivergence often comes disab ility, largely as a product of outside barriers that limit neurodivergent success (Kapp, 2020). Post-secondary programs, for example, are usually designed for neurotypical students (Fabiano et al., 2018), creating unnecessary challenges for neurodivergent students, who then struggle with  mental health, sensory processing disorders, and communication (White et al., 2011). Standard accommodations, even when provided, do not mitigate this problem (Sarrett, 2018). Most neurodivergent students drop out of four-year post-secondary institutions (Wei et al., 2014) despite high levels of interest in CS (Wei et al., 2013). CS teaching practices and learning environments can be designed for inclusivity, but in order to do this, there is a need for research to identify incongruities between student needs and existing practices. For example, instructors teaching facial recognition software co ursework in Artificial Intelligence classes may ass ume that students can, inherently, correctly interpret facial expressions. Neurodivergent students do not always possess this skill, and accommodations like time on tests or extended deadlines will not be sufficient. But what if instructors in this case taught about facial expression as well, or created collaborative projects where students were expected to examine their biases about facial expression int erpretation? This is just one example of an opportu nity to research student needs and design for better, more inclusive learning. We suggest that the first step research step in des igning for inclusivity for neurodivergence in CS is to determine reliable metrics of student success. Grad es and graduation rates are insufficient because th ey do not accurately reflect learning for neurodivergent students. Instead, we believe that a sense of belonging, a necessary factor for both neurodivergent and neurotypical students (Morrow & Ackermann, 2012; Vaccaro et al., 2015), can be used to formatively and summatively assess how all students are succeeding. Belonging for students with disabilities depends on—and in turn impacts—student s’ sense of mastery of their student role, ability to selfadvocate, and ability to develop relationships with  peers and faculty in their program. Accordingly, m easuring belonging requires measurement of these underlying variables. Table 1 provides examples of survey instrument questions for each. Additionally, an intersectional  (Collins & Bilge, 2020) perspective suggests the n eed for questions that acknowledge the complex experiences of discrimination and privilege students face. Women and many ethnic or racial groups remain underrepresented in Computer Science as a field, for example, but are also least likely to receive diagnoses and services for autism and other neurodivergent conditions. Existing instruments account for belonging among underrepresented studen ts in CS based on gender and ethnicity (e.g., Moone y & Becker, 2020), but to date, no survey instrument al so accounts for neurodiversity. In addition to meas uring mastery, self-advocacy, and relationships in a way that accounts for intersectionality (see Table 1, Relationships Example), measures of neurodivergent student success should also address demographic questions about whether students consider themselves a minority (Mooney & B ecker, 2020) or neurodivergent. There are many reas ons students may lack formal diagnosis, but still rely on additional supports that could be provided witho ut risk of unfairness (e.g., providing facial expression interpretation guides). Although most universities rely on a medical model of disability to determine fair access to sup ports, many support services recommend accepting se lfdiagnosis as evidence of student needs. Taking this approach in research will support identification of the ways that teaching practices and learning environments can change to meet the needs of all students.
