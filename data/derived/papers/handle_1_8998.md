# Eye Movement Tracking During an Embodied Collective Activity in Science Teacher Training

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Middle-school science teachers who trained in the field of embodied pedagogy for teaching and learning physics, experienced an embodied collective activity for learning the concept of angular velocity. We tracked the teachers' eye movements during the collective activity in order to characterize their gaze behavior. The findings show that the subjects looked ahead during the activity, in contrast to their verbal report but in line with studies showing that eye movements can precede body movements.

## Background

Neuro-pedagogy is an interdisciplinary field that integrates perspectives of the learning sciences and neuroscience. One of the main areas of this field is the study of teaching and learning strategies. Observing the brain can enable an understanding of the effects of different teaching environments and pedagogics. This is a central objective of the research presented in this poster. The pedagogical aspect of our research is based on embodied pedagogy for teaching and learning physics (Zohar et al., 2017).  The neurobiological aspect of our research is base d on eyegaze tracking to discover interesting patterns from the field of vision (Duchowski, 2002). A unique feature of this study is the use of tracking-eye  for investigating collaborative learning of complex concepts in body-full movement, which to the best of our knowledge has not been studied before. The concept of angular velocity and the embodied collective activity pertaining to it Embodied pedagogy for teaching and learning physics (Zohar et al.,2017) coheres with the embodiment paradigm that affected educational research (Abrahamson et a l., 2020). The assumption underlying this pedagogy is that actual physical experience can be used as a unique resource for learning complex concepts (e.g. angular velocity in physics) by associating them with daily bodily activities. Angular velocity is defined as the angular displacement of a body over time in a circular path. In Figure 1, we see an example depicting the concept of angular velocity and the embodied collective activity (“collective circular exercise”), which pertains to learning this concept according t o our approach. The angular velocity of points A and B moving from their respective point 1 to point 2 is the same because the segments connecting each of the points to the center of the circle advance at the same time at the same angle (see Figure 1a, the angle is 60°). In the collective circular exercise, the learners are asked to walk together around a bottle (see Figure 1b). Using trial and error, they negotiate, verbally and using gestures, a collective method to circle the bottle together. They move to keep the row intact with those farther away from the bottle who walk faster. ## Methods and Analysis

Thirty middle-school science teachers experienced the collective circular exercise described above, moving in 6 groups. The teachers were unfamiliar with the concept of angular velocity. This study examined the 12 teachers who stood at the edges of the moving lines (see Fig ure 1b). We collected data from video recordings and eye movements. We used Pupil Labs eye trackers, which w ere connected to a cell phone attached to the subje ct's clothing. This eye tracker device has two cameras for each eye and a head world camera (see Figure 2a). The collective circular exercise lasted approx. 30 seconds and data from 6 of the 12 subjects was reliable enough for analysis based on the Pupil Labs software. Video tracking analysis used the Pupil Labs software to track the subjects’ gaze points during the walking activity. Figure 2b shows a snapshot of the activity as viewe d by the subject standing near the bottle at a certain time. The green dot circled in yellow is this subject’s gaze point. The output from this subject’s two eye cameras is shown in small square images in the bottom right of the figure. The blue and yellow curved arrows indicate the directio ns of the teachers’ movement. Calculations using th e software’s output provided a measure of the distanc e gaze from the teachers’ walking line in each snap shot in which the gaze could be seen. The distance gaze from the walking line is represented by  an  azimuthal angle, which is a horizontal angle where the vertex is the subject’s head.

## Findings and Discussion

Looking-ahead during the embodied collective activity-The graphs in Figure 3 show the distance gaze of every subject from the walking line relative to time during the collective circular exercise. The four graphs at the top of Figure 3 pertain to the subjects who stood near the bottle, and the two graphs at the bottom of Figure 3 pertain to the subjects who stood at the end of the walking line. Dots on the horizontal axis represent a situation where the subject was looking at the walking line, so the measured azimuthal angle is zero. In calculating the average time the subjects looked at the walking line, we found t hat this was the case in only about 16% of the dura tion. This finding contradicts the subjects’ verbal report, st ating that they were constantly looking at the othe r teachers in the line. However, this coheres with studies showing that eye movements can precede body movements We used a binomial test to examine whether the subjects ind eed looked at the walking line for 50% of the activ ity time, and for each subject, the statistical test showed rejection at a level of p <0.05.
