# GenAI as a Peer Review Support: Impact on Feedback Quality, Quantity, and Student Perceptions

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Peer feedback is an efficient way to give students feedback while promoting their learning. However, peer feedback often lacks the depth and volume of expert instructor feedback. This study explored whether generative AI (GenAI) could be harnessed to boost the quantity or quality of peer feedback. In a crossover study, 29 college students wrote reviews of a peer team’s scientific paper with and without using ChatGPT to revise their reviews. When students used GenAI, their reviews contained more feedback, more explanations, and more positive and descriptive comments. Evidence suggests that GenAI was most helpful as a peer feedback support for certain topics – those for which students had low prior knowledge. When receiving reviews of their own work, students preferred GenAI-supported peer reviews compared to those written by peers alone, even though they could not accurately distinguish between them. GenAI shows promise as a feedback support tool when used alongside human peers.

## Introduction

Feedback is one of the most powerful tools for promoting learning. In a meta-analysis of educational interventions, Hattie & Timperley (2007) found that feedback had one of the largest effects on student achievement (d=.79). In fact, one could argue that it is impossible to learn any complex knowledge or skill without some sort of feedback (Schwartz et al., 2016). And yet, good feedback can  be hard to come by, largely because educators lack  the time to give it in sufficient depth, detail, or frequency (Wiggins, 2012). An elegant solution to this problem is to have peer s provide feedback to one another. Research demonstrates that peer feedback has positive effects on student learning, with meta-analyses showing that learners who receive peer feedback outperform those who  receive no external feedback on both cognitive and noncognitive outcomes (Double et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021). Moreover, studies have highlighted the dual benefits of peer feedback, as both giving and receiving feedback can promote learning (Wu & Schunn, 2021). Though peer feedback is an effective educational pr actice, it is not without its challenges. For insta nce, studies comparing expert feedback to peer feedback found that expert feedback includes more ideas, pro vides more detailed explanations, and offers le ss praise, whereas peer feedback often lacks depth and contains inaccuracies (Cho and MacArthur, 2010). Additionall y, peers’ limited domain expertise can hinder their  ability to produce high-quality feedback (Nelson & Schunn, 2009). These lim itations highlight the need for strategies that enhance the quality of peer feedback without imposing additional burdens on educators. Generative AI (GenAI) has unique capabilities to ad dress these challenges. Large Language Models (LLMs) can provide near-instant, extensive, and personalized feedback to students, reducing the workload for educators. LLMs are also adept at sparking new idea s (Joosten et al., 2024), suggesting they might als o inspire peers with new ideas for feedback. Furthermore, although LLM-generated claims must be verified for accuracy, these models draw on a vast knowledge base from billions of data sources and can provide domain expertise that may support novice students. Recognizing this poten tial, many scholars are harnessing the power of Gen AI to provide feedback to learners in a variety of new Ge nAI-supported feedback tools, which are currently being researched (Chien et al., 2024; Sandu et al., 2024).

Despite its promise, the use of GenAI in feedback is nascent, and early research has yielded mixed results. For instance, one study found that ChatGPT-generated feedback on student writing improved stud ent performance, task motivation, and positive emotions compared to human-generated feedback (Meyer et al., 2024). However, another study reported that ChatGPT-generated feedback was less effective than human fe edback in terms of accuracy, actionable suggestions, prioriti zation of critical features, and use of a supportiv e tone (Steiss et al., 2024). Still yet another study found that G enAI and human feedback had complementary strengths: while GenAI provided more descriptive feedback on writing style, peers were better at identifying specific problems in the work (Banihashem et al., 2024). Further research is needed to explore the potential and limitations of using GenAI to enhance peer feedback, and our study is a step in this direction.

To address this question, we chose to apply a “huma n-in-the-loop” approach, using GenAI to co-create peer feedback, leveraging the unique strengths of b oth GenAI and human peers while addressing key conc erns, such as GenAI hallucinations and potential misuse b y students. We hoped that having a human evaluate a nd selectively choose and adapt the GenAI feedback would reduce inaccuracies in the feedback. To stave off cheating or having the GenAI do all the productive cognitive work for learners, we asked students to f irst provide their own written feedback, then revise that feedback based on feedback suggestions from GenAI. Ultimately, we hoped this GenAI+Peer feedback model would enhance both the quantity and quality of peer feedback. The current study To test the efficacy of using GenAI as a peer feedb ack support, we contrasted two conditions:  GenAI+P eer vs. Peer-Only. We sought to answer the following research questions: ● RQ 1: How do students use GenAI to revise their feedback? ● RQ 2: How do students perceive the feedback suggestions from GenAI? ● RQ 3: How does GenAI influence the quantity of peer feedback? ● RQ 4: How does GenAI influence the quality of peer feedback? ● RQ 5: How does GenAI influence students’ perceptions of peer feedback quality?

## Participants

Participants were advanced undergraduate students e nrolled in a bioengineering design course at an elite university in the United States. Throughout the course, students worked in teams o f 4–5 members on design projects in diverse topics, such as genetically eng ineered bacteria to improve soil drought resistance  and implantable sensors to measure cardiac output in infants. Although 37 students participated in some portion of the study, this paper's analyses focus solely on data from the 29 participants who completed all parts of the study. The sample was reasonably diverse: 55% of the sample wa s female, 42% Asian or Asian American, 21% White or European, 14% Hispanic or Latino/a, 10% Black or Af rican American, 7% American Indian or Alaska Native, and 6% mixed race.

## Study design and procedures

This two-part, within-subjects experimental study compared two feedback support conditions: GenAI+Peer versus Peer-Only. The giving feedback part of the study  examined the impact of using GenAI to revise peer f eedback reviews. The receiving feedback portion of the study  investigated how the feedback condition influenced participants' perceptions of peer reviews. All feed back was directed towards  the methodological approach described in students’ bioengineering design papers.

Part 1: Giving feedback The giving feedback portion of the study used a cross-over design, in which each participant experienced all study conditions. This design is powerful because it allows us to compare how the same participants performed in both conditions. In the first session, participants wrote peer reviews and revised them either with or without the support of GenAI. Two weeks later, they switched conditions and gave feedback on a separate paper. Both sessions were conducted in-person during class, with an average duration of 27 minutes per session (SD = 11.0). Students were assigned to experience the conditions in a specific  order (GenAI+Peer-1st or GenAI+Peer-2nd) using random stratified assignment, ensuring that an equal number of students from each  team experienced the GenAI+peer condition first or second. Figure 1 illustrates the study design. The first feedback session addressed teams' “Milest one 1” paper, and the second, “Milestone 2.” Both outlined project methods, but by Milestone 2, stude nts had progressed further, often shifting from lit erature reviews and measurement validation (Milestone 1) to experimental studies and prototype testing (Milestone 2). Peer reviews focused on 3 core topics of the methods section in bioengineering design papers: ● Reproducibility: how well a skilled engineer could reproduce the methods described in the paper ● Congruence: how well the methods could help the authors achieve their research and design goals ● Rigor: how well the methods ensured the reliability and validity of results To ensure data privacy, our team developed a secure  interface that mimicked ChatGPT, submitted user prompts to ChatGPT 3.5, and responded with ChatGPT 3.5’s output. However, the data submitted to ChatGP T was anonymized and could not be used to train future models. In the GenAI+Peer condition, students read a peer t eam’s paper, wrote their own review, consulted our GenAI system for feedback suggestions, and revised their review as they saw fit. Students in the GenAI +Peer condition were instructed to feed their peer’s paper into our GenAI system and pose 3 prompts to solicit feedback on each of the 3 core topics described above. Promp ts were written by the first author and given verba tim to participants. As an example, the prompt about repro ducibility was: “ The methods section should document the methods with enough detail that a skilled engineer could understand the methods and reproduce them wit hout assistance. Offer constructive feedback on the effe ctiveness of the Methods section in achieving this objective. Describe strengths, weaknesses, and specific sugges tions for improvement if  needed.” Students were also encouraged to ask some of their own prompts and engage in discussion with the GenAI. In the Peer-Only condition, students followed the same steps to  construct and revise their reviews but did not use GenAI. They were given the same prompts that the GenAI+Peer condition fed into GenAI, however, they were merely asked to reflect on them and revise their reviews accordingly. They were explici tly told not to use any tools beyond their own head (no Googling, for example). Figure 1

## Measures and coding

Part 1: Feedback generation experience Participants answered several survey questions about their experience generating their peer reviews. Our analysis will focus on these 3 questions, which were posed of the GenAI+Peer condition only: ● Did ChatGPT influence your peer review? If yes, how so? If not, why not? (open-ended response) ● Approximately what percentage of your final peer re view contains text that was copied verbatim from ChatGPT? (response options ranged from 0-100% in increments of 10) ● Would you use ChatGPT in the future to get feedback on your own paper? (response options: yes, maybe, no) In addition, we logged all prompts and responses to our GenAI system. We analyzed these data to check whether students entered our given prompts and whether they posed additional prompts of their own. Part 1: Peer reviews Two teaching assistants from the course with expert ise in bioengineering design were trained to code t he peer review data. In collaboration with the first author, they developed a codebook. The two coders both se gmented the data and applied all codes to peer reviews in an iterative fashion, ultimately achieving good interrater reliability on all codes (Kappas ranged from.73 to.91). Peer reviews were coded for quantity and quality. To assess the quantity of feedback, peer reviews were segmented into “feedback units,” defined as “a cont iguous comment referring to a single topic, accompl ishing a specific goal, and adding a new piece of informatio n” (adapted from Nelson & Schunn, 2009). The length  of a feedback unit varied from a single clause to 2 sent ences. Feedback units were further coded as “accura te” or “inaccurate” according to the coder’s expert judgme nt. Only accurate feedback un its were included in counts of total feedback units, to ensure that our measures reflected reasonable feedback. Quality of feedback was assessed on several dimensions: inaccuracy, explanatory, and type. We counted the number of “inaccurate” feedback units to assess whether GenAI’s hallucinations might impact peers’ reviews. We also coded each “accurate” feedback uni t to determine if it was “explanatory” or not. Expl anatory feedback included an explanation of why the feedback was appropriate – a rationale or justification. Explanatory feedback is particularly effective at promoting learning (Chi et al., 1994; Vander Kleij et al., 2015), and we suspected that LLMs’ often detailed and extensive output might support peers in producing more explanatory feedback. Finally, we assigned each “accurate” feedback unit to a feedback type category. There were five types of mutually exclusive feedback type codes: descript ion, praise, critique, suggestions for improvement,  and questions. The praise, critique, and suggestions codes were loosely based on peer feedback coding work done by Nelson & Schunn (2009). Exploratory analysis of the feedback in our study yielded two additional feedback types: description and questions. See Table 1 for descriptions of each code. To examine how students’ reviews changed after our feedback intervention, we computed gain scores – the change in the total number of feedback units of  various types from pre to post reviews. Gain score s help to control for individual variation (for example in how much feedback different students tend to write) and variation across papers (some papers will have more things to critique than others). ## Results

RQ1: How do students use GenAI when revising their feedback? To get a sense for how students used GenAI to revis e their reviews, we started with a simple analysis of the prompts students entered into our GenAI system. We discovered that students rarely posed their own pro mpts. 90% of students asked the prompts we gave them and nothing more. This means only 10% of students (3 ou t of

29) posed their own prompt, and these interactions were limited to just one or two simple follow-up questions per

student (e.g. "Provide even more feedback" and "Can you explain how the two securement methods interact with one another? "). Students primarily relied on the structured pro mpts we provided rather than engaging in an extended dialogue with GenAI.

Next, we investigated how students integrated GenAI’s feedback suggestions into their review. First, we explored whether participants engaged in significant copy-pasting of the GenAI output. When asked what percent of their review text was copied verbatim from GenAI, about 76% of the sample reported copying 20% or l ess of their review text from GenAI. Another 17% reported copying between 30-50% of their review from GenAI. Only 7% of the sample (2 students) said they copied 100% of their review from GenAI.  These results suggest that most students were quite selective in their use of GenAI-created content, preferring to perhaps synthesize or incorporate small snippets of its feedback suggestions rather than adopt them wholesale. Our informal observations of student revision revea led a tendency toward additive rather than transformative changes from pre-to post-reviews, regardless of whether students were workin g with or without GenAI support. Rather than substantively revising t heir existing text, students often added new senten ces to the end of their reviews. This suggests that students viewed revision as an opportunity to add supplementa ry content rather than edit or restructure their original feedback. RQ4: How does GenAI influence the quality of peer feedback? Our analysis of peer feedback quality explored 3 di mensions: (1) inaccuracy, (2) explanatory feedback,  and (3) feedback type.

## Discussion

Students provided  more feedback and incorporated more explanations f or their comments when GenAI was involved. Thi s finding is promising, as previous research has shown that t he length of peer reviews predicts learning outcomes (Yu & Schunn, 2023), and there is extensive research on the learning value of both receiving and producing explanatory f eedback (Chi et al., 1994; Vander Kleij et al., 2015). GenAI also shifted the nature of feedback, increasing descriptive comments and praise. While some theories of feedback argue that negative feedback and actionable suggest ions are the most beneficial forms of feedback, praise has shown positive effects on both motivation and learn ing (Kluger and DeNisi, 1996). Moreover, d escriptive feedback can illustrate how reviewers interpret the  work, offering valuable insights to the author. This suggests that GenAI amplifies some key elements of peer review that drive learning, such as elaboration, justification, and motivation.

Despite concerns about GenAI-generated hallucinations, using GenAI as a support tool did not significantly impact the amount of inaccurate feedb ack in peers’ reviews. Perhaps our human-in-the-loop model worked as intended such that students were able to detect GenAI’s hallucinations and avoided including  them in their reviews. When used as a revision tool, GenAI can be a reliable adjunct to human feedback, preser ving accuracy while augmenting review quality.

But perhaps the most important evaluation of feedba ck quality comes from the students themselves. On several dimensions, students preferred reviews co-developed by GenAI and humans over those developed by humans alone. Students also had largely positive perceptions of GenAI’s feedback suggestions, and most students would consider using GenAI for feedback on their ow n work in the future. However, some students surfaced negative perceptions of GenAI’s feedback suggestion s, reinforcing the importance of maintaining a huma n-inthe-loop approach to ensure thoughtful engagement with GenAI feedback. This study also provides insight into when GenAI is  most effective in supporting peer feedback. GenAI had a greater impact on the quantity of feedback students wrote for particular topics. Our interpretation is that GenAI was more beneficial for topics where students had low prior knowledge. However, more research is needed to rule out alternative explanations and to test this hypothesis directly. A common concern among educators is that students might rely on GenAI to do their thinking for them. However, in our study, students rarely copied and p asted GenAI’s responses but rather selected key bit s of feedback or synthesized and distilled long sections of output down to a few sentences in their revised peer reviews. This suggests a complementary relationship where GenAI augments, rather than replaces human insight, making the feedback richer and more effective. This behavi or counters fears of over-reliance on GenAI and suggests a constructive role for these tools in student learning.

Finally, GenAI tools are rapidly evolving. This research was conducted with ChatGPT 3.5, but the latest ChatGPT models are even more reliable, accurate, and efficient. Student perceptions and use of GenAI a s a feedback support tool are likely to evolve as well. Continued research is essential to track these developments and refine best practices for integrating GenAI into the peer feedback process. Future research Future research could explore the generalizability of these results by examining larger and different student populations, domains, and course contexts.

This paper examined the impact of GenAI on peer feedback, but future research could delve deeper into how GenAI-augmented peer feedback influences student learning  outcomes. Does it provide fresh perspectives or encourage deeper thinking, or does it hinder learning by doing too much of the cognitive work? The role of AI literacy in peer feedback is also important. In our study, students rarely created their own prompts, suggesting the need to examine the effects  of student-generated versus experimenter-provided prompts and the impact of prompt-engineering training.

Comparing GenAI-supported peer feedback to traditional approaches, such as internet search, could reveal whether the feedback benefits of GenAI merely replicates what is already possible with familiar methods. We are now designing a study on the order of GenAI use. Students will either revise their initial reviews with GenAI or refine a GenAI-generated review. Results could inform optimal mode ls for human-GenAI cocreation.
