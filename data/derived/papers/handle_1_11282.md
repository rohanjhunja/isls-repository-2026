# Exploring Emotion-related Individual Differences in Adolescents' Daily Lives: An Analysis Using Large Language Models

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Diary methods can help us understand how adolescents recognize and manage emotions in their daily lives, which aids in optimizing social-emotional learning practices. This exploratory study invited 30 students to write their daily entries. These unstructured texts were then automatically coded with the help of large language models, which performed comparably to human raters. Importantly, two indicators (semantic similarity and emotional event alignment) were used to assess how an individual's feelings in specific contexts align with the common understanding of those contexts. We found positive associations between diary-derived indicators for positive entries and performance on emotion perception tasks, while students with lower semantic similarity for negative entries showed higher performance on the emotion regulation task, indicating self-regulation in negative contexts. The study highlighted the potential of using large language models to characterize students' daily emotions and preliminarily demonstrated how individual differences manifest in adolescents' emotional lives.

## Introduction

Adolescence involves substantial changes in emotional functioning, with vulnerability to emotional problems (Silvers, 2022). To design social-emotional learning practices during this critical p eriod, it is crucial to characterize how adolescents recognize and manage emotions, especially in their daily lives. Two methods are commonly used to characterize these emotion-related individual differences  (Austin, 2004). The first method relies on performance data from emotion-related tasks, in which individuals ' responses are compared to predetermined answers to assess their ability (Floman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2021). Another approach utilizes self-report questionnaires, asking people to reflect on and report their perceptions  (Petrides et al., 2007). While both methods have offer ed valuable insights  for understanding adolescents' emotion-related individual differences, they typically offer a holistic overview of these differences, making it difficult to cover more nuanced information about what happens in adolescents' daily lives. The diary method is an important data collection tool  for tracking daily entries (Scott, 2022). It is also suggested that it holds the potential to reveal emotional patterns related to individual dif ferences. For instance, researchers have used diary methods to track the dynamic process of an individual's emotions during the pandemic and found that higher emotional intelligence predicted lower intensity of  fear, anxiety, and sadness  (Moroń & Biolik-Moroń, 2021). Another study specifically explored the effectiveness of electron ic diaries in youth populations and suggested that such diaries could provide important information on emotional functioning (Suveg et al., 2010). However, much of the existing research has focused on the intensity and frequency of emotions, sometimes overlooking the contextual information related to the emotions. As widely acknowledged in social psychology, emotions are inherently context-dependent (Greenaway et al., 2018). It is necessary to anchor the emotion within the specific context that triggers it to achieve a thorough understanding (Dejonckheere et al., 2019; Lapate & Heller, 2020). Therefore, we aim to explore daily emotion al patterns by analyzing the contextual information of adolescents' daily entries in conjunction with the corresponding emotions. Nevertheless, reporting contextual information in diaries may generate unstructured text, which requires manual c oding—a process that is both time-consuming and resource-intensive.

L arge language models (LLMs) provide an efficient ap proach for processing unstructured data (Gilardi et al., 2023), e.g., sentiment analysis of written responses (Hur et al., 2024; Rathje et al., 2024). The present study aims to further examine the feasibility of using LLMs to explore emotion-related individual differences from adolescents' diary texts. Importantly, rather than separately categorizing contexts and emotions (e.g., categorizing contexts into academic, family, and school relationships, and categorizing emotions into joy, sadness, etc.), analyzing each context together with its corresponding emotions allows for a more nuanced understanding of the unique characteristics of each specific entry. This will also provide us with an opportunity to characterize individualized emotional responses that m ay be more accurately reflected in subtle contextual i nformation. Therefore, inspired by the semantic similarity analysis commonly used in natural language processing (Araque et al., 2019), this study employs an  "emotional event alignment" metric  to integrate LLM-rated emotion scores by computing the distance between context and emotion. We expect that this metric can show how individuals' actual emotions in a specific context (quantified by the emotional rat ings of the subjective feeling s) differ from the common understanding for this context (quantified by the emotion scores of the context if we consider the LLMrated scores as a generalized emotional reflection of the context). As a supplement, we also computed the semantic similarity metric  to directly capture semantic distances between contextual information  and corresponding emotions based on their embeddings, as the embeddings contain rich human knowledge (Grand et al., 2022). B y calculating semantic similarity  and emotional event alignment, we can integrat e the analyses of context and emotions for each entry. We hypothesize that these indicators  may be associated with individual differences in emotion-related tasks, as they quantitatively describe adolescents' emotional patterns that happen in daily life. For instance, when an individual's actual emotional response is more consistent with the emot ions expected to be triggered in an entry, it may indicate the person  c an more accurately perceive  the emotions involved. However, the relationship could be complex. For example, the high consistency with consensus does not necessarily mean a positive outcome, especially  in the case of negative entries. It may suggest that the individual is unable to effectively regulate and ma nage emotions, making them more susceptible to bein g influenced by negative entries. Nevertheless, as this study is still in its preliminary and exploratory phase, we seek to address two questions  focusing on early-stage insights  here: 1) Can LLMs rate the emotions expressed in students' diary entries? 2) Is the diary-derived indicator, which links the context of specific entries to corresponding feelings, associated with adolescents' differences in recognizing and managing emotions?

## Participants and data collection

Thirty first-year middle school students aged 15 to 16 years participated in the diary-based data collection and received small non-monetary gifts (e.g., stationery) as compensation. During the data collection, the content of the tasks was first explained to the students. Then, students filled out online emotion diaries for two consecutive weeks (M = 4.2 days, SD = 3.4 days). Each day at 5 PM (after school), students receive d the diary link on their mobile phones. They were asked to write about one negative entry and one positive entry and to describe the emotions they felt in each. For example, a student shared a positive entry, where the context was that their class was practicing singing together. The student reported feeling happy, satisfied, and connected to their classmates because of everyone's improvement. Students could submit their diary entries until 7 AM the next day. Any entries after that were not included. Students reported a total of 126 emotion daily entries. Before analysis, all data were anonymized, and any potentially identifiable information, such as the names of students mentioned in the entries, was masked. Students also participate d in emotion perception and emotion regulation tasks derived from Wang et al. (2021). In the emotion perception task,  students need ed to recognize emotions from faces, while in the emotion regulation task, students needed to choose the worst/best strategies to achieve emotion regulation goals. Their emotion-related individual differences were quantified base d on the corresponding task performance s, which were converted into z-scores. The emotion understanding task was not included due to technical issues. Text analysis based on LLMs To integrate the context information and emotions, this study use s two indicators for text analysis. The first is emotional event alignment. This yields an emotional rating vector for each context and a corresponding vector for students' feelings. The first vector can be regarded as a common emotional pattern triggered by the entr y, assuming that  GPT's scoring reflects human emotional consensus. ## Results and discussion

First, we visualized the LLM ratings for  context texts (positive and negative), as well as t he subjective feeling texts (positive and negative) on the 12 emotional d imensions. As shown in Figure 1, the LLM ratings indeed assign higher scores to the emotional dimensions that correspond to the respective emotion categories of entries. Figure 1 Bar Plots of LLM Ratings on the 12 Emotional Dimensions Here, the positive context (or feeling) indicated that the context was reported in a positive entry. The same goes for negative ones. The errorbar represents S.E.M.

Then, we calculated the correlation between LLM ratings  and human rat ings for further validation. As shown in Table 1, the scores from the LLM and human raters showed significant positive correlations, with high correlation coefficients across most of the emotional dimensions. This demonstrates the effectiveness of the LLM in automatic text coding on emotional dimensions, consistent with (Amin et al., 2024; Niu et al., 2024). However, it is important to note the relatively lower correlation coefficients (<0.4) for 'guilt,' 'curiosity,' and 'fascination'. A possible explanation could be that these dimensions  lack clear linguistic indicators, making them more  difficult to accurately infer from text, while emotions like 'sadness' are often expressed through more explicit  cues. Therefore, the consistency between human and LLM ratings in these dimensions is relatively weaker, suggesting the need to further improve accuracy.

Students' performance in the emotion perception task was positively correlated with their emotional event alignment scores for positive events, which was consistent with our hypothesis, i.e., an individual's feelings aligning with the ex pected emotions of an entry may indicate better perception and understanding of its emotional content. This finding was replicated in the semantic similarity scores with a lower coefficient. We also found that students with higher emotion regulation performance showed a negative correlation with their semantic similarity scores for negative entries. This might suggest that they could manage or downplay their negative emotional responses, leading to a reactivity that is less aligned with the typical patterns associated with negative entries. The resu lts are consistent with previous findings that repo rted individuals with high emo tion regulation abilities have decreased tendencies to experience negative feeling s (Mikolajczak et al., 2008). One possible explanation is that emotion regulation processes might influence students more broadly, affecting not only their emotional aspect of subjective feelings but also their cognitive and behavioral responses.  For instance, a well-regulated student might view the high-pressure context as a challenge rather than a threat, which can lead them to approach the context with a more proactive mindset, influencing their overall perception and response to the situation.  This richer information beyond emotions can be captu red by embeddings (Grand et al., 2022), unlike emotional e vent alignment, which focuses solely on the emotion al dimension of the text. However, it is important to note that emotional event alignment offers better interpretability, because of its clearer psychological meaning over s emantic similarity. Also, the difference between positive and negative entries underscores the necessity of considering entries with different valences when exploring emotionrelated individual differences in daily situations. ## Conclusion and future direction

In summary, the present study highlights the potential of using LLMs to characterize students' tendencies when dealing with everyday entries from diary texts and preliminarily demonstrates how individual differences manifest in adolescents' daily lives. Further efforts are needed to inform the development of tailored SEL programs that offer more personalized and effective interventions by detecting the emotional patterns unique to each student. In addition, it is important to be cautious about the hallucination issue  of LLMs, which m ay benefit from manual validation. Moreover, the preliminary and explorato ry nature of this study should be taken into consid eration given the relatively small sample size. Additionally, note that the Cronbach's alpha values of the task performances are marginally acceptable (around 0.7). F urther investigation is needed to  better capture emotion-related individual differences.
