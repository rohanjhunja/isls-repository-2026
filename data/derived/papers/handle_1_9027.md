# E-Textile Fashion: Designing Maker Activity for Chinese Migrant Girls

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We present a low-cost STEAM workshop for twenty-five Chinese migrant girls to learn electronics through making hats. Data sources include video recordings of all classroom activities and post-interviews of students. We analyzed post-interviews of all participants and then narrowed our focus to Meimei, a girl who expressed an interest in art design. We hope this study will inspire future designers and educators to design maker activities for local schools.

## Introduction

In the last three decades, China experienced the world’s largest rural to urban migration. Because migrant families do not possess an urban household registration, children from these families are excluded from full access to free compulsory education (Goodburn, 2015). Therefore, m igrant schools, usually funded by charities, become  an option for migrant children. Lacking stable funding for resources and qualified teachers, children in these schools likely do not receive an education as good as their peers in public schools (Lu & Zhou, 2013). Compounding the issue of unequal resource distribution, female stud ents in China experience additional documented ineq uities based on cultural traditions (Goodburn, 2015). With these inequity issues in mind, our project aims at providing more learning opportunities to those underrepresented students. More specifically, we would like to engage young migrant female students in making activities as a form of STEAM learning. Design Our workshop was designed for an internal grant wit h the goal to empower Asia-Pacific women in STEM education. We focused on the migrant girls in Shenzhen, China, because it has the most migrant population in the country. We  expect our making-oriented workshop to offer an entry point for migrant students to join local maker events. The design of our workshop referenced Buchh olz’s work (2014) that e-textile toolkits offer fem ales expanded access to materials and tools and provide with them opportunities to take leading roles in ad vanced technical activities. Our workshop consists of five sessions (60-120 minutes per session). Each session has explicit learning goals to reduce teachers’ concern that making activities are not directly connected to curriculum standards. In the first four sessions, students worked in group of two to design hats, sew circuits, and prepare posters in the innovation room. The layout of this classroom natur ally encourages group work, as opposed to tradition al classrooms that desks were lined up and facing the front. On the last day, they presented their projec ts to their peers, teachers, and Makers from the local community in an open space.

## Context and participants

We implemented the workshop in an elementary school  in Shenzhen, China. Twenty-five 5 th and 6th graders participated in this workshop. The workshop was led  by three researchers, who designed the workshop collaboratively. Students worked in groups of two or three to participate in four sessions of making activities. On the last day, students gave poster presentations to students and teachers in the participating elementary school and makers from the local community.

## Data analysis

We analyzed the post-interviews with the purpose to understand students’ design goal, and their motivation in the workshop, what difficulties they had and how they s olved problems they had during the workshop. With t he analysis, we found a girl, Meimei, a fifth-grade student, who explicitly demonstrated interest in desi gn and challenged advanced materials. Meimei’s story was the epitome of how 25 migrant girls engaged in our workshop. We applied video analysis on Meimei and her partner  Lei’s work in classroom. Based on Meimei’s intervi ew response, we focused on the difficulty Meimei faced  in classroom, and re-watched video of focusing on what Meimei’s team has learned through making, their att itudes towards making, and how they collectively wo rk on the project and solve problems.

## Results

In the post-interviews, seventy percent of the grou ps mentioned the most difficult parts were 1) calcu lating hat dimensions from measured head circumference  and 2)  sewing the circuit. And fifty percent of the group s recognized the importance of working with their teammates to solve problems collaboratively.  Meimei is one of the girls who explicitly said her dream is to “be a fashion designer” in the future, and she was very interested in fashion and design. Meimei was a member of the Art Club, and she was one of the few students who tried  to connect a tri-color LED in her in design. In Meimei ’s interview, she also mentioned that she faced sev eral problems like other students, such as measuring and  sewing the circuit. But her intrinsic motivation t o advance her design helped her to explore how to sew the circuit. Meimei’s case: sewing a circuit with her partner To better understand how Meimei was engaged with wo rkshop, we focus on Day 3,  the first day when we introduced circuits to students, with the goal to light up an an LED light on their hat. We found Meimei and her teamate Lei worked collaboratively to solve problem s and actively participated in the workshop. Throug h tinkering with the materials, they recognized the concepts of positive and negative ends in an electro nic circuit. In the beginning, Meimei’s group had difficulty connecting the circuit. Lei, Meimei’s teammate, sought help from teachers and peers, while having Meimei concentrate on tinkering with their toolkit. They both partici pated and allocated their time wisely. After getting brief fa cilitation and rounds of tinkering, Meimei started to sew the circuit on their hat, while Lei observed and offered verbal instructions. There was a moment that Lei pointed out that the circuit was in the wrong direction. Then s he pointed it out to Meimei. But Meimei was confident of the direction because she identified the negative end of the circuit. Here, Meimei used a formal science term, “negative end,” to refer to the direction of conductive thread, a concept she had never learned. Meimei mentioned the lights were in different shapes as she thought, but she se emed comfortable with the new materials. Applying f ormal science language to unfamiliar materials is a sign that Meimei started to understand the concept. Besides, during the workshop, Meimei and Lei were always collaboratively and actively participating in making. When they were sewing a circuit to their hat,  Meimei was in charge of sewing at the beginning. Lei soon nudged Meimei and said, “Let me sew, let me sew, let me sew.” Meimei secured the knot for Lei and passed her the needle. Even though only one of them was needed to sew the circuit, Meimei offered Lei help and observed her sewing closely. In their collaboration, they found ways to address each othe r’s feedback and suggestion. They also rotated role s so that they both had almost the same amount of time to try  working on the materials. There were also examples  of Meimei and Lei working closely together on their project in the video data, we hope to present more data in our final presentation.

## Significance

In this study, we designed a low-cost e-textile wor kshop for migrant girls in Shenzhen, China. Within the oneweek workshop, students worked in groups, designed a hat, and sewed simple circuits on their hats. In our postinterview analysis, young girls mentioned the works hop was “a meaningful learning experience for learn ing electronic circuit, sewing, and design” and “would love to introduce the workshop to our friends.” Our  focused analysis on a girl named Meimei demonstrated how sh e took the initiative in her hat design project, ov ercame difficulties, and collaborated with others. Our workshop provided girls with different learning experiences as they claimed. With our follow-up communications with the  participating school in 2021, the participating te achers were able to run another round of e-textile in the school. This also showed the potential to design ac cessible, meaningful hands-on integrated STEAM learning activities for young girls. We call  upon maker educators and curriculum designers in China and other countries who are interested in gender equity to consider learning through making and develop activities relevant to students’ local communities.
