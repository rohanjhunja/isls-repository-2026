# Critical Reasoning for a Changing Climate

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Amid climate disruption and contested digital information, this qualitative case study examines how upper-elementary students evaluate climate information and craft persuasive arguments. Twenty-two Grade 5–6 students from four Canadian schools participated in semi-structured interviews. Guided by critical information literacy (CIL) and socioscientific reasoning (SSR), interviews included a source-ranking task and an audience-focused persuasion scenario using institutional datasets (NASA/NOAA), local records, and personal/community narratives etc. Students privileged scientific and educational authorities, required traceability, and were skeptical of open platforms (e.g., YouTube) and influencer content; several also attended to domain-specific expertise. When persuading others, students layered personal/local stories with empirical evidence and tailored choices to audience identity and interest—early expressions of SSR. Findings indicate that brief, explicit prompts can surface and strengthen elementary learners’ credibility he uristics and rhetorical judgment. We argue for integrating CIL-SSR practices into elementary climate and science education to foster digital discernment, ethical reflection, and civic agency. Rationale and purpose Climate change remains a pressing, society-wide challenge that demands informed public engagement (IPCC, 2023; UNESCO, 2021). Yet the topic circulates within a crowded, often contradictory digital ecosystem in which claims range from rigorous to misleading and are shaped by platform logics as much as by evidence (Zeidler, 2014; Leu et al., 2017; Wineburg & McGrew, 2017). Preparing the next generation, therefore, requires more thandelivering climate facts; it calls for cultivating critical information literacy (CIL) so learners can locate, evaluate, and use information judiciously on contested issues (Kapitzke, 2003; Tewell, 2018). In parallel, because climate change is a paradigmatic socioscientific issue (SSI)— scientifically grounded yet ethically and poli tically charged—students alsoneed socioscientific reasoning (SSR) capacities to weigh evidence, consider perspectives, and communicate responsibly under uncertainty (Sadler & Zeidler, 2005). Although research documents CIL/SSR development among adolescents and university students, far less is known about how elementary learners evaluate sources and mobilize evidence in SSI contexts such as climate change (McGrew et al., 2017; Sadler & Zeidler, 2005). Early studies suggest that with explicit scaffolding, younger students can begin to question authorship and intent, check provenance, and justify claims with evidence (Breakstone et al., 2018; Gregory & Higgins, 2017). However, we lack fine-grained accounts of the processes by which upper-elementary students judge credibility across heterogeneous sources (e.g., institutional sites, news, social media, community narratives) and how those judgments translate into persuasive climate communicati on. Research purpose and research questions This study addresses that gap by examining how Grade 5/6 students evaluate the credibility of climate-related information and how they select, sequence, and justify evidence when tasked with persuading a peer. Guided by CIL and SSR, we analyze students’ cr edibility talk and argumentative choices during two interactive interview tasks (source ranking; audience-focused persuasion). The purpose is twofold: (a) to illuminate the early contours of CIL and SSR in elementary classrooms, and (b) to inform practical, developmentally appropriate approaches for embedding credibility evaluation and evidence-based communication within climate and science education. ## Design, setting, and participants

A qualitative, descriptive, interpretive case study (Merriam, 1998; Yin, 2018) examined how Grade 5 and 6 students evaluate sources and construct persuasive climate arguments. An interpretive stance attended to meanings co-constructed through interview dialogue.

The study involved 22 elementary students in Grade 5 (n=16) and 6 (n=6) from four schools in two provinces in Canada, one is a coastal province,  and the other one is in maintain areas. Before participating in the interviews, all the students in their school had learned basic knowledge about climate change, such as greenhouse effect, and local and global impacts.

Triangulated data sources included: (1) student artifacts (journals, posters, multimedia presentations) and (2) semistructured group interviews (primary data). Voluntary interviews (pairs/trios) began with an open prompt, followed by two tasks:

• Source ranking:  Students ordered cards (e.g., scientists, teachers, books, news sites, YouTube/social

media, celebrities, local/community narratives) by credibility and justified placements; an “other” card allowed additions.

• Persuasion scenario: Students selected and sequenced evidence to convince a peer that climate change is

real/urgent, drawing on NASA/NOAA indicators, NASA observations, local weather records, personal/family stories, news reports, social media posts, and global community narratives; they justified choices and sequencing.

Iterative thematic coding —open, axial, selective—was employed (Flick, 2006; Williams & Moser, 2019). We focused on CIL-aligned credibility heuristics (authority, traceability, platform skepticism, awareness of manipulation) and SSR-aligned persuasive moves (evidence selection/ordering, audience-aware framing). The 1433 research team  reviewed transcripts and codebooks, challenging interpretations; analytic memos documented decisions.

Theme 1: Credibility judgments are authority-and provenance-sensitive (with emerging nuance) Students generally anchored trust in recognized scientific and educational authorities. Scientists and teachers were ranked highest because they “ do research” and “teach us science,” and books used in class inherited credibility from teacher vetting. Yet this wasn’t blind deference: several students probed domain specificity—as one (Cris) put it, they’d want to know “what subject the scientist is working in… if not climate change… then I don’t know whether I should trust them.” This signals early attention to the relevance of expertise, not just its presence. Credibility judgments were often contingent on provenance and traceability. Students repeatedly asked to “see where they got it ” and requested the original article before accepting a claim. Some described informal lateral moves (checking who runs a site, looking for the same fact “ on NASA or UN”), indicating nascent crosssource verification. When a familiar person (friend/family) was the messenger, students separated relational from epistemic trust: the relationship alone was insufficient without a verifiable source. Open platforms (especially YouTube) were treated with qualified skepticism. Students flagged the difficulty of judging creators’ credentials and the risk that videos could be edited or “photoshopped.” At the same time, they articulated conditional trust for institutional channels (“ NASA on YouTube is different from a random YouTuber”), showing they were distinguishing platform from publisher. A few admitted being swayed, at times, by professional polish—then immediately cautioned that “ good-looking” doesn’t guarantee truth, revealing an awareness of appearance bias and a corrective impulse.

Across pairs/trios, credibility rankings were negotiated. Disagreements commonly turned on evidence of authorship, institutional backing, or whether a source cited data. Students sometimes elevated news sites when they were perceived to quote scientists or link to studies, but lowered them when stories lacked references. Several explicitly mentioned the possibility of fake or spoofed sites, advocating “look closely” before sharing—an ethical stance against passing along questionable content.

Takeaway: By late elementary, students can mobilize multiple CIL heuristics —authority, domain relevance, provenance, and platform-publisher distinctions —while remaining alert to manipulation. Their talk shows the beginnings of epistemic agency: a willingness to verify, withhold endorsement, and justify credibility judgments.

Theme 2: Persuasive communication is layered, place-based, and audience-aware When asked to persuade a peer that climate change is real and urgent, most students chose personal/local narratives to spark interest and empathy (e.g., beach erosion on Island; wildfire experiences in the Rockies). They framed these stories as making othe rs “feel what’s happening,” then layered them with empirical indicators (global temperature graphs, CO ₂ trends, sea-level rise) to establish scope and generality. Others preferred the reverse sequence—“ facts first, then stories”—to anchor the conversation before inviting emotional connection. Either way, students recognized that different evidence types serve different functions: narratives as affective/ethical warrants; data as epistemic warrants.

Students also demonstrated audience-aware tailoring. They selected examples based on a peer’s place (“If they’re from Alberta, use wildfire ”) or interest level (“ If they’re interested enough, I’ll show them more ”). Several acknowledged uncertainty and complexity (e.g., different places affected differently), yet still advanced actionable claims, often emphasizing credible sources (NASA/NOAA) and visuals (charts, maps) to increase salience. A few explicitly discus sed tone and ethics —avoiding scare tactics w hile communicating urgency— suggesting budding attention to responsible persuasion. S tudents used scaled reasoning, moving from lived experience → community impacts → global trends, to build coherence and momentum. They also described contingent strategies: switching the order of story/data when a listener was skeptical, or adding a second independent source to “double-check” a striking claim. Takeaway: Students are already composing multimodal, audience-sensitive arguments that bridge emotion and evidence —signature SSR moves involving evidence selection/ordering, perspective-taking, and ethical justification.

Theme 3: Critical evaluation remains vulnerable to affective framing and often depends on prompting 1434 Although students could articulate sound criteria for credibility, they did not always apply those criteria spontaneously to the materials in front of them. One pair, Aiden and Jade, was strongly influenced by the “mood” of a source: because the accompanying image looked “happy,” they initially inferred that the data being presented had positive implications for climate change. Their first reading rested less on the meaning of the information than on the affective tone surrounding it.

Only after interviewer prompting did they reconsider this interpretation and begin to separate the emotional framing of the source from the claim or data themselves. This distinction matters. It suggests that students’ evaluative talk was not yet fully internalized as an independent habit of mind. When tasks explicitly foregrounded credibility, students could name appropriate heuristics. Yet in multimodal, emotionally saturated contexts, they could still rely on surface cues as interpretive shortcuts.  Students were not simply successful or unsuccessful evaluators; rather, they showed emerging competence that coexisted with clear vulnerabilities. Their reasoning could be sophisticated, but also susceptible to manipulation through imagery, tone, and design, especially when no adult prompt interrupted their first impression. Takeaway: Elementary students can begin to engage in credible source evaluation, but they may still need structured support to notice when affective or visual cues are steering them away from the underlying evidence.

This study shows that Grade 5 and 6 students can enact foundational CIL and SSR when given brief, explicit opportunities to do so. Learners prioritized domain-relevant scientific and educational authorities, asked for traceability (“show the article”), voiced platform skepticism, and distinguished between publisher and platform. In constructing arguments, they layered personal or local narratives with empirical indicators and adapted sequencing to audience and purpose, reflecting core SSR moves of evidence use, perspective-taking, and ethical persuasion.

At the same time, the findings caution against overstating students’ independence. Some of their strongest reasoning emerged only after interviewer prompts, and some interpretations were shaped by the mood or surface presentation of a source rather than it s evidentiary content. The interaction involving A iden and Jade is instructive here: they initially inferred positive implications from climate-related information because the accompanying image appeared “happy,” suggesting that affective framing could st eer interpretation before the claim itself had been scrutinized. Students, then, were able to mobilize many important skills, but they were not yet consistently able to detect their own vulnerabilities without support. This nuance matters for climate education because contemporary information environments rarely present data in a neutral form. Claims are often packaged with images, tone, and design features that shape first impressions. Instruction, therefore, should move beyond teaching students who to trust to also teaching how sources persuade. Students need repeated opportunities to separate what a source makes them feel from what it actually shows, to inspect captions, labels, and provenance, and to corroborate interpretations across independent sources before drawing conclusions.

Although this was not a full curricular intervention, the semi-structured tasks made reasoning itself visible and suggest practical directions for classroom work. Credibility-ranking discussions, comparison of differently framed visuals, and audience-focused persuasion tasks can help students externalize and reflect on their criteria. These activities should be paired with modelling, guided questioning, and metacognitive prompts that help students revisit first impressions and notice when aesthetic or emotional cues are shaping judgment. In this sense, elementary climate education should treat critical evaluation not only as a cognitive skill, but as a practice of slowing down, checking interpretation, and resisting manipulation.

## Limitations and future directions

As a qualitative case in specific Canadian contexts, transferability is bounded. Explicit scaffolds likely amplified students’ displayed CIL/SSR and, in some cases, may have been necessary for students to notice weaknesses in their own interpretations. Typical classrooms may offer fewer prompts and fewer opportunities for such reflective talk. Future work should therefore examine not only how students reason with support, but also what they notice and overlook when evaluating multimodal climate information more independently.
