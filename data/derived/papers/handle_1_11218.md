# Learning in Productive Failure

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In Productive Failure (PF), students are prepared for subsequent instruction by activating prior knowledge and becoming aware of own knowledge gaps when generating solution attempts to an unknown problem. Intermediate knowledge after solution generation likely affords the subsequent integration of new knowledge, but has only seldom been assessed in composite instructional designs such as PF. We investigated how the learning processes and (intermediate) knowledge in PF vary when manipulating the PF tasks’ goal formulations (learning vs. problem-solving goals). As hypothesized, learning goals led to higher gap awareness prior to instruction, higher closing of gaps after instruction as well as better learning outcomes compared to the problem-solving goals. Moreover, broad knowledge activation during the first learning phase correlated with the intermediate knowledge state. Contrary to our hypotheses, knowledge activation and gap awareness did not mediate the learning outcomes.

## Introduction

Research on Productive Failure (PF) has established  that students who attempt to solve a problem prior  to receiving explicit instruction are able to build more structured conceptual knowle dge than those who engage in traditional tell-and-practice instruction designs (e.g., Loibl et al., 2 017; Sinha & Kapur, 2021). In composite designs such as PF, specific learning processes are assumed to prepare students for the subsequent learning phase, prompted by elements of the instructional design (L oibl et al., 2024). To allow for successful preparation in composite designs, students therefore need to show intermediate knowledge after the first learning phase that the second learning phase can build on (Loibl et al., 2 024). However, it is unclear under which conditions  learning processes during the first phase lead to an intermediate knowledge that optimally prepares students for subsequent learning. Loibl and colleagues therefore called for  further systematic studies that assess (1) the lin ks between students’ learning processes and evolving knowledge in composite instructional designs such as PF, and (2) how intermediate knowledge establishes the interconnect edness of multiple instructional phases. As a found ation for this systematic research and to achieve a better un derstanding of the inter play between instructional design elements, students’ learning processes and knowledg e building, they provided a framework for the cogni tive analysis of composite instructional designs (CID framework) (Loibl et al., 2024). On the instructional design level of the CID framew ork, the PF task during the first learning phase as ks students to generate their own, yet still erroneous, solution attempts to a given problem (Kapur & Bie laczyc, 2012). On the level of learning processes, we assum e that this task prompts students to activate their  own prior knowledge, become aware of the gaps in their knowledge, and realize first deep features of the t argeted concept (Loibl et al., 2017). The learning processes in thi s first learning phase help students to evolve thei r prior knowledge: students reach an intermediate knowledge  state that prepares them for the subsequent explic it instruction (Loibl et al., 2024). We assume that th is intermediate knowledge subsumes different types of knowledge such as (1) still activated prior knowledge from generating solutions, and (2) metacognitive knowledge that stems f rom students’ awareness of their knowledge gaps (se e also Loibl et al., 2024). It may also include negative knowledge (i.e., knowledge about how not to solve a problem or what is not part of the targeted concept), when students realize their current knowledge is no t helpful to solve the given problem (Loibl et al., 2024; Loibl & Rummel, 2014b). This intermediate knowledge then can be used as an anchor for the construction of ne w knowledge in the second learning phase (Loibl et al., 2024). In our analyses, we aim to investigate how students  need to activate their prior knowledge and become aware of their knowledge gaps during the first lear ning phase of a PF setting in order to show interme diate knowledge that prepares them for subsequent instruc tion. We manipulate students’ prior knowledge activ ation through the level of instructional design, providin g students with a task that either uses a learning or problemsolving goal formulation. With this, we expect to m ake changes in students’ learn ing processes (i.e, prior knowledge activation), evolving knowledge (i.e., intermediate knowledge), and target knowledge (i.e, conceptual knowledge after instruction) salient and contribute  to a better understanding of how learning mechanis ms and knowledge in PF composite instructional designs play together. Prior knowledge activation and intermediate knowledge in Productive Failure Prior studies suggest that students need to activat e prior knowledge broadly by generating a high numb er of conceptually different solutions (e.g., Kapur, 2015). Students’ generated solution attempts are regarded as a proxy for their prior knowledge activation (Sinha & Kapur, 2021). In a prior study, Brand and colleagues (2024) aimed to experimentally manipulate students’ prior knowledge activation and intermediate knowledge to investigate how prior knowledge activation affects students’ concep tual learning through their intermediate knowledge.  As students’ prior knowledge activation is difficult to manipulate, they asked students to study solution attempts (see Vicarious Failure, VF, as compared to PF; Kapur 201 4), which were comparable to the erroneous solution attempts students would generate themselves in a solution generation setting. The solution attempts varied in their coverage of conceptual components (i.e., referring to features of the targeted concept). Br and and colleagues

(2024) assumed that students activate the covered c onceptual components. They expected students who st udied

solution attempts with a high coverage of conceptua l components to show this coverage in their interme diate knowledge after the first learning phase. This high  coverage in intermediate knowledge should, in turn, prepare students for the integration of new knowledge durin g subsequent instruction (Brand et al., 2024). Alth ough they did not find a mediation effect, they confirmed tha t the intermediate knowledge and the conceptual kno wledge after instruction mostly corresponded to the conceptual components that were activated during the solution study phase. However, it remains unclear whether these findings also hold true in solution generation settings such as PF. Manipulating students’ prior knowledge activati on through their solution generation process  might lead to further insights into how students’ intermediate knowledge links knowledge activation and knowledge building. In solution generation  settings such as PF, students’ prior knowledge acti vation and intermediate knowledge is more difficult to manipulate. One way could be by prompting students to approach the prob lem in different ways, for instance, by providing students  with different goal  formulations. A study by Kapur (2015) suggests that the goal formulation of the prompt us ed during the first learning phase may impact stude nts’ prior knowledge activation. In the study, the goal formul ation affected the relevance of  solution attempts that were generated: students who received a problem-solving task generated more relevant solution attempts than students who received a problem-posing task (i.e., additional to problem solving, t hey developed own problems and questions for the same data set the other group received). Research in problem solving or inquiry research mor e specifically differentiated between different goa l formulations and their effect for students’ learnin g, for instance, by prompting students to adopt a l earning goal versus a problem-solving goal (e.g., Künsting et al., 2012; Wirth et  al., 2009). Learning goals, as opposed to the problem-solving goals (as used in PF), ask students to unde rstand the underlying structure of the problem (Künsting et al., 2012; Wirth et al., 2009). Thus, when generating solution attempts with a learning goal, students likely explore broad aspects of the problem. As students’ solution attempts are still erroneous prior to instruction and learning goals ask students to focus on understanding the problem’s structure instead of just finding a solution, students may gain a more specific awareness of know ledge gaps-or even build negative knowledge. Loibl and Rummel (2014) discuss that when students become aware of the gaps in their knowledge, specifying this gap may play a relevant role in their preparation for subsequent learning a s opposed to only showing global awareness of the limits of their knowledge. Specifying knowledge  gaps might support the building of negative knowle dge as part of students’ intermediate knowledge (Loibl et al., 2024) that can serve as an additional anchor for knowledge integration in subsequent explicit instruction. Thu s, both the still activated prior knowledge and a s pecific awareness of knowledge gaps form parts of an interm ediate knowledge that could help students to proces s the subsequent explicit instruction more easily (Loibl et al., 2024). In comparison, problem-solving goals likely prompt a more goal-oriented exploration (Künsting et al., 2012; Wirth et al., 2009), leading to a narrow prior knowledge activation and facilitates no or only a global knowledge gap awareness. ## Participants and design

We compared 206 secondary school students in a PF solution generation setting that differed concerning the goal formulations of the tasks that students received during the first learning phase (learning vs. problem-solving goal) (Post-hoc power-analysis, Gpower: α =.05, 1-β =.95, f = 0.25). Students in both conditions were asked to generate solution attempts to an unknown problem, but in one condition, the tasks’ goal was formulated as a learning goal (n = 104), in the other condition, it was formulated a s a problem-solving goal (n = 102). The original study employed a 2x2 design and additionally investigated  a second factor of goal formulation (i.e., goal sp ecificity: unspecified vs. specified goal). In this contribution, we focus on the factor goal type (learning vs. problem-solving goal). Participants were randomly assigned to one of the conditions.

## Materials and measures

We relied on materials from previous studies on the concept of mean absolute deviation in PF (e.g., Kapur, 2012; Loibl & Rummel, 2014b). Originally, the task was to find the most consistent soccer player based on a data set of three players’ scores across 10 years. We manipulat ed the tasks’ goal formulation: students who receiv ed a learning goal were asked to learn about a measure which selects the most consistent soccer player from a data set by providing solution attempts and reasons why thes e might help to solve the problem  (“Find out which mathematical measure you need to determine consiste ncy. Try to give a reason why th is measure is suitable to determine consistency.”). The problem-solving condition only needed to provide solution attempts and select their player of choice (“Find the most consistent player. Then mark the most consistent player.”). As control variables, we assessed students’ prior k nowledge on prerequisite concepts in a short test o n descriptive statistics, students’ mathematical abil ity based on their most recent grades, and mathemat ical selfconcept with eight items on a 6-point Likert scale (Rost & Sparfeldt, 2002). In the first part of the analyses, we used three de pendent variables: We counted students’ generated solution attempts, measuring students’ breadth of p rior knowledge activation during the first learning  phase. A second rater coded 100% of students’ generated solu tions, showing nearly perfect agreement in the numb er of solution attempts, ICC(2,1) =.92, CI [.89, 94]. Students’ global awareness of knowledge gaps after the first learning phase was measured with a questionnaire using seven items on a six-point Likert scale (Glogger-Frey et al., 2015). Students’ conceptual knowledge after th e explicit instruction was measured with a knowledg e test on the targeted concept of mean absolute deviation (Loibl & Rummel, 2014). In our mediation analysis, we additionally relied o n students’ self-reported behavior during the first learning phase to assess intermediate knowledge. As students only provided a report of their previou s behavior, we did not expect it to affect their subsequent per formance during instruction and post-test. We coded (1) the number of self-reported solution attempts as students’ still activ ated prior knowledge and (2) the  specificity of their knowledge gap awareness in their self-reports. For the specificity of students’ knowledge gap awareness, we developed a coding scheme (see Table 1) that differ entiated the specificity of knowledge gap awareness: 0 indicated no awarene ss of knowledge gaps in the self-reports, 1 a global awareness of knowledge gaps, and 2 a specific awareness of knowledge gaps. The coding of level 1 corresponded to the items used in the questionnaire on students’ global knowledge gap awareness (Glogge r-Frey et al., 2015). We added a third level, negativ e knowledge, in order to assess whether students alre ady formed knowledge that was based on their specif ic awareness of gaps (see Loibl et al., 2024 for more information on negative knowledge in composite instructional designs). However, this level did not appear in the data and therefore was omitted from the analyses. For the explorative analyses, we assessed students’  self-reported global knowledge gap awareness after the explicit instruction based on the same questionnaire that was used after the first learning phase (Glogger-Frey et al., 2015). We computed a difference variable from the global knowledge gap awareness after the first learning phase and after the explicit instruction indicating whether students’ gap awareness increased (negative values) or decreased (positive values).

## Procedure

The study was conducted within an out-of-school lab, in which entire school classes participated. First, all students filled in a prior knowledge test, reported their gr ades in mathematics (i.e., to assess mathematical a bility), and responded to a questionnaire on mathematical self-concept. Within each class, students were randomly assigned to the goal formulation conditions for the first learning phase. All students worked individually on the same problem for 40 minutes. Subsequently, students reported their global knowledge gap awareness in a questionnaire and summarized their behavior during the first learning phase (as a basis for the coding of still activated knowledge and specificity of knowledge gap awareness). After a 20-minute-explicit instruction on the targeted concept, students filled in a conceptual knowledge test, and reported global knowledge gap awareness for a second time. Statistical analysis To test hypotheses 1a-c, we calculate a MANOVA with condition as the inde pendent variable, students’ breadth of prior knowledge (represented by the number of ge nerated solution attempts), still activated prior k nowledge (represented by the number of self-reported solution attempts after the first learning phase), and their self-reported knowledge gap awareness (based on a questionnaire) as dependent variables. To test hypotheses 2a-c, a more indepth analysis uses a mediation model. The model in cludes condition as the independent variable (i.e.,  learning versus problem-solving goal), conceptual knowledge after the instr uction as dependent variable, and three mediators: the two serial mediators number of solution attempts during the first learning phase (M1) and number of self-reported solution attempts after the first learning phase (i.e., before the instruction) (M2), as well as a third parallel mediator, namely the specificity of knowledge gap awareness (M3). Students’ mathematical ability, prior knowledge, and mathematical self-concept were included as covariates in the mediation model. For the mediation analysis, we used the SPSS macro PROCESS (Hayes, 20 22; 5000 bootstrap samples, 95% bootstrap confidenc e intervals, alpha level = 5%, model 80).

With further exploratory analyses, we assess how students' global knowledge gap awareness changed over time, indicating whether students closed their  global gaps after instruction and overcame the fee ling of failure. For this, we calculate a n ANCOVA with repeated measures as within-subject factor and condition as between-subject factor. Measurements were taken at two poin ts of time: (1) at intermediate state after the fir st learning phase, and (2) after the explicit instruct ion. Students’ prior knowledge, m athematical ability, and mathematical self-concept are added as covariates in the ANCOVA. In exploratory analyses, we calculate Pearson corre lations between the difference variable of students ’ global knowledge gap awareness over time (i.e., changes from the intermediate state to the state after instruction) with their learning outcomes.

## Discussion and conclusion

We investigated how the goal formulation (i.e., pre senting a learning goal versus problem-solving goal) in a PF composite instructional design affected students’ l earning processes and knowledge building. For this,  we examined students’ prior knowledge activation in th e first learning phase and their intermediate knowl edge including still activated prior knowledge, global knowledge gap awareness, and the specificity of this knowledge gap awareness. We expected that lear ning goals would facilitate students to activate pr ior knowledge broadly during the first learning phase and at the intermed iate state and would lead to a higher global knowledge gap awareness. We assumed students’ prior knowledge act ivation during the fir st learning phase and at the intermediate state as well as students’ specificity  of knowledge gap awareness to mediate the effect o f the goal formulation on learning. Additionally, we explored how students’ global knowledge gap awareness changed from intermediate state to after instruction.

Contrary to our hypothesis, students with learning goals did not activate knowledge more broadly than those with problem-solving goals. Also, breadth of prior knowledge activation during the first learning phase was not related to students’ learning outcomes. This al igns with previous findings in PF research, indicat ing that generating a high number of solution attempts is not sufficient as preparation for subsequent learning (e.g., Loibl & Rummel, 2014a). However, we found that rather than the number of solution attempts students generated during the first learning phase, the breadth of still activated knowledge at the intermediate state was relevant for learning. Thus, it is more important for students to represen t their generated solution attempts in their interm ediate knowledge and only then it prepares them for subseq uent instruction. This is substantiated in our mediation analysis, which found that students’ activation during the first learning  phase predicted what they represented in their intermediate knowledge. In this regard, our findings match the results previously found in Vicarious Failure settings (Brand et al., 2024).

However, no mediation effect was found as goal form ulation did not specifically affect the breadth of prior knowledge activation. Rather, learning goals led to higher global knowledge gap awareness and le arning outcomes. As students in the learning goal condition were encouraged to provide reasons for the validity of their solution attempts, they likely processed the proble m’s structure more deeply, spending more time and e ffort on understanding their solutions and how these helped (or did not help) solving  the problem instead of generating high numbers of solution attempts. As students are not expected to solve the problem prior to instruct ion (Loibl et al., 2017), this exploration likely led to a ben eficial experience of failure: students became awar e of t heir knowledge gaps but were able to close their gaps after instruction. Students in the problem-solving condition were asked to provide a solution to the problem and sele ct the most consistent player of their choice, whic h did not necessarily lead to an experience of failure as all students were able to provide at least one solution attempt. Thus, they displayed lower awareness of knowledge gaps af ter the first learning phase. Previous studies show ed that it is vital for students to experience failure and not  to overestimate their own competence during the fi rst learning phase in order to be prepared for subsequent instruction (Loibl & Rummel, 2015; Nachtigall et al., 2020). Indeed, students' knowledge gaps stayed constant after inst ruction in the problem-solving condition, indicating that students in the problem-solving goal condition were less successful in closing their gaps. Learning was associated with lower knowledge gaps after instruction and clo sing of knowledge gaps, therefore explaining the di fference between the learning goal and problem-solving goal conditions in conceptual knowledge. Overall, our analyses present two innovative findin gs for research on composite instructional designs such as PF. First, intermediate knowledge has seldom been assessed in prior studies; we were able to provide first evidence for the importance of intermediate knowled ge for students’ learning, as proposed by Loibl and colleagues (2024) in the CID framework. We also sub stantiated descriptive findings by Brand and collea gues

(2024) that students represent knowledge activated in the first learning phase in the ir intermediate knowledge.

Secondly, prior studies in PS-I have exclusively employed tasks using problem-solving goals, our findings highlight the potential of learning goals, which fo ster learning and knowledge gap awareness, likely t hrough further explorations of the problem beyond its surface features, whereas problem-solving do not necessarily foster students’ experience of failure and knowledge gap a wareness. This aligns with previous studies that have found students in PF to largely focus on solution generation (Brand et al., 2025) and only seldom specify their knowledge gaps (Lee et al., 2024). Indeed, we found that without specific guidance, students were unlikely to build negative knowledge by themselves, but rather experienced glo bal knowledge gap awareness. Future studies should focus on the role of students’ negative knowledge for their intermediate knowledge and learning and further investigate students’ learning processes when provided with learning goals instead of problem-solving goals.
