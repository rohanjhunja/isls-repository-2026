# Oral Exams as Social Practice: Questions, Joint Remembering, and Co-construction

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We adopt a sociocultural perspective to explore a case of oral assessment in a Norwegian secondary school. Our analysis, drawing from video data of a classroom session and subsequent oral examinations in a Norwegian school, underscores that oral assessments serve as more than just tools for measuring content knowledge: they enable epistemological practices specific to talk-based interaction. We argue that oral assessments allow students to dynamically enact and negotiate their subject-specific knowledge with support from an examiner. Indeed, this is an assessment format that is inherently relational, as the examiner and students co-construct meaning and draw on shared background knowledge to display competence. As such, the potential of oral assessment is not an inherent trait but needs to be realized in situ. This research thereby contributes an empirical investigation of oral assessment practices, highlighting their potential benefits and implications for educational contexts.

## Introduction

The choice of assessment formats in schools mirrors underlying epistemological conceptions, or what it really means to know something (Knight et al., 2014).  Often, assessment formats are considered in terms  of whether they measure what they claim to measure and their aptness for conveying mental representations and processes that are not outwardly visible (Pellegrino, 2022). We offer a different perspective on student assessment than that of seeking student knowledge in static inner representations. Rather, we employ  a sociocultural perspective on how students apply and negotiate subject-specific knowledge interactively and dynamically  in one type of assessment format: oral assessment. Prior work on assessment from a sociocultural perspective has highlighted a need for assessment formats that display how studen ts dynamically enact knowledge when using artefacts while interacting with others (Poehner & Lantolf, 2005). The sociocultural perspective itself considers knowledge and memory to be mediated processes (Vygotsky, 1978) and focuses on how culturally produced artefacts (e.g., forms of talk, signs, and symbols) shape and are shaped by humans’ engagement with others (Daniels, 2010). Drawn from a larger corpus of video  data, this study depicts a 90-minute classroom session in which a teacher introduces a specific topic before moving on to analyzing how this specific topic was talked about during oral examination months later. Through interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Sidnell & Stivers, 2012), we argue that oral assessment is more than just a tool for measuring learning: it involves epistemological practices, that is, ways of using knowledge, acquiring knowledge, and applying knowledge (Sawyer, 2022) that are distinct from talk-based interaction.

A sociocultural perspective on oral assessment practices Sociocultural perspectives have brought increased awareness to the ways learning contexts are collectively shaped through the utilization of conceptual, cognitive, a nd physical tools (Daniels, 2010; Hood et al., 1980). Here, we follow a line of work on interaction and learning that explores how society and the mind are mutually constitutive by investigating co-constructional processes, such as the joint creation of a stance, interpretation, skill, or emotion (Jacoby & Ochs, 1995, p. 171). This view on learning and knowing stands in contras t to the way assessment has often been practiced in schools, with a focus on students’ ability to internalize knowledge and reproduce it without use of external resources (Black & Wiliam, 1998; Collins & Halverson, 2018). This represents a current problem, as education systems on all levels struggle with aligning social and collaborative learning with assessment (Boud & Bearman, 2022). The Vygotskian tradition emphasizes surpassing one’s current proficiency level with support from others, and assessment situations may be designed to assess students ’ capacity to interact with their environment, apply concepts to solve problems, and exceed their current mastery level (Poehner & Lantolf, 2005). However, this does not mean that  classroom assessment formats  are sociocultural or cognitive  in themselves. Theoretical perspectives may aid the design of an assessment type, but they are also pertinent in directing attention and interpreting performance  during assessment. For example, many assessment formats rely  on student s’ memory without a theoretical conception of memory practices. From a sociocultural perspective, it is relevant to pay close attention to how students interact with t heir environment and how  joint remembering is done using artefacts and dialogue—a conception that contrasts with conceiving memory as an individual, mental phenomenon (Mäkitalo et al., 2017).

Student assessment inherently relates to institutional contexts, and the notion of  learning outcomes is now an integrated part of educational policy across the Western Hemisphere and an important tool for governing educational systems (Prøitz, 2010). The concept of learning outcomes has a somewhat metaphorical undertone as a static end product of learning rather than a dynamic and interactive concept (Hontvedt et al., 2023; Prøitz, 2010). Nonetheless, how learning outcomes are treated and employed in schools is an empirical question: rather than being set measures for competence, learning outcome s from a curriculum are artefacts that are negotiated in interaction (Silseth et al., 2023). Like in other international education systems, Norwegian teachers are often held accountable for reaching a set of predefined learni ng outcomes on the one hand and for designing assessments that support student agency and learning on the other. This causes a predicament for many teachers because they must balance thorough evaluation of competence with relevance to real-life contexts. This presents a fine line that requires investigation of  how such complex issues are handled in situ, as  the assessment format influences classroom learning activities as well as students’ conceptions of knowledge. Oral exams in Norwegian secondary schools Norway has a strong tradition of oral assessment during secondary school  (Vonen et al., 2023; Wiig, 2023). Students undergo high-stakes oral and written exams in at least one schoo l subject after year 10 and potentially several oral exams in upper secondary school. The i nstitutional goal of these exams is to assess students’ competence within a school discipline, such as Norw egian, mathematics, or science. As such, oral exams assess students’ content knowledge in school subjects and their oral skills more generally. In addition to content knowledge, current Norwegian education reforms place an increased focus on core elements, defined as “central concepts, methods, ways of thinking, areas of knowl edge, and forms of expressions in the subject” and that have connections to recent developments within the learning sciences (Sundby & Karseth, 2022, p. 433). Oral exams are not standardized tests but take the format of a subject conversation between the student and an internal examiner (normally the student’s teacher). An external examiner, normally a teacher from another school district, also attends the exam, and in the event of disagreement about the student ’s grade, the external examiner is given primary rights to decide  on the final grade (Forskrift til opplæringslova, 2006, § 3-28; Solem et al., 2023). While there are national guidelines on how to conduct an oral exam, most relate to the administration of the exams, with limited guidance on how teachers  should assess students’ performance. The examiners  have primary responsibility for designing the questions,  as well as evaluating students’ performance, and g uidelines concerning the actual examination are restricted to the rather vague formulation that the  examination should provide students with “the opportunities to show as  broad competence as possible” (Norwegiandirectora te for Education and Training, 2019).

Prior research has focused on which competence Norwegian oral exams measure (e.g., Bøhn, 2016; Kaldahl, 2019) and how students and teachers experience oral exams (e.g., Kvifte, 2011; Maugesten, 2011). Oral exams have been found to vary substantially in time use and the parts of the curriculum that they cover (Syverud, in review). However, only a few studies have looked  at how oral exams are realized as actual social practices. Recent conversation analytic studies  have shown that the examiner’s conduct influences student performance (Sandlund & Sundqvist, 2011; Sikveland et al., 2021; Skovholt et al., 2021; Vonen et al., 2022). Oral examinations are also limited in terms of what types of competence s students candemonstrate within this format, as they are more suited to test content knowledge than interactional competence (Vonen et al., 2023). In this study, we shed light on how Norwegian oral exams draw on shared classroom experiences through analysis of video data from classroom activities and oral exams. We pose the following research questions:

• How do oral examinations provide opportunities and challenges for students’ displays of knowledge?

• What are the implications of acknowledging oral assessment as social practice?

## Methods and research design

This study is part of the  project Learning Outcomes across Policy and Practice (LOaPP), which examines the implementation of learning outcome –oriented education policy in school s. Our research was conducted in three lower secondary schools located in different parts of Norway  over 18 months. The data collection  involved multiple stages, starting with document analysis, participatory observation  and interviews at the three schools, followed by video observation in four ninth and tenth grade classes. This last phase generated a total of 107 hours of video. For this paper, we zoom in on a 90-minute Norwegian language lesson that took place in February and a full day of oral exam s in May involving the same students. We selected this particular lesson because it dealt with a topic that was frequently addressed in the o ral exams. The case is not chosen as a best practice example, but because it enabled us to analyze the interaction between the same teacher and students in a classroom setting and in the roles of examiner and students at the oral exam. The data were analyzed in depth using interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Sidnell & Stivers, 2012), which allowed for a detailed examination of the relation between a topic introduced in a teaching session that later became the topic in the oral examination. Based on this analysis, we purposefully dr ew three extracts  that represent different ways that the same topic was introduced and discussed during the exam. Our findings cannot be generalized to how oral assessment in schools usually unfolds, but we argue that the extracts highlight central dilemmas and issues participants in this social practice need to address in situ. The study was conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines  and received approval from the Norwegian Centre for Research Data.

Little Miss Eye Flap The target session was dedicated to the book and fi lm adaptation of Little Miss Eye Flap, a story about a young woman caught between two cultures. The students engaged in a combination of teacher-led classroom discussions and group discussions where the teacher circulated between groups. Figure 1 A Depiction of the Learning Design for the 90-Minute Little Miss Eye Flap Session As displayed in Figure 1, the 90-minute session about Little Miss Eye Flap  started with the teacher introducing the story, highlighting some possible key concepts for analysis, and presenting the task on the digital blackboard. The students then worked in collaboration to answer the teacher’s task: “investigate topics, messages, and values in Little Miss Eye Flap.” The tasks related to learning outcomes in the curriculum, such as to “read and analyze a wide selection of texts in different genres and med ia in Bokmål and Nynorsk and communicate possible interpretations” and to “describe the interaction between aesthetic devices in composite texts and reflect on how we are influenced by sound, language, and images” (NOR1-05, 2013). While discussing Little Miss Eye Flap, the students wrote responses to the tasks on an online notice board used in their schools. This was the end product for the lesson, available to all the students as a shared object that they could return to throughout the semester. After the teacher’s introduction to the task, the class engaged in group discussions, while the teacher provided assistance to the groups. During both rounds of group discussions, the teacher responded to questions from the group s. On several occasions, she oriented the students toward subject-specific concepts and how to use them when analyzing Little Miss Eye Flap.

There is a preference in oral exams for student ini tiative, and when students elaborate on a topic, the teacher tends to actively support this, typically using continuers such as “mhm” to signal to the student that they are doing well and should continue talking (Sikveland et al., 2021). However, in line 19, the examiner challenges the student to specify the choices Haq made when making the movie. Here, the examiner relies on their common experiences in the classroom, urging the student to “remember” a speci fic example presented in the session about Little Miss Eye Flap (lines 19–20). The teacher repeats (and clarifies) the initial question to redirect the student’s explanation  from the general content of the film to a more anal ytical approach, allowing the student to demonstrate the competence the question demands. She then narrows down the question, prompting the student to reflect on the connection between the book and the film. We see here how the examiner executes epistemic authority  to take responsibility for ensuring the student’s display of “valid knowledge” during the exam. The examiner relies on their profe ssional expertise and understands that for the student to demonstrate hig h competence, mere content summaries are not enough. As such, she signals that subject-specific terminology and the ability to be specific  about cinematic techniques are necessary. The shared classroom experiences enable her to direct the student toward a specific example where the student candisplay “the interaction between aesthetic devices in composite texts,” as expressed in the curriculum. In this case, the specification is successful, as the student “remembers” the exact example from the text. In our analysis, we found several examples of how teachers’ use shared classroom experiences to frame open and more specific questions in oral exams.  This can be viewed from two different perspectives. First, the student’s response does not provide a valid representation of her actual knowledge, as the teacher significantly cont ributes to the answer. The student did not introduce the answer on her own; instead, she showed that she could respond as long as the teacher guided her in the right direction. In this way, the  teacher's reformulation of the question act ed as a scaffold, allowing the student to demonstrate competence that she otherwise could not reach.

## Discussion

This analysis has highlighted the interactive nature of oral assessment and shown how teacher-student interaction shapes students’ opportunities to enact subject-specific knowledge. The first research question was: How do oral examinations provide opportunities and challenges for students’ display of knowledge? Our analysis presented three extracts of teacher-student interactions in which they discussed Little Miss Eye F lap. The first extract demonstrated that shared classroom experiences were used to frame questions in the oral exams and that the student was able to thematize texts and topics related to their prior work. The second extract displayed a frequent practice during the exams: the teacher suppor ted the student’s elaborations on subject knowledge and also guide d her into more in-depth explanations with the use of subjectspecific concepts.  The third extract revealed how the teacher elicited an answer to a specific question before entering a mode of co-construction when the student need ed support. In all extracts, the teacher used memories from previous shared knowledge as a resource. We saw how the teacher uses terms like “remember” and “can you think of” when talking about Little Miss Eye Flap as an entry point for eliciting in-depth conceptual knowledge. During the teaching session described in Extract 3, the teacher and students discussed “in medias res” and the “retrospective teller technique,” as well as how the film utilizes these specific techniques to portray Little Miss Eye Flap’s personal development. The teacher drew upon these shared experiences when posing questions, and they also lay ground to statements such as “I knew that you knew it” (Extract 3, line 9). Based on how the teacher invited the student to thematize texts and  how the students and teachers jointly reconstruct ed prior experiences, we argue that that the fundamentally interactional characteristic of the situation provides opportunities and challenges for the students’ display of competence.

In terms of opportunities, open-ended questions invite student participation and agency. For example, in Extract 1, the teacher invited the student to choose a text to talk about,  albeit within some topical boundaries. Furthermore, oral exams allow students to enact subject-specific academic practices by using knowledge interactively in a dialogue. The student and teacher negotiate what is relevant and the correct knowledge to display in this setting, as well as  re-construct prior shared experiences. In this complex  interaction, the subject’s key concepts and content knowledge interplay with the s tudent-teacher relation ship. Thus, our study reveals that learning outcomes in oral exams cannot be seen as predefined end products, but rather as co-constructed through practice. Furthermore, in light of connecting student collabo ration and assessment (Boud & Bearman, 2022), the oral exam offers student s the opportunity to draw upon their prior discussion s with peers, as well as display the ability to partake in discussions and us e specific concepts dynamically in interaction with the teacher/examiner. In line with prior research, we see learning outcome formulations as key  policy tools within this interaction (Hontvedt, et al., 2023; Prøitz, 2010; Silseth, et al., 2023). In terms of challenges, oral exams are vulnerable t o issues such as teachers' lack of sensitivity towa rd students' individual ways of communicating and weak rela tionships with the students. We argue that it is cr ucial to acknowledge the teacher-student relationship in oral assessment. While it i s possible to imagine ways to minimize the impact of relational factors in oral a ssessment, such as using external evaluators who lack a close relationship with the student, teachers who are fam iliar with their students may be better positioned to help them demonstrate their knowledge and learning outcomes. Personal feelings, past experiences, and preconceptions about the student do influence the assessment process. However, since education is inherently relational, we argue that these challenges necessitate increased examine r sensitivity rather than act as a reason not to pe rform oral assessment.

Our second research question was: What are the implications of acknowledging oral assessment as social practice? Acknowledging oral assessment as a social practice involves sensitivity to  how students engage in dialogue, build upon examiners’ comments, and enact subject-specific concepts in their argumentation. Our analysis featured the different ways teachers and students refer to previ ous classroom experiences during an oral exam and displayed how these shared experiences are  re-interpreted and re-contextualized in the exam  context. Opportunities to thematize different topics, voice opinions, and exert agency that are salient in oral exams should not be conceived as a stable and inherent trait of the assessment format in itself, but as potentials that need to be realized in situ. For example, students may have trouble exerting the  agency that open-ended questions provide (Skovholt & Solem, 2023; Vonen et al., 2023). As the competence assessed is achieved through inte raction, the examiner is evidently a co-participant and co-constructer of the competence assessed (Sandlund & Sundquist, 2011). This requires the examiner to acknowledge how they influence students’ opportunities to demonstrate their competence and the level of sensitivity needed to best support the students. Our theoretical framework highlights that humans actively participate in shaping possibilities for thought and action through speech and in turn are shaped by their interactions in the wor ld (Daniels, 2010; Hood et al., 1980). As such, the teacher/examiner and student s engage in an epistemological practice in which they co-construct and re-contextualize prior experiences and expand on them in relation to the larger knowledge structures of the discipline.  In this regard, the focus on core practices in addi tion to content knowledge  in curriculum and policy documents (Sundby & Karseth, 2022) shows a possible avenue to combine student agency and assessment. For example, t he examiner’s orientation to concepts in all the extracts enabled her to elicit subject-relevant knowledge while still leaving it to the students to thematize different texts and voice their own opinions. Similar to Poehner and Lantolf (2005) we found that the interaction between learners and assessors can be vital to getting a full picture of what students are capable of. To this backdrop, we need more research on oral exams, and how co-construction may be acknowledged and topicalized during exams and in the internal and external examiner’s grading. Future research could explore the recent developments in video and AI  technologies to ensure fairness and transparency in oral assessment. However, considering the deeply relational nature of oral assessment, we  believe such technologies should build on a more extensive foundation of empirical researc h on today’s practices for doing oral assessment. Related, research could explore questions of external validity to the way knowledge is used in out-of-school conversations. It is key to consider the framing of oral assessmen t, and how to increase reliability and transparency, without losing relevance to talk-based interaction outside school.

In conclusion, no assessment format encompasses the  whole picture of student learning, though oral assessment provide s some distinct affordances in terms of being an inte ractive situation in which students are positioned as active contributors in using knowledg e. However, the se affordances are inherently linked to the relational traits of th is assessment format. Having a firm and shared concept ion of the oral assessment between the students and examiner is key to realizing its potential.
