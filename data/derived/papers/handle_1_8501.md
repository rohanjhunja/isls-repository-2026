# An Exploration on the Individualistic and Collective Moves in Relation to Equitable Participation in Collaborative Game-Design Processes

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Collaborative learning environments are not inherently equitable. Learning opportunities might favor some group members over others and an inequitable collaborative learning environment would emerge. To promote equity in participation structure, we explore the relationship between individualistic/collective patterns and (in)equitable participation of one group of three girls. We analyzed a 75-minute session of game design using Bloxels and revealed the complexity in the nested relationships between individualistic and collective moves and (in)equitable participation structure for learning groups. We further discuss the implications for supporting an equitable learning environment.

Designers often work in collaborative teams to solve design problems (Brown & Wyatt, 2010). Integrating design into learning environments could provide ple nty of opportunities for children to develop collaborative competence. The current study takes place in such c ollaborative design learning environments for child ren where student groups were engaged in a game-design activity. Collaborative learning is a complex phenomenon that involves cognitive, socio-emotional, metacognitive, socio-cultural, and historical aspects where individual and social processes play critical roles. For this reason, collaborative learning processes are often inequitable, with some group members having less access to learning opportunities than others. The understanding of collaboration as a complex phenomenon calls for a research method that looks at the collaborative process from iterative relationships between individualistic and collective moves (Gillespie & Cornish, 2009). Thus, there is a need to move beyond individuals or groups as units of analysis toward identifying individualistic and collective learner moves and their effects on collaborative learning processes and outcomes more generally (Borge & Merc ier, 2019). Each individualistic or collective move  can contribute to the overall social process to amplify or attenuate the learning opportunities for individuals or the group. As such, we answer the following research question:  what is the relationship between individualistic/c ollective patterns and (in)equitable participation in small g roup collaborative learning? To answer this questio n, we conduct interaction analysis to first explore the different  individualistic and collective moves, and then ana lyze how those moves help or hinder the equitable participation from all members.

## Methods

In the afterschool program, small groups of 3rd to 6th graders were tasked with solving design challenges. One activity was to use Bloxels to design, play, and share their  games using various tools, such as paper and penci ls, iPads, and Bloxels kits. Student designers began by using grid paper or the Bloxels kit to create game elements such as characters, backgrounds, and bonuses. Then, designers needed to use the iPad Bloxels application to scan sketches or the Bloxels board to import, edit, and store designs, and further link game elements together. In this paper, we analyzed Group 4 (G4) of three 4th-grade girls– Sadie, Serena, and Evelyn (pseudonyms), as they were sketching, importing, and editing designs in the Bloxels application during Session 3. We conducted multimodal interaction analysis, dra wing upon Jordan and Henderson’s (1995) and Jewitt et al.’s (2016) works. We identified “hotspots” of individual istic and collective moves as G4 interacted with each other and with tools and artifacts. We analyzed discursive practice (Stahl, 2006), spatial practice (Xia & Borge, 2020), and mediational tools/artifacts (Wertsch et al., 1993). Two types of spatial practices were examined: 1) the participation struc ture of the group members (i.e., the bodily posture  that group members assume, how they position themselves) and 2) the spatial organization of tools and artifacts (i.e., how and where the tools and artifacts were placed in relation to the participants), as who was using the tools and producing the artifacts for how long and where these tools were placed and by whom are key to understanding the inequity issues. ## Findings

We found six discursive and tool-based actions and practices associated with individualistic moves. Discursive actions included 1) seeking teacher/facilitator support bef ore asking or problem-solving with groupmates, 2) r efusing collaborative work invitations and insisting on individual design, and 3) not take responsibility to communicate ideas clearly to the group. In relation to tool-based practices, 4) tools and artifacts are physically placed in ways that offer partial or zero access from other group members, 5) individuals dominate the use of tools or artifacts, or 6) members fight over the use of tools and artifacts. Overall, individualistic actions did not contribute to the group design process nor create opportunities for group members to learn  and grow. For example, seeking out facilitator sup port without first seeking group support did not create prevented opportunities for collaborative problem-solving. Also, dominance over iPad use, created inequitable participation. However, it is possible to transform these individualistic actions into collective moves. In the case of seeking help from facilitators, if the facilitator or other group mem bers address the problem in front of the whole group, it can create opportunities for collective reflection and problem solving. Also, if the group member who insists on doing individual design can find ways to incorporate their designs into the collective design, they can still contribute to the group design process. However, individualistic actions need early intervention or else risk developing into more problematic patterns detrimental to collaboration. We found ten discursive and tool-based actions and practices associated with collective moves. Discursive actions included, 1) actively engaging each member in social interactions; 2) actively bringing members to the group design process by explaining what they have done, or propose/accept joint working; 3) actively self-disclosing: group members speaking out loud about what they work on; 4) actively seeking feedback or patiently waiting for input from each member, integrating all members' ideas into the group design artifacts; 5) proposing rules or social scenarios that are inclusive to all ideas; 6) actively volunteerin g expertise in technology or design; and 7) activel y volunteering perspective or information, positioning oneself as a valuable group member and her knowledge as resour ces. Toolbased practices included, 8) placing tools and arti facts in ways that offer access from other group me mbers, 9) respecting other member's access to the tools, and 10) positioning themselves in ways that grant easy participation. These collective moves favored collective sense-mak ing processes and indicated inclusive intentions. N onetheless, some collective moves could inadvertently lead to i nequitable participation. For example, if one membe r’s active discursive moves did not get uptake from others, th ey might retreat into individual work. Also, we rec ognize that volunteering expertise is important for the volunteer in developing identity as a capable collaborator and designer, but in one example, one group member volunteered her expertise multiple times, and the other member responded, “yeah, and you just want to use my TURN,” which showed how this move barred the other from engaging. In this session, we describe an instance from the d ata to further explain different types of actions a nd their relations to inequitable participation in the small group. Session 3 began with a brief introduction b etween the three girls. Sadie and Serena had worked together in prev ious sessions, but Evelyn had not. Sadie took Evely n’s feelings into account when introducing the existing designs to her, saying “you’re gonna be mad at us [herself and Serena], but we kind of have a lot of designs.” Presenting shared ideas with Evelyn created an opportunity for her to become an equal contributor, leading to continued reasonin g within the group throughout the activity (Mercer,  1996). Collective moves such as this provided a foundation for team equity and effective collaboration. However, around 9 minutes into the session when Evelyn introduced the story block that she had designed, Serena, who did not understand the story block process, quickly asked for the facilitator without consulting teammates first. This was one of the many times that Serena used this individualistic move; s he often sought facilitators and did not rely on he r teammates as reliable resources. Evelyn nevertheless responded t o Serena’s question after the facilitator responded, which was a collective move to help ensure the team understood her meaning of the term “story block.” Even though groups might encounter problems that may eventually require exte rnal help, problems are learning opportunities wher e members can turn to each other as valuable resources (Simps on et al., 2017) and allow for more creative proble m-solving to occur. Seeking early facilitator support eliminates this potential learning opportunity. Furthermore, this individualistic move could potentially lead to trust issues as othe r members do not feel valued as intellectual equals  (Edmondson, 1999). Halfway through the session, the group showed a drastic change from distanced interactions with individualistic moves to closer interactions with the three working  together, displaying the complex relationship betw een individualistic and collective moves and the participation structure, shown in the episode. ## Discussion and conclusion

This study examined the specific interaction moves while focusing on the equity issues in participation structures. Our group of three girls showed less individualistic types of moves than collective ones. In general, collective moves are encouraged since these moves are oriented towards achieving group level learning processes. However, our findings show that not all collective moves equate to equity. For example, while we encourage help offering and  seeking to foster peer learning and group solidarity, if this type of learning behavior is not coordinated well a nd one group member constantly offers expertise by taking away the critical tool, i.e., the iPad application in this case, and directly working on the tool, this takes away other members’ opportunities to learn. One suggestion in this case is to go beyond encouraging students to help each other, but also sensitizing them in terms of how to offer help such that all can learn from the experience.

We also want to highlight that in the illustrative episode, one student dominated the use of the iPad but then transformed the individualistic pattern into a coll ective one by consciously adjusting the tool and ar tifacts while incorporating every member’s idea. Though the commonly used digital technologies, such as personal computers or mobile tablets, are easier for individual users given that it is designed for single users, our group of three girls showed their transformational agency in using tools that h elped them break free from the given limits of tool s. As such, we believe that in collaborative learning, student age ncy needs to be considered at the group level, whic h requires the members to be aware that the way they and the tools are positioned could be inclusive or exclusive and thus they need to be cognizant of each other and their group work.

All these findings are important to facilitators in such learning environments. For example, while facilitators might naturally identify the help seeking from group members to be desirable and showing their willingness to learn, our findings indicate that this might compromise the group’s problem-solving opportunities. As such, facilitators are encouraged to be more cognizant of when and how to offer their own expertise. In future work, it would be interesting to factor into gender and conduct analysis on all m ale groups or mixed gender groups to see if there a re other individualistic or collective moves that are not ca ptured in the current study. Also, conducting a lon gitudinal study such as following the same group over one semester and even longer would yield insights into how children learn to balance their individual and group efforts to achie ve equitable participation and, more importantly, h ow facilitators can help ensure the goal of achieving equity.
