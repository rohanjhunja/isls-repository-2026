# Youth Climate Storytelling as Transformative Learning: A Scoping Review of Designs, Contexts, and Digital Practices

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This scoping review synthesizes 30 peer-reviewed studies (2014– 2024) on youth storytelling in climate change education, illuminating how storytelling approaches foster transformative climate change learning. Findings reveal that storytelling functions as relational infrastructure while relating climate complexity into lived experience, cultivating eco-emotional awareness and agency, and sustaining Indigenous and community knowledge. Across formal and informal settings, educators and youth partnered to co-create stories that merged data, emotion, and cultural meaning. Digital tools such as participatory video, multimedia, and augmented reality expanded access and impact but also raised questions of equity and authorship. By mapping designs, scaffolds, and digital practices, the review offers design implications for research-practice partnerships that nurture belonging, imagination, and civic participation through climate storytelling. The need and the goal As the climate crisis intensifies, educators face an urgent challenge: how to cultivate young people’s capacity to understand, feel, and act in relation to a rapidly changing world. Traditional climate change education often privileges scientific facts, da ta, and predictive models but rarely engages the emotional, cultural, and ethical dimensions necessary for sustained transformation (Walsh, 2022). Transformative climate change education reframes learning as an ethical, relational, and action-oriented process that centers justice, hope, and collective capacity (Leite, 2024; Sezen-Barrie et al., 2025). Within this reframing, storytelling emerges as a powerful pedagogical and epistemological approach that binds scientific sense-making with identity, emotion, culture, and community (Gottschall, 2012). Storytelling makes climate data meaningful, futures imaginable, and collective agency possible (Bentz & O’Brien, 2019; Bloomfield, 2024). It allows youth to relate abstract climate concepts into local experiences and shared narratives of resilience, repositioning them as authors of change rather than passive recipients of information. Yet, despite growing interest, the field lacks a compr ehensive synthesis of how storytelling is designed, scaffolded, and studied across learning contexts. Moreover, as digita l media and participatory technologies proliferate from personal storytelling platforms to augmented reality, the need for design frameworks that ensure equity, authenticity, and purpose becomes more pressing. This study conducted a scoping review  to map and synthesize research on storytelling in climate change education, examining how storytelling approaches have been used across diverse contexts. A scoping review was appropriate because it “explores the breadth or extent of the literature, maps and summarizes the evidence, and informs future research” (Tricco et al., 2016). The review followed the methodological framework of Arksey and O’Malley (2005), with refinements by Peters et al. (2020), including stages of identifying, selecting, charting, and analyzing studies. Using content analysis and constant comparative techniques (Creswell & Creswell, 2018), we coded for recurring themes such as research design, instructional scaffolding, digital storytelling tools, and representation of Indigenous knowledge. Descriptive frequencies  were calculated to identify dominant patterns (e.g., qualitative versus mixed-methods studies), while cross-case co mparison highlighted theoretical and methodological gaps across contexts.

The analysis of 30 peer-reviewed studies reveals distinct patterns in how storytelling is designed, implemented, and studied within climate change education. The findings are organized into three major areas: research designs and learning contexts, the functions and scaffolds of storytelling, and the use of digital tools to support inclusive and transformative learning.

Designs and contexts Across the 30 studies reviewed, 22 (73%) employed qualitative research designs, 4 (13%) used mixed-methods approaches, and only 1 (3%) relied solely on quantitative methods. The remaining 3 studies (10%) were nonempirical conceptual or review articles. Learning contexts were diverse: 10  studies (33%) took place in formal education settings such as secondary and university classrooms, 17 (57%) occurred in informal or nonformal spaces including youth media labs, outdoor programs, and community theater, and 3 (10%) operated in hybrid environments that bridged school and community learning (Kumpulainen et al., 2023). Participant numbers varied widely from small design-based studies with fewer than 20 participants to large-scale, multi-country initiatives such as Di Blas (2022), which engaged over 41,000 students and teachers across 18 nations. This variation reflects both intensive and scaled models of storytelling integration. Geographically, the research was most concentrated in the United States (n = 9) and the United Kingdom (n = 5), with additional representation from Europe, Oceania, Latin America, Africa, and Asia. Several projects intentionally centered marginalized or underrepresented youth, including Māori and Pacific students in Aotearoa New Zealand, Native American learners in the U.S., and urban youth in Guatemala City (Littrell et al., 2020; McMeeking et al., 2024), demonstrating a growing global and equity-oriented scope in the field of climate storytelling research.

Functions, scaffolds of storytelling in climate education Across the 30 studies reviewed, storytelling in climate change education serves several intertwined purposes that shape how learners think, feel, and act in relation to the planetary crisis. Four key functions emerged: translating complexity, constructing identity and eco-emotions, sustaining cultural resilience and Indigenous knowledge, and fostering futures thinking and civic imagination. Each of these functions show how stories operate as tools for sense-making, self-making, and world-making.

Storytelling first functions as a way of translating complexity, making abstract scientific concepts tangible and personally meaningful. Narratives help learners connect data and theory to lived experience, bridging cognition and emotion (Adamson et al., 2021; Bell et al., 2024; Di Blas, 2022). For instance, multimedia storytelling in Climate Explorers translated global climate systems into local experience, while youth filmmaking linked data visualization with personal narrative. Such practices demonstrate how storytelling transforms science into a lived phenomenon, fostering relational and systems-based understanding. A second function centers on identity formation and eco-emotional engagement. Storytelling offers young people a way to navigate emotions such as anxiety, grief, and hope while constructing a sense of agency and belonging (Finnegan, 2023; Marks et al., 2023; Plummer et al., 2022; Walsh & Cordero, 2019). In participatory storytelling programs, youth expressed climate distress creatively and collectively, turning fear into civic agency. Educators who embraced narrative reflection found that attending to emoti on deepened learning, highlighting that climate literacy is as much affective and ethical as it is cognitive. Third, stories sustain cultural resilience and Indigenous knowledge, preserving ecological wisdom while resisting epistemic erasure (Fernández -Llamazares & Cabeza, 2018; McMeeking et al., 2024). Indigenous and community-based storytelling foregrounds interdependence and reciprocity, reframing science as a relational rather than extractive practice. In Māori and Pacific contexts, youth used stories to reconnect ancestral values with present-day climate adaptation (McMeeking et al., 2024). Such approaches model decolonial pedagogy, centering local sovereignty and plural ways of knowing.

1879 Finally, storytelling nurtures futures thinking and civic imagination, empowering learners to envision alternative worlds. Through speculative, place-based narratives—plays, podcasts, or augmented stories —students explored sustainable futures rooted in community and creativity (Kumpulainen et al., 2023). These narrative experiments transform imagination into participatory design for change, cultivating hope as a civic practice. To support these narrative purposes, about half of the studies employed deliberate instructional scaffolding. Educators integrated digital tools (e.g., climate datasets, films, augmented reality apps) to connect storytelling with inquiry (Van Borek et al.,  2024), and used strategies such as participatory video, multimodal composition, and “letters from the future” prompts (Finnegan, 2023; Walsh & Cordero, 2019). Two design frameworks exemplify formalized narrative pedagogy: Seidel et al.’s (2023) Science-in-Action Storytelling Model links inquiry cycles to narrative turning points, while the STORCIT framework—Storytelling in Citizen Science — offers a co-designed model emphasizing inclusion, authenticity, and reflexivity in public engagement (Veeckman et al., 2023). Structured around five phases: setting the scene, generating knowledge and learning, sharing personal narratives, developing stories,  and exhibiting them publicly. STORCIT illustrates how storytelling candemocratize science and make citizen participation more inclusive. A significant portion of the corpus (n = 23) also explicitly engaged Indigenous representation and epistemic justice, positioning storytelling as a process of co-governance rather than extraction (Leeuw et al., 2017). Such works advocate community authorship and knowledge sovereignty (Whyte, 2017), reframing Indigenous communities as co-theorists and collaborators (Littrell et al., 2022; Walsh & Cordero, 2019). Use of digital tools in storytelling Digital storytelling emerged as a central theme across 18 of the 30 reviewed studies, illustrating how technology enhances engagement, emotional connection, and inclusion in climate change education. Three main approaches were identified. The first followed the Center for Digital Storytelling (CDS)  framework (Lambert, 2013), emphasizing short, first-person, emotionally resonant narratives that support science learning, creativity, and agency (Walsh & Cordero, 2019; Finnegan, 2023). These programs helped students express climate emotions and communicate complex ideas through concise, reflective digital stories. The second group adopted broader, multimedia approaches—students created videos, podcasts, posters, web-based projects, and augmented reality (AR) stories to explore environmental relationships and local impacts (Bell et al., 2024; Kumpulainen et al., 2023; Van Borek et al., 2024). These projects fostered innovation and ecological imagination, positioning youth as knowledge producers and community storytellers.  The third approach involved learners engaging with curated digital stories or interactive documentaries to cultivate empathy and global awareness (Horta Parreira, 2022). Across all approaches, digital storytelling proved particularly effective in addressing emotional and ethical dimensions of climate learning. However, scholars cautioned that without equity-focused design, digital tools risk reproducing existing inequities. Overall, digital storytelling offers a powerful, participatory pathway for connecting scientific understanding with cultural and emotional meaning. This review underscores storytelling as a catalytic practice for co-creating knowledge, relationships, and agency in climate change education. Across the corpus, storytelling functioned not only as a method of communication but as a relational infrastructure for learning: it translated scientific complexity into lived meaning, fostered emotional resilience, and elevated cultural and Indigenous ways of knowing. The reviewed studies reveal that partnerships thrive when youth are positioned as co-authors and co-researchers, educators as facilitators of care and creativity, and communities as repositories of wisdom and possibility. Digital tools further expand these partnerships, linking local narratives to global audiences through multimedia, participatory video, and augmented reality. However, findings also caution that technology alone does not guarantee inclusion. I ntentional design, equitable access, and ethical co-authorship are essential. The most impactful projects integrated reciprocity, authentic voice, and partner ing with purpose, reflecting the ICLS 2026 vision of research that “unites technical and socio-cultural expertise to create more impactful, connected learning experiences.” In sum, climate storytelling offers an integrative model of learning that partners cognitive rigor with cultural empathy and collective imagination. It invites educators and researchers to move beyond transmission toward transformative collaboration, where  stories become both the medium and the outcome of purposeful partnership in navigating a changing climate.
