# Characterizing Maker-Material Encounters in Collaborative Creative Making

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The article aims to contribute to the understanding of relational dynamics of maker-material assemblages in collaborative making. In this study, we examine the encounters between makers and material entities in design problem solving scenarios. We specifically look at data from two collaborative making contexts: 1) a medical device innovation camp, 2) a maker activity-centered workshop, to characterize maker-material encounters and argue the emergent nature of creative making. Our analysis suggests that makers and materials engage in various forms of conversations where maker-material audits occur, maker-material synchrony shapes up, material configure collaboration, and materials exhibit epistemic characteristics.

## Introduction

Making is inherently non-linear progressions of sociomaterially entangled opportunities for engagement with the world, knowledge building and creative movements. B eing open innovation spaces, makerspaces are conduc tive to the gathering of makers, sharing of knowledge, i nflow of ideas and material resources, development of meaningful artifacts. The collaborative emergences taking shape in thes e network of actors is conceptualized to be spread across social, material and temporal dimensions of the creative phenomenon (Sawyer & Dezutter, 2009). As per the notion of “design as conversation with the situation” (Schön, 1983), makers continue to be in iterative cycles of conversation with the making context, and  materiality of the context “talk back” to makers. In these “talk backs”, agency is not centered around makers but in unfolding states as the maker-material configurations take shape in dynamic modes (Suchman, 1987). As makers and materials collide, the mangle of practice involving agential exchanges can result in being, doing, and becoming material conve rsations (Gravel & Svihla, 2021). Thereupon, materials cannot be considered as dormant entitie s (Pickering,  1993), not only responding to the actions performed by makers but also directing the makers to creative actions. Decentering humans from the sole source of actions, the posthuman approaches can bring forth the vitality of materials in the events of learning and creative phenomena. This point of departure from th e humanistic accounts of practices can reframe the human and non-human encounters enabling the reconsideration of emergent relationships (Barad, 2007). With this study, we look at characterizing the maker-material entanglements by following the ways in which makers and materials come and make together, by specifically examining data from two collabora tive making contexts: 1) a medical device innovation cam p, 2) a maker activity-centered workshop. The medical device innovation camp was attended by medical, des ign, marketing, and engineering domain exper ts, whereas the maker workshop was attended by second-year mechanical engineering undergraduate students.  We consider making as a collaborative practice between human and nonhuman entities, in which material actively eng ages with the movements of the maker.

## Methods

For this study, we looked at two collaborative making contexts: 1) a medical device innovation camp, 2) a maker activity-centered workshop. The five-day medical device innovation camp was organized by  the Biomedical Engineering & Technology Incubation Centre at a leading engineering institute in India. The organizers facilitated team formation to form a total of 16 teams around a predefined set of twenty medical problems generated by multiple doctors. The audio and video data along with field notes and observation logs for day 1 and day 2, of two teams-Team A and Team B-formed the data set from this setting, as the rest of the data was not available due to hard-disk storage failure. Team A consisted of a doctor-Subject Matter Expert (SME) (male), designer (male), mechanical engineer-M.E  (male), and an electrical & electronics engine er-E & E. Engg (female). Team B consisted of a doctor-Subject Matter Expert (SME) (male), designer (male), software engineer (male), and market expert (male). Teams were provided with knives, scissors, screwdri vers, hand drill, pillar drill, pens, pencils, rulers, drawing chart papers, plastic sheets, tapes, glue, sticky notes, breadboard, LEDs, jump wires, battery. The one-day maker activity-centered workshop was organized at a leading engineering institute in India. The participants comprised eight second-year mechanical engineering undergraduate students who responded to an open invitation. Four teams-P, Q, R, and S-were formed based on the order of their response wi th each team consisting of two members.  After the introductory session, participants were g iven a design challenge to conceptualize and model a semi-automated assembly line with static and dynamic r obots using the  resources available in the makerspace. A facilitator (F) was present during the making sessions for technical support. Team P consisted of one female student (G1) and one male student (B1).Two male students (B2, B3) constituted Team Q, Team R had two male students (B4, B5) and Team S  with two male students (B6, B7). The workshop was conducted at a makerspace with a work table, a whit eboard, a computer, and a desktop FDM 3D printer. T he teams had access to two Lego Mindstorms EV3 kits, a 3D printing pen, knives, scissors, screwdrivers, cardboards, pens, pencils, chopst icks, play-doh modeling compounds, cable ties, tapes, glue, st yrofoam sheets, used cables, wires, defective electronic devices like earphones.

We used the content logs and making segments of the data set to conduct interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) with a posthumanist turn, emphasiz ing maker-material agency (Svihla et al., 2020).  By considering the posthumanist turn, we looked at the  “material turns” in the flow of events, and ways o f participations where makers and materials come together in non-hierarchical fashion.  We viewed the assemblage of data sources – videos, transcripts, photographs and field notes-simultaneously and iteratively. We identified ‘significant moments’ for analysis by looking for d iscussions on design decisions, participants talkin g with and through materials, ideational dialogic exchanges be tween participants, and shared agency (Gravel & Svi hla, 2021). The fine-grain micro-engagement details derived using interaction analys is helped in consolidating and following specific patterns of maker-material entanglements (see Table 1), which are projected at the macro level as emergent themes (Braun &Clarke, 2006).

## Discussion and conclusion

In this article, we have illustrated a few represen tative episodes of making from two collaborative ma king contexts: 1) a medical device innovation camp, 2) a maker activity-centered workshop, to show the ways in which makers and materials make together, where maker-material audits occur, maker-material synchrony shapes up, materials configure collaboration, and materials ex hibit epistemic characteristics, directing creative making assemblages. By focusing on two different making contexts, we pointed out th e micro-engagements that defined different modes of maker-material entanglements. We found that the material components acted as co-makers in opening up possibilities and providing insights via “talk backs” in collaboration. In both the making contexts, we have seen instances where  auditions occur among  makers and materials to translate abstract ideas into tangible forms, materials directing maker actions, supporting idea realization, and resist ing making actions (Pickering, 1993). The situated material entities were also crucial in arranging and supporting the m aker collaboration  by establishing joint attention. The intermediate prot otypes and dynamic maker-material configurations augmented the knowledge building process, which further directed the making process. With this article, we attempt to characterize maker-material encounters in collaborative making contexts resulting in creative outcomes. Acknowledging the s implifications that can surface with a reductionist  approach, we still believe that the characterizations arrived as findings in the study can be used to get a macro level view on similar phenomena featured by maker-material entanglements. Perhaps, as researchers we should look forward to explore the ways of combining reductionist and diff ractive ways of  reading research data to further our understanding of becomings with the world. Advanced studies are required to understand and mark further modes of maker-material entanglements in collaborative creative making.
