# Affordances and Constraints of Online Museum Experiences for at-Home Science Engagement During the COVID-19 Pandemic

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Online museum experiences, or OMEs, expand access to museums beyond physical buildings. With limited visitation during the COVID-19 pandemic, many museums increased OME offerings, including those marketed toward families. However, as most OMEs are developed for K-12 audiences, we have yet to understand how OMEs support–or fail to support– families’ engagement with science. To investigate how OMEs facilitated or constrained families’ engagement with at-home science learning, we conducted semi-structured interviews with parents and used quantitative discourse analysis to identify emergent affordances and constraints. These findings can inform the design of OMEs that effectively support families’ athome engagement with science.

## Introduction

During the COVID-19 pandemic, many museums develope d distance learning programs to support visitors’ engagement and learning in lieu of physical visits (Ennes, 2021). With limitations on physical visits,  online programs offered alternative avenues for connecting visitors with museums. These programs, which we refer to as online museum experiences (OMEs), range from digitized collections to recorded tours and live investigations of phenomena (Chong & Smith, 2017). Over 1,200 OMEs are available through an online database curated by the American Alliance of Museums (AAM), with filters for content area, age range, and more (AAM, 2021). While OMEs are available to the public, including families, we have a limited understanding of the ways that OMEs support families’ engagement with science learning. Pre-pandemic, most OMEs were designed for use by K-12 students and teachers (Ennes, 2021). Howeve r, the pandemic incentivized development of OMEs fo r broader audiences (Ennes, 2021). Given the relative ly new development of OMEs for families, we have ye t to identify how OMEs support families’ engagement and learning, including with science topics. We focus on this issue for two reasons. First, parents/guardians (he reafter “parents”) play a pivotal role in supportin g remote learning during the pandemic (Budhrani et al., 2021; Garbe et al., 2020). Second, designers cannot ass ume that what works in physical museums will automatically t ranslate to OMEs, or that OMEs should aim to replic ate physical visits given the unique affordances of online environments (Din, 2015; Ennes, 2021). In response, OME designers must identify key factors that support ease of use, learning, and enjoyment for families (Lin et al., 2012). To this end, we analyzed 11 families’ participatio n in science-focused OMEs for at-home learning during the COVID-19 pandemic. Our research questions were:  which characteristics of OMEs facilitated families’ engagement with at-home science learning, and which  characteristics constrained engagement? We used quantitative discourse analysis (Mercer, 2004) to develop a coding scheme that identified emergent affordances and constraints of OMEs based on parents’ feedback as shared during semi-structured interviews. This p aper reports on affordances and constraints for at-home science learning among families, with the potential to inform future design of effective, engaging OMEs.

## Theoretical Background

Pre-pandemic, most digital programs designed by mus eums were intended for K-12 audiences (Ennes, 2021). Virtual field trips (VFTs) are designed as multimedia-rich experiences that reduce financial and logistical barriers, generate access to experts, and emphasize learner-c entered pedagogies (Klemm & Tuthill, 2003; Lacina, 2004; Zanetis, 2010). Yet, VFTs can unintentionally recreate barriers to access, such as by requiring Englis h fluency, Internet access, and quiet environments (Klemm & Tuthill, 2003; Stylianou-Lambert & Stylianou, 2010). Also, VFTs differ in quality of design, including alignment to educational standards (Klemm & Tuthill, 2003; Zanetis, 2010). While VFTs offer alternative means of accessing museums globally, they are not without drawbacks. The pandemic incentivized adaptation of existing di gital programs and creation of new programs for wider audiences as delivered through synchronous me etings, social media, websites, and email (Ennes, 2 021; Mitchell, 2019). These programs could supplement subject areas de-emphasized by remote learning, such as the arts and physical education (Budhrani et al., 2021). Yet, this benefit was  less available to families without resources and/or self-efficacy to use technology (G arbe et al., 2020). Given families’ status as a rel atively new audience for digital programs, program designers mu st work with families to identify their needs and e stablish value, especially for families overwhelmed by remote learning (Flaccus & Gecker, 2020; Garbe et al., 2020). This paper investigates the perceived affordances and constraints of OMEs that impacted families’ engagement with at-home science learning toward the goal of informing designs for online informal learning experiences that effectively engage families.

## Methods

This study focused on families’ use of OMEs during the COVID-19 pandemic, with “family” referring to adults and children in the same household. We studied fami lies because of parents’ important role in supporti ng emergency remote learning (Budhrani et al., 2021; G arbe et al., 2020). We recruited 11 families throug h social media and e-newsletters and interviewed one parent per household (n = 11). Of 11 parent interviewees, seven identified as female, three as male, and one as gen derqueer. Ten parents were white, and one was South  Asian. Parents’ mean age was 41 years old. Most parents (8 2%) had completed at least a master’s degree. Child ren included 11 male children, six female children, and one non-binary child. Seventeen children were white, and one was South Asian. Children’s mean age was 6 years old. Dependent on age, children experienced remote learning and/or parent-supervised activities in lieu of school or daycare. All families had Internet access at home. Data sources included 11 semi-structured interviews with parents conducted from Dec. 2020 – Mar. 2021 by the first author. Each parent reflected on at least one self-selected, science-focused OME during the interview. Interviews included the following questions: (1) Ho w would you describe the OME you shared with your child(ren)? (2) What kinds of goals did you have, b roadly, when you started that experience? (3) What kinds of science learning goals did you have when you starte d that experience? (4) What do you remember from yo ur experience? (5) How did you see your children learn  during the experience? (6) Have your children ment ioned the experience since? (7) Would you recommend this experience to others? (8) In your opinion, what tools in this experience were the most useful for learning? (9) Did you continue to investigate this topic afterward? If so, what other resources did you use? (10) What kinds of exp eriences or resources would you like to use in the future? Probes to elaborate were used when appropriate. Interviews lasted from 12 to 45 minutes (mean = 23 minutes). We analyzed interviews using quantitative discourse  analysis (Mercer, 2004) to develop an emergent coding scheme from parents’ responses. We watched i nterviews independently to generate possible codes, then developed a coding scheme based on overlapping obse rvations. We checked interrater reliability through independent coding of over 20% of the data (249 tur ns of 1,004 total turns of talk). Global Cohen’s ka ppa was 0.90, which is considered “strong” (McHugh, 2012). Any differences were resolved through discussion. W e independently coded remaining turns, then calculate d the percentage of families mentioning each code a nd the percentage of individual codes within total turns of talk. Details of the coding scheme (including individual kappa values, descriptions, and examples) can be found here: https://bit.ly/3Jj3uHB.

## Findings

During interviews, all parents shared their motivation for choosing to interact with a specific OME. Families were most often motivated by content or subject area (91% of families), followed by place or geographic location (64%). Five families mentioned that the study itself motivated them to interact with an OME (45%), while two families (18%) selected OMEs based on school or homeschool assignments. Parents mentioned 14 characteristics of OMEs that facilitated their engagement (see Table 1 below). All identified affordances were emergent, not predeterm ined. Parents most frequently described the multimo dal, interest-driven, and entertaining nature of OMEs. F our affordances specifically focused on support for engagement with science, such as exposing children to science topics and developing interest. Most par ents thought OMEs could promote science and engineering practices, such as developing experiments and makin g predictions. Parents also saw OMEs as pedagogical supports that helped them to support their children’s learning. Several parents reflected on their own experiences with K-12 science education. We observed variety in parents’ science backgrounds; one parent received little K-12 science instruction while another had been a science teacher. Parents also described constraints of OMEs for supporting at-home learning (see Table 2). Many families preferred physical activities and felt that OMEs we re lacking compared to physical or outdoor activiti es. Also, OMEs were not considered to be as good as the “real  thing” when visiting museums. Some parents preferr ed books over OMEs when supporting children’s learning  about science. Others mentioned concerns over scre en time required by digital programs, especially in addition to remote learning, and wished to limit screen time even while recognizing potential benefits of OMEs. Parents also noticed when OMEs provided insufficient instructions, ICLS2022 Proceedings 934 © ISLS follow-up resources, or support for varying backgro und knowledge. Last, parents noted when topics were interesting to some, but not all, family members.

## Discussion & Conclusion

We aimed to understand how OMEs impacted families’ engagement with at-home science learning during the COVID-19 pandemic. By analyzing semi-structured int erviews, we identified 14 affordances that facilita ted families’ engagement, with four focused on science,  along with six constraints on engagement. These fi ndings have implications for the design of OMEs that support informal learning about science in home settings. Science content played a major role in families’ selection of a particular OME, which we expected based on prior research about families’ motivations for v isiting museum websites (Lopatovska, 2015) and the general interest-driven nature of informal learning. This study also revealed that a museum’s geographic location played a major role in families’ selection of OMEs, with OMEs allowing families to virtually travel to places that could not be accessed during the pandemic. Further invest igation of the geographic appeal of OMEs is warrant ed to understand how OMEs can be designed to teach science from both nearby and distant locales. We were initially surprised by the schooling motivation, given our fo cus on informal learning, but we found that homesch ooling families already used OMEs on a regular basis to support their self-directed learning goals. This study expands on earlier research about museum practices during the pandemic (e.g., Ennes, 2021) by analyzing emergent feedback from parents, rather  than predetermined characteristics (e.g., Lin et a l., 2012), with attention to constraints, too. Identifying both affordances and constraints expands the field’s understanding of “good” design for family-friendly OMEs, with insights for genuinely supportive digital experiences that avoid common pitfalls. Our findings reiterate prior resea rch about family-friendly characteristics, both dig ital (Lin et al., 2012) and physical (Borun et al., 2008), in that experiences should be multimodal, fun, and social. However, the ways in which physical affordances extend to on line environments must be carefully considered, as OMEs present unique possibilities and limitations beyond  replication of physical exhibits (Ennes, 2021). As  the pandemic introduces new technologies for self-direct ed learning, designers of informal and/or online le arning may benefit from research about technology-specific affordances and their impacts on science education. Parents with access to education and resources may be more inclined to engage in learning a ctivities such as OMEs during leisure time (Hood, 1983). To investigate this connection between parents’ education and interest in OMEs, we plan to analyze surveys collected during this study about families’ use of OMEs before and during the pandemic and their attitudes toward teac hing and learning about science. We noted that two demographically similar families had very different experiences with OMEs; we plan to analyze their experiences as contrasting cases. We also plan to examine videos of families’ interactions for evidence of scientific reasoning and/or learning. We would like to expand this study  with a greater diversity of families, including mo re homeschooling families, to learn more about OMEs' role in self-directed learning.

In conclusion, this paper contributes emergent insi ghts about the affordances and constraints of onlin e museum experiences for at-home science learning. While families engaged with different OMEs, their interviews revealed common observations about the design of OMEs that facilitated or constrained engagement. This paper also emphasizes the role of families as relatively new and unique users of OMEs. Together, these insig hts can inform designs for OMEs that effectively support family-friendly engagement and learning.
