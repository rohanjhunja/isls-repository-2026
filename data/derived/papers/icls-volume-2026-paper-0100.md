# Tool, Tutor, or TBD? Examining Learners’ Perception Trajectories and Interaction Patterns through the Lens of Metacognitive Support

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how students’ perceptions of StatPal, an AI conversational agent for statistics learning, evolved and shaped interaction patterns over the course of a college statistics class. Using survey data, open-ended reflections and conversational exchanges, we identified five distinct perception trajectories that began with students initially viewing the AI as either a Tool, a Tutor, or To-Be-Decided, and ended with two final orientations: Tool-oriented or Tutor-oriented. Tutor-oriented students reported receiving more metacognitive support in procedural and conditional knowledge. Using Epistemic-Network-Analysis, we found that perceptions of AI shaped both quantity and quality of learning interactions. Tutor-oriented students engaged with the AI in active exploration with cognitive activation and procedural inquiries, while tool-oriented students engaged in passive, surface-level inquiries relying primarily on declarative assistance. These findings connect learners’ evolving conceptualizations of AI with the depth of their metacognitive engagement, providing empirical guidance for designing educational AI that promotes deeper metacognitive engagement.

Advances in artificial intelligence (AI)  are transforming how students seek  help, interact with instructional content, and engage in academic problem-solving. Among these innovations, AI conversational agents have emerged as a particularly promising form of support in higher education for their potential to facilitate personalized dialog-based learning interactions (Crompton & Burke, 2023; Yusuf et al., 2025). AI systems are especially well-suited for supporting cognitively demanding domains, such as mathematics and statistics, where students must not only recall concepts but also apply, connect, and reflect on them across contexts. In statistics learning, for instance, students face the challenge of interpreting data, reasoning under uncertainty, and selecting appropriate analytical strategies (Kandeel, 2019; Tran & Tarr, 2018). Meeting these challenges requires metacognitive awareness: understanding what one kn ows, how to perform key procedures, and when and why to apply them. These abilities extend beyond factual knowledge to the regulation of one’s own thinking, which is a dimension of learning that AI conversational agents are uniquely positioned to support through interactive, reflective dialogue.AI conversational agents can play a meaningful role in helping students develop metacognitive awareness by supporting the thinking processes involved in problem-solving. By engaging learners in conversation, these systems can clarify concepts, walk through procedures, and encourage reflection on when and why certain strategies apply. Rather than just assisting with immediate tasks, they can create space for students to think more deeply and develop a better understanding of how they learn (Labadze et al., 2023). However, little is known about the specific ways these systems support such metacognitive engagement during authentic learning situations.

Yet, the degree to which this potential is realized depends not only on the kind of support the AI provides but also on how learners perceive and position it within their learning process. Learners’ engagement with AI is shaped by their underlying mental model of the system: some may approach it as a tool for accessing information, while others see it as a collaborative partner in constructing understanding together  (Schmidt & Loidolt, 2023). Importantly, such orientations are not fixed; students’ perceptions of the AI can evolve as they gain experience, confront new challenges, or recognize different affordances of the system. These evolving perceptions influence the types of questions students ask, the depth of their inquir ies, and the depth of metacognitive engagement they achieve. Understanding how learners form and adjust these perceptions is therefore essential for interpreting their interactions with AI and the learning opportunities those interactions afford. Despite growing attention to AI in education, limited research has examined how these systems support metacognitive knowledge in authentic learning contexts and how learners’ perceptions of AI evolve and shape the kinds of support that unfold in interactions. To address these gaps, the present study examines StatPal (StatPal, n.d.), an AI conversational agent  integrated into an undergraduate statistics course, to explore how learners conceptualize, use, and reinterpret the agent’s role over time.  We draw on  Schraw and Dennison’s (1994) 879 framework of metacognitive knowledge awareness, which identifies three interrelated forms of knowledge that learners develop about their cognitive processes: declarative knowledge (understanding what one knows), procedural knowledge (knowing how to perform specific strategies or tasks), and conditional knowledge (knowing when and why to apply them). This framework provides a structured lens for analyzing the types of metacognitive support that emerge in learner-AI interactions and for understanding how StatPal engages students in making sense of their own thinking while learning statistics. By connecting analysis of conversational data with learners’ self-reported perceptions, the study offers insight into the mechanisms by which AI agents contribute to metacognitive learning processes and provides an empirical basis for designing educational  AI that responds to learners’ cognitive and strategic needs. The study addresses the following questions: RQ1.   How do students’ perceptions of an AI agent’s role evolve over the course of a statistics class? RQ2.   What types of metacognitive knowledge support did students receive from the AI agent, and how did this support vary with students’ evolving perceptions of the AI agent’s role? RQ2a.  How do students with different  perceptions of AI  describe the kinds of metacognitive knowledge support they receive from it?

RQ2b.  How do students with different perceptions of AI  differ in their patterns of interaction with it, and how do these patterns change over time for students who shift from a tool-oriented to a tutor-oriented view?

RQ3.   How do students interpret the factors that contributed to evolving or stability in how they perceived and used the AI throughout the course?

## Participants and context

The study was conducted at a highly selective public land-grant research university in the western United States, Participants were drawn from a single lower-division 10-week statistics course offered in person during the spring quarter of 2025. Of the 45 students enrolled, 44 consented to allow their coursework to be used for research (10 males, 34 females; 10 freshmen, 13 sophomores, 12 juniors, and 9 seniors). All students had previously completed one required lower-division social science research design course. Although the statistics course was housed in the School of Education, five participants (11.4%) were psychology majors, one (2.3%) was a social ecology major, and the remaining 38 students (86.4%) were education sciences majors.  Of the 45 students, 33 who indicated that they had used StatPal were included in the analytic sample. Statistical AI agent–StatPal StatPal is an LLM-based conversational agent customized to the course content  and integrated within the university’s learning management system (Canvas). It provides just-in-time, personalized support outside the classroom, allowing students to ask natural-language questions about statistical concepts, formulas, or procedures and receive immediate, tailored responses that guide problem solving and offer strategy-focused hints to promote deeper learning (see Figure 1). A briefing was conducted in Week 4 of the course to introduce StatPal and instruct students on its use for homework completion, exam preparation, review of complex statistical concepts, and other related learning activities.

Please provide your reasoning.” Metacognitive support A survey measuring learners’ metacognitive knowledge related to their use of the AI agent was administered at the end of the semester. Nine items were adapted from Schraw and Dennison’s (1994) Metacognitive Awareness Inventory (α =.93) and covered three subscales: Declarative Knowledge, Procedural Knowledge, and Conditional Knowledge, with three items for each. Declarative Knowledge was measured with items such as “StatPal helped me recognize my strengths and weaknesses in learning statistics.” Procedural Knowledge included items like “StatPal helped me apply step-by-step strategies when working through statistics problems,” and Conditional Knowledge was assessed with items such as “StatPal supported my ability to decide when to move from guided support to solving problems on my own.” Participants responded using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from Strongly Disagree (1) to Strongly Agree (5).

Interaction behaviors in StatPal Students’ interaction behaviors were captured through the student-StatPal conversation history. Throughout the semester, students engaged with StatPal while working on statistics problems, generating a total of 1,170 conversational threads. Each interaction was automatically recorded, including both student-initiated queries and StatPal’s responses. For example, in one exchange, a student asked, “Can you tell me about dependent and independent variables?” StatPal responded, “Certainly! In statistics: Independent Variable (IV): The variable you manipulate or control to see its effect on the dependent variable. Dependent Variable (DV): The variable you measure, which is affected by changes in the independent variable.”

## Data analysis

We employed a combination of thematic analysis, content analysis, descriptive statistics, and Epistemic Network open-ended survey responses about StatPal’s role and reasons for perception shifts. Content analysis and ENA were used for RQ2 to analyze learner–AI interactions. For all analyses, a team of researchers independently coded a subset of excerpts to develop preliminary codebooks, refined them through discussion, and resolved discrepancies by consensus. The finalized codebooks were then applied to the full dataset, with each excerpt coded by at least two researchers.

RQ1. Learners’ perception trajectories across the statistics course Open-ended survey responses from the beginning and end of the semester were analyzed using thematic analysis. Themes were first generated inductively from the mid-semester survey and then applied deductively to the final survey (see OSF Supplementary File 1: https://osf.io/hs9nb/files/ey5h7). RQ2. Metacognitive interaction patterns reflected in the trajectories of learners’ perception Descriptive statistical analysis was applied to understand patterns of perceived AI support for metacognitive knowledge development across perception trajectories. Mean scores were calculated for each metacognitive knowledge subscale within each perception trajectory group (Tutor to Tutor, Tool to Tutor, TBD to Tutor, Tool to Tool, and TBD to Tool).  To better understand the metacognitive knowledge patterns emerging from learners’ perception trajectories, we employed two complementary analytical approaches: content analysis of conversational logs and ENA of interaction patterns.

Content analysis: The Learner–AI conversational exchanges, including both learner inputs and AI agent responses, were analyzed using a combination of deductive and inductive coding approaches. The analysis began with a deductive framework informed by the three types of metacognitive knowledge, within which AI responses were classified as Declarative, Procedural, or Conditional Knowledge Support and students’ inputs were classified as Concept Inquiry, Interpretation and Application, and Computation Process Inquiry. Followin g this deductive foundation, an inductive coding process was applied to capture emergent interaction patterns not anticipated by the metacognitive knowledge framework. This iterative process revealed additional learner behaviors including Unprocessed Problem Forwarding, Clarification Seeking, Exploratory What-If Thinking, Prompted Problem 881 Practice, and Social/Off-Topic Prompts. These complete sets of student input codes reflect varying levels of learner agency, from minimal effort to building conceptual understanding and reasoning to active exploration (see OSF Supplementary File 2: https://osf.io/45mtn).The inductive process also identified emergent StatPal response patterns: Cognitive Activation, Affective Support, and Management Support. Together with the theory-driven codes, the complete set of AI codes captured the StatPal’s  multifaceted role in not only providing managerial, affective, and metacognitive support, but also actively inviting learners to engage in practice (see OSF Supplementary File 3: https://osf.io/bszcd).

ENA: To understand how students’ orientations toward the StatPal (as tool vs. tutor) manifest in distinct patterns of interaction, and how these patterns evolve when students shift their perspectives, we use the rENA package in R to model the relationships between student interaction patterns and StatPal responses. We modeled the networks of the eight student behaviors and six StatPal response types and defined units of analysis as unique combinations of support orientation type (Tool-Oriented vs. Tutor-Oriented), student ID, and day. The model employed an infinite window size to capture all co-occurrences within each student’s daily interactions. This initial model established a stable coordinate space defined by Tool-Oriented and Tutor-Oriented interaction patterns. In the second phase, we examined students who shifted their orientation from Tool to Tutor (Tool-to-Tutor shifters) over the semester. We constructed separate networks for shifters’ Early and Late semester interactions and projected both into the coordinate space established by the Tool vs. Tutor model. This projection technique allowed us to interpret shifters’ temporal network changes  relative to the reference frame defined by stable orientation types, revealing how interaction patterns evolved from Tool-like to Tutor-like configurations. RQ3. Learner interpretations of evolving or stable perception trajectories Participants were categorized according to their StatPal perception trajectories. Open-ended responses describing StatPal’s role and the reasons for changes or stability in perception were analyzed using inductive coding to identify recurring patterns and themes.

RQ1. Learners’ perception trajectories across the statistics course Figure 2 depicts whether or in what ways students’ perceptions of StatPal’s role evolved throughout the semester. Overall, students’ perceptions of StatPal ’s role changed substantially over the ten-week course. While most students initially viewed StatPal as a Tool (n = 17), more than three quarters (n = 13) came to see it as a Tutor by the end of the quarter, indicating increased recognition of its instructional value. A smaller group (n = 4) maintained their original Tool perception. Those who initially saw StatPal already as a Tutor at the beginning of the course (n = 6) remained consistent in this view. Among students who were undecided (TBD, n = 10) at the beginning, perceptions split evenly, with five identifying it as a Tutor and five as a Tool by the end of the course. TBD was operationalized in our coding that explicitly expressed uncertainty, conditional expectations, or intent to ‘try it out’ (e.g., I’m not sure how I’ll use it).

To interpret the interaction patterns, we organized codes into a coordinate space defined by two dimensions. The y-axis represents student agency, ranging from Passive to Active, indicating the degree of initiative and effortful engagement students exhibit. Strikingly, the interactions for two groups concentrated in entirely different regions of the network. ToolOriented interactions center on the right side toward Understand, dominated by Concept Inquiry, Declarative Support, Unprocessed Problem Forwarding,  and Interpretation & Application. Students engage in receptive learning, seeking answers, definitions and explanations with minimal conditional support from StatPal. In contrast, Tutor-Oriented interactions form a dense cluster toward Explore, spanning both passive and active quadrants. Students actively seek understanding while engaging in Clarification Seeking and Computation Process Inquiry, supported by strong Procedural and Affective Support. Most notably, Cognitive Activation appears prominently, connecting to both declarative and procedural support and challenging students to apply knowledge. How students perceive the AI shapes not just quantity but the qualitative nature of their interactions, transforming learning from passive reception to active, multi-dimensional knowledge construction. ## Tool-oriented vs Tutor-oriented ENA Results

We further analyzed patterns for the group of students whose perception changed from Tool to Tutor. The results reveal that students ’ perception trajectories are fundamentally associated with qualitatively different learning experiences. Figure 6 illustrates the comparison of the early-semester network (before mid-survey) and the late-semester network (after mid-survey).

In the early semester, Tool to Tutor  shifters exhibited patterns similar to general tool-oriented students. Yet, unlike tool-oriented students in general, the early network revealed that Cognitive Activation and Procedural 884 Support were already connected to Declarative Support, suggesting that StatPal  was attempting to activate thinking and provide procedural guidance even as students sought conceptual understanding. By the late semester, these shifters exhibited patterns resembling those of the general tutor-oriented group, as this group accounted for most of the network composition at that stage. The subtracted mean network highlighted a distinct configuration not seen in the overall comparisons: thick blue connections linking Cognitive Activation and Affective Support with Declarative Support. Relative to the early semester, StatPal offered both cognitive challenges and emotional encouragement alongside conceptual explanations, shaping a learni ng environment that was simultaneously supportive and activating. It suggests that transitioning from tool to tutor perception requires AI to change its pedagogical approach: not just explaining concepts, but actively prompting students to use them, while providing multidimensional support, especially the affective support needed to persist through learning tasks.

RQ3. Learner Interpretations of Evolving or Stable Perception Trajectories Students’ perceptions of StatPal’s role in their learning evolved in distinct ways over the semester. The thematic and contextual factors.

Among students whose perceptions remained stable, distinct patterns emerged. Those who consistently described StatPal as a tool often used it sparingly, primarily for clarification or as a backup when other resources fell short. Whereas students who consistently viewed StatPal as a tutor framed it as a persistent, accessible source of instructional support throughout the semester. These participants emphasized its in-time feedback and usefulness across assignments, noting that their perception stayed because StatPal had reliably fulfilled a tutoring role from the outset: “StatPal seems more like an online tutor that’s always there ready to answer questions.” Another described it as “my assistant in any problems I encounter in the assignments.” For students who shifted from viewing StatPal as a tool to seeing it as a tutor, the change was often driven by the recognition of its instructional capabilities. Initially viewed as a backup resource, StatPal soon came to be seen as more than a passive to ol. These students recognized features resembling those of a human tutor, such as clear explanations, providing practice problems, and memory aids, which prompted them to reevaluate its instructional role. One student explained, “At first I saw it as a ref erence... but over time it gradually became a tutor... problem sets it would give me or creative ways to remember different concepts.” Some described StatPal as a reliable substitute for human support, especially when limited by time or access to peers or instructors: “I didn’t have time to squeeze in an actual tutor... nice to have something there to ask questions to.” Other students also noted that its adaptive responses and step-by-step clarifications made it feel like a dynamic tutor rather than a static tool: “When I said that please explain one step more specifically, it responded very patiently.” 885 Students who hold a more uncertain view of StatPal at the beginning of the semester but ultimately adopted tutor-based framing also cited both functional and affective influences. In some cases, even if StatPal was not heavily used at first, its reliability over time gradually positioned it as a go-to support mechanism. A few students expressed initial skepticism, r ooted in general distrust of AI, but reported gaining confidence in StatPal after seeing peers use it effectively. This social validation, coupled with positive personal experiences, contributed to a more stable perception of StatPal as a tutor by the end of the semester, “I was very hesitant at first but warmed up to it as time went on.” In contrast, some students began with unclear expectations and ultimately settled on viewing StatPal as a tool. They felt that lectures and instructor explanations provided sufficient support, making StatPal unnecessary for deeper engagement: “I didn't use it as much as time went on since the lecture was able to cover most of my questions.” This shift from ambiguity to instrumentality reflects a narrowing of perception shaped by minimal reliance and overlap with existing resources. The findings suggest that how students frame an AI system shape their learning experiences, and these perceptions can evolve over the course of a semester. Students who framed StatPal as a tool interacted in ways that reflected an instrumental view of machines: limited use for clarification, conceptual definitions, or verification when other resources fell short. In contrast, students who framed StatPal as a tutor not only described it differently but also engaged with it differently, asking follow-up questions, pursuing step-by-step explanations, and drawing on affective support. This aligns with Schmidt and Loidolt’s (2023) argument that AI has “lost its character as mere instruments,” as learners increasingly construe these systems as collaborators or par tners. Our results extend that argument to an educational setting, showing that perception trajectory  is associated not only with different interpretations of AI’s role but also with qualitatively distinct patterns of interaction. Building on this distinction, the findings point to a critical shift in how AI support should be understood, not as the delivery of information but as the orchestration of pedagogical experiences. When students came to view StatPal as a tutor, their intera ctions moved beyond passive understanding based on declarative support and shifted toward active exploration and deeper understanding. StatPal played a multidimensional role in this process. StatPal not only clarified concepts but also activated thinking through prompts, scaffolded procedural reasoning, and offered affective support that sustained persistence. This combination enabled deeper engagement with statistical concepts and mirrored human-like tutoring, helping students reframe the AI from a tool to a tutor. The findings suggest that effective AI educational tools must be designed not only to deliver declarative knowledge but to act as responsive learning partners capable of activating cognition, offering affective reinforcement, and adapting over time to student needs. Static or purely informational responses are insufficient to sustain deeper engagement. AI like StatPal is  most impactful when they simulate tutor-like behaviors like prompting students to reflect, practice, and persist. While current designs often focus on conceptual clarity and procedural guidance, enhancing conditional support would help students determine when and why to apply specific strategies which remain a critical area for future development. For instructors, integrating such AI agents into coursework may help alleviate the demand for one-on-one tutoring, especially in resource-constrained environments. However, intentional framing is essential; without guidance, students may default to engage  these systems as basic references rather than interactive tutors. Encouraging metacognitive use through demonstration, modeling, or structured prompts can help shift student orientation. Finally, for learners, how they perceive and position AI within their learning process is not trivial. Viewing AI as a tutor rather than a tool can open pathways to more agentive, and strategic engagement, ultimately shaping not just what they learn, but how they learn. This study highlights how students’ evolving perceptions of an AI conversational agent meaningfully shape their learning engagement and metacognitive development. Rather than acting solely as an informational resource, StatPal functioned as a pedagogical p artner for students who came to view it as a tutor that prompted them to reflect, apply, and persist. These tutor-oriented interactions were distinguished by multidimensional scaffolding, including cognitive activation and affective support, enabling students to shift from passive inquiry to active exploration. Importantly, these shifts were not automatic; they emerged through both the design of the AI and students’ framing of its role. The findings underscore the importance of designing AI systems that don’t merely deliver content but cultivate strategic thinking and resilience that mirror the responsive, adaptive qualities of human tutoring. Supporting such transformative engagement requires not only thoughtful AI design but also intentional integration in to learning environments that encourage students to view these tools as collaborative learning companions.
