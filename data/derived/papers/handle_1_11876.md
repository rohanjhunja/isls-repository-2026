# Educational Makerspaces: Embodied Learning for World-Making

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
How can we educate for worldmaking in makerspaces? In our study on Makerspaces in the Norwegian context we conducted research-practice partnerships in several school-based makers spaces over a period of three years. Through engaging in the teachers and students everyday school life and context, we were able to follow, learn and co-create the events unfolding in them. In this paper we present two learning settings and the understandings that were developed in them. The two settings both pointed at opportunities for worldmaking in relation to personal sense making through material interactions and the role of the environment for equitable engagement and inclusivity. We also saw the importance of having enough time for planning truly meaningful learning experiences that involve open ended embodied exploration of materials and technologies.

## Introduction

World-making can be seen as process in which students actively engage with, and shape, their personal or shared worlds or worldview s towards preferred ones (Agid, 2012; Iivari, 2020). Makerspaces offers a good base for transformative and creative hands-on collaboration due to their technology rich environments,  including rapid fabrication tools such as 3D printers, laser-and vinyl cutters, enabling material-based learning (Schad & Jones, 2020; Clapp et al. 2017). Such artefact-oriented learning through making derives from the Maker movement but has its roots in constructivist pedagogies and the creative fields of arts, design and crafts (Blikstein, 201 3; Halverson & Sheridan, 2014; Peppler et al., 2023). The theory of collaborative knowledge creation learning has been developed as the conceptual framework for tech nology enhanced pedagogic practices that will culti vate creative competencies at school (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 201 4). Similarly STEM learning, popular in makerspaces, has incorporated the arts to form  STEAM learning, that incorporate science, technology, engineering, arts, and mathematics. However, in a recent research project in Norway we found that, creative practices involving material exploration, drawing and prototyping is partly missing in the Norwegian educational makerspaces, as many of the teachers use ready-made STEM solutions that are predesigned for effective and controlled learning sessions (Søyland et al. 2025).

As arts and crafts teachers, we believe that t angible engagement with materials and thinking thro ugh making are vital parts of the sense  making process in learning  (Schilhab & Groth, 2024)  and that this is a vital aspect of participating in world-making. This is not only a cognitive process but involves the wh ole person, previous experiences, cultures and beliefs as well as embodied knowledge of materials and actions gained through trial and error (Nithikul & Groth, 2024). Embodied experiences are thus key to making sense of our encounters with the world. Such bodily interactions with materials are seen as  especially crucial for young learners (Kiefer & Trumpp, 2012). The Norwegian school subject of arts and crafts is seen as essential for developing students’ practical skills through embodied making and learning (Groth & Gulli ksen, 2024). Thus, we find it is especially important to engage students in maker-centered learning tasks that highlight tangible and  explorative material processes. Based on this, w e investigated how we could introduce perspectives from the arts a nd crafts subject into natural science themes in makerspace and incor porate knowledge about maker-centered learning (Clapp et al., 2017; Korhonen et al., 2023) through collaboration with the teachers at the schools that we investigated. The two case studies presented in this paper are part of a larger research project; Maker-centered learning: cultivating creativity in tomorrow’s schools (Maker) that runs over a period of four years, and which aim s to develop pedagogical best practices for makerspaces and promote teaching for creativity. In the first part of the project, we conducted the said overview study of the makersp aces in the country to explore the state-of-the-art situation in them. In this  second part of the project, we have engaged in Educational Design Research (EDR) (McKenney & Reeves 2019) together with the teachers in local schools that we had interviewed earlier, and who have shown an interest in the maker pedagogies in o ur previous interactions with them. We utilized a form of researcher practice partnerships  (Coburn & Penuel, 2016)  with the se teachers, developing and testing several different forms of maker projects and pedagogical approaches over a period of two years the researchers, who are also teacher educators, are actively supporting the teachers in developing maker-centered learning activities and practices iteratively while also conducting observations and interviews with both teachers and students during and after the activities. The researchers have also engaged the teachers in hands on maker workshops on the University campus, and they have arranged reading seminars and discussions with the teachers that cover recent research and examples of maker pedagogy. The researchers have al so found funding for a new makerspace for one of th e participating schools and the second case was conducted in this new maker environment. Data collected includes fieldnotes, photographs, video and audio  recordings and interview transcripts. The material has been analyzed through a hybrid thematic content analysis (Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006). Two examples of embodied learning and world-making We exemplify our points through the brief presentations of two examples, drawn from the collaborations with the teachers at two different schools. The first exampl e concerns smaller children in the age of 8 years old, and the importance of embodied learning, tangible experiences and the deep involvement and real-world relevance of the tasks and projects that the students are engaged in  for them to be truly meaningful. The second exampl e on the other hand concerns students of the age 12, and here we look at the role the makerspace as a learning environment had on students’ equitable engagement, self-efficacy, s ense of contribution and social recognition  within the group. Both examples highlight the embodied and tangible experiences tha t enabled a change in the process of sense making and agency for the students.

The importance of tangible experiences and embodied interaction with quality materials and longitudinal processes Researchers from the Maker project worked with local schoolteachers in a researcher-practice partnership that involved 38 third graders (two classes), and four teachers. The teachers involved in the collaboration have  been part of building the local makerspace at their school and they are thus interested in learning more about maker pedagogy and practice s. The teacher group of four has  competencies and backgrounds in areas such as natural sciences, arts and crafts, and language subjects. The study included observation, co-teaching and interviewing the teachers and a selection of the students. All the observed students filed informed consent forms together with their parents. The guiding research question in this first case is as follows: How do primary school pupils make se nse of the world through exploration and crafting with traditional and new materials and technologies in a n educational makerspace?

In the maker activities, Teacher1 introduces the task of papermaking by telling the students' where paper comes from and how it is used in our everyday life. In addition, information on how different types of paper have different properties and qualities  is discussed. The students are then included in each stage of the paper-making process. It became clear that the idea of paper, how it can be recycled and made intonew paper did not become clear to the students as an idea but needed to be c oncretely shown through the act of making a large p aper sheet and inviting the student to make their own. One pupil shouted: “It became a sheet!”, as if he could not believe his own eyes. In the interview, the Teacher1 says the process of papermaking did not make sense for the pupil in the example, not until he had this concrete tangible experience of seeing and feeling the paper in front of him. After the paper-making project, Teacher1 says that now the students also understand why they should throw paper in the recycle trash and that it can become new paper  again. The tangible experience was central to the students' sense making of paper as a concept. However, the same teacher reflects on the challenge s of creating a space for experimentation and exploration within the framework of curricula demands. She notes: "When we teachers panic, we stick to the structured way of teaching because it is safe. We then fail to create room for discove ry and creativity”. She also explains how the transition from structured to open tea ching is challenging for the teachers, and that they need to practice setting boundaries a nd then loosening up in teaching. However, this process is important in educating the students to be more self-reliant and for them to feel that the activities ar e meaningful for them. The teacher has observed that students take more ownership of their projects when they are  given the freedom to explore. The project then transforms fro m being school-owned to pupil-owned, leading to full engagement and sparking creativity on the part of the students. Another issue that came up in the interviews with the students themselves was how the fact that they can make something by themselves is important for them,  as opposed to the teacher making something on beha lf of them. This was especially the case in their contact with technology, that due to the number of students, the difficult operations w ere largely handled by the teachers. They also express ed that using a laser cutter was not as fun because "It was not really us who did it" as one de scribes. This age group needs a lot of help with ad vanced technology like the laser cutter and they do not ge t as involved in the process  as many parts need to be made by the teachers. The students experience the laser cutter  as a more distant process than the lino carving, as they do not feel that they have agency to affect the highly  technological processes.  Blending the structured teaching methodology that is normally characteristic of Norwegian schooling, with the more open teaching approach inherent in the educational maker culture, appears vital to preserving the balance between techn ology, craft, and exploration in students' making. The freedom to explore materials and technology through crafting is essential for students to assume ownership of their processes and make sen se of the ir world and how they can affect it. Activities such as papermaking, which are genuine and closely tied to students' daily lives, enable them to broaden their perspectives, for instance, understanding rec ycling. This emphasizes the intrinsic linkage betwe en worldmaking and the act of making. Embodied communication and equitable engagement In this second case example we look at equity in the school  and the makerspace as a place for hands-on learning that helped newcomers communicate with local children. The guiding research question in this case is as follows: How can collaborative hands-on educational practices in makerspaces foster equi table engagement among immigrant students? We base our approach on the premise that education for equitable engagement is vital for ensuring that students from various socioeconomic a nd cultural backgrounds can participate fully and meaningfully in a mutual world-making with their fellow students. The study took place in a Norwegian 7th-grade makerspace classroom (12-years old students) where twenty-five students worked on a project to create a board game using 3D printers and vinyl cutters. The focus of our research was on five Ukrainian students who, prior to this project, did not frequently collaborate with one another nor with their Norwegian classmate s. We were particularly interested in how the relationships be tween the boys developed through the course of the project period of three weeks. We observed and documented the children while they were working on the project with a special focus on their instances of collaboration, verbal and physical interactions, and noted several types of other engagement. We also conducted interviews with the five Ukrainian students. The interview questions focused on the students' reflections on their makerspace experience, perceived changes in peer relationships, and comparisons with their prior schooling in Ukraine.

The makerspace project provided Ukrainian students with equal access to resources, enabling them to actively participate in collaborative activities. Initially, cultural differences in resource management were evident: Ukrainian students sought to maximize their use of 3D printers, reflecting survival-oriented values shaped by their prior experiences. Norwegian peers, accustomed to shared resource use, adapted without conflict. Over time, the Ukrainian students began to share resources more eq uitably, demonstrating the makerspace's role in fos tering a democratic learning environment where diverse cultu ral practices can harmonize. The makerspace encoura ged the Ukrainian students to engage in meaningful collaboration, both within their group and with Norwegian peers. This enhanced peer communication bridged social and  cultural divides, fostering a dynamic space where ideas and skills were exchanged freely. Through hands-on activities, students integrated their prior knowledge with new technical skills, aligning with research on knowledge creation learning (Kajamaa et al., 2018). One student became particularly adept at using the 3D printer, evolving into becoming a resource for his classmates and demonstrating the reciprocal nature of collaborative learning.

By bringing their unique perspectives into the makerspace, Ukrainian students contributed to innovative and creative solutions, enriching the project outco mes. The process allowed them to see themselves as capable creators and innovators in STEAM, supporting their positive identity development. The makerspace evolved into a participatory space where students from different  cultural and linguistic backgrounds could engage a s equals. Ukrainian students, who initially refrained from in itiating inter actions, be gan collaborating openly with  their Norwegian peers, gradually establishing cross-cultural connections. As noted by Nasir and Vakil (2017), such inclusive spaces candisrupt inequities in urban classrooms, fostering understanding and mutual respect. Integrating digital tools, such as 3D printers and vinyl cutters, played a critical role in facilitati ng the collaborative learning process. These technologies supported the students in linking their existing knowledge with new skills, enabling innovative approaches to the p roject. The findings align with research emphasizin g the transformative potential of computer-supported collaborative learning in enriching educa tional experiences and promoting epistemic practices (Kajamaa & Kumpulainen, 2020). Through equitable resource access, collaborative design, and the integration of technology, the make rspace served as a microcosm of participatory democ racy, fostering not only knowledge creation but also soci al integration and mutual respect among diverse stu dent groups.

## Discussion

Embodied and tangible experiences seem to be importa nt when young students are about to learn more complicated use of technology. Making the tasks sim ple and contextualizing the subject  matter helps them feel connected to the processes they are about to engage in. The sense of agency and opportunities to actively engage in all aspects of technology feels important to the m. Makerspace activities present students with opportunities to experience how processes can be done by hand, thus lowering the threshold for  using new digital technology. While fabrication technologies such as laser cutters offer new possibilities for making, very young students may become more passive when too complicated and distant technologies are integr ated into their creative work and the need for the teachers to do the work for them becomes necessary. The absence of deep involvement may lead to students feeling detached from the process. Deep involvement is alsonecessary when utilizing new technology for students to become world makers. Autonomous engagement with technologies is a critical aspect of students' agency in world-making as the students’ participation in and opportunities for exploration are fundamental f or their sense-making (see also Iivari, 2020). Embodied learning through making and the ir sense of agency, can elucidate the distinction between contributing independently or merely following instructions, to fully depending on teacher assistance. It is through active involvement that they evolve into "world makers". For older students on the other hand, Makerspaces may present a n opportunity to foster equitable engagement and positive identity development  through gaining a capacity to excel in the use of t echnology. Especially in cases where different social or cultural groups meet, such as is becoming the norm in many schools, hands-on activities can  helping bridge cultural gaps and supporting democra tic, inclusive learning spaces.  The integration of equitable engagement principles in u rban education, particularly through the lens of ma kerspaces, not only enhances the learning experience for all students but also fosters a culture of inclusivity and innovation. By leveraging the diver se perspectives of students and creating environmen ts that encourage collaboration and creativity, educators can cultivate a more equitabl e educational landscape.  Further, the artifacts and material interactions become means for communication and knowledge sharing throu gh actions and gestures  (Härkki, 2018; Sormunen et al., 2020). This type of learning environment has the potential to help overcome language and culture barriers. It can offer ways of breaking the ice between membe rs of groups that have formed outside the classroom. Such hands-on collaboration gives students opportunities for develop their iden tities and community roles in a group (Malin, 2012). As learners build mastery through making artifacts,  they develop critical and reflective dispositions  through dynamic interactions with others (Peppler e t al., 2023). Catterall and Peppler

(2007) discuss the impact making things together  has on general self-efficacy in disadvantaged groups and posit

that such activities  foster a positive view of  one’s capabilities and achievements. In our study we could see evidence of equitable engagement that was brought by the nature of hands-on making and collaborating that took place through maker-centered teaching and learning in the setting of a makerspace. As urban schools become increasingly diverse, education for equitable engag ement becomes vital for ensuring that students from  various socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds can participate fully and meaningfully.

## Conclusions

We conclude that learning through making, for example in makerspaces, offer opportunities to educate for worldmaking through engaging the student’s personal world’s first, before they can turn to cha nging the worlds they are situated in. While world-making relates to constructivist notions of knowled ge creation and building of new preferred futures, we believe there are also deeper  and more complex understandings related to the con cept. Making sense of oneself in relation to others and the social and material environment is fundamental for building resilience and agency. Without these, the threshold for participating in building mutual worlds with others might be high. A holistic view on facilitating learning grounded in the personal experiences of the individual student is ideal, however many teachers struggle with the re ality of large student groups and a strict curricular agenda. We suggest that makerspaces offer a freer space for out of the box teaching and learning that  has the possibility for flexibility that many other learning contexts do not provide. Makerspaces additionally provide an environment for embodied learning that transcends verbal formulations in the way many activities are experienced materially. For very small children this experience-based type of learning makes the topics more concrete and  has the possibility to get closer to their lived experience  and everyday life. Thus, they can also see how they themselves can participate in world-making on a concrete level, without having to expli cate their ideas in verbal form. Similarly, for older children, learning through making and hands on showing can help studen ts who are not yet verbally capable of joining collaborations that req uire complicated verbalizations of ideas and opinions. Even when ideas and decisions are taken, drawings and th ree-dimensional artefacts can mediate the communication s. To be able to participate in world-making one needs to be able to feel included and respected in the group of peers. The topics need to be felt as important and relevant, and one needs to be able to see one’s own role in the activities and the meaningfulness of them. Makerspaces as learning environments have the potential for offering such arenas if the teachers have the recourses to  facilitate embodied and individual learning experiences  in an  inclusive atmosphere.
