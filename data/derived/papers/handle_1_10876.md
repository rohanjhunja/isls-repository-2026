# Researchers as Learners: Exploring Nature, Experiences, and Biases in Data Collection and Analysis

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this study researchers investigated their own prior experiences and biases related to nature in the study of experiential learning. The researchers participated in an outdoor activity in a local park, in which their accompanying family members video recorded their exploration of the park. Analysis of the researchers’ personal narratives, individual video recording summaries, and open codes revealed that personal views of nature related to affect and safety appeared to influence researcher focus and interpretation.

## Introduction and grounding

In this study we, as researchers, take our analytic lens to ourselves as we explore learning in nature. Our goal was to understand how our individual lenses and varied experiences with nature influenced our interpretations of what we saw. In the current study, we consider  our data collection efforts in a nature environment, drawing on data gathered with a family on a walk in a wooded park as they explored nature. Nature impacts humans in several ways (e.g., reduced anxiety (Beyer et al., 2014); increased happiness and well-being (Larson  et al., 2016), domain-specific learning and science expertise (Marin & Ba ng, 2018)). Given that, ignoring nature's influence on our experiences and their impact on our perceptions and actions would be shortsighted. Grounding our work in constructivism, noting how prior experiences are used in the interpretation of new events (e.g., Cunningham, 1992), we consider how our experiences in and out of nature influence how we conduct research, including data analysis.

Qualitative researchers are encouraged to set aside prior beliefs or prejudgments, i.e., bracketi ng, in conducting research (Merriam & Tisdell, 2015). In our study we intentio nally did not bracket prior experiences and interpretations but set them out as something t o study to better understand how they affect our analysis. In this way, we attempt to work towards identifying, monitoring, and clarifying potential researcher biases (whether positive or negative) related to nature and our exp erience in nature (Glesne, 2016, Merriam & Tisdell, 2015). As Glesne (2016) mentioned, researchers should ask the mselves “Why did you notice what you noticed” and “ How can you interpret what you noticed” (p. 166). While  video is theory laden (Hall, 2000),  our interpretations are experience laden as well. Given that, the question that guided this study was: How do prior experiences of nature influence researchers’ interpretation of data gathered and analyzed?

## Methods

In this qualitative study, participants were the researchers and their families; the study exempt from IRB oversight. The family members engaged in an unstructured outdo or activity in a local wooded park, lasting approxi mately one hour. Our immediate goal was to test Go-Pro cameras for future  research use with children and adults. Cameras were cap mounted on Skylark (age 9 male), and Pine (age 41 father), shoulder mounted on Hawk (age 6 male). Lavendar (age 3 female) wore no camera. The researchers were moderate-participants, walking near the family group, engaging periodically, and commenting informally.  One researcher (Ricardo) also wore a shoulder mount camera. For this analysis, we included one 5-min event captured on the video cameras: Creek Ravine. We chose this event because it provided participant interaction with one another and the environment. Our dataset comprised personal narratives from rese arch team members detailing their nature experiences before the activity.  In addition, data included individual summary notes and open coding of Creek Ravine (Merriam & Tisdell, 2015), which we refer to  as our “individual coding.” In open coding, we aim ed to identify pertinent notes on experience, nature, and learning. In our work, we intentionally took a wide-angle lens, instead of bracketing out personal understandings. We analyzed  our data as follows: 1. In a round-robin fashion, each member reviewed the other three members' indiv idual coding, given their narratives. 2. The original cod er read the other three researchers’ comments, adding information to allow for interpretation of  what we had seen.

3. We collectively coded pre-event narratives, forming our category list including affect, safety, and 23 others. 4.

Each member categorized the individual coding of their teammates. 5. We developed themes related to our own biases related to analyzing videos captured in nature via review of combinations of categories to develop themes.

## Findings

Our team brought varied experiences in nature to their work that appeared in their video summaries and individual coding. Our narratives present ed distinct perspectives on our interest in nature, influenced by individual experiences and backgrounds. Ricardo's narrative demonstrated a deep familial and cultural connection to nature, ingrained from a young age. Mandy's narrative evolved from childhood explorations to a professional and personal commitment to environmental education, highlighting a journey of appreciation a nd respect for nature. K athy's narrative reflected a changing relationship with the outdoors, from nei ghborhood play in childhood to deliberate exploration and appreciation of nature in adulthood. Faiaz's narrative depicted a transition from limited exposure to nature in an urban environment to a growing interest and engagement with nature through travel and recreation. Here we share one group of categories (affect and safety) to illustrate how our narratives linked with our differing video interpretations. For example, while everyone noted positive aspects about outdoors/nature in their narratives, three of us also indicated negative aff ect related to incidents in nature (Mandy noted poison ivy and a continuing fear of wasps, Kathy noted being lost in the woods and also having challenging discussions with family in nature environments, Faiaz noted nature as being a harsh environment), pointing to tendencies to see worry or fear. In contrast, Ricardo’s outdoor learning was developed through family engagement with nature as a learning environment, and his family, the teachers. Rather than affect, he brought a skills-based interpretation of nature. Given our narratives, affect and safety were evident to different degree in individual codes, highlighting different aspects of the video data. We illustrate this with one 50-sec incident when Lavendar, Mandy’s daughter, descended down a natural path that included apparent large steps of stone. Each team member open coded this as both affect and safety (except for Kathy, who excluded affect). Mandy, who throughout the data had a focus on family safety, indicated that she was worried about Lavendar. Faiaz’s pronounced concern of the incident differed from Mandy’s in degree, describing his concern, “Lavendar stumbles while climbing down into the ravine, Pine chuckles and Lavendar immediately yells, "I'm okay!"... I found this to be very interesting because I would have been more alarmed in a similar situation, running down to help my daughter.” Later in his account, he caught his own reaction, “At 16:16, when Pine laughs about Lavendar stumbling, you can notice me gasping at the event.” While Kathy had mentioned nature as a place to process challeng ing topics and a safety issue (getting lost), these did not provide ongoing fears. In her coding, she emphasized family members watching out for each other's safety, and this incident saying to Lavendar “that’s a pretty big step” which she then descended  on her bottom. In Ricardo’s individual coding, we noted only one incident that we coded as safety. “Pine said, ‘she got it.’ Maybe Pine was trying to calm down Mandy [who verbally express ed worry] by showing that he trusted on Lavendar's ability to go down into the ravine.” As with other video summaries, we noted that Ricardo had a skills focus about nature – nature was a place where problems (e.g., climbing down big rocks) were to be solved.

## Conclusion

Our narratives focused on data interpretation and coding processes, prompt ing reflection on the reasons behind observations. With affect and safety as differing aspects of our narratives trickling into our summaries and coding, our experiences in nature appeared to subtly influence what each saw or focused on and how they interpreted.
