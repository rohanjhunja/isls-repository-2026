# Improving Math Instruction at Scale: High-Quality Coaching as Socially Mediated Professional Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
High-quality instructional coaching can improve teachers’ ambitious instructional practice in mathematics, and in turn, positively impact student achievement. This study provides a quasi-experimental design on prospectively matched mathematics teachers receiving job-embedded one-on-one coaching. Utilizing multiple imputation methods, we present evidence to support a treatment effect in a full representative sample of 250 mathematics teachers in two distinct outcome measures of teaching. We further explore whether any coaching measures were associated with teaching growth and contributed to an explanation of any observed treatment. This paper demonstrates treatment effects from survey-based and observation measures of the effect of this coaching model to improve teachers’ instructional practices. This contributes to research on how the effects of coaching change instruction and contributes methodological insights to potential mechanisms for teaching improvement.

## Introduction

A vexing problem in the field of education has been  how to build research-based evidence for how professional learning influences teaching practice. Borko (2004)  suggested the field needed primary large-scale research studies moving forward, yet researchers continue to engage in secondary analyses to attempt to build knowledge about features of professional development and learning that influence student learning by changes in teachers’ instructional practice (Asterhan & Leftstein, 2023; Sims et al., 2023). However, the indirect relationships between teachers’ participation in professional learning and student outcomes are to a degree mediated by teachers’ knowledge and instructional practice (Meissel et al., 2016). While learning scientists continue to build theoretical knowledge in small-scale studies of teaching practice change (e.g., Saclarides & Munson, 2024), it remains the case that few quantitative empirical studies have demonstrated associations between mediational mechanisms for teacher learning and teaching practice change (see,  e.g., Munter & Correnti, 2017). This is in contrast to a behaviorist mechanistic view of learning as a stimulus-process-response structure of causation leading to learning (Seidel & Shavelson, 2007). Evidence has emerged that sophisticated conversational routines among teachers which included explicit attention to instructional practice, mathematical concepts, and student thinking led to more ambitious instructional practice (Horn & Kane, 2015). Specifically, high-quality coaching contains more intentional reflective moves thandirective moves in conversational routines (Gillespie et al., 2025; Witherspoon et al., 2021). We view these conversations from a  situated learning perspective w here individuals engage in professional learning through a social structure  that mediates the learning process and context, coach-teacher conversational routines (Horn & Garner, 2022). The work presented here  provides evidence, as part of a  largescale study, to build a potential link between high-quality conversational coaching practices and improvements to math instructional practice.

Hiebert and Grouws (2007) identified explicit atten tion to concepts (EAC) and students’ opportunity to struggle (SOS) as key teaching features that foster  mathematical conceptual understanding. Pursuing students’ conceptual understanding requires teachers to adopt  complex practices, such as open-ended, cognitively challenging tasks with alternative solution paths that connect with mathem atical concepts (Stein et al., 2008). Prior work demon strated evidence of the effectiveness of the Tennessee Math  Coaching  Model (MCM), in a prospectively matched representative sample of 250 teachers, on student achievement during the year of coaching. Importantly, achievement gains were also seen one y ear after the coaching was completed with new cohor ts of students in the treated condition relative to the comparison condition (Stein et al., 2017). This longitudinal study demonstrated a proof of concept for the potential  of the  MCM. Additional work focused on  how the MCM influenced growth in teaching practice over two years for a treatment-only study of 103 teachers (Russell et al., 2020; Witherspoon et al., 2021). These findings suggested  that the depth and specificity of mathematical conversational coaching routines  in a structured  pre-conference were associated with higher growth slope s in teaching practice.  Finally, a main effects comparison between teacher-reported measures of coaching in both conditions demonstrated more socially mediated practices in th e treatment condition, allowing  for an abductive inference that the number of socially mediated coaching conversations were a potential mechanism through which teaching practice improved (Correnti et al., accepted pending revision). In the current study we seek to test the conjecture about socially mediated conversations as one potential mechanism for changing teaching practice. Crucially, the current analyses  use multiple imputation methods to generate (k = 20) complete data sets for analysis. Using complete data,  we examine change in two teaching outcomes – pre-post videoed observations, and self-reported ambitious mathematics teaching practice on teacher surveys. We aim to  establish inductive inferences of the  association between  measures of coaching and both observed and self-reported changes in teaching practice. In that way,  we also contribute methodological insight for future quantitative studies  emphasizing quasi-experimental design  and seeking to measure complex sociocultural processes of teacher learning.

## Methods

To explore the association between measures of coac hing practice and changes in teaching, we used data from a rigorous quasi-experimental design – a prospectively matched study of mathematics coaching (Correnti et al., in process). In this study, 160 treatment teachers worked with mathematics coaches trained by the IFL-Tennessee math coaching model. 90 Comparison teachers were also coached as a requirem ent of study participation. The “active” comparison condition represents business-as-usual (BAU) coaching. This distinction is important for identifying the elements which contribute to the adoption of  ambitious instructional practice. We compiled demographic data and context characteristics from the state educational authority. This included prior achievement of teachers’ classrooms going back five years, teacher pedagogical content knowledge score at baseline, years teaching, percent of economically disadvantaged students,  percent of under-represented minorities, professional experience, years teaching at grade level, teacher race, and teacher ethnicity. Primary d ata collection was multifaceted including teaching surveys, teacher-reported logs, coachreported logs, and  observations. Given we collected coaching logs from all teacher p articipants, we will use measures of teacher-reported coaching as one of our main sets of IVs. For the treatment teachers, we also collected coach-reported logs of their coaching events. Thus, we will also test whether any coach-reported measures predict variation in teaching growth. Finally, we also admi nistered our teacher surveys to coaches to understa nd the coaches’ vision for ideal teaching practice given their teachers’ contexts.

Using Stata v.18.0 we used the multiple imputed chained equations (MICE) command to simultaneously impute 20 full data sets for analysis. Multiple imputation has been argued as the best approach for analyzing data that are at least missing-at-random (Enders et al., 2016; Woods et al., 2023).

## Measures of teaching

Teacher pre-post self-report survey of teaching practice (TS) was administered to identify teachers’ practices on two primary constructs-students’ opportunities to struggle (SOS) and explicit attention to concepts (EAC) (Hiebert & Grouws, 2007). Likert-scale items of teacher self-reports of their math instruction, and Likert-scale items about their beliefs about their math teaching were analyzed in a second-order two factor model (Stein et al., 2017). In our analysis, we focus on the secondary constructs students are not ready for conceptual teaching representative of SOS and conceptual teaching with minimal teacher input representative of EAC. Pre-post assessment of videoed observations (VO) of teaching practice were collected for teachers with complete data generated by multiple imputations. These VO were scored by expert raters to identify two main measures of ambitious, standards-based instruction: the teachers’ maintenance of cognitive demand of the task during the lesson (Hiebert & Grouws, 2007), and whether teachers provided students with opportunities to engage in and make public their (conceptual) thinking (Rigby et al., 2017).

## Measures of coaching

Teacher-reported logs (TL) were completed by the teacher after each interactio n to identify and measure the coaching activities and practices they engaged in. Teachers identified the type of activities they par ticipated in, such as informal conversation, lesson planning, pre-conference, observation, post conference, professional development, etc. Teachers also provided information about their coac hing interactions that allowed us to form constructs. First,  teacher resistance during coaching  measured if the teacher did not actively engage in the coaching process possibly due to perceiving coachin g as evaluative or an indictment of their current t eaching practice. Second, teacher thinking during coaching measured the degree to which the teacher felt the c oach engaged them in mathematically rich thinking during the interaction (Campbell & Griffin, 2017). The coach-reported logs (CL) were completed by the MCM coaches after each interaction to measure the coaches’ perceptions of the partner teacher’s responsiveness to coaching  and their reports of the type(s) of interactions. Coach surveys (CS) were completed by MCM coaches at basel ine. Coaches were asked to  respond to items in the teacher survey with their vision for the ideal practices given their teachers’ context(s). We interpret this as the coaches’ vision for practice on each construct of SOS and EAC. Analysis Building from prior analyses, we examined covariate-adjusted regression models to unearth the extent to  which the complete data revealed a treatment effect with both types of teaching measures. We further explored whether any coaching measures were associated with teaching  growth and whether we observed such measures “explaining” any observed treatment effect. We used  a one-tailed t-value as our criteria for significance as  this was a directional hypothesis – we are testing for a positive treatment effect o nly. Using our imputed data, we examined the following research questions:

1. Across all teachers, is there a dosage effect of teacher-reported socially mediated pre-and post-conference coaching conversations on changes in teaching practice?

2. What other patterns emerge in how coaching measures predict change in teaching practice?

3. Across treatment teachers, does the number of coach-reported socially mediated pre-and post-conference coaching conversations predict the amount of change in teaching practice?

4. What other patterns emerge in how coaching measures predict change in teaching practice?

## Results

The first set of models that we examined identified  greater change in teaching practice for teachers receiving MCM coaching versus BAU coaching. In Table 1, model 1 shows this difference in both the videoed observations outcome (ES=.50) as well as teachers’  self-reported practice for students not ready for conceptual teaching (ES=.31) and conceptual teaching with minimal teacher input  (ES=.34). Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) for the MCM group were calculated as the co-efficient divided by the pooled standard deviation of the pre and post measure of teaching (Tomczak & Tomczak, 2014). Importantly, a negative score on students are not ready for conceptual teaching is our desired  outcome as  it means the teachers  reject the idea that students should not struggle in learning. All demographic data described above were included as control variables in all models and were  nonsignificant except for the control variables of prior measure of the teaching outcome. In model 2, we added measures of coaching to understand whether variation in coaching practice helped explain the observed treatment effect. In each case, we found a dosage effect  the count of socially mediated learning opportunities with their coach was signifi cant. Moreover, once this coaching measure was adde d, the treatment effect was no longer significant. In addition, because teachers in both conditions all filled out coaching logs, we could also examine teacher-reported measures about the content of their coaching conversati ons. We found that teachers’ resistance to coaching was a positive predictor for the mindset that students are not ready for conceptual teaching. In contrast, when teachers reported being pushed to think and reason about their practice in new ways, the construct of teacher thinking during coaching, they were more likely to engage in student-centered conceptual instruction. These measures supplement, but do not replace, findings about the dosage effect. ## Discussion

We set out to understand whether we could find evid ence that variation in coaching practices predicted teaching change in a relatively large-scale study of teacher professional learning. Specifically, how coaching conversations contribute to ambitious instructional practice. We found evidence of a treatment effect across both teaching outcomes. Furthermore, we found the number of pre-and post-conference conversations teachers reported on their coaching logs  as a good proxy for the dosage effect. This is i mportant for two reasons. One, it is further support for a causal effect of the MCM as a dosage effect is one facet among Hill’s criteria for causa tion (1965). Two, it provides more direct evidence that the vari ation in teachers’ reports of coaching predicts their teaching practice change both between MCM and BAU conditions – Table 1; as well as within the MCM condition – Table

2. This fits our situated learning  theory about the role of socially  mediated conversations as an important

mechanism for teaching change. In addition, the structure and research-based coaching practices engaged in the MCM support the role of sophisticated conversational routines in developing ambitious instructional practices. Finally, our exploration within the treatment group  revealed that different measures from the MCM teachers’ and coaches’ perspectives also provided further substantive clues about the content of professional oneon-one coaching conversations that might predict teaching change. The continuing significance of the coaches’ vision of the teaching outcome and the teachers’ re ported resistance to coaching offer support to the importance of the coach-teacher relationship and of an adequate fit between both to contribute to the teacher’s professional growth.

These findings are relevant for building theory for how coaching influences teaching. We encourage the field to move away from behaviorist explanations of  practice change, and for researchers to adopt socio-cultural learning theor ies to explain the complex socially mediated means that lead to professional learning and instructional practice change. Also, our methodological processes could help in the design of future quantitative investigations that seek to identify mechanisms for how to improve teaching practice.
