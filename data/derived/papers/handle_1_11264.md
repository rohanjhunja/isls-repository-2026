# Community, Creativity, and Identity in Generative Role-Play Based Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines learner experiences in generative role-playing game-based learning implemented in a university science fiction literature course. Through complex systems thematic network analysis of student reflections, we investigated how principles from learning theories manifested in the game-based learning experience. Analysis revealed five distinct clusters of principles, with constructionist elements showing the highest network centrality. The findings suggest that game creation and world-building serve as powerful tools for literary education, with students navigating multiple identities—both real and fictional—throughout the course. The study identified an important prerequisite for engagement: students must either leverage existing gamer identities or develop new ones before fully engaging in the creative process. This research contributes to understanding how game-based learning can facilitate transformative learning experiences in literature courses, while highlighting the need for better integration of productive failure and tinkering principles in future design iterations.

## Introduction and background

In the Spring of 2024, we designed and observed the creation of a generative role-playing game titled “The Ballad of Proxima-b” by the students of ENGL 434, a Science Fiction Literature course at Texas A&M University. This course was originally designed by the course instru ctor (Cooper) and two learning scientists (Chowdury and Donaldson), but we have chosen to include the perspective of  one of the students (Pawson) who played the generative game that semester. This generative role-playing game took place in four distinct phases that stretched across the semester. At every step, students were tasked with the creation of the game, and for the final step, they played the game they created. Details about “The Ballad of  Proxima-b” can be found on the wikifandom page created by members of the course (Fandom Contributo rs, 2024). In particular, this wiki served as a com munal repository of memory through which students playing “The Ballad of Proxima-b” could continue to interact with previous generations of players. While we had no co ntrol over the choices made by students as they con structed their fantasy worlds, races, and conflicts, we did work to ensure that the game met the learning outcomes for the course while providing a transformational experience. Because this is a literature course, it was relatively easy to think critically about elements related to characte rs, setting, and narratives. The transformational e xperience is, however, key to creating lifelong learners—in this case, students who continue to read science fiction and fantasy because it is now part of their identity.

The theoretical framework in which we grounded our analysis focused on complex systems theory (Jacobson & Wilensky, 2022), which allowed us to as sume that learning experiences are complex systems in which self-organizing behaviors between a large number of  elements lead to emergent properties that can be understood through network analysis (Thurner et al., 2018). The theoretical framework in which we grounded the course design included cognitive constructivist learning theory (Piaget, 1952), social constructivist learning theory (Vygotsky, 1978), constructionist learning theory (Kafai, 2006), situated learning theory (Lave & Wenger, 1991), transformative learning theory (Mezirow, 2018), self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000), and the dynamic systems model of role identity (Kaplan & Garner, 2017).

## Methods

This study investigates the learning experiences of  students in the second iteration of a design-based research study (Barab, 2022), although our focus in this paper is not on crafting design moves for the next iteration. In this complex systems thematic n etwork analysis study, reflection papers were gathe red from the twenty students in the course at the end of the semester. These were e ntered into the MAXQDA Analytics Pro software where  they were coded according to an a priori codebook containing 61 codes for principles of game-based learning and principles from the theories in which the learning experience was grounded: cognitive constructivist l earning theory, social constructivist learning theory, cons tructionist learning theory, situated learning theo ry, transformative learning theory, self-determination theory, and the dynamic systems model  of role identity. Pearson’s correlations (1-tailed) for all possible combinations of pairs of c odes were calculated. Using a symmetrical correlation matrix at P < 0.05, we conducted network analysis in the UCINET  software. We conducted Girvan-Newman cluster analysis (Girvan & Newman, 2002) to understand the community structure regarding how learner experiences aligned with principles from theories and elements of game-based learning. In the network map, the node sizes were adjusted to re flect Eigenvector values where larger nodes represent principles that are more integral to the structure of the complex system (Nicolis & Nicolis, 2012).

## Findings

The network analysis resulted in a network map with  5 clusters at Q = 0.402, and larger nodes reflecting greater Eigenvector values (see Figure 1). The red circles cluster contains the principles that are most integ ral (highest Eigenvector values) to the learning experience (i.e., the complex system), followed by principles in t he pink squares cluster.

Figure 1 Network Map of Learning Theory Elements in Generative Game-Based Learning The red circles and pink squares indicate two distinct aspects of “gameplay.” The red circles involve the creation of the game. The theories aligned with the red circles reflect this. Students had to immerse themselves in the game world, where they had to tinker and reflect. Here more than anywhere else, s tudents engaged in constructionist making that allowed them to tap int o their imagination. Within the red circles, it is also clear that students are engaging with the process; rather than focusing on the end product, they understand the inherent value of the process itself. Interestingly, the gray squa res only connect to the red circles, and even there  via a single node. The gray squares indicate that part of the pr ocess of making the game forced students to questio n their assumptions and to engage in productive failure. One task for future designs will be to better integrate the theories correlated to the gray squares and to create more nodes between those squares and the red circles. One way to do this might be to include structured thought activities tha t engage with the productive failures involved when  one is trying to take on new and different perspectives.

The pink squares involved the more “active” aspects  of the game play: rolling dice, making decisions, opening treasures, and slaying monsters. The pink s quares are better integrated with the red circles t han the gray squares, but increased integration here  could be useful. During the making phase of the gam e, students do not report strong feelings of agency, autonomy, or authority. One way to address this lack of integration between the pink squares and red circles would be to focus on “ role-storming” or ot her similar brainstorming exercises. In “role-storming,” a student is asked to think about the day’s readings, but they are asked to think about it from the perspective of their fictional character. In this way, students can begin “playing” their character—thereby building agency—before the actual dice-rolling, “active” game sessions begin. The black and blue triangles present a difficult ca se. If there could be said to be a “starting point, ” it would be the blue triangles. In this cluster, we fi nd three elements: community, playing the game, and  identity exploration. “Community” bridges the gap, indicatin g the formation of community that happens early in the semester when students begin creating the fictional  world in which they will play. Starting from commu nity, it seems that students who had already possessed an id entity as a tabletop gamer could move quickly to th e red circles and begin the process of building the world  (red circles). Conversely, some students must trav erse the cluster of black triangles before proceeding to make the game (red circles). The black triangles involve rethinking perceptions and identity, and for that reason [the first author] theorizes that for many students, the y had to begin cultivating a gamer identity before they could imme rse themselves in the game. This observation reiter ates the need for early gaming sessions that help students navigate their identity as someone who plays imaginative games. As one of the participating students, I (Adam Pawso n) had my own interpretations of the game as well. I am a college undergraduate with a creative writin g background and several professional science-fiction publications. While my education is in science, I r egard myself as a creative person and enjoy both pl aying and designing tabletop games, single-player story-driven video games, and D&D-esque roleplaying games. I think my background with gaming and science fiction was cert ainly applicable to the in-class project, though, through my experience and observation, I believe that the more  crucial identity for a student to hold was one of a creative. The nature of creating something involves exposing parts of yourself to criticism, and it is a large h urdle in the process that takes practice to comfortably vault. Some students were notably more willing to put their ideas front and center than others, and the former worked bette r at incorporating feedback and criticism into thei r working ideas than the latter. Understandably, the red node for creativity is linked to the red node of confidence–I believe those who were more open to being vulnerable in the project, including taking on the task of leading a small group campaign, ultimately saw more success in creating u nique characters, scenarios, and worlds. In additio n to personal identities, I believe, through the lens of a student, that the situational identity of the class as a whole was beneficial to the fostering of the game. These iden tities are transient, shifti ng between social settings; everyone has experienced a class where the air felt awkward and brittle–all of the creative talent and gaming experience would be for naught if the class itself didn’t foster an identity of engagement. This identity is one that needs to be fostered between students, as well as with their professor(s), but can feel nebulous and abstract, if not completely random.

## Implication and conclusion

The network analysis of students' reflections reveals the strong clustering of contructionist principles (red circles) with high Eigenvector values (i.e., role playing, worldbuilding, creating, designing a game). This suggests that generative game-based learning experiences are grounded in the act of creation and world-building. This finding demonstrates that game creation can serve as a powe rful learning tool in literature courses (Kafai, 20 06). Fundamentally, elements of narrative, setting, character, and  point of view emerge naturally for the process of world creation. In science fiction and fantasy this  worldbuilding is formalized and theorized to a gre ater extent (Wolf, 2021), leading to possible future synergies between literary and learning theories. The findings also emphasize the role of constructionist theory in fostering identity work (Kafai & Burke, 2014). Through creative tasks like world-building and character creation, learners engage in  acts of construction that are deeply tied to identity fo rmation (Kaplan & Garner, 2017). Over the length of  the course students are asked to navigate several real world identities (student, reader, gamer, creator) while navigating a s elf-created, fictional identity. This fictional identity serves,  when leveraged by the instructor, as a powerful tool for thinkin g critically about identity in real life, thereby cre ating a process of real-life transformation through identity simulation. This supports the theoretical perspective that "making" is not merely a task but an integral component of "becoming" (Donaldson & Allen-Handy, 2023), positioning constructionist practices  as not only effective but essential for fostering transformative learning and identity exploration. Another point to note is that the emergence of iden tity (black and blue triangles) displays how transformative learning operates in game-based learning contexts. Findings suggest that stud ents need to either leverage existing role identities or develop new ones (Kaplan & Garner, 2017) before fully engaging in the creative process. Although this was a traditional literature  course, the act of making the game opened up into creative writing, a pedagogical realm not often represented in theoretical literature. This understanding of identity illustrates Mezirow’s (2018) transformative learning theory by emphasizing how identity transformation can be a prerequisite for, rather than just an outcome of, transformative learning experience. Perhaps first and foremost, our research has transf ormed what we once perceived to be an ‘entry-level barrier’ to actually be a complex navigation of identity that needs to be explored on a larger scale and using crossdisciplinary samples. Some students might be intimidated by the idea of playing a complex role-playing game for the first time, but they seem primed to accept the identity of ‘gamer’ because society has primed them  for it. Whether it be from Stranger Things or The Big Bang Theory, people know about Dungeons & Dragons. Our research reveals they are actually navigating their  identities as part of a group and as creators. As creators, they have to be taught about tinkering and productive failure. Creative writers know that these practices are at the heart of the creative process. Accordingly, the research should do more to emphasize and integrate these pri nciples of successful failure into the design. Tinkering and productive failure are theoretically related (Kafai, 2006; Kafai & Burke, 2014; Kapur & Bielaczyc, 2012), but in this study productive failure was not within the main cl usters. Further research must investigate ways in which des igns for learning could integrate productive failur e and tinkering more into the learning experience.

Cultivating this creator mentality appears to be th e first step in fostering a sense of community, an area that needs to be investigated more thoroughly. In p revious design move brainstorming sessions, we thou ght that it would be good to begin with a mini-game that familiarizes students with the game mecha nics, but this study reveals that the game is not as intimidating as the idea of being a creator and creating without guardrails. Instead, community and community building strategies must be  emphasized fro m the start, because it is this communal identity that begins the process of transforming th eir identities. In accordance with Maslow’s hierarc hy of needs (1943), these basics of community must be met befor e anyone can begin the higher order thinking involv ed in identity exploration and transformation.

Further research must also be done regarding constr uctionism as a vehicle not just for learning but fo r complex identity transformation through critical ch allenges to one’s assumptions. One way to emphasize  this constructionism might be through “role-storming” a thought exercise that asks students to take on various roles so as to position and situate their analysis (Michalko, 2010). Such techniques could foster an understanding of the relationship between environment and perspective wh ile allowing students  to create an identity to do so. In literature courses, such role-play might involve reading a text as if one were an  alien from another planet. In professional development courses, they might involv e role-playing the desired profession. For young people, it may even be a valuable tool for imagining their life and future selves (Gee, 2005). These possibilities point to the pressing need for a large cross-disciplinary study to determine which elements of t his design translate over into other contexts and which do not. This research could show that generative role-play gaming has a powerful effect on learners of any discipline.
