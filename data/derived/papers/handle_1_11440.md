# Centering the Lived Experiences of Youth Who Have Been Marginalized to Co-Design Social Justice Campaigns

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines the design of the Youth Activism and Advocacy program within the Clubhouse Network. Young people at ten of the Clubhouse sites around the world used their creativity, lived experiences, and civic imagination to develop and sustain social justice campaigns in their communities. This paper focuses on the design elements that allow young people who have been marginalized to leverage their lived experiences in their research, development, and implementation of their campaigns. Qualitative results indicate that providing participatory design training to the adults supporting the campaigns allowed the youth participants to take relevant civic action that led to positive impacts within their communities.

## Introduction

Historically, researchers and educators have caused harm to marginalized communities. As a researcher, this harm can be caused by extracting data without providing results and feedback to the community it was taken from or producing artifacts without imple menting the input of the community (Costanza-Chock, 2019). As an educator, this could look like reinforcing the idea that youth who have been marginalized  do not come to learning spaces with their own unique knowledge and need to assimil ate. For this reaso n, a transformative justice framework approach should be applied when working with young people from these communities. This framework allow s for both acknowledging the past harms and co-creating a sustainable future that dismantles oppre ssive systems and mitigates harm. While it is not possible to guarantee a place without harm, one can co-create an environment that allows people’s voices and opinions to be valu ed and considered, creating a path forward. Researc hers, educators, and others engaging youth from marginali zed communities in creative learning environments m ust explore the historical context of their own work, self-reflect, and listen to differing opinions (Erete, et al. 2018).

In this paper, I introduce and evaluate the design methods used to develo p an initiative within the Clubhouse Network, the Youth Activism and Advocacy program – a cohort of Clubhouse Coordinators supporting youth-led social justice campaigns. The program allows young people to use their lived experiences and civic imagination to take civic action.

## Background

The Clubhouse Network For more than 30 years, The Clubhouse Network has served youth in historically underserved communities around the world, providing them with a space to engage in creative learning and the opportunity to develop technological fluency (Resnick et al., 1998). Clubhouse Network is comprised of more than 100 learning spaces in 20 different countries that put constructionist learning principles into practice (Kafai et. al, 2009). The Network uses creative learning principles to reinforce a learning model t hat allows young people to learn through designing their own projects, cultivate an environment of respect and trust,  and build confidence using technology. (Resnick, 2017). The learning model emphasizes the fluidity of roles (Kafai et al., 2009), allowin g young people multiple entry points into projects in a variety of roles as they explore ideas and technologies. I collaborated with The Clubhouse Network to design and implement the Youth Activism and Advocacy program to create an infrastructure that supports y outh in activism projects/initiatives on an ongoing  basis. The program was proposed in 2020, shortly after George Floyd was killed by the Minneapolis Police Departme nt. Five Clubhouses were in the city limits of Minneapolis at the time, and it  was evident that young people who attended Clubhouses were impacted by the growing social justice efforts in their comm unity. Many intersecting issues impact young people in Clubhouses worldwide due to both their physical locations and socioeconomic status. The program was designed as a cohort model to support the Coordinators who were supporting the youthled campaigns. The cohort met monthly to co-design and share their ideas, experiences, and resources. The ten sites were located across the United States, Palestine, Australia, South Africa, and Panama. Youth Civic Engagement Asset and action-based approaches to civic learning are situated at the intersection of civic action and organizing, critical civic, race, and ethnic studies, and youth development and community action (Ito & Cross, 2022). One of the expected outcomes of the Youth Activism and Advocacy program is that Clubhouse youth use their creativity to imagine new possibilities for their communit y using technology as a tool to communicate the chang es they wish to see.

Young people at Clubhouses have multiple social identities that they bring to civic engagement projects. Asset-based approaches in civic learning highlight the st rengths and resources that youth bring to their communities and civic engagements. These approaches are grounded in the belief that all youth possess valuable assets and strengths that can contribute to positiv e community change (Ginwright, 2011; Generation Cit izen, 2019). The Youth Activism and Advocacy program builds on this by encouraging young people to select research and address issues in their community that they have a personal connection to. The Youth Activism and Advocacy program supports young people in taking civic actio n. Action-based approaches to civic engagement, such as youth organizing and participatory action research (PAR), enable youth to identify and address systemi c issues affecting their communities. These approaches emphasize c ritical consciousness, civic efficacy, and collecti ve action as mechanisms for social change (Cammarota & Fine, 2008; Rubin & Hayes, 2010). While the Clubhouse Network has a history of projects rooted and civic awareness, the Youth Activism and Advocacy program encourages youth to combine their creativity, lived  experience, and civic imagination to take action within their community.

## Methods

The design of the Youth Activism and Advocacy progr am combines theoretical frameworks, human-centered design methods, and my own experiences as a learner, educator, community organizer, and designer. The Youth Activism and Advocacy program was designed to cente r the lived experience of youth at Clubhouses. Usin g design justice principles and transformative justice methods, the program explicitly and intentionally pushes back on the dominant narrative of how young people learn  and contribute to society, particularly those from  non-dominant backgrounds.

Design occurred in three different contexts within the Youth Activism and Advocacy program.

• Designing the cohort experience. I designed a learning experience for the Youth Activism and

Advocacy cohort that reinforced the existing creative learning model within Clubhouses and applied a transformative justice framework and design justice principles. Additionally, I used an autoethnography analysis to make design decisions.

• Co-designing and data analysis with the cohort. I used qualitative design methods to co-design

individual site experiences, and aggregate and analyze data. The co-design sessions consisted of activities and workshops in the community of practice meetings, as well as one-on-one sessions.

• Youth co-design sessions. Cohort members co-designed social justice campaigns with the young people

at their Clubhouses and their communities. They used methods such as community-driven knowledge, interviews, and surveys to collect data to inform the design of their campaigns. Applying a transformative justice framework Transformative justice is a  political framework  that aims to create social change through harm reduction, addressing the root causes of issues, and building thriving communities. The goal is to transform syst ems. A transformative justice approach takes a look at the  full picture and considers the conditions  that allowed for the violence, harm, or abuse to take place and addresse s them. Traditionally, this framework is used to address harm on an individual basis and can “be applied to any context where harm exists ca used by systemic oppression” (Erete et al., 2021).

Clubhouses are unique spaces because the members wh o gather there are impacted by multiple systems of oppression daily due to their race, age, gender,  socio-economic status, and geographical location. One pla ce where they experience systemic oppression is in their learning spaces. Young people, especially those from nondominant communities, historically enter learning s paces with the expectation to assimilate rather tha n showing up with and leveraging their own unique knowledge. The Clubhouse Network combats this by providing you ng people with a constructionist learning experience. This is emphasized in the design of the Youth Activ ism and Advocacy program by ensuring all projects were youth-led. To apply a transformative justice lens, I used principles from the Design Justice Network  to guide the design of the Youth Activism and Advocacy program. The principles allowed each site to center the vo ices of young people who have been marginalized, prioritize  emergent needs, facilitate learning, consider lived experiences as expertise, share design tools and re sources, and collaborate with other commun ity-based organizations.

Autoethnography Autoethnography is a form of ethnographic research that connects the researcher’s personal experience with a cultural experience.  I am inspired by the tradition of autoethnography i n Black feminist anthropology to use my positionality as an authoritative voice in my writi ng (McClaurin, 2001). While many researchers provide a positionality statement in qualitative research (Holmes, 2020), my own expe riences and relationships with the community I worked with afforded me to make design decisions I would not have otherwise considered. I am a Black woman who grew up in a low-income home in an under-resourced community in Tacoma, WA. I grew up attending free after-school programs that prepared me to be a first-generation college student and graduate. One of those programs was the Tacoma Clubhouse. Joining the Tacoma Clubhouse in the seventh grade was my entry point into the Clubhouse Network. In m y time as a member (seventh through twelfth grade),  my Clubhouse received many grants and participated in many advocacy projects. As a college student, I was a mentor for the Girls’ Day program and continued to mentor after graduating. I then became a Clubhouse Coordin ator at the Tacoma Clubhouse, where I served in that capaci ty for four years before departing for graduate sch ool. As a Coordinator, I wrote grants and designed advocacy projects for the members. At that time, I also began community organizing with the Tacoma Action Collective. As a graduate student in the Lifelong Kindergarten group, I collaborated with the Clubhouse Network on many dif ferent workshops and initiatives. My many different roles and experiences within the Clubhouse allowed me to make design decisions about the Youth Activism and Advocacy program that I wouldn’t have made otherwise.

Co-design Co-design is a participatory design approach where the  designer and stakeholders work collaboratively.  In many research and design approaches in HCI (e.g., partic ipatory design, action research, and co-design), the power structure is set up in a way where the input of the community is secondary to that of the researcher. This is evident in instances where t he researcher/designer gets input and brings it bac k to the community instead of creating alongside them. Allowing communities to have a voice, in a meaningful way, empowers them to create solutions and processes that work for them as opposed to solu tions that have been forced upon them  (Harrington et al., 2019).

The lived experiences of people from communities that have been marginalized should be considered as expertise, and their voices, concerns, and opinions  should be valued throughout the design and impleme ntation process, not just in the planning stage. In the community of practice or cohort meetings, participants were provided with strategies to engage their youth in co-design. In the context of the Clubhouse Network we referred to this process as “youth-led”, where youth were driving the design decision s while the adults, or project leads, were there as facilitators. These methods include inquiry-based coaching, shared definitions, and facilitation coaching. Interviews and community-driven knowledge Interviews took place at several levels of this project. I interviewed Clubhouse Network staff to better understand the activism and advocacy already happening within the Network. This allowed me to build upon prior knowledge and work as opposed to duplicating initiatives already underway. I also conducted interviews with cohort members to explore how the Youth Activism and Advocacy program progressed at their sites. Young people at all ten sites conducted interviews with stakeholders and community members in their design process. Additionally, youth took time to compile information that they already knew,  from their own experiences, to help guide their interview process and questions.

## Data analysis

To analyze the data that was collected, I used a th ematic analysis to identify themes and patterns acr oss the ten sites. Through my analysis, I was able to iterative ly design the community of practice meetings where co-design sessions occurred and provide recommendations for future programming. In several cohort meetings, participants engaged in co-analysis activities. We used Google Jam Board as a tool to share and sort data from the co-design session. One example was to support the Clubhouse  2 College, Clubhouse 2 Career (C2C) program. Throughout the year, cohort members took note of the skills their members demonstrated, developed, and strengthened during the program. In a cohort session, we aggregated the  data using Google Jam Board, and participants used  the information to take back to their Clubhouses to 1) help their members understand and communicate the skills they learned and 2) share outcomes with potential funders.

## Findings

The Youth Activism and Advocacy program provided Clubhouse Members the space and tools to use their lived experiences to take civic action within their communities. Through a thematic analysis of interviews from participants, I found that the Coordinator’s participation in co-design sessions was critical to the design of the Youth Activism and Advocacy program.

Many of the adult participants who had engaged in youth development w ork did not have experience with youth-led work but had experience with youth voice, where their opinions are valued and considered. The co-design sessions that occurred in the form of activi ties and workshops allowed the cohort the opportuni ty to engage in and develop experience facilitating parti cipatory design methods before implementi ng these practices with youth. For participants who had never supported a youth-led initiative before, this gave them a concrete way to allow young people to lead the research, design,  and implementation of their social justice campaig ns. In contrast, many youth development programs are modeled after instructionist learning, where the adult lead decides learning outcomes as opposed to facilitating interest-based learning. We saw that this approach of engaging young people as co-designers was important in several ways: for identi fying relevant issues and in the opportunities it provided to collaborate with others in their community, working on similar goals. At all ten of the sites, the issues that young people identified and addressed were both relevant and timely. Because the topics came from the youth's lived expe rience, they had a sense of what was important in t heir community. Through the process of the program, incl uding identifying root causes, they developed a dee per and broader understanding of the issues and how they could address them. Across the ten sites, young people not only co-designed with their Coordinators but also worked closely with local community members throughout their desig n process. They reached out to see what work was already being done and how their project could contribute t o ongoing efforts. In some instances, the young peo ple combined their efforts with community partners to a void duplicating work. In Minneapolis, the Clubhouse members saw the lack of reliable transportation as an issue in their community and began to address it  by advocating for free public transportation for youth. They saw safety as a concern, as many of the members get to places by walking or biking. Through their research, they found an organization called Our Streets Min neapolis, which shared the same concerns about safety. Through this  partnership, they learned about the history of the ir community and how it once was a thriving Black and Jewish neighborhood that was split down the middle by a bypass. Upon further investigation, they discovered  that the decision to develop a bypass in the commu nity was rooted in racism and discrimination. They also real ized that avoiding cars wouldn’t change the dangero us intersection in front of their Clubhouse. With the help of Our Street Minneapolis, they launc hed a campaign to design and install crosswalk murals along the bypass to increase the visibility of pedestrians and slow down traffic. While this was just one project, it was the beginni ng of a partnership towards transportation justice in Minneapolis.

## Discussion

Youth identified core issues in their communities, and using human-centered design methods, were able to identify the root causes of the issues, identify community n eeds, and partner with community organizations to b uild campaigns that addressed the issues. An outcome of practicing these design methods was the development  and strengthening of a broad range of skills that youth can apply to future education and career opportunities. Clubhouse members produced podcasts, mobile apps, and websites, and built things such as sustainable 3D printers, to address the issues in their communi ty. Engaging young people as co-designers allowed them to explore human-centered design methods they may not have otherwise. Through co-design, they were able to conduct research in their communities and design a social justice campaign that responded to a community need. In the application process, they shared issues that  they had experience with and stated  what they believed to be the root cause of these issues. Through methods suc h as interviewing community members, they were able  to identify root causes and investigate if their propo sed campaigns would meet the needs of their communi ty. This led to an ite rative design process, where they continued to make  changes to their campaign. Using co-design methods as an approach to youth participation diffe rs from many youth development programs that encour age youth voices. Co-design affords youth to participate by  taking action on their ideas as opposed to only voi cing them.

The lived experiences of young people from identities that have been marginalized should be considered an asset. While young people's level of expertise m ay vary, it can be meaningfully leveraged as an ent ry point into participation. Each young person who participa ted in the Youth Activism and Advocacy program ente red their space with the knowledge to contribute. Building on this knowledge  was important and provided an asset-based approach to civic learning. Their lived exper iences also served as motivation  to complete and implement their campaigns, as they would be directly impacted by the outcomes.
