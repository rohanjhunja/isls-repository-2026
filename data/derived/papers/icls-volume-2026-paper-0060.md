# Stories of Practice as a Means to promote Teachers' Professional Learning: The Role of AI -Based Chatbots

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how AI conversational chatbots can support teachers’ professional learning through writing reflective stories of practice within networks of research– practice partnerships (RPPs). We developed “Tell Me Your Story”—a chatbot designed to guide teachers in recounting meaningful classroom experiences and generate structured narratives. Grounded in a framework that conceptualizes identities as story-based, and learning as identity development, the study examined how such interactions help teachers articulate identity stories and promote their professional learning. Ten teachers who integrated citizen science initiatives in their teaching engaged in chatbot-assisted storytelling followed by a meta-reflective interview. The resulting narratives met the criteria of identity stories, with most participants developing new or reinforced insights interpreted as professional identity development. These findings suggest that chatbot-supported storytelling can generate knowledge artifacts that may facilitate collective learning across networks of RPPs.

Teachers’ professional learning unfolds throughout their careers as an ongoing, non-linear process composed of countless moments of reflection, realization, and small shifts in understanding (Clarke & Hollingworth, 2002; Superfine et al., 2022). These lived insights, often subtle yet profound, capture teachers’ growing awareness of their practice and professional identity (Vokatis & Zhang, 2016). Research has shown that such moments can be productively translated into stories, for example through teachers’ professional blogs, which serve as powerful means to capture and articulate their lived experiences. Engaging in such storytelling not only deepens teachers’ reflection but also fosters the development of their professional identity (Luehmann, 2008; Luehmann & Frink, 2009). Moreover, teachers’ stories of practice can be meaningful for other teachers who are in a similar stage of implementing changes, providing insights about practice that resonate across contexts (Kali et al., 2020). Such insights are crucial for educational change (Datnow, 2020). When teachers collaborate with learning scientists in research–practice partnerships (RPPs), stories of practice can become powerful vehicles for co-constructing knowledge that bridges research and classroom realities, potentially driving educational change (Hod et al., 2018). Once made public, these stories can be recognized as valuable knowledge artifacts in their own right, acknowledging teachers' unique insights and transforming them into shared epistemic resources that enable teachers and researchers to engage as equal partners in the co-construction of knowledge (Kali et al., 2020). This resonates with the conference theme, which calls for building "effective research-practice partnerships through cross-sector collaboration". Yet, despite the recognized value of stories of practice that involve deep reflection, the generation of such stories is a challenging and time-consuming task (Selland, 2017; Shank, 2006). It often requires partners who are not always available (Arefian et al., 2024). As a result, teachers seldom engage in such storytelling, and the opportunities to capture, share, and build upon their experiential knowledge remain limited. This is where we see the potential of AI-based agents, such as conversational chatbots, to facilitate the capturing of teachers’ stories of practice for use as shared knowledge resources within RPPs, as well as larger networks of RPPs. While stories of practice hold great promise as knowledge artifacts within RPP networks, we focus here on the process of creating them, and on the potential of conversational chatbots to support this process. Specifically, we examine how teachers experience and perceive the act of crafting their stories through this interaction, and the significance this process may hold for their professional learning and identity development. Central to our approach is the notion of an identity story (Sfard & Prusak, 2005), a narrative derived from the teacher’s personal experiences and impressions that represents a facet of their identity. We draw on a conception of learning as identity development, specifically as the process of reducing the gap bet ween an individual’s actual identity (their present self), and a designated identity (a future-oriented vision of themselves (Sfard & Prusak, 2005). To facilitate the creation of identity stories that can support meaningful reflection, we developed “Tell Me Your Story,” an AI conversational chatbot. This tool was designed for teachers' use in the context of school-based citizen science, a growing field that redefines teachers’ pedagogical roles as they guide students’ participation in authentic research (Kali et al., 2023). The chatbot combines a scripted dialogue structure 503 with responsive interaction to guide teachers in recounting and reflecting on meaningful classroom experiences from the incorporation of citizen science into their teaching. Based on the teachers’ input, the chatbot produces a structured, externally genera ted narrative. We argue that this narrative can serve as an identity story, offering a unique opportunity for reflection and professional learning. By examining the experiences of ten teachers who interacted with the chatbot and reflected on the stories it generated, we ask:

1. To what extent do teachers’ interactions with the “Tell Me Your Story” chatbot lead to narratives that

represent identity stories?

2. How does the process of chatbot-assisted storytelling support teachers' learning through the development

of their professional identity?

## Theoretical background

Facilitating teacher reflection through the use of AI chatbots Recent studies, performed mainly within student audiences, illustrate that dialogic interaction with conversational chatbots can promote critical thinking, self-awareness, self-efficacy, and reflection (Demir & Özdemir, 2024; Li et al., 2024; Neshaei et al., 2025; Tsai et al., 2025). Furthermore, chatbots have recently been shown to function as reflective partners, guiding students to articulate experiences, evaluate decisions, and construct meaning from their actions (Kumar et al., 2024). Effective chatbot  design for reflection often incorporates features such as positive reinforcement and socio-emotional support, rephrasing or revoicing of learners’ statements, and the use of sequenced prompts that sustain conversational momentum (Arefian et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2024; Park et al., 2021).

Despite these developments, teachers’ chatbot-assisted reflection remains a largely unexplored research domain. Existing studies have focused primarily on chatbots as instructional or regulatory tools for students, while relatively little is known about th eir use as reflective partners for teachers’ own meaning-making and identity construction. In the realm of teacher education, studies are beginning to suggest that AI-mediated dialogue can scaffold teachers’ reflective inquiry into practice, especially when they confront novel pedagogical challenges. Specifically, chatbot-assisted reflection can help novice teachers regulate emotions, link theoretical constructs to classroom realities, and make sense of complex teaching encounters. For experienced teachers,  such interactions can catalyze conceptual renewal and innovation, prompting a re-examination of previously established beliefs and practices (Arefian et al., 2024). Additionally, chatbots can also support collective reflection, where teachers share chatbot-generated insights within professional communities, using them to stimulate peer dialogue, compare perspectives, and jointly reinterpret practice (Arefian et al., 2024; Kartal, 2024). Narratives, identity development, and teacher learning Building on the discussion of reflection as a dialogic and narrative process that may be facilitated through chatbot interaction, we now turn to a complementary theoretical perspective that connects storytelling with identity development as a central mechanism of learning. Sfard and Prusak (2005) operationalize the concepts of identity and identity development  in the context of learning. Drawing on prior scholarship that defines identity as a narrative construct (Gee, 2001; Hall, 1996; Holland et al., 1998), they define identities  as “collections of stories about persons” (2005, p. 16).

For a story to count as an identity story, that is, one of a set of stories that constitute identity, it must qualify as reifying, significant, and endorsable. A reifying  story attributes stable characteristics to a person (e.g., “I am a good teacher”, “She can make any student feel confident”), rather than simply describing their actions. A significant story holds personal importance, such that any change in it would affect how the storyteller feels about the person the story is about. An endorsable story is one which the storyteller would testify as faithfully reflecting reality. According to this framework, learning is defined as reducing the gap between actual and designated identities. An actual identity  consists of stories reflecting the current state of affairs, while a designated identity consists of stories that reflect an expected state of affairs (e.g., “I want to be a better teacher”, “She should encourage every student to find their own voice”). Crucially, identity is treated not as an inner esse nce but as an activity of identifying, enacted in communication. Sfard and Prusak therefore advocate for focusing empirical intangible and indefinable entity.

Building on this framework, we conceptualize teacher learning as the development of professional identity towards the progressive alignment of actual identities with designated visions of practice. Given that our participants are teachers at early stages o f adopting non-traditional roles to incorporate citizen science into their classroom, the designated identities associated with this professional change are likely emergent rather than fully 504 articulated. Accordingly, we treat the identification and articulation of new professional goals, i.e., formulating endorsed, significant, future-oriented reifying stories, as a form of learning alongside the transformation of actual identities.

## Context

This study was conducted within the Taking Citizen Science to S chool (TCSS) research center, which supports and studies the incorporation of citizen science within schools. The center coordinates a network of RPPs, where network-wide learning and collective knowledge-building is sought through sharing of insights across partnerships (Kali et al., 2020). The “Tell Me Your Story” chatbot was developed as part of a digital infrastructure designed to support this learning and knowledge building. Intended for use by various network participants (e.g., teachers, scientists), the chatbot generates stories from users’ inputs, which are added to a shared repository that supports learning across the partnership network.

## Participants and the citizen science initiatives they took part in

Ten teachers from various backgrounds, affiliated with six different citizen science initiatives within the network volunteered to participate in this study (Table 1). All teachers provided informed consent to use the chatbot, contribute their stories to the network’s shared repository, and take part in the study. The citizen science initiatives that teachers incorporated in their teaching included: (1) Co-generating accessible walking paths: students used a mapping app to survey accessibility in their town and contribute data towards automatic calculation of accessible walking routes for people with visual impairments; (2) Deciphering history through ancient manuscripts: students transcribed digitized ancient texts to help train and explore ways to improve AI for automatic transcription; (3) Restoring the Tzipori  stream: students contributed to a long-term study of a stream under restoration by taking measurements and recording local flora and fauna; (4) Nature, cities, and emotions: students logged their emotions in various urban settings and analyzed the data to  explore environmental effects on well-being; (5) Sleep, a third of our life: students maintained sleep diaries and analyzed individual and class data for a study on adolescent sleeping patterns; (6) The mystery of the Bermuda Buttercup: students mapped and categorized flower types to support research on changes in reproduction patterns in this invasive species. Interaction with the chatbot The “Tell Me Your Story” chatbot serves as a reflective partner (Arefian et al., 2024), guiding teachers through a narrative process via a prompt-based interface powered by ChatGPT. Each session begins with a brief onboarding dialogue to collect contextual details (e.g., name, role, citizen science initiative). Teachers then choose one of three story types: (1) a significant event; (2) the course of participation and outcomes, or (3) curricular adjustments made during participation. The chatbot follows with a conversation guided by pre-programmed questions tailored to the selected story type, asking for a description of the events, teachers’ impressions, and preliminary reflections. It is designed to provide empathic responses, seek additional details or clarifications as needed, and generate intermediate summaries to reflect and scaffold the storytelling. At the end, the chatbot produces a narrative with a title, an introduction of the narrator, and a story body (~300– 400 words). Teachers are invited to review the story and request revisions, leading tonew drafts as needed, until they approve the final story. Each teacher participated in a 45-minute video session with a researcher, consisting of three parts: an introduction (~10 minutes), a chatbot conversation resulting in a story (~15 minutes), and a follow-up semi-structured, metareflective interview (~20 m inutes). The chatbot interaction was conducted via a research-dedicated ChatGPT account on the researcher’s computer. Teachers viewed the chatbot interface through screen sharing and responded either by typing in the video chat (copied by the researcher into the interface), or by speaking aloud with speech-to-text transcription. The interview included four questions: (1) What do you think about the story?

(2) How did the chatbot support your storytelling? (3) Did the experience yield new insights about the initiative’s

impact on you or your students? (4) Did it lead tonew insights about yourself and your role as a teacher? Data sources include transcripts of the chatbot conversations and the reflective interviews. Ethical considerations and guardrails To address potential risks in AI-assisted identity work, such as bias reinforcement  or "echo chamber" validation (Glickman & Sharot, 2025), several safeguards were implemented. First, p rivacy was ensured by preventing storage of data in the chatbot’s long-term memory or its use in model training. Second, the chatbot was pre-instructed to provide structured and supportive feedback that prioritized professional growth and pedagogical potential. Lastly, a human-in-the-loop approach was maintained, with the researcher closely monitoring and mediating all teacher-chatbot interactions. These measures ensured that the short-term mediated engagement functioned as a focused reflective session, rather than an unmonitored or open-ended intervention.

## Data analysis

To address RQ1, we drew on Sfard and Prusak’s (2005) conceptualization of an identity story as one that is reifying, significant, and endorsable. We analyzed transcripts of the chatbot conversations, coding each teacher utterance as a unit of analysis, according to working definitions of these traits (Table 2). For endorsability, we also examined relevant excerpts from the follow-up interviews. Our analysis focused on what the teachers shared in their interaction with the chatbot and not on the chatbot-generated stories. Identifying these traits served both to determine whether teachers’ accounts qualified as identity stories, and to evaluate the authenticity of the coconstructed narratives, mitigating concerns about potential distortions or “hallucinations” typical of generative AI outputs (Huang et al., 2025).

New and reinforced insights were identified through teachers’ explicit statements or through evidence for active meaning-making. For both RQ1 and the identification of new or reinforced insights in RQ2, one researcher coded each transcript, and a second independently reviewed the codes, with high inter-coder agreements (80–100%). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion between the two coders or, when needed, with a third researcher.

For RQ2, we further analyzed only new and reinforced insights (20 in total across all teachers), to assess whether they reflected professional learning. Drawing on Sfard and Prusak’s (2005) view of learning as identity development and an inductive reading of the data, we developed a coding scheme to characterize forms of professional learning (Table 3). One researcher applied the codes, and two others independently reviewed them. Full agreement was reached in 14 cases, partial in 3, and for the remaining 3,  the first researcher made the final decision after considering reviewer comments.

## Statements indicating significance

All teachers referred multiple times to the significance of the events they were narrating to the chatbot  (2–13 utterances per teacher). These references highlighted moments perceived as especially meaningful or impactful, or emphasized the emotional and developmental weight of specific experiences: “There was one reaction  that I particularly remember. The principal asked the professor whether he considered the students’ data analysis as genuine scientific findings, and he said, of course! I’m sure this was a very significant moment for the students.” (Maya), or “The students really enjoyed themselves. I remember one student who sent a thank-you letter, saying 507 that the experience of presenting to an audience that included his father was one of the most significant things he had done recently.” (Guy).

Endorsability statements All teachers endorsed the generated stories (3–15 utterances per teacher). In the interviews, teachers acknowledged the chatbot’s role in shaping their stories, and felt the narratives authentically reflected their experiences: “I liked the story. Usually, we write briefly and concisely, but here, I wrote the whole story with all the important details.” (Rada, referring to the chatbot-generated story as one she wrote), or “I’ve written about these experiences before, but here, it emphasized the importance of what we did. It even expressed it in truer words. It made the story more coherent and more understandable.” (Rachel).

Overall, teacher–chatbot interactions produced stories of practice that qualify as identity stories: they reified professional roles, emphasized significant experiences, and were endorsed as authentic. Beyond demonstrating the chatbot’s capacity to elicit such stories, these findings suggest that the interaction can catalyze reflection: by guiding teachers to articulate and affirm stories about their practice, the chatbot provides a foundation for professional learning through narrative work. Teachers’ insights into professional learning (RQ2) All but one teacher (Abigail) expressed at least one new or reinforced insight during the chatbot interaction or subsequent interview, resulting in 20 such insights. Of these, 15 insights identified across eight teachers (excluding Sheila) were classified as instances of professional identity development within Sfard and Prusak’s

(2005) conceptual framework: seven reflected strengthened awareness of a designated identity, four indicated

strengthened awareness of an actual identity, and four involved i dentification of a practice bridging actual and designated identities (Table 3). For brevity, we focus on three illustrative cases that demonstrate how teachers developed their insights as forms of professional learning. Case 1: Rachel and her insight regarding connecting students to academia Rachel is an experienced history teacher whose story centered on a significant event in the citizen science initiative, where her students met with the lead scientist of the initiative through a video call. Her story highlighted the students’ excitement and motivation in deciphering texts, as well as her conviction to expose them more directly to the university environment in the future. Before interacting with the chatbot, Rachel shared with the researcher her interest in connecting students with the acade mia: “I always thought schools should collaborate with the university. For many years I've been participating in a program by the national library, taking students who really like to read. I take the really good students and march them forward.”. Later, in her conversation with the chatbot, Rachel expressed her wish to bring students to the university, affirming her professional identity as a humanities teacher committed to fostering appreciation of her discipline and to connecting students with the academic world: I want to take the students to the university [pause]. To talk with them there, not in school. There [emphasized]. To get them curious, take them to the library, to the labs. To show them the academic world of the humanities. The humanities [emphasized]. T o explain why they are important.

The chatbot plays a role in reinforcing this orientation, responding: Your vision of bringing students into an academic environment and showing them the importance of the humanities is inspiring. Taking them to the university, introducing them to experts, and letting them see labs could really broaden their horizons. When prompted by the chatbot about her gained insights, Rachel broadened this vision: “I think the students should take part in more citizen science initiatives. For each child, whatever interests them.”. During the reflective interview, she reiterated this idea. Then she stopped and shifted her stance as she spoke with the interviewer: 508 Look, when the university used to offer that I send the really good students to all these special programs, I did so. I did it instinctively [emphasized], because I know that it provides these children with a good learning environment. But these opportunities should be open for everybody. Not just the special ones, everybody [emphasized]. The special ones will find their way, but… Let’s give everyone the same opportunities.

Before joining the citizen science initiative, Rachel’s professional identity has already centered on promoting humanities studies among her students and connecting the strongest ones to academia.  Participating in the initiative reinforced her conviction that such exposure is valuable, and through the chatbot interaction she expressed a goal of bringing students to the university and engaging them in future citizen science projects. However, in the reflective interview she articulated a new and significant  insight: that all students, not only the high achievers, should benefit from such opportunities. We interpret this as a strengthened awareness of a designated identity. Rachel moved from reaffirming her actual professional identity, to a new goal of supporting equal access to academic enrichment. In Sfard and Prusak’s (2005) terms, she expanded her designated identity by redefining her professional goals.

## Case 2: Naomi and her insight about the significance of outdoor learning

Naomi is a leading science teacher in her school. In her story she described guiding her biology students in a citizen science initiative along a local stream, combining ecological field research with community engagement. When the chatbot asked Naomi about the contributions of the initiative to her teaching, she replied: “I always like to go outside, if I have a chance. Learning ecology outside is completely different than learning in the classroom. The experience is completely different.” This statement illustrates a reification of her actual identity as a teacher who consistently engages students in outdoor learning. Later, during the reflective interview, she was asked whether the chatbot interaction and story generation have led tonew professional insights, Naomi initially reaffirmed this long-standing aspect of her practice: “I’ve been doing this for 30 years. For 30 years I’ve been going out with students, on excursions and all that”. Yet her reflections did not stop there. She continues: “I’m thinking, what did it do to the students? They were really [pause], I don’t know.”. Then, she recalls a specific moment which was not part of the story: “The third outdoor encounter was difficult because we were so cold. But still, they [the students] kept thanking us [emphasized], and saying that  [pause] they had so much fun, thanking us for their participation”.

This reflection was classified as a reinforced insight. While Naomi had already noted students’ enjoyment during the chatbot dialogue, in the interview she revisited and reconsidered these reactions, ultimately reinforcing her belief in the positive impact of outdoor learning. We interpret this as a strengthened awareness of an actual identity. Through narrating her experiences to the chatbot and reading its generated story, Naomi deepened her recognition of herself as a teacher who creates meaningful, outdoor learning experiences that benefit her students.

Case 3: Rada and her insight on the importance of reflection Rada is the director of a gifted education center, where high-achieving students engage in enrichment activities outside of their regular school studies. Her students participated in the Co-generating accessible walking paths initiative. In her story, Rada  described how students prepared for the mapping activities by meeting with representatives of an association for people with blindness. In her reflective interview, Rada emphasized how her interaction with the chatbot supported her thinking: “During the dialogue with the chatbot, things became clear to me. It's like a reflection, where you can see your thoughts.”. Later, when asked about new teaching practices she might adopt following participation in the citizen science initiative, she explicitly connected her answer to the chatbot interaction: What was good about the chatbot wasn't just the technology. It was its guidance through a series of questions. I would like to take this process for ourselves [teachers in her team], to perform reflections after teaching experiences. For instance, when we hold some significant event, we do so many things.... We could use this reflective process to break it down and notice what was significant in the activity. Yes, this reflective thinking is very important, very useful. This statement was coded as a new insight, interpreted as the identification of a practice bridging actual and designated identities. Although Rada already saw herself as an educator committed to designing meaningful 509 enrichment activities, the chatbot interaction helped her recognize reflection as a concrete tool for advancing professional goals. By articulating the value of structured post-teaching reflection, she identified a strategy bridging her current identity as  a program leader and a designated identity as one who fosters deeper learning through continuous improvement.

The findings of this study demonstrate that a short, guided interaction with a chatbot can elicit narratives that teachers recognize as authentically their own. These narratives not only met the criteria of identity stories as defined by Sfard and Prusak (2005), they also fostered reflection that depicted meaningful forms of professional learning. Specifically, using the chatbot, teachers strengthened their awareness of aspects of their actual identities, clarified visions of designated identities, and identified practices that can bridge the two. In this sense, the chatbot acted as a reflective partner (Arefian et al., 2024) whose immediate story output created a concrete artifact teachers could refine, endorse, and interrogate.

Our design deliberately foregrounded brief, single-session reflections. Therefore, we caution against over-interpreting immediate gains as long-term professional transformation. Nonetheless, the results reinforce the potential of storytelling experiences to stimulate reflection and professional learning (Luehmann, 2008; Luehmann & Frink, 2009; Shalev & Gidalevich, 2024; Shank, 2006), and demonstrate that chatbots can help teachers overcome challenges in articulating their experiences and learning from them  (Arefian et al., 2024; Kartal, 2024). The scaffolding offered by the chatbot is significant, underscoring the potential scalability of the approach: within a 15-minute session, teachers produced a meaningful story of practice that supports professional learning. Future research is required to carefully map design features with meaningful reflection. That said, preliminary evidence from this research shows that teachers described the chatbot-assisted storytelling as “making things clear” and “focusing” attention, echoing existing evidence that chatbots foster reflection through structured narrative formation and responsive feedback (Kumar et al., 2024; Park et al., 2021). In this study, the chatbot design includes the immediate generation of a coherent written story. We suggest that this tangible mirror of the narrative enhances the reflective process by making teachers’ thinking visible and revisitable. Overall, our findings indicate that the potential of chatbots as storytelling partners to foster teacher learning lies in the connection drawn between (a) the power of storytelling as a reflective practice (Luehmann, 2008; Luehmann & Frink, 2009) and (b) the narrative construction of identity (Sfard & Prusak, 2005). In the current study, this connection was portrayed in evidence indicating that the “Tell Me Your Story” chatbot successfully scaffolded teachers' identity work by guiding them to recount, elaborate, and reframe their experiences, transforming them into structured stories that can be examined for reification, significance, and endorsability. Through this process of externalization, teachers can reflectively evaluate their actual and designated identities and prepare to narrow that gap between them. As Sfard and Prusak (2005) note, “ identity talk makes us able to cope with new situations in terms of our past experience and gives us tools to plan for the future” (p. 16). Within the context of chatbot-assisted stories of practice, this analytic framework provides a means of understanding how reflective dialogue with an AI partner may support teacher learning as identity development. We envision the use of AI-based agents, similar to the “Tell Me Your Story” chatbot, to assist teachers in generating stories of practice and sharing them among RPP and networks of RPP participants. We suggest that the same qualities that supported individual reflection in the current study: reification of professional identities, significance of the narrated events, and endorsement of the stories as reflecting authentic experiences, may also render these stories valuable to peers. Furthermore, when paired with structured opportunities for collaborative reflection and inquiry, the stories can serve as testimonies of teachers’ experiences that inspire others (teachers, scientists, community partners) to reflect on and enrich their own knowledge. In this way, teachers’ stories of practice can become epistemic resources for network-level knowledge building and cross-partnership improvement — processes that are considered crucial in fostering and sustaining educational change (Datnow, 2020; Hod et al., 2018).

Finally, this study highlights the potential of AI conversational chatbots to act as a reflective partner, supporting teachers in co-constructing authentic stories of practice that can foster individual learning. By transforming teachers’ experiences into shared, analyzable narratives, such tools can enhance the visibility and impact of teachers’ experiential knowledge within RPPs and networks of RPPs, meeting the conference theme of purposeful collaboration.
