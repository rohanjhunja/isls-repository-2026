# Sowing Seeds for Applying Productive Failure to Reading: Pre-Reading Questions Alone are Not Enough to Reap the Benefits

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
There is no known work mapping Productive Failure (PF)—a popular pedagogical design within the Learning Sciences—to reading. Therefore, through a secondary data analysis of students reading several expository biology excerpts, the current work attempts to bridge PF and reading. One group of students answered questions before reading the passages, while the other group did not. The results revealed that pre-reading questions had no effect on the acquisition of causal language, but the different passages did affect causal language pickup.

Productive failure and prereading questions Evidence suggests that embracing failure in educational settings promotes learning (Williams-Pierce et al., 2021), with Productive Failure (PF) being one effective learning design (Sinha & Kapur, 2021). PF studies demonstrate enhanced learning, particularly in STEM (Sinha & Ka pur, 2021), but there's limited exploration in non-STEM areas despite calls for such research (Loibl et al., 2017). Our focus  here is on applying PF to reading  through examining the impact of prereading questions on und erstanding causal language pickup (Cromley et al., 2020). While there's no direct link between PF and reading, we argue prereading questions are one bridge  (Lewis & Mensink, 2012). Bringing together PF and reading offers a generative opportunity for the Learning Sciences, especially because there are apparent shared mechanisms. Productive Failure involves two phases: (1) a probl em-solving phase where students generate solutions to a complex problem they don't know how to solve, leading to initial failure, and (2) a direct instru ction phase introducing the canonical solution (Sinha & Kapur, 2021). It incorporates des ign considerations across the activity, participation structures, and social surr ound. Students in PF activities often outperform those re ceiving instruction first, and the mechanisms said to contr ibute to PF's effectiveness include activating prior knowledge, highlighting critical conceptual features, explaining and elaborating on concepts, and organizing crit ical features into concepts (Sinha & Kapur, 2021).

Pre-reading questions influence reading processes, including the identification of causal language crucial for forming a comprehensive situation model (Cromley et al., 2020). The situation model is a mental construct of the text that extends beyond the comprehension of individual words and sentences. Considering some mechanisms in the context of Productive Failure (PF), this research  investigates how prereading questions influence the identification of causal language.  Firstly, answering questions before reading a text can activate pertinent prior knowledge about a topic (Alemi & Ebadi, 2010). We p redict that this activation will prompt readers to notice relevant causal language necessary to answer the questions while they are reading the text. Secondly, we anticipate that readers will be drawn to pre-reading related text elements, thereby increasing the likelihood of identifying the causal language within the text (Lewis & Mensink, 2012). With these two mechanistic predictions in mind, we pose the following research question: Do prereading questions lead to greater identification of causal language during the reading process?

## Method

This is a secondary data analysis from a project fo cused on creating a biological reasoning measure. A  subset of participants was selected from the original study (see Cromley et al., 2020). These participants were randomly assigned to prereading questions (n = 23) or no prereading questions (control, n = 22) conditions (total n = 45). Thirty-six participants identified as female and nine iden tified as male. They ranged in age from 19 years ol d to 21 years old (M = 19.9, SD = 0.75). Participants were compensated $35 for their time. Participants were told to study and read a series o f seven short, illustrated passages from an unfamil iar textbook (Sadava et al., 2012) about different aspe cts of the immune system. Our analyses focus ed on the first three passages because all participants completed these passages  within the  allotted time. Participants were instructed to think aloud while reading the text and were given 40 minutes to complete the task. Coding and analysis approach Causal language in passages was identified through action verbs and explicit causal terms. Each participant's think-aloud was coded for causal language, with agreement  reached among three coders. Words were scored as "picked up" if used in paraphrases or summaries during reading. An example of pickup includes incorporating an action verb into a paraphrase, while a lack of pick up occurs when the paraphrase does not capture the causal relationship or key action verb (See Cromley et al., 2020 for more detailed examples). We conducted a 2-way mixed ANOVA with a within-subject factor of passage (i.e., 1, 2, and 3) and a  between-subject factor of group (pre-reading question vs no pre-reading question). Proceeding with this approach was app ropriate because assumptions of normality, homogeneity of variance, homogeneity of covariance, and sphericity were not violated.

## Results

The mean pickup of casual language for the group that answered prereading questions was 9.42, and the mean for the control group (no prereading questions) was 11.0. Contrary to our hypothesis this differenc e is (1) in the opposite direction (i.e., students who did not have pre-reading questions picked up more causal language),  and

(2) was non-significant (F (1, 43) = 1.09,  p =.30). There was a main effect for passage  (F (2, 86) = 17.84,  p

<.001). This only suggests there were more causal connections across each passage, and these differences did not differ between groups (i.e., there was a non-significant interaction, F (2, 86) = 1.13, p =.33). Because this main effect is not relevant to our research question  and due to space  limitations, this finding  is not further unpacked here.

## Discussion, limitations, and a few paths forward

We aimed to progress toward applying the concepts of PF to reading. More specifically, we explored pre-reading questions relationship to two PF mechanisms: (1) completing pre-reading questions  would activate prior knowledge about science concepts related to the to-be-read text and (2) direct attention to critical features of the text while reading, thereby facilitating the pickup of causal language. Our hypotheses were not confirmed. There are several limitations of this work. One large limitation concerns the design. O ur study is a secondary data analysis and did not allow us to design  a learning environment appeal ing to all four PF mechanisms. Notably, the participants were not provided a clear follow-up instruction phase focusing specifically on their failures in the pre-questions. Instead, we relied on the text  for that instruction, which may have been a bridge too far.  We argue our research can  still serve as a launching pad for further work. For instance, there is room for future work to explore what it means to de sign PF for reading across the design layers of PF (i.e., the activity, the participation structure, and the soci al surround), such as considering the difficulty of  the text, comparing reading alone or in groups, and working toward fostering a social surround that embraces mistakes and failures in reading. Overall, we hope that this work plants some seeds to help map PF to reading and prepares the ground for much fruitful work soon.
