# Overview of Research: BlackCrit and Social-Design Experiment

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This research agenda examines how Black youth and teachers resist anti-Blackness through critical literacy, STEM learning, and counternarrative practices in urban schools. Grounded in Critical Race Theory and BlackCrit, the work positions race and anti-Blackness as central forces shaping educational experiences while centering Black joy, agency, and knowledge production. Using social design experiment, this research implements justice-oriented dual-credit courses and documents the pedagogical practices of Black teachers. Across iterative cycles of design and analysis, the studies aim to generate new theories of learning rooted in Black cultural wealth while materially improving educational opportunities, well-being, and academic pathways for Black youth.

## Overview of Research: BlackCrit and Social-Design Experiment

Using social design experiment, this research implements justiceoriented dual-credit courses and documents the pedagogical practices of Black teachers. Across iterative cycles of design and analysis, the studies aim to generate new theories of learning rooted in Black cultural wealth while materially improving educational opportunities, wellbeing, and academic pathways for Black youth. As a literacy and urban education researcher, my work focuses broadly on Blackness/anti-Blackness in education. Specifically, I seek to better understand the literate lives of Black youth in urban K-12 schools and the pedagogical practices of Black teachers. Amid sweeping federal and state reforms banning books and critical theories about race and racism (Alexander et al., 2023), Black teachers and Black youth stand on a precipice which we have seen many times before. The concentrated effort by school districts and politicians to recommit to colorblindness and the myth of meritocracy are only veiled attempts at maintaining the status quo: white supremacy (Crenshaw et al, 2019). We know these reforms, along with state sanctioned violence, cause significant harm to Black teachers and youth, so it begs the question(s):

• RQ1: What are the psychological and mental impacts of anti-Blackness on urban students’ perception of

K-12 education?

• RQ2: How can Black students’ and teachers’ resistance to anti-Blackness inform theories of learning?

• RQ3: How do Black urban youth leverage critical literacy practices and STEM learning to create

counternarratives?

## Theoretical framework

Critical race theory and blackCrit This work is situated in Critical Race Theory (CRT) (Bell, 1980; Bell, 1992; Delgado, 1989; Delgado, 2001; Dixson & Lynn, 2013; Matsuda, 1993). CRT offers a framework for race and racism as central and unshakeable foundations of our society. The theory has several important tenets that are relevant to my work—like interest convergence and racial realism—but the most focal tenet in my research is the understanding of counternarratives (Delgado, 1989; Harris et al., 2023, Kinloch et al., 2020; Solórzano & Yosso, 2002). Counternarrative, or counter storying, “represents the knowledges, perspectives, and experiences of Black people and others marginalized by racial oppression and white dominance” (Kinloch et al., 2020, p. 387). My work seeks to amplify the voices of Black students and teachers in a way that leverages counternarratives, specifically, in an educational context (Ladson-Billings & Tate, 2017). This can work in conjunction with elevating student voice in a dialogic setting (Segal et al., 2017). While CRT places an emphasis on the impact of race and racism more broadly, BlackCrit offers a parallel and connected framework aimed at better understanding anti-Black suffering, injustice, and educational practices (Dumas, 2016; Dumas & ross, 2016; Givens, 2021a; Sobti and Welsh, 2023). These two interconnected frameworks directly inform the work I do with literacy and STEM in urban schools. My prospective research studies will utilize the tradition of social design experiment (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016). The main goal, informed by more traditional approaches to design research (Anderson & Shattuck, 2012; Barab et al., 2004;Collins et al., 2004; Tinoca et al., 2022), will be to implement educational interventions that materially improve the lives of Black youth in an urban school. Rather than focusing on transforming the social and material lives of the broader group coined “nondominant communities” (Gutiérrez, 2008), the BlackCrit theoretical framing works with this design experiment to specifically address the deleterious impacts of anti-Blackness in an educational setting.

Gutiérrez & Jurow (2016) keenly note that, “the relation between everyday knowledge and scientific knowledge is of particular theoretical interest to us as well as to the learning sciences” (p. 572). Through studies like this, I plan to elucidate the everyday knowledges of Black youth (counternarratives) by implementing a dualcredit college course at predominantly Black urban high schools in the Rust Belt Northeast. Additionally, as a teacher of this prospective course, I will build on my dissertation research to act as what Barab et al. (2004) refer to as a “change agent” (p. 255). The course will be a 100-level humanities course that focuses on the blending of science, technology, engineering, and math (STEM) and literacy. My work intersects with STEM through a shared goal of challenging the deficit narratives that marginalize minoritized youth in these disciplines often face from educators (van Es et al., 2025). Moreover, the course will aim to provide justice-oriented and inquiry-based practices (Damşa & Nerland, 2016; Luehmann et al., 2023) around STEM. This course will be based on similar work I have conducted with urban youth where a STEM and literacy design experiment were implemented (O’Neil-White & Hunter, in press). Following the delivery of courses like this, I will utilize design principles to assess the feasibility of Black counternarratives as a conceptual tool for learning. I have already attained approval through multiple school districts to conduct this work—with data collection already under way. In addition to the social design experiment work, my work also extends into critical ethnography (Madison, 2012). Specifically, I have begun conducting research in a classroom in a first-ring suburban school to better understand the pedagogical practices of a Black male English teacher—a group that has been the victim of anti-Blackness and exclusion in the education field (Bell, 2017; Brockenbrough, 2015; King, 1993; Young & Young 2020). Through these ethnographic methods, I will disseminate findings that will provide an innovative Black male literacy teaching framework.

Lastly, my research has deep roots in my classroom teaching experience. As a longtime English teacher, the gap between the theoretical world of educational research and the challenges teachers are facing on the ground was often a difficult hurtle. I bring my experiences as a classroom teacher to much of the research I conduct, including a recent practitioner article in the English Journal that highlights my own efforts to decolonize my classroom to meet the needs of diverse students. While I’m no longer a classroom teacher, I’m keenly aware that teachers need research that can “meet them where they are at” in ways that are helpful, transformative, and refreshing.
