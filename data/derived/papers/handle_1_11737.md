# From Deficit Thinking to Reflective Practice: Examining Teacher Sensemaking about Student Participation and its Shift

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines a novice middle school science teacher’s evolving sensemaking about students not participating in science discussions. Drawing on 15 lesson debriefs, this study shows that the teacher initially framed student silence through deficit and hierarchical discourses—attributing silence to students’ cognitive limitations (“cannot think”) or lack of effort (“unwilling to think”). Over time, her sensemaking shifted toward a more reflective stance, focusing on how instructional practices, especially questioning strategies, could foster participation.

## Introduction

Efforts to advance equity in science education are often impeded by problematic assumptions about learning and learners, which can lead tonegative judgments abou t students based on specific social markers such as  race and gender (NASEM, 2024). These socially and culturally  constructed assumptions influence how teachers mak e sense of students’ participation in learning activities (Watkins et al., 2021). For instance, deficit assumptions may inform hierarchical categorizations of students’ ca pabilities (e.g., “high” and “low”), resulting in d ifferentiated instruction that perpetuates inequitable access to educational resources (Louie, 2019). It is essen tial to support shifts in teachers’ sensemaking  from problematic assumptions to productive and equi table perspectives  as a crucial aspect of teacher learning that key to promoting equity in science education. Contrary to linear models of teacher development, t his study adopts a dynamic view of teacher sensemaking as evolving and context-sensitive (Hunter et al., 2019; Philip et al., 2024). Prior research shows that teachers draw on multiple, and sometimes conflicting, ideological resources that shift over time and across settings (Daniel et al., 2023; Philip, 2011). While ideology is often viewed as a barrier, Louie (2019) frames it as a neutral resource that teachers use to interpret their profe ssional practice. This study adopts this framing and examines how ideologies and social discourses function as to ols in teachers’ evolving sensemaking about student participation.

My objective is to describe a teacher’s sensemaking about student participation and to identify how the teacher’s sensemaking shifts across time and context, which may provide i mplications for teacher learning. This study focuses on student participation in science discussions, which I define as various discursive practices (e.g., problematization, reasoning, and argumentation) t hat students participate in to construct scientific  meaning. Specifically, I ask: How does a novice science teac her make sense of students not participating in sci ence discussions (i.e., students who were silent through out these activities)? To address this question, I analyze the teacher’s discourse in debriefs after lessons.

## Methods

This single case study is part of a larger design-based project conducted at a top-tier private middle school in Shanghai during the 2020 –21 academic year; the larger project aimed to reform science classrooms through the promotion of argumentation. The study focuses on Ms. W, a novice science teacher with one year of expe rience, who collaborated with the research team to implemen t argumentation instruction in her two sixth-grade classes (46 and 47 students respectively).

In 2020, a nationwide policy mandating simultaneous  admissions for public and private schools shifted the student population from selective enrollment to lottery-based placement. The sixth graders in this study were the first cohort admitted by lottery, contrasting with the upper grades who had been admitted through competitive selection.

The data source includes 15 debriefing  sessions (totaling 475 minutes) with Ms. W. These meetings focused on reflecting on lessons and planning for a rgumentation instruction. During  two of the debriefs, I transcribed classroom episodes from lesson video recordings to facilitate our reflections on discursive practic es. For data analysis, I identified all instances where  Ms. W explicitly or implicitly expressed her descr iptions and explanations of student participation in science di scussions. For example, during a de brief about a convection lesson, Ms. W introduced the planned learning sequence, recalled asking, “Can convection happen in solids?” and followed with  an explanation of  what she believed  contributed to students’ silence. Then, I interpreted the meanings Ms. W constructed and the taken-for-granted assumptions in her discourse. I focused on discursive markers of Ms. W referring to students (e.g., “good kids” and “students at a high level”) and the claims that Ms. W used to explain why students did not talk.

## Findings

Ms. W’s initial sensemaking drew heavily on the deficit and hierarchical narratives. She frequently  claimed that students “cannot think,” equating silence with a lack of intelligence. This reflects a dominant narrative in science education: if you do not get it, it is because you are not smart. Her discourse often invoked comparative language such as “students at a high level” or “good kids,” positioning learners along a perceived continuum of  smartness and good behavior.

These views were reinforced by the recent admission  policy shift, which  Ms. W believed had reduced the academic caliber of the student population. Ms. W compared her sixth-grade students—admitted via lottery— with older cohorts who were selectively admitted, e xpressing concern that the former lacked flexibilit y in reasoning. This contributed to a hierarchical understanding of smartness across and within grade levels. In addition to perceived ability, Ms. W attributed students’ silence to a lack of academic effort. Ear ly in the semester, she suggested some students were “unw illing to think,” even when tasks were relatively s imple. This aligns with another dominant discourse: if you  try hard enough, you cando science. Her interpret ation focused on student shortcomings rather than instructional gaps. However, this framing began to shift over time. Ms.  W started reflecting on how instructional choices, particularly questioning strategies, shaped participation. In a debrief, she reconsidered a moment when a question failed to elicit responses —not as a sign of student failure, but as a prompt t o revisit the question’s relevance or clarity. This marked a shift toward reflective, student-centered teaching. She proposed changes to her questioning approach to support more meaningful discussions. She emphasized that effective questions should be open-ended and connect with students’ knowledge, lived experie nces, and interests.  This shift marked a move away from deficit thinking and toward a pedagogical orientation that foregrounded the teacher’s role.

## Discussion

Based on the analysis, Ms. W perceived students’ th inking abilities as existing on different levels, r eflecting the pervasive ideology of hierarchy, particularly in th e context of the shift the admission policy. Althou gh the teacher’s discourse in earlier debriefs was characterized by blaming students for “not being able” or “not being willing” to think, the teacher’s sensemaking transf ormed from focusing on students toward reflecting o n the role of teaching practices in facilitating student participation. The transformation highlights the importance of closely addressing how the teacher’s sensemaking changes, which has implications for teacher learning. Deficit narratives about ability and effort could perpetuate inequitie s by positioning some students as less capable than others. Ms. W’s transformation highlights the need to design op portunities for self-reflection and professional dialogue to shift teachers’ ideological orientations and instructional strategies, a topic further explored in the full paper.
