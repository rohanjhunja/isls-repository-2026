# Breaking Out of Mathematical Silos: Exploring Expansive Framing and Value Beliefs in Undergraduate STEM Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This ongoing study investigates how Expansive Framing (EF) might foster transfer of mathematics learning in undergraduate STEM education through a conceptual framework that integrates EF with Expectancy Value (EV) theory. Using a convergent mixed-methods design, we analyzed instructor interviews, and plan to analyze classroom observations and student surveys, to examine how EF principles operate in STEM contexts. Preliminary findings suggest that EF may provide a pathway for breaking traditional disciplinary boundaries while enhancing undergraduate students' value beliefs in STEM courses.

## Introduction

Mathematics is foundational to scientific inquiry, yet undergraduate instruction is often siloed, confining students to learning topics within an isolated context (Keeling et al., 2007). As a result, students’ ability to apply mathematical knowledge to science contexts is limit ed. For example, s tudents in lower-division undergraduate physics and chemistry courses struggle with topics that have a strong mathematical basis (Potgieter et al., 2008; Turşucu et al., 2017). In order to  address this challenge, we present a conceptual framework (Figure 1) that integrates Expansive Framing (EF) theory (Engle, 20 06; Engle et al., 2012) with Expectancy Value (EV) theory (Eccles et al., 1983). In this poster, we share ongoing research of this framework in the context of undergraduate STEM courses at a university in the western United States, from both the instructors’ and students’ perspectives. Framing perspective This research brings together theories of Expansive Framing (EF) and Expectancy Value (EV). EF is a theory and an instructional technique for promoting transfer. Broadly, transfer is defined as a person’s ability to apply knowledge learned in one setting to another. While research on transfer is extensive (Barnett & Ceci, 2002), it remains a no toriously thorny construct (National Research Counc il, 2000). EF acknowledges the controversy surrounding transfer while reconceptualizing the notion by viewing it through a situative lens, suggesting that content knowledge and context of use are linked (Engle et al., 2012). Intercontextuality is when connections are made between contexts, and it can be facilitated through EF (Engle, 2006). Forming intercontextuality has been shown to foster transfer of learning; in contrast, bounded framing fails to create intercontextuality and discourages transfer (Engle et al., 2011).

Further, when  students do not  see value in what they are learning, they  experience lower levels of motivation and interest (Priniski et al., 2018) and  lack confidence (Hulleman et al., 2017).  Further, the value a student perceives for learning a topic (i.e., their  value beliefs) tends to decrease over time (Gaspar d et al, 2021) which is particularly problematic for adult undergr aduates. EV theory asserts that the amount of value a student holds for learning a topic will directly influence motivation and academic success. Relatedly,  EF theory asserts that instructional framing of context affects trans fer, and because broad framing sets the expectation  that a topic will continue to hold relevance (Engle et al., 2012), the way content is framed also affects value beliefs. More research is needed that ties together transfer, framing, and value beliefs. Figure 1 Intercontextuality’s Theorized Effect on Value Beliefs and Transfer As shown in Figure 1, mathematics instruction is traditionally siloed  (represented within the "box"). However, EF – through broad framing across contexts  and promotion of student authorship (Lam et al., 2014)  – can potentially break down the silos and barriers. This "breaking out" process leads to intercontextuality, which we hypothesize leads to improved value beliefs for mathematics and trans fer of learning to other contexts (such as science courses). While the present study does n ot purport to measure transfer, it is inherent in E F theory that intercontextuality will foster transfer and improve value beliefs for mathematics (Beck & Shumway, 2025). Thus, our framework suggests that EF and EV may offer a pathway to address these challenges. In this poster, we explore this framework through t hree interconnected aims:  u nderstanding the perspectives of instructors of undergraduate STEM courses on students’ mathematical transfer; investigating how the instructors’ classroom practices in the STEM courses align with EF principles; and examining the value beliefs of students enrolled in the STEM classes in relation to these practices.

## Methods

This study focuses on undergraduate science courses at a university in the western United States. The focal courses were identified by analyzing the university’s cours e catalog, available syllabi, and through discussio ns with relevant department heads. Science courses with str ong mathematical demands and corresponding pre-requisite mathematics courses were isolated as the unit of st udy. Participants include experienced instructors of these undergraduate courses and the undergraduate students in the courses. To investigate our conceptual framework, we employed a convergent mixed-methods design (Creswell,

2022) that examines each component through multiple data sources and analyses. For example, to understand the

"breaking out" process, we conducted semi-structured interviews with STEM instructors to asse ss their current views on transfer (e.g., how successfully students are transferring mathematics, their opinions on how  best to support transfer, etc.). Interview transcripts were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke,

2022) to identify patterns in instructor perspectives on transfer and their alignment with EF principles.

Additionally, classroom observations will be  conducted using the Classroom Observation Protocol for Undergraduate STEM (COPUS; Smith et al., 2013) to d ocument instructional practices. COPUS data will be analyzed to quantify the frequency of EF-aligned behaviors and compare across classes. For examining intercontextuality development, we will conduct reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2022) of class transcripts to identify inst ances of EF implementation. This analysis focuses o n how instructors create connections between mathematical concepts and their applications in other STEM contexts. We will develop a coding scheme based on EF principles to categorize and quantify intercontextual connections made during instruction.

To assess value beliefs, we will administer the Value Beliefs survey (Gaspard et al., 2015) to students at the beginning (pre) and end (post) of the course. This survey assesses perceptions of various types of value beliefs using 37 Likert-scale items. We will conduct paired t-tests to analyze changes in individual students' value beliefs from pre-to-post. Additionally, because students are nested in classes, we will conduct multilevel modeling to examine how course-level factors (e.g., instructor EF alignment) relate to changes in student value beliefs. The integration of data sources employs a matrix ap proach to combine qualitative and quantitative findings, aligning instructor interview themes, cla ssroom observation data, and student value belief o utcomes. This integration allows us to explore how instructor perspectives and practice s relate to changes in student value beliefs, providing a comprehensive view of the conceptual framework in action.

## Discussion and conclusion

Transfer remains a thorny topic, yet additional res earch is necessary to better understand how EF may foster transfer. The present study  advances our understanding of how Expansive Framing  (EF) might foster mathematical transfer by empirically exploring our conceptual framework through multiple complementary  data sources. For example, instructor interviews provide a more comprehensive view of teacher beliefs surrou nding transfer. Teaching observations will illuminate how content and context are framed within the classroom. Student survey responses will inform the level of value that students in mathematics and science courses hold for learning mathematics. Researchers (e.g., Engle et al. (2012); Lam et al., 2014)) have identified a need for additional studies on how framing affects transfer; this mixed methods analysis is a step toward advancing this work. The present study offers a novel approach for understanding these relationships while maintaining flexibility for application across various STEM disciplines. Furthermore, this poster provides a launching point for productive discussions among learning scientists surrounding EF as a mode to foster transfer and its connection to value beliefs. Our findings build upon previous research presented  at ICLS (e.g., Lam et al., 2014) and extend the conversation about EF's role in fostering transfer and its connection to student value beliefs.
