# One Size Fits All? Learner-Adaptive Peer Feedback and Perceived Feedback Adequacy in Pre-Service Teacher Education

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Peer feedback can effectively facilitate pre-service teachers’ evidence-informed reasoning in CSCL environments. Based on the RESOLV-model, we examine if peer feedback providers plan and formulate their peer feedback messages differently depending on the perceived skill level of the peer feedback recipient. T his will be analyzed by assessing the feedback feature constellations of feedback provided by N =297 pre-service teachers during a psychology course. We expect that the more feedback providers adapt their feedback, the more positively this will affect recipients’ acceptance. Aims and theoretical background Online peer feedback is widely used in the computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) context to foster learning outcomes (Tan & Chen, 2022). It has great potential as a pedagogical approach (Double et al., 2020), as it structures peer interaction in a way that ensures transactivity (Vogel et al., 2016): Optimally, students thoroughly process their peers’ contributions, assess them, react to them, and build upon them to extend their peers’, but also their own understanding of subject-matter information (Yu & Schunn, 2023). Yet, feedback recipients do not necessarily benefit from peer feedback (Li et al., 2020). While it is crucial for recipients to understand their peer feedback in order to learn from it (Nelson & Schunn, 2009), they can get overwhelmed by, for example, very comprehensive and complex feedback which address es multiple interrelated points (Shulgina et al., 2024). This could be especially challenging for recipients with limited prior knowledge of the topic. To mitigate this, feedback providers need to adapt the scope and depth of their feedback message to the recipient’s skill level. If they do so, this should positively affect the recipient’s assessment of the adequacy of the feedback, leading to more likely implementation of th e feedback in the subsequent revision, and ultimately to better learning outcomes (Strijbos et al., 2021). Accordingly, more adaptive peer feedback messages are expected to improve learning outcomes and are therefore relevant within the CSCL context. Surprisingly, prior CSCL research seems to assume that certain feedback features (such as the presence of explanations and solutions) work in a one-size-fits-all fashion (t o be generally beneficial for subsequent learning, i.e., independently from the recipient’s skill level; e.g., Wu & Schunn, 2020). Yet, in line with research on the expertise-reversal effect (Kalyuga et al., 2003), we assume that the relative importance of feedback features—such as explanations and solutions, which are deemed essential to the recipient’s learning outcome (Wu & Schunn, 2020)—depends on the recipient’s skill level, as evidenced by the quality of their initial task solution performance. Specifically, detailed explanations/solutions would be more important for recipients who have not yet mastered the task as successfully as more competent recipients. In turn, these more competent learners likely require only identification of problems in their solutions. Ideally, the feedback provider should be able to recognize these differences in the recipient’s skill levels and tailor their feedback message accordingly. Based on the RESOLV-model, the feedback provider would evaluate the author’s expertise (Rouet et al., 2017). The feedback provider judges the level of competence exhibited via the feedback recipient’s task performance and based on this, recognizes their skill level. Next, the feedback provider would formulate a goal and a plan on how to adjust their message to match the recipient’s skill level. This aligns with Zimmermann’s (2 002) forethought phase, in which the feedback provider sets the goal to adapt their feedback message to the level of competence they assume the recipient to have. Thereby, feedback providers balance their message between completeness and ease of comprehension. We assume that these kinds of cognitive processes determine the proportion of feedback features (in this case: solutions and explanations) in the feedback message (see Fig. 1). Hypotheses We assume that university students are able to adapt their feedback to their peers’ skills levels, at least to a certain extent. We therefore hypothesize that the worse the recipient’s initial performance is, the more explanations and solutions the provider’s feedback contains (H1), as illustrated in Figure 1. Most students were in their fifth semester of studies (M = 5.21, SD = 1.66). The participants engaged in an asynchronous online  peer feedback activity over the duration of four weeks, embedded in parallel psychology courses. The course goal was to develop evidence-informed reasoning skills. Therefore, students analyzed  p roblematic classroom situations utilizing educational and psychological theories following an evidence-informed reasoning scheme (problem identification, description, explanation, goal derivation, and action plan; Greisel et al., 202 2). In the university’s learning management system, the students submitted their work and collaborated via the computer-supported peer feedback tool ‘getFeedback!’ (Fey et al., 2023), which  structures the peer feedback process from start to finish. This encompasses  the provision of instructions, scaffolding, assign ment of  peer feedback pairs, and delivering feedback messages. In Week 1, students crafted an initial task performance by analyzing problematic classroom situations. In Week 2, they provided feedback on the initial task performance of two randomly assigned peers (scaffolded through content-related guiding questions and a corresponding worked example). In Week 3, students revised their feedback. In Week 4, students received feedback from two peers and revised their initial task performance. Two independent coders assessed the quality of the initial task performance regarding evidence-informed reasoning skill level according to Greisel et al. (2022) with an interrater-reliability of Gwet’s AC2 =.84. To assess the presence of feedback features, the provided messages were segmented into topical segments (Gwet’s AC1  =.82) according to the expected evidence-informed reasoning scheme. The features will then be coded depending on their presence, resulting in dummy coded variables,  based on a scheme by Wu and Schunn (2020). Coded features encompass: Praise, identification, explanation, hedges, suggestion, solution, accuracy, and importance. The coding process is currently under development, and coder training will aim for an interrater-reliability >.80. Feedback adequacy was assessed through the Feedback Perception Questionnaire by Strijbos et al. (2021), for each of the two feedback messages separately, encompassing three dimensions (fairness, utility, acceptance) measured on a Likert-scale ranging from 1 = completely disagree to 9 = completely agree with McDonald’s ωh =.82/.85. Multilevel regression analyses will be applied to test the effects of evidence-informed reasoning skill level of the initial task performance on the relative proportion of solutions and explanations, that is, the presence of explanations and solutions divided by the number of all segments. With exploratory cluster analyses, we will further investigate whether combinations of feedback features are influenced by the task performance level. Feedback adequacy will be anal yzed through analyses of variances, accounting for the adaptivity of feedback messages. Potential results and implications As the coding process is still ongoing, we cannot yet present results. Should providers formulate their feedback adaptively as predicted and thereby foster a more adequate perception of the feedback message, the one-size-fitsall assumption of peer feedback quality needs to be rethought. Ideally, scaffolding for feedback providers could be made adaptive to initial performance levels.  For example, peer feedback systems, like ’getFeedback!’, could tailor whether they prompt the feedback provider to explain in accordance with the initial performance level. This could improve the learning experience and outcomes for both the feedback providers and the recipients (Kalyuga et al., 2003; Nelson & Schunn, 2009).
