# Social Studies Teachers’ Goals for Teaching Online Evaluations

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teachers need professional learning opportunities aligned with their existing goals to help them prepare to teach digital media literacy. Using interview data from 23 high school social studies teachers, we analyzed how teachers described their goals for teaching online evaluations and the alignment they saw between those goals and their overall goals as social studies teachers. Implications for digital media literacy curriculum development and professional learning are explored.

## Introduction and framing

Increasingly, secondary teachers are asked to teach  students to evaluate online information (e.g., Med ia Literacy Now, 2023). To successfully teach digital media literacy, teachers need multifaceted professional learning (PL) opportunities that are aligned with their professional goals (Fishman et al., 2014; Garet et al., 2001; M cGrew, 2021). Understanding teachers’ goals for teaching online evaluation skills, and how teachers view those as aligned with their overall goals, is thus an important part of designing effective PL on teaching digital media literacy. This study examined teachers’ goals for teaching digital media literacy  by focusing on one specific group: social studies teachers. As the ability to d iscern credible from less credible digital content is increasingly a requirement for informed citizenship (e.g. Educat ing for Americandemocracy, 2021), digital media  has a natural home in social studies. This conceptual connection between the goals of social studies courses and lessons in digital media literacy has resulted in a growing  base of research on social studies teachers’ approaches to teaching digital media literacy (Clark et al., 2020; Garrett et al., 2021; McGrew, 2022; Stoddard, 2022). However, few studies to date have investigated social studies teachers’ goals for teaching online evaluations in practice and how those align with their overall goals as social studies teachers (see Reynolds, 2025 for an excepti on). We investigated teachers’ goals for teaching students to evaluate online information through analyses of interviews conducted with teachers after they participated in PL opportunities and taught a series of digital med ia literacy lessons. We asked: How d o social studies teachers’ goals for teaching stude nts to evaluate online information align with their goals as social studies teachers?

## Method

Interviews were conducted with 23 teachers from fou r sites who collaborated in research projects to de sign and test the efficacy of digital media literacy lessons. The studies in Sites 1 and 2 were led by the Stan ford History Education Group (now the Digital Inquiry Group), of which the first author was a part. The studies in Sites 3 and 4 were led by the authors. All participating teachers volunteered to teach the lessons and consented to participate in the study and received a stipend for fully parti cipating. All participating teachers had similar le vels of collaboration with the research team. First, over t he cou rse of four to six hours of PL time, teachers were introduced to students’ typical approaches to evalu ating online information, reviewed effective evalua tive strategies, and planned for teaching online evaluat ion strategies with their students. Next, teach ers reviewed lessons and suggested revisions to make them access ible for their students. While the objectives for i ndividual lessons were clear, the overarching goals or motivations for teaching students to evaluate online information were not explicitly laid out in the PL or lesson materials.

After teachers finished teaching the lessons, interviews were conducted and recorded via Zoom. The first author conducted all interviews for teachers at Sites 1, 2, and 3; the second author conducted interviews at Site 4. Interviews lasted 20-30 minutes. An interview protocol was developed for  teachers at Site 1 and then used with minor modifications for Sites 2, 3, and 4. In the s ection of the interview that is the focus of this s tudy, we asked two questions  about teachers’ purposes for teaching students to evaluat e online information, and, more specifically, how those purposes fit within their overall goals as social studies teachers. Interviews were anonymized, transcribed by a transc ription service, and manually reviewed for correctness. The authors independently completed multiple passes of the interview data and generated initial drafts of a codebook to capture how teachers viewed their purposes for teaching students to evaluate online information in the context of their social studies classes. Relevant codes were applied to each teacher’s interview responses. The authors met to discuss the codes and then used additional data passes to condense and refine the c odebook, resulting in 3 codes and 5 subcodes (see Table 1). One author then coded all interview data.

## Findings

Broadly, teachers’ ideas about how their goals for teaching students to evaluate online information al igned with their goals as social studies teachers can be grouped into three categories (see Table 1). First, teachers described connections to curricular goals for students, seeing direct links between stu dents’ ability to evaluate online information and their success on school-related learning goals. Second, teachers saw online  evaluation skills as aligned with their civic goals for students. Instead of viewing these skills as most applicable within school walls, these teachers wanted students to be able to evaluate online information in their day-to-day lives. Finally, a small number of teachers connected their goals for teachi ng students to evaluate online content n ot to social studies content or civic goals, but to the need for their instruction to be responsive to students’ lives outside the classroom. ## Discussion and conclusion

There were consistent patterns in the purposes that  social studies teachers described for teaching stu dents to evaluate online information. These goals position s tudents differently and provide insight into how te achers conceptualize the work they do in students’ lives. The 43% of teachers who named only curricular-oriented goals focused on helping students succeed in school-related tasks like class performance and college su ccess. This is perhaps unsurprising, as this is typically seen as teachers’ main jo b; however, these teachers may have stopped short of helping students see how the skills they l earned were applicable in their lives outside the c lassroom. A smaller number of teachers expressed goals that wer e broader in scope: They described the aim of p reparing students for the civic world outside the classroom where a critical skill is being able to competently  evaluate online information. Still, these teachers were spli t in whether they viewed students primarily as vuln erable and needing protection from online ills, or as budding civic actors whoneeded to prepare to be fully info rmed and empowered. Though continued research on teachers’ g oals for teaching online evaluations is needed, thi s study suggests that digital media literacy curriculum and  PL fo r social studies teachers should connect to teacher ’s curricular goals by emphasizing connections to acad emic skills and content. Further, resources for tea chers can emphasize that teaching students to evaluate online  information can support civic goals and provide mo dels for how teachers might achieve these goals through the lessons.
