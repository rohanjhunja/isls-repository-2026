# The Overlooked Potential of Gamebooks

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Gamebooks are non-linear narratives where reader choice determines what happens next in the story. While such books are popular with readers, there is limited research on their use as educational resources. This paper presents the initial results of a literature review on research on educational gamebooks, exploring the context in which gamebooks are used, the impact they have on readers and the theory that underpins gamebook design. It is argued that the educational potential of gamebooks has been overlooked and that they are worthy of further research.

Gamebooks are non-linear branching narratives, where reader choice shapes the story and affects the ending. They may be paper based (e.g., Fighting Fantasy, Jackson & Livingstone, 2024) or digital (e.g., 80 Day, inkle, 2014). Gamebooks are not  interactive stor ies where readers are quizzed on their knowledge and understanding to progress, nor are they hyperlink or “lift the flap ” books where readers can seek further information only if desired. They are typified by th e Choose-Your-Own-AdventureTM series, which  is the 5 th bestselling series in history, demonstrating that gamebooks are certainly popula r with readers. It focuses on 1) what are the contexts in which gamebooks are currently used ? 2) why are  gamebooks used as educational resources? and 3) what theory underpins the use of gamebooks as educational resources? ## Methods

The literature review focused on articles where gamebooks are used as educational resources t o affect change in readers’ knowledge, beliefs or behaviour in any domain. The review followed guidelines by Gough (2007). Six journal databases  (British Educational Index, Child Development & Adol escent Studies, Education Abstracts, ERIC, PychInfo & Teacher Reference Centre) were sea rched for relevant sources. Sources were included i f the following terms were present in the article title, abstract or keyworks: gamebook, choose your own adventure, COYA, interactive fiction, interactive narrative, i nteractive story, non-linear narrative, branching narrative, hyperlink text; hypertext fiction; text adventure; branching narrative. No limit was placed on the publication date of the article, sources were identified in December 2023. The search returned 1803 sources. Next, abstracts were screened for sources where gamebooks were used as an educational resource. For example, use of terms including or related to attitude, belief, behaviour, knowledg e, understanding, skill, competence, education, tea ching, learning, pedagogy, educational resource or intervention. This step reduced the sample to 25 empirical sources, primarily due to the  large number of sources that were interactive stori es or advertisements in educational supplements for newly published gamebooks. Remaining sources were read and data  extracted to answer the research questions above. The results presented here are based on an initial analysis of this data set. Now, go to ‘Findings’ for a summary of the results.

## Where are gamebooks used: context

To answer research question one, sources were reviewed for the context in which gamebooks were used. Context covers multiple aspects, including how gamebooks are embedded in educational activities. In the sample, 56% of studies used gamebooks as a standalone resource, whereas 44% focused on the gamebook as prompt for further activities. Gamebooks were used as educational resources across the life span, where an age-range was specified, and grouping by traditional phases of education, this included students in Primary school (5-to 11-years-old, n = 2), Secondary school (11-to 18-years-old, n = 10) and Higher/adult education (18+ years-old, n = 12). In all the 25 sources gamebooks were digital resources rather than paper-based books. Context also refers to what can be termed the subject area of the gamebook (e.g., science education). The majority of sources focused on gamebooks as a resource to develop reade rs knowledge about a specific subject area. This included knowledge of procedures in medical education (n = 3), for example, selecting appropriate tests (Wilson-Stewart, 2017); understanding of concepts in scienc e education (n = 1), specifically, Newton’s Laws (Flynn & Hardman, 2019); knowledge and understanding related to language and  literature education (n = 10), for example, feminist critique of prose (Holm, 2010); and knowledge related to  religious education (n = 1), specifically, understanding the lives of common people  (Lester, 2018). A separate group of sources focused on using gamebooks to change reader beliefs and, potentially, behaviour. These included gamebooks being used in social science education (n = 3), for example to change beliefs on social support for immigrants (Parrott, Carpentier & Northup, 2017) and communication in re lationships (Vicary & Fraley, 2007). Furthermore, gamebooks were used in preventative health educatio n (n = 7) on topics such as safe sex (Kok et al., 2006) and Asthma self-management (Sarasmita et al., 2021). Exploring the context in which gamebooks are used  shows their potential as an educational resource. They have been developed for, and used in, a diverse range of subject areas to improve readers’ knowledge and understanding, along with aiming to change beliefs and behaviour. What are gamebooks used for: impact Having established that gamebooks are used in a ran ge of subject areas, research question two explore s what gamebooks are used for. That is, what impact may they have on readers knowledge and understanding, beliefs, and behaviour. The sources reviewed suggest that gamebooks have the potential to support change in relation to a number of key educational aims. This includes gam ebooks leading to increased knowledge and understanding in a subject area. For example, understanding of forces (Flynn & Hardman, 2019) or identification of sexual violence (Gilliam et al., 2016). Gamebooks that supported readers in develop ing practical skills, such as clinical procedures (Wilson-Stewart, 2017) or goal setting ability (Winskell et al., 2018). There is evidence that gamebooks lead to changes in beliefs related to psy chological constructs including readers sense of agency and improvements in self-efficacy (e.g., Yin, Ring & Bickmore, 2012). Such changes may be due to gamebooks potential to support readers in taking the perspect ive of another, for example,  the lived experience of migrants when crossing boarders (Parrott, Carpentier & Northup, 2017). Finally, it could be that gamebooks support behavioural change, for example, regarding readers potential future safe sex practices (Kok et al., 2006). Expanding the scope from using gamebooks as a resource to foster some form of change in readers, there were studies where g amebooks were used as an assessment tool. That is, process data from readers use of gamebooks was collected and analysed to measure cer tain constructs; for example,  profiling readers ethical orientation (Santos, Khan & Markopoulos, 2018). Furthermore, the majority of the studies reviewed investigated readers’ experience of using gamebooks. A shared finding across articles was that gamebooks  are an engaging medium, with participants in studies reporting positive experiences when reading gamebooks and often having a desire to reread the gamebook to explore alternative pathways and endings. Detailed discussion of research methods is outside the scope of this short paper; therefore, the validity and reliability of the sources findings is not considered  further. However, review of the sources identified does suggest that research on gamebooks as an educational resource is often, what may be termed, early-stage research. Much of the research conducted focuses on the initial design of gamebooks and smal l-scale efficacy tests, using questionnaires and interviews to evaluate changes in knowledge, be liefs and behaviour, along with user experience.

How are gamebooks designed: theoretical grounding The studies reviewed suggest that g amebooks are an effective resource  for facilitating change in readers knowledge, skills, beliefs and possible future behaviour. Research question three investigated why they may result in such outcomes. In short, what is the theoretical grounding that underpins the design and use of gamebooks. For this question, two related areas of theory are considered: pedagogical theory and design theory. Theory specific to the field or subject area for which gamebooks were designed is not included (e.g., conceptual change in science education on forces). This section provides a concise summary of key theories present in the reviewed papers, rather thandetailed reporting on individual articles.

Pedagogical theory refers to concepts from the field of educati on that are used as a rationale for how gamebooks lead to changes in readers knowledge, beliefs or behaviour. Some individual sources alluded to ideas of constructivism and active learning. However, the majority of the sources reviewed drew on social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1986)  and related concepts such as observational learning and  self-efficacy to provide a justification for the use of gamebooks. The general  argument being that gamebooks allow designers to present readers with imagined social environments where they can choose to engage, or not engage, in certain behaviours. As readers take on the role of the protagonist in gamebooks, they are able to observe the consequences of the ir action, in what could be considered  a form of vicarious learning as readers learn throu gh the experience of a character in the story. Expanding on gamebooks use in facilitating vicarious learning, narratives can be designed so readers take control of a character who differs from them in a specific way. This is argued to supp ort readers in taking another’s perspective, therefore, developing a better understanding of a phenomenon based on the lived experience of the other. Such understanding may contribute to changes in knowledge, beliefs and behaviour. Branching narrative pathways  enable designers to reinforce, positively or negati vely, the choices that readers make. Potentially, resulting in changes to readers’ beliefs that underpin the target behaviour. As a form of fiction, gamebooks provide a safe space to explore imagined futures, that is, there are no real-world consequences to choices made. Additionally, gamebooks can allow readers the oppor tunity to repeat choices or make similar choices in different contexts within a story. Both of which may increase readers’ self-efficacy, their confidence in their ability to perf orm specific behaviour or act in a certain way, and self-regulation, their ability to monitor and control their behaviour to meet a goal. Building on this, and assuming a level of intention ality, gamebooks may be effective resources for supporting readers in making choices that have a meaningful impact on their lives. That is, developing their sense of agency, which in  turn may result in changes to readers’ identity, their sense of self and role in society.

Gamebooks were also used as a prompt or inciting incident for further e ducational activities. In such situations effective supporting activities were based on the Zone of Proximal Development (Vygotsky, 1978) and scaffolding (Wood, Bruner & Ross, 19 76), involving activities where learners were supported, by educators or structured prompts, in developing increased knowledge of a topic or skill in an area of action. More collaborative activities were based  on ideas of dialogic education (Alexander, 200 6), suggesting that readers draw on their experience of reading the gamebook  to discuss choices made and the impact of these choices. The implication being that a group of readers can collaboratively construct a shared understanding about a concept or behaviour. Design theory refers to frameworks and concepts related to the design of and reader experience of gamebooks, for example, how the narrative is structured or the user interface. Sources often referred to game-based learning or Serious Games, in relation to gam ebooks being seen as a subset of video games, which can be designed to be both fun and educational. Drawing on  the field of video game design, creation of gamebooks was based on, for example, Gee’s (2005) learning principles. Additionally, s ome sources  discuss the need for developers to consider cognitive load (Sweller, 198 8), activity-goal alignment (Shelton, 2007) and users game literacy (Buckingham & Burn, 2007). The main implication is that gamebo oks allow for narrative-centered learning resulting in an immersive  environment that engages readers and increases motivation. Exac t details on how and why this occurs is often limited.

## Discussion

Review of research on the use of gamebooks in education suggests that this is an emerging area, given the small number of published articles returned that focused specifically on how gamebooks can be used as educational resources to support change in reader knowledge, belief or be haviour. The majority of sources reviewed were early-stage exploratory studies, focusing on gamebook design and user experience, or small-scale efficacy studies. This is not a criticism. Rather, again, it shows that research on gamebooks is a nascent field. One, however, that has potential. The findings of the review indicate that gamebooks are associated with changes and gains in readers knowledge and understanding, beliefs and potentially their future behaviour. They can be used with learners  of different ages, from those in the early stages of education through to adults, and in a wide range of subject areas. Gamebooks potential to be used in d iffuse contexts means they may be a beneficial resource for current educational priorities, including enabling learners to envisage possible futures and to become agents of change in the world. The following suggestions are offered for those creating or exploring gamebooks in education: 1) more research is needed on the theory that underpins the use and design of gamebooks; 2) future publications need detailed reporting on how gamebooks were created and how they are used in  practice. The remainder of th is discussion expands on these two points.

M any of the sources review ed would have benefited from greater discussion of underpinning theory. Again, this is not intended  as criticism, studies were often conducted by researchers outside  of the field of education, applying educational theory to design resources for their specialist field. Building on this existing work, future studies should explore what makes gamebooks an effective educational resource and how they supp ort learning processes. This could be facilitated by using conjecture mapping (Sandoval, 2014) to specify and evaluate the relationship between theoretical design and learning processes and outcomes. The findings of th is review show that current ideas on how gamebooks support learning are primarily based on social cognitive theory. To expand this theoretical grounding, the following  offers four theoretical conjectures as to how gamebooks may support learnin g. Firstly, considering cognition and memory, gamebooks encourage readers to reread content to explore the outcome of different choices and experience multiple endings. If gamebooks are being used as a resource to support knowledge acquisition, then this repetition may result in increased encoding of content. Secondly, the core mechanic of gamebooks is reader choice; therefore, if the educational aim relates choosing to engage in a target behaviour, this allows gamebook developers to intrinsically integrate learning content with game mechanics  (e.g., Habgood & Ainsworth, 2011). Third ly, the Reasoned Action Approach (Fishbein & Ajzen, 2010) could provide a theoretical framework when designing gamebooks for behavioral change. This has origins in social cognitive theory and has been shown to be successful in modeling how certain beliefs predict a specific behaviour. Consequently, gameboo ks can be designed to target influential beliefs thereby supporting behaviour change (see Halls & Ainsworth, 2023). Finally, these proposals have viewed gamebooks as a stand-alone tool, but they may also serve as an inciting incident for an educational activity. Prior studies have followed gamebook reading with dialogic activities to further explore a target concept. It is proposed that gamebooks, compared to traditional linear media, can be particularly effective at fostering dialogue, as readers will have experience d different narrative pathways and endings. This mean s they will have gained different perspectives from the gamebook, potentially resulting in richer  dialogue or even supporting  collaborative argumentation (Andriessen & Baker, 2022).

Moving to the design of gamebooks, it is argued tha t future publications should  provide greater details on the design process. Design-based research (Bakker, 2018) offers both a methodological approach and a way of reporting gamebook design. It also aligns with the development process of gamebooks, which may necessitate cycles of testing and revision, along with input from co-designers (e.g., end users). Given the limited research on gamebooks it would be prudent to report the early D BR stages of gamebook design; for example, REDACTED. As mentioned, using conjecture mapping could link design to outcomes, furthermore, results of such research can produce design principles that can be used by other researchers when developing gamebooks. Until there exists a larger body of research on gamebooks, design princi ples can be drawn from similar resources. Sitting at an intersection between books and video games, the des ign of digital gamebooks would benefit from both literature on storytelling (e.g., Cleaver, 2010) and games for learning (see Fields & Kafai, 2018). Finally, there are a range of tools for creating digital gamebooks. Simple text-only gamebooks can be created with text editors using hyperlinks to connect narrative sections, while authoring tools, such as Scratch, can be used to create gamebooks that incorporate a range o f media. However, for those looking to create complex gamebo oks with multiple branches and tracking of choices, ink is a free to use narrative scripting language (inkle, 2021) than can generate browser-based gamebooks or be integrated into Unity and Unreal game engines. The results of this literature review suggest that the educational potential of gamebooks has been overlooked. Investigating the u se of gamebooks as educational resources suggests they may be effective for a range of subject areas when aiming to facilitate changes in readers knowledge, beliefs and behaviour. The limited sources found indicates that this is an emerging area of research but one that is worthy of further exploration. To expand the knowledge base on educational gamebooks, future  research should  1) have a greater focus on the theory that underpins the use and design of gamebooks, and 2) ensure detailed reporting on how gamebooks were created and how they are used in practice.
