# Exploring Black Communities’ Futures-Thinking Towards Education and STEM Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how Black residents in the United States envision liberatory futures for STEM education amid enduring racialized harm. Drawing on Black futurity and a community-practice partnership, we analyze interviews and archival data to examine how local histories and lived experiences shape Black dreams for STEM learning. Findings highlight desires for holistic, affirming, and community-rooted STEM spaces, offering insights for designing culturally sustaining education grounded in Black educational traditions, community expertise, and collective care.

## Introduction

A growing body of research calls for STEM education that supports and advances Black liberation (Morton et al., 2022). Yet, mainstream STEM education is rooted in and continues to reproduce neoliberal and white-settler logics (Vossoughi & Vakil, 2018), resulting in schooling and curriculum that dehumanize Black children. Scholars have extensively documented how K-12 STEM classrooms marginalize Black students (Martin, 2019; McGee & White, 2021), as well as the contested politics of STEM in Black and urban communities (Bullock, 2017). However, alongside these narratives of harm, there is research that highlights pedagogies, design, and programming that uplift the humanity and STEM brill iance of Black children, both in and out of school (Joseph et al., 2019). Our study contributes to this conver sation by examining the potential role of STEM educ ation in Black dreams for education and liberation. We take an ethnographic approach grounded in the historical and local context of a Midwestern city (MWC) in the U.S. This study is part of a broader research community-practice partnership (RCPP), which has evolved over several years in collaboration with local community members  and schools. This proposal presents preliminary findings in response to the following research questions:

1) How do Black residents in MWC make sense of racialized harm in local and broader contexts?

2) How might these perspectives inform Black visions for STEM learning and education for Black youth?

## Theoretical framework

We draw on the concept of Black futurity to understand how Black people imagine worlds where Black life thrives despite ongoing anti-Black oppression. Rooted in Afrofuturism, Black fem inism, and the Black radical tradition, Black futurity offers a “grammar of possibility” (Campt, 2017, p. 17) that helps theorize both the futures we dream of and the present conditions necessary to realize them. This lens emphasizes the everyday practices t hrough which Black communities enact liberatory futures in  the now.  Black futurity challenges deficit narratives by centering agency, imagination, and lived experience. This framework centers the social, cultural, and li beratory aspects of learning, aligning with  learning science perspectives that view learning as a transfo rmative process embedded in social contexts and cultural practices.

## Methodology and context

This research is part of a larger project focused o n co-designing equitable and sustainable STEM learning w ith Black students through a historically informed RCPP  in MWC. Like many U.S. cities, MWC has a complex political relationship with its Black residents. Our methodological approach combines the RCPP model (Ishimaru et al., 2022) with an extended ethnographic case st udy (Hall & Jurow, 2015). RCPPs offer a framework f or collaborative research that centers the knowledge a nd experiences of marginalized co mmunities. Case study methods allow us to trace organizational and cultural shifts over time (Coburn, 2001; Yin, 1984). Data collection for this study include d semi-structured interviews (Johnson, 2002; Seidman, 2006) with 12 prominent Black community members—educators, parents, and leaders—alongside archival data documenting Black life in MWC. Participants were selected for their deep knowledge  of the community, its history, and its educational  landscape. While some were connected to STEM organizations, ot hers were involved in broader community and youth development spaces, including athletics.  Interviews explored participants’ personal and comm unity educational experiences, encounters with school closures, and their visions for the future of STEM learning for Black students in MWC.  W e analyzed data through two coding cycles (Saldana,  2016). The initial descriptive coding cycle included categories such as “anti-black policy,” “positive schooling experiences” “Bl ack dreams for education” “local histories,” “school closures,” “Black defini tions of STEM,” and “STEM careers.” The second cycl e examined interactions with structural, political, a nd social contexts, producing broader themes like “ shared visions,” “disagreements,” “rejection,” and “acceptance.” We used the framework of Black futurity to support in deciding and interpreting codes and themes. We then analyzed which codes had frequent co-occurrences to support in the development of themes, discovering r elationships between anti-blackness and Black dreams for education, and local histories and Black dreams for STEM. This led to the cultivation of themes around how local histories of anti-blackness and educational resistance shape dreams for STEM education.

## Findings and discussion

Our findings highlight how Black residents in MWC m ake sense of STEM education within the broader cont ext of enduring racialized harm. Participants emphasize d the importance of local history, particularly the profound impact of school closures and other school-based traumas that continue to shape educational possibilities for Black youth. These histories were not abstract; they were  deeply personal, carried in the stories of elders, parents, and educators who’ve witnessed the systematic dismantling of institutions that once served their communities. Across interviews and conversations, community members exp ressed a clear desire for STEM learning experiences beyond traditional school settings and spoke about spaces where Black youth could enga ge in STEM without navigating the constraints and violences often embedded in anti-Black schools. Notably, participants’ dreams for STEM education we re shaped by their own experiences with historically Black educational spaces. Many recalle d the affirming, culturally grounded environments o f predominantly Black schools as places where they felt seen, challenged, and s upported. These memories served as a foundation for imagining STEM education that i s community-centered, family-engaged, and grounded in collective values. One Black educator  shared that participants’ visions for STEM we nt beyond curriculum  and content to hands-on and Black-affirming educational experiences. In this framing, STEM is positioned as a tool for liberation, for joy, and community well-being. These dreams are a part of a rich legacy of Black-led educational resistance and innovation.

These findings offer important implications for res earchers, practitioners, and community members engaged in co-designing STEM learning environments, particularly within RCPPs. They highlight the need to center local histories and community dynamics as foundational to equitable design, drawing on strategies of Black educational traditions to inform contemporary movement. Additionally, this work views communities as sources of vision, strategy, and expertise. Ultimately thes e insights provide practical guidance for creating STEM spaces that are culturally sustaining and grounded in communal care and historical accountability.
