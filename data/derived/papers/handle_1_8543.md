# Museum-Led Design of Student-Centered K-12 Learning: Where Informal Perspectives Meet Formal Requirements

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Many museums create educational content for K-12 students and classrooms, bringing informal learning opportunities to formal educational contexts. The design considerations prioritized by these teams and the assumptions that influence these priorities impact the final product they create. In this paper, we describe how a museum-based design team created a learning module for use in K-12 classrooms. We report our analysis of five design team meetings and five individual semi-structured interviews, spaced strategically throughout the design process. We share what design considerations the team prioritized over time and highlight how team members verbalized assumptions about student-centered learning.

## Introduction

Museum education departments are charged with carrying out the educational mission of museums, which often involves the design of activities, and materials for K-12 students and teachers. These learning experiences leverage museum collections and informal learning pedagogies  while supporting and/or meeting educational standa rds (Feinstein et al., 2013; Garcia, 2012; IMLS, 1996; Kisiel, 2014; Kristinsdóttir, 2017). For interdisciplinary teams of educators, curators, and researchers developing learning experiences, their design processes incorporate explicit and implicit assumptions and values about K-12 student learning (Garcia, 2012; Kisiel, 2014). However, these values and assumptions are not always visible, and different interpretations of the same concept may exist within the same team,  especially with varying expertise on an interdisci plinary design team. Creating greater transparency into these design processes, and especially assumptions about studentcentered learning, can help design team members to understand how they communicate and prioritize desi gn considerations when making decisions that impact students and teachers. To better understand how the assumptions of design teams influence the development of K-12 learning experiences, we examined how a museum-based design team developed a new inquiry investigation. Our goa l was to identify the team’s key design considerations and changing prioritizations based on analysis of meetings and semi-structured interviews. We also aimed to identify assumptions about student-centered learning based on team members’ language about situating themselves in the minds of students. We used values-led participatory design (Iversen et al., 2012) to identify the underlying values and assumptions that drove prioritization of design considerations and justifications. In examining how power is distributed among stakeholders, values-led participatory design specifically attends to stakeholders’ values and their influence on the design process. In this study, we examined how individual design team members revealed their values and assumptions about science learning during meetings and interviews, with specific attention to the design considerations in play; the prioritization of different considerations over time; and the language used to communicate how team members situated themselves within the mind of a student. While the team under study did not actively invite students and teachers as co-designers, we found this lens helpful as a way to identify the values present and how those are shaping the design of the learning experience.

## Methods

The design team at a museum in the American West ha s collaborated for approximately four years and consists of eight members. All team members agreed to take p art in this study. The team’s design efforts focus on an initiative about developing inquiry-based investigations for K-12 students and teachers that support engagement with NGSS science and engineering practices, especially gathering and analyzing data. Students receive guidance from museum scientists as they gather data and anal yze data from digitized museum specimens, models, a nd simulations. Together, the activities incorporated into these investigations are designed to mimic the  work of scientists at the museum as “authentic” science learning experiences. The first author is an established member of the team and obtained consent to record team meetings over Zoom. She also conducted semi-structured interviews  with one team member each week on a rotating basis. Interviews lasted an average of 20 minutes and incl uded questions about how they felt the meeting went, what ideas stood out to them, and how decisions made during the meeting would impact the product and eventual users. ## Findings

Our findings are presented as three components: design considerations; temporal analysis; and student-centered perspectives. Table 2 reports the frequency of each  design consideration across meetings and interview s. We found that “authentic data and methods” were mentioned most often (80% of meetings, 60% of interviews)  by team members. “Student reasoning” was another frequ ently occurring design consideration (80% of meetin gs, 40% of interviews), as well as “alignment to NGSS” (60% of meetings, 40% of interviews). These considerations seem to be high priority to the design team. We also noted that several design considerations were mentioned in meetings or interviews, but not both. For example, “student prior knowledge” is discussed in 40% of me etings but no interviews, and “constraints on scope/scale” are discussed in 40% of interviews but no meetings. ## Discussion & Conclusion

Museum design teams are uniquely positioned to cont ribute to K-12 learning spaces by designing learnin g experiences that incorporate informal institutional  assets. This study aimed to investigate a museum-b ased interdisciplinary design team’s identification and prioritization of design considerations discussed d uring team meetings and individual interviews when creating in quiry-based investigations. We also noted language that indicated how team members situated themselves in t he student role as a way to express and justify des ign decisions. In our analysis of selected meetings and interviews, we found that the team most frequently prioritized “authentic data and methods,” followed by “alignmen t to NGSS” and “student reasoning.” These design considerations became a greater focus of discussion later in the design process. The emphasis on “authentic data and methods” and th e “alignment to NGSS ” throughout the design process illustrate two key goals of both formal and  informal science education: authentic experiences and ICLS2022 Proceedings 1575 © ISLS baselines for science understanding. Prior literature notes the importance of considering formal education contexts and assets when planning successful cross-over experiences between formal and informal learning environments (Garcia, 2012; Kisiel, 2014). The inclusion of NGSS  alignment as a goal results from experience workin g with and designing for K-12 teachers and students. Meanwhile, “authentic data and methods” draws upon the team’s experiences as informal educators and unique strengths of the museum’s collections and scientists.  The emphasis on both authenticity and educational standards in team meetings and interviews illustrates how the team considers both formal and informal learning assumptions and requirements. This finding highlights museums’ role as spaces that blur the lines between formal and informal education settings (Kisiel, 2014; Kristinsdóttir, 2017). As an interdisciplinary team, different team member s have different experiences with both formal and informal education settings and likely hold different assumptions related to teaching, learning, and design. These assumptions became apparent in team meetings and in dividual interviews. For example, “authentic data a nd methods,” “alignment to NGSS,” and “student reasoni ng” appeared frequently throughout meetings and interviews, indicating that these are important design considerations for the whole team. In contrast, “constraints on scope/scale” appeared in two interviews, indicating that this design consideration may be more salient for some team members than others. The differences in assumptions held by team members mean that the common goal of designing student-centered experiences may be interpreted and acted on in a variety of ways, ultimately impacting the final product and its users (e.g., K-12 students and teachers). These findings have implications for other design teams situated in environments that span informal a nd formal learning. Future analysis of team data wi ll use discourse analysis of negotiated meanings and their relationship to assumptions used to guide design decisions. While this museum design team’s approach does not explicitly incorporate participatory design, it does connect stakeholders representing different values,  experiences, power dynamics, and goals to collabor atively create a product (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016; Kristinsdóttir, 2017). This attempt to democratize the design process is characteristic of participatory design projects and, in this way, the project resembles some of the challenges and opportunities that come from participatory design a pproaches. The lens of values-led participatory des ign highlights whose ideas are being heard, and whose are not, and draws on the ways values are being repr esented and influence the design process and products in ways that will ultimately impact the users.

In conclusion, this study provided insights into the design considerations prioritized by a museum design team and the ways that underlying assumptions about  student-centered learning were expressed throughou t different points in the design process. These insights serve as a first step toward deeper analysis of team members' discourse that shapes how design considerations are  introduced, negotiated, and implemented. We plan t o investigate how design considerations that appear to be the same on the surface (e.g., “alignment to NGSS”) can be interpreted and applied differently based on the  experiences and assumptions of individual team mem bers. Understanding the underlying assumptions about and negotiation of design considerations can show a pat h for moving more intentionally towards shared consensus that benefits the design team members and eventual users.
