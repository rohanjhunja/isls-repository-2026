# Principles for a Balanced Narrative-Paradigmatic Approach for Climate Change Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Narratives are fundamental means through which humans organize experiences, interpret phenomena, and construct meaning by weaving events into coherent structures. This intrinsic role in sensemaking makes them particularly valuable in Climate Change Education. However, within the context of science education, they also present certain limitations and risks. Drawing on Bruner’s theory of the dual modes of thought—narrative and paradigmatic—this study introduces two core design principles aimed at fostering a balanced educational approach. From this perspective, narratives are not merely tools for communication but represent a distinct mode of thinking that is essential to the learning process to be integrated with logico-scientific reasoning.

## Introduction

The growing need for innovative approaches in Clima te Change Education (CCE) has opened space for the adoption of powerful tools that enhance both scient ific understanding and learner engagement —among them, narratives. Traditionally seen as communication tools, narratives are increasingly being reinterpreted by scholars as a mode of thinking in their own right (Fletcher,  2023; Gottschall, 2013), capable of shaping how in dividuals make sense of the world. This shift is rooted in Br uner’s theory of dual modes  of thought (1986), which frames narrative and paradigmatic thinking as irreducible and complementary cognitive processes —two distinct yet equally legitimate ways of knowing and describing r eality. In this view, narratives are not merely a m eans to simplify or embellish scientific content but are foundatio nal to processes of meaning-making, particularly in contexts of complexity and uncertainty such as climate change. While narratives have demonstrated the ability to humanize scientific concepts and enhance  emotional engagement, their limitations have also b een noted — particularly their challenges in representing non-human agency, long-term dynamics, and systemic phenomena (Ghosh, 2016; Soares et al., 2024). Responding to these challenges, this study explores how Bruner’s perspective can inform a more balanced CCE approach that levera ges both narrative and paradigmatic reasoning. A ke y step in this direction involves addressing the conceptual ambiguity surrounding the term “narrative,” which is applied inconsistently across disciplines and encompasses a wide r ange of artefacts and meanings (D'Orto & Tasquier, 2025). To navigate this, we developed a theoretical  framework grounded in the literary concept of narr ativity, which identifies a hierarchical set of narrative fe atures—such as temporality, events, and genre —and organizes them into a four-layer model for characterizing CC narratives. Build ing on this model, we formulated a set of design principles, following the methodology of Lev rini et al. (2019), which guided the development of  a CCE course implemented in t wo Italian secondary school classes and served as a testing ground for examining how narratives, understood as modes of thought, can foster critical, imaginative, and action-oriented competencies.

## A theoretical framework based on narrativity

The recent surge in the popularity of the term narr ative has significantly diluted its meaning (Byung-Chul, 2024; Ryan, 2005). While many scholars have sought to ide ntify its essential features, their efforts often f all into two opposing extremes: on one hand, overly rigid defini tions that risk excluding atypical but educationall y valuable forms; on the other, overly inclusive approaches th at treat all narratives as equivalent, thus overloo king their diverse educational potential. To address this tens ion, in a previous study we developed a theoretical  framework grounded in the concept of narrativity (Ryan, 2005), which enables the evaluation of the extent to whi ch a text, activity, or artefact incorporates core narrative f eatures. This framework allows for s ystematic comparison of narrative forms and their educational function in C limate Change discourse (D'Orto & Tasquier, 2025). The resulting narrativity model is structured as a four-layer pyramid (see Fig. 1), where each layer repres ents a progressively higher degree of narrativity. Crucially, the model cr eates a spectrum ranging from paradigmatic to highly narrative artefacts and modes of thinking. In doing so, the model not only characterizes narrative artefacts but also maps their potential to mediate between narrative and logico-scientific ways of knowing. ## Methods: from a theoretical model to design principles

Drawing from our theoretical analysis, we developed  two core design principles that guided the creatio n of a Climate Change (CC) course aimed at exploring the educational potential of balancing narrative and paradigmatic modes of thinking. These principles  translate the narrativity model into pedagogical pr actice, shaping learning experiences that move beyond narratives as mere sto rytelling devices, toward narratives as cognitive t ools that complement scientific reasoning. The course, implemented in three secondary school classes in Italy (grades 11– 12), served as a context for investigating how this  integrated approach can foster deeper understandin g and promote the development of critical, imaginative, and action-oriented competencies. The two principles are: From storytelling to storythinking This principle reframes narratives not simply as to ols for engagement or communication, but as epistem ic practices that help students make sense of climate complexity. It aims to cultivate students’ ability to identify and work with narrative structures—such as temporality, agency, and genre—and to distinguish between narrative as a literary form (high narrativity) and as a mode of scientific reasoning (low narrativity). Scaffold learning through narrativity layers This principle proposes a gradual progression throu gh different levels of narrativity, allowing educators to leverage specific narrativity layers and introduce the right amount of narrative complexity  in relation to specific learning objectives. It is grounded in the concept of narrative features as boundary objects —bridging scientific and literary practices—and supports the co-development of analytical and imaginative skills essential for climate literacy.

## Conclusion

This article proposed two core principles for the design of courses that aim at  integrating Bruner’s theory of narrative and paradigmatic thinking into CCE. They were developed b y leveraging a framework that employes narrativity to categorize different types of CC artefact and position them in a spectrum in relation to the two modes of thought. Each principle aims to help educators highlight the potential for narrative  thinking to complement paradigmatic approaches, offering a more holistic p erspective on climate change. Future work could exp lore the long-term impacts of such courses on students’ competencies and sense-making.
