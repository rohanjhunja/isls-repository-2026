# In Pursuit of Creativity in Minecraft: A Mixed-Method Approach

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite Minecraft’s incredible popularity, we know very little about how the game might enable creativity. To assess and support the creativity of the structures that players create in Minecraft, we need to understand how Minecrafters describe creativity in their communities, and how similar or different those descriptions match the creativity literature. In this study, we used a modified rubric for creativity and investigated if we could find evidence for this rubric in how Minecraft builds are being evaluated in YouTube. We also used natural language processing (NLP) to develop automatic coding algorithms for future use. Insights here would inform the literature, research, and educational practices of creativity.

## Introduction

Creativity is one of the essential skills for success in life in the 21st-century (Gray, 2016; Glaveanu et al., 2020). In our ever-changing world, we need people to think, feel, and act creatively to solve new or unexpected problems (Resnick, 2018). Since the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic, creativity has been quintessential to designing effective vaccines (e.g., Tan et al., 2021) and sus taining economic infrastructures. For instance, res taurants, schools, and businesses have had to come up with creative means to remain open. Because creativity is valuable to society, attention has been placed on understanding the various ways creativity manifests in everyday life, the so-called little-c creativity (Kaufman & Beghetto, 2009; Richards, 2010)—the focus of this study. To assess littlec creativity, researchers use divergent thinking to examine creative thinking or creative potentials (Runco & Acar, 2012). Guilford (1956) operationalized divergent th inking using its four facets: flexibility (e.g., the number of categories or themes used when solving a problem or  the ability to come up with relevant ideas from di fferent categories or themes); fluency (the ability to produce a large number of relevant ideas); originality (the ability to produce statistically rare ideas); and elaboration (the ability to implement and expand on an idea in detail and high quality).

One promising medium to assess and enhance creativity is video games (e.g., Rahimi & Shute, 2021a; Shute & Rahimi, 2021). This is because video games, in their ubiquity, reach broad audiences all around the world (Rahimi & Shute, 2021). Through the often-repeated practices, people obtain creative skills that may transfer to other parts of life, thus enhancing their overall creativity in life (Green & Kaufman, 2015). Well-designed video games may enable people to practice being creative (Rahimi & Shute, 2021b). To support creativity in v ideo games, one must first accurately assess it, however, assessing creativity is challenging. For instance, assessing creativity could be subjectively in the eyes of the  beholder. Some researchers have even suggested we need to drop the idea of objectively assessing creativity (Amabile, 1996). However, others have used new asses sment techniques such as stealth assessment to objectively (i.e., automatically) assess creativity (Shute & Rahimi, 2021). Stealth assessment uses log files generated in an immersive environment (e.g., digital games) to make inferences about learners’ level of competency on the skill at hand (e.g., creativity). Before a stealth assessment is designed and embedded in a digital game environment, it is e ssential to find appropriate indicators in the asse ssment or learning environment (e.g., a digital game) to provide evidence for the competency of interest (e.g., creativity). One of the most popular games of all time is Minecraft (Gilbert, 2018). Minecraft has shown promising results in improving people’s creativity (e.g., Bla nco-Herrera et al., 2019). Minecraft includes five modes of gameplay: creative, survival, adventure, spectator,  and hardcore. In the creative mode, players usuall y create various structures (e.g., a recreation of real-world buildings, creating fantasy worlds). These structures has been showcased online via various platforms such as YouTube which consistently ranks Minecraft as one of the most popular terms searched on the platform globally (Ha rdwick, 2020). In fact, others have fruitfully leve raged YouTube to examine Minecraft productions (Walker et al., 2019) to find out how they are represented and constructed at scale. Therefore, YouTube content about Minecraft has a lot of potential for creativity researchers to investigate how creativity is manifested in this video game. To assess and support the creativity of the structu res that players create in Minecraft, we need to understand how Minecrafters describe creativity in their communities and how similar or different thos e descriptions are compared to the classical creativity definitions in the literature. Among the YouTube videos about Minecraft are some videos created to showcase and evaluate t op Minecraft constructions created by others that ICLS2022 Proceedings 1397 © ISLS could be classified as creative (e.g., top 10 const ructions in Minecraft). These videos usually have a voice-over describing why those 10 builds, for example, are am ong the top Minecraft constructions. The evaluators’ descriptions could potentially include the language used to describe creativity discussed in literature. In this study, we will focus on analyzing the evaluative videos’ transcripts to address the following research questions:

1) To what extent the YouTube-based Minecraft evaluato rs’ comments are consistent with our rubric of

creativity (see Table 1; adapted from Rahimi, 2020)?

2) Are there new facets of creativity emerging in how Minecraft content evaluators describe creative

structures on YouTube?

Insights here would extend our understanding about the ways creativity manifests in online, sandbox video games such as Minecraft; thus, enhance learning-interventions and assessment of creativity in the future.

## Method

The current study uses a combination of traditional deductive and inductive qualitative coding (Ravitch & Carl,

2019) and natural language processing (NLP) to examine the transcripts of a set of evaluative Minecraft videos

hosted on YouTube. We specifically looked for patterns of common descriptions of creative constructions in the video transcripts and categorized them into the sev en categories defined in Table 1. We also looked fo r new emerging categories if any.

Selection of videos To find the most appropriate YouTube videos, we fir st consulted with two Minecraft experts (with more than three years of experience in Minecraft), and then we came up with appropriate search terms and video selection criteria. The search terms included “Minecraft builds” “top Minecraft buildings” “best Minecraft constructions” “top ten” “top five” “Minecraft” and “creative.” Ou r search terms and inclusion criteria yielded thous ands of results on YouTube. After several iterations, we developed the criteria to only include videos that had narrations. In addition, we included videos evaluating Minecraft builds and excluded videos about competitions or about the other Minecraft modes (e.g., survival). With the criteria, each aut hor created a list of videos with the YouTube links they found. After individual screening and consulting with our Minecraft experts, we collectively selected a sample of 61 videos. Then, three authors went throu gh an additional screening to determine the duplica tes and apply the inclusion/exclusion criteria until 100% consensus was reached. The final data set included a collection of 36 videos (about 360 unique builds in our analysis). Next, we briefly explain how we extracted the transcripts. Extracting the transcripts Using YouTube’s publicly accessible application program interface (API), we wrote a code to read the YouTube URLs from a Google spreadsheet, extract each video’ s transcript, and generate a dataset with all the t ranscripts ICLS2022 Proceedings 1398 © ISLS for each video in it. The variables in that dataset included: video ID, caption (as they would appear on the screen), time stamp, and duration for each caption. Our final dataset included 14,860 lines of caption. Coding process For this study, which is a work-in-progress, we chose the first two videos in our dataset (20 builds), created three separate spreadsheets for each of the authors, and applied the codes in Table 1 to this unit of analysis. Codes were applied deductively to the transcripts. We also inductively reasoned on parts of the transcripts that could be coded as a new category but were not captured in our exis ting codebook. In doing this we identified themes t hat were then consolidated into a formal code (see Table 2). Codes were not mutually exclusive and could co-occur in each excerpt. In the first round of coding, three author s independently coded a subset of the transcripts a nd met to compare and resolve code application disagreements until 100% consensus was reached. To get a sense of code distribution for each build and across the entire sample we generated descriptive statistics in the form of frequency counts per code. In addition, we invited another au thor, who has expertise in learning analytics to he lp us understand and focus on connecting human codes with  NLP and machine learning classification algorithms. Developing an NLP model can help us automatically code transcripts of similar videos in the future at scale.

## Results

In the process of coding, we developed twonew codes: (1) effort referring to how much effort it seemed a build took to be created. For example, “almost a full year of work was put into bringing this creation to life” was coded as evidence for effort. We found ten incidents for effort in the ten builds we coded; and (2) realism which refers to how real the build was in evaluator’s view. It i s hard to create real-looking constructions using b locks in Minecraft because the scales should be accurately estimated, and it takes a lot of detail. Therefore, when a building resembles a real building, the evaluators found it as doing something creative. We found 28 incidents for realism in the ten builds we coded. The rest of the codes’ appearance are listed in Table 2. The most frequent codes were elaboration, aesthetics, surprise, and complexity. We have not found any explicit statements to be coded for humor in the ten builds that we coded. However, it is too soon to decide if we need to drop these codes as we still have about 34 videos to code.

Our preliminary analysis using NLP and machine lear ning classification algorithms revealed insights about the possibility of automated creativity evaluations. We achieved close to 70% accuracy (using 5-fold CV) to distinguish the sentences that showed evidence of creativity. Similarly, close to 90% accuracy was acquired to categorize the sentences based on the proposed dimensions of creativity. We used a logistic regression algorithm and a multi-label adapted kNN classifier with the term-frequency inverse document-frequency vectorization. ## Discussion & Conclusion

In this study, we used a modified rubric for creativity and investigated if we could find evidence for this rubric in how Minecraft builds are being evaluated in YouTube. Due to lack of time for this proposal, we have so far coded the caption related to 20 builds. We found evidence  for most of the codes or sub-facets of the rubrics  we used except for humor and novel use of materials. We also found twonew codes: effort and realism. These codes are significant because they highlight how platform-specific features shape how creativity manifests itself. In other ICLS2022 Proceedings 1399 © ISLS words, creativity codes can in many ways be general ized, however, video games and their diverse contex t may manifest creativity in different terms. Because of our small sample of builds so far (only 20 builds) we cannot make a conclusion about which code has appeared more compared to the other codes. However, it is notic eable how often surprise, elaboration, aesthetics, and realism appeared in the captions from those 20 builds. Moreover, our NLP analysis and classification algorithms could successfully identify the syntactic and semantic differences among the creativity dimension s. However, the analysis was conducted with a relat ively small set of sample sentences for both training and evaluation, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Hence, we encourage a future focus on expanding the labeled dataset to evaluate the capacities of our algorithms for automated coding.
