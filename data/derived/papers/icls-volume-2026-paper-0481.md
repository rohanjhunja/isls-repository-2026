# A Preliminary Study of Digital Tool Practices and Knowledge Building in Pre -service Teacher Learning within an Emerging Technology

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how pre-service teachers engage in digital tool practices and develop knowledge-building competencies within an Emerging Technology Applications course at a national university in Taiwan. Guided by the frameworks of Knowledge Buildi ng (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2010), reflective practice (Schön, 1983), and the Teaching Artist perspective (Eisner, 2002), the research examines how emerging technologies such as Padlet can transform teacher education into a creative and collaborative learn ing environment. Seventeen pre-service teachers participated in a three-phase design-based course: (1) personal expression and problem awareness, (2) collaborative feedback and campus inquiry, and (3) Sandbox co-creation of interactive materials integratin g SDGs or SEL. Data sources included the AI-TPACK questionnaire and Padlet discourse analyzed through theory-driven content analysis. Results show a developmental progression from individual tool use to community-oriented knowledge building, with Padlet evolving from a platform for expression to a site of co-construction and epistemic agency. The study contributes a replicable model demonstrating how digital design tasks can foster creativity, reflection, and sustained idea improvement in pre-service teacher education.

In the era of artificial intelligence and rapidly emerging technologies, teacher education faces new challenges and opportunities. Pre-service teachers are expected not only to master pedagogical techniques but also to transform technology into a medium for knowledge building—the collective creation and continual improvement of ideas within a community of learners (Scardamalia & Berei ter, 2010).Schön’s (1983) notion of the reflective practitioner highlights that professional growth emerges through an ongoing cycle of action and reflection. Drawing on the perspective of the Teaching Artist (Eisner, 2002), this study re-conceptualizes pre-service teachers as creative designers who use digital tools to craft innovative learning environments and aesthetic experiences. Within this framing, the classroom becomes a sandbox—a safe, open, and collaborative space where participants can experiment, fail, and reconstruct ideas (Zhu et al., 2022).The present study explores how pre-service teachers engage in knowledge-building practices through digital tools in an Emerging Technology Applications course. Two research questions guided the inquiry:

(1) What are the characteristics of pre-service teachers’ digital-tool practices?

(2) How do these practices foster processes of knowledge building?

The research was conducted in a course titled Applications of Emerging Technology in Teaching, offered by a College of Education at a national university in Taiwan.  Seventeen pre-service teachers, most of whom planned to teach in elementary schools, participated. Padlet served as the central platform for collaboration, enabling a three-phase progression from individual expression to communal knowledge co-construction. In phase 1 Personal Expression and Problem Awareness, the instructor introduced current examples of educational technology and asked students to articulate initial perceptions on Padlet.  At this stage, Padlet functioned as a mirror, reflecting each learner’s prior experiences and intuitive assumptions about technology in education. 2871 In phase 2 Collaborative Feedback and Campus Inquiry invited pairs of students to enact the idea of campus as curriculum.

Using mobile devices, they photographed elements of the campus that could become potential teaching resources and uploaded their observations to Padlet.  Peer responses were guided by Knowledge Building Principle Cards such as Improvable Ideas and Community Knowledge & Collective Responsibility, transforming Padlet into a bridge that linked authentic contexts with pedagogical design. In phase 3 Sandbox Co-creation and Knowledge Building Practice, each group designed an interactive learning module within a Padlet Sandbox.  Their tasks required integrating a science-related topic with an educational issue such as multiculturalism, social-emotional learning (SEL), or the SDGs, and formulating inquiry prompts inspired by selected KB principles. Groups documented the evolution of their thinking across stages, thereby turning Padlet into a space that supported equitable participation, multiple perspectives, and epistemic agency. Data sources included (1) the AI-TPACK questionnaire (Hwang et al., 2022) measuring participants’ self-perceived technological-pedagogical competence, and (2) Padlet discourse from all three phases. Qualitative analysis followed Schreier’s (2012) theory-driven content-analysis procedure, using the twelve KB principles (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2010) as the coding framework. The AI-TPACK results indicated a moderate overall level (M = 4.40).  Among sub-dimensions, Pedagogical Knowledge scored highest (M = 4.67), while Content Knowledge was lowest (M = 4.05). This pattern suggests that participants were pedagogically sensitive and comfortable operating digital tools, yet less adept at deeply integrating disciplinary content. Padlet discourse revealed a developmental trajectory from individual experience toward community-level knowledge building. Early posts were intuitive and instrumental—for instance, “AI can speed up lesson preparation” or “Digital platforms increase participation.”  During the collaborative phase, however, peer feedback began to demonstrate improvement-oriented reasoning and a sense of collective responsibility, as students commented that peers could “add steps for student discussion” or “connect with more reading experiences.” By the Sandbox stage, participants were engaging in authentic, design-based inquiry that embodied KB principles. One group transformed the campus “green wall” into a sustainability-education module asking, “If the wall plants gradually wither, what challenges would the campus face in maintaining sustainability?” Another group designed a mathematics lesson on ratio and proportion by measuring stair heights and lengths to discuss “How can we estimate building safety through measurement?” Such examples illustrate the participants’ increasing capacity to integrate real contexts, subject content, and educational issues, thus exhibiting Improvable Ideas and Community Knowledge in practice. Overall, the Padlet environment evolved from a simple sharing platform into a site of knowledge co-creation. Through iterative reflection, students gradually demonstrated epistemic agency —taking ownership of ideas, coordinating perspectives, and engaging in sustained improvement across cycles of inquiry.

The study highlights a clear developmental pattern in pre-service teachers’ digital-tool practices, progressing from individual use to collaborative and community-based knowledge building. When digital tools function merely as repositories for expression, learners’ engagement tends to remain superficial. However, when tools are intentionally designed as knowledge-building environments, learners exhibit deeper reflection, creative integration, and social responsibility.  Within this course, Padlet underwent a conceptual transformation—from mirror to bridge to space —symbolizing the participants’ movement from self-expression toward collective creation. These findings align with Chen and Hong’s (2016) conception of schools as knowledge-building organizations and demonstrate the pedagogical potential of coupling creative design with emerging technologies in teacher education. The three-phase structure combining Campus-as-Curriculum exploration and Sandbox Cocreation proved effective in enabling pre-service teachers to experience authentic design, cross-disciplinary integration, and communal inquiry within a short course. Theoretically, this study provides empirical evidence for applying knowledge-building principles within the AI-TPACK framework, illustrating how reflective design tasks can cultivate teachers’ epistemic agency. Practically, it offers a replicable model for teacher-education programs seeking to foster creativity, collaboration, and sustained idea improvement through digital tools. Future research should extend this work across different subject domains and longitudinal contexts to trace how pre-service teachers continue knowledge-building practices once they enter professional classrooms.
