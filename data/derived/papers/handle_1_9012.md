# Tinkering in Parallel: Pathways for Professional Learning in Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores professional learning that occurred during a summer program in which educators engaged with a series of tinkering activities facilitated online. Analysis revealed two primary themes: (1) generative starting points with multiple pathways for exploration, and (2) resolution of sticking points through social connections. An analytic frame of parallel process, adapted from counseling psychology, highlights the way these themes simultaneously speak to learning through tinkering and to professional development. This study explores the learning experiences of a group of STEM educators who completed an informal online professional development program in summer 2020 that featured a series of tinkering activities.

## Background

Educators must develop a skillset that is well grounded in learning theory and empirically supported pedagogical techniques, yet also flexible enough to respond to students’ needs (Athanases et al., 2020). Learning to facilitate tinkering activities is a prime example of this challenge, since these activities are open-ended and invite creative exploration rather than fixed lesson plans (Vossoughi & Bevan, 2014). The ways educators have adapted to pandemic challenges underscore the need to conceptualize learning in  terms of repertoires of practice that are flexible, responsive, and grounded in supportive pedagogical relationships (Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003).

## Research design

Data come from 23 interviews with 12 educators who engaged with a s eries of tinkering activities facilitated online by the Exploratorium: Exploring Spinning, Chain Reaction, Cranky Cont raptions, and Video Sensing. Activity prompts were introduced by email, and participants wer e encouraged to document their process and connect with others on social media. Interviews were semi-structure d and were designed to elicit narrative accounts of participants’ tinkering experiences. Analysis began with open coding to identify high-level topics that were salient across narratives; subsequent analysis elaborated u pon each code in turn. Here we focus on two crosscutting themes that emerged in the second phase of analysis:  (1) generative starting points with multiple pathways for exploration, and (2) resolution of sticking points through social connections.

## Findings

Generative starting points with multiple pathways for exp loration In all 23 interviews, educators described the need to find a personally meaningful starting point to begin engaging with each activity. Generative starting points were associated with a sense of emotional resonance and playfulness; emotions that frequently came up in participants’ narratives include d excitement, inspiration, and curiosity. Because starting points were linked with personal sense making, many participants favored prompts that were open-ended enough to leave space for creative exploration while al so providing a clear sense of direction. We believe these tinkering activities engendered fruitful learning in part because they were amenable to a variety of starting points and multiple pathways for exploration. We identified five types of starting point that generated engagement; Table 1 provides a description of each type, including observed frequency. Resolution of sticking points through social connections In 17 of the 23 interviews, educators described encountering sticking points in their exploration. These moments were characterized by intense frustration and interruption in the flow  of engagement with an activity. Sticking points typically arose when participants encountered limitations, either in the material resources available to them at home, or in their individual knowledge about a particular tool or res ource. In these moments, there was a disjunction between their original idea or vision and the implementa tion of that idea, which led to emotional overwhelm and impasse. For many participants, resolution was facilitated by social interactions, either with family members in person or with colleagues online. In these connections, participants found emotional support to persist through difficult moments. While sticking points typically arose for technical reasons, their resolution tended to be characterized not by technical troubleshooting, but by a shift in perspective and emotional regulation brought about by supportive, encouraging interactions and relational care. ## Discussion

Since our participants were educators who engaged with these acti vities for the purpose of professional development, we note that each theme discussed above may ser ve a dual analytic function, simultaneously speaking to the nature of learning through tinkering and to the nature of prof essional development. Tinkering is recognized as a valuable mode for students’ STEM learning (Resnick & Rosenbaum, 2013); here we note that it may be just as valuable for professional learning. The benefit of activities that include diverse learning pathways and ample opportunities for social collaboration may be equally ap plicable to facilitation of tinkering in STEM education and to provision of professional development. When considering educa tors as learners, it is useful to attend to both sides of their dual role in professional learning. Le arning through tinkering allows educators to explore affordances of activities with their own practices in m ind, developing a flexible pedagogical repertoire. Emphasis on adaptive practice is useful to address parallel cha llenges that arise in student learning and professional learning, such as shared challenges of adapting to pandemic constraints. In the context of counseling psychology, parallel process refers to dynamics in clinical practice that are mirrored in professional supervision (Sarnat, 2019). This concept is a useful analytic frame to concept ualize the value of tinkering for professional learning, where an educator likewise holds a dual role with respect to teaching and learning.
