# Students’ Expressions of Community Health Concerns in the Third Space in a Community-Based Science Class

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This qualitative study analyses a mechanism 7th and 8th grade Black and Latinx students used to express their understandings and knowledge of a socioscientific phenomenon when engaging in a community-based science curriculum. Findings demonstrate how students created third spaces in science learning, and provide educators with a conceptual understanding to be mindful of, and account for, when engaging in community-based science learning.

## Introduction

Science is situated in its own subculture that includes ways of knowing and expressing that are not always aligned with the social, cultural, and community-oriented conceptualizations that Black and Latinx s tudents bring to learning (Barton et al. 2008). Their ways of expressing science concepts, and language use to express those concepts, are frequently positioned as unscientific, as they do not align with hegemonic notions or util ize “academic language.” Despite research showing that students demonstrate complex ways to explain phenomenon (Brown, 2019), teachers do not always recognize these import ant sense-making moments  as demonstrating understanding. There is a need, therefore, to understand the mecha nisms that students use to explain their understandings of scientific phenomenon, specifically, to support teachers in recognizing indicators of student sensemaking and understanding in instruction.

Student sensemaking in the third space The literature has noted one such mechanism for students to demonstrate sensemaking is through the development of third spaces, or hybrid “navigational zones,” between school and home that expand the tradition al and official boundaries of school discourse by linking articulat ions of phenomenon within students’ social words and disciplinary concepts (Gutierrez et al. 1999). Within science, s tudies have claimed that in third spaces students candemonstrate their conceptual understandings in meaningful ways, b y connecting scientific concepts to community contexts  through family and community funds of knowledge (FO K), or the knowledge and skills students acquire from their households and communities (Barton & Tan, 2009; Moje et al. 2004). These FOK in the third space support students to "explore their ideas, explain their information to others, engineer/experiment solution, and extend their insights for different c ontexts” (McCurdy et al.  2020 p. 31). Informed by this body of research, this paper describes a sensemaking mechanism that Black and Latinx urban middle school students used to create third spaces, in both their social worlds and science concepts, to demonstrate their complex understandings of community socioscientific phenomenon around nutrition and food (in)justice. Therefore, I ask the question: How do urban Black and Latinx students conceptualize nutrition and food (in)justice  during the enactment of a community-based science curriculum?

## Context, methodology and methods

Data for this study w as derived from a four-year participatory design-based research study (Bang & V ossoughi,

2016) on the co-development, co-design, and co-enactment of a project-based learning (Juuti et al. 2021)

community-based science curriculum in an urban Black and Latinx middle school in Southern California. Here, I present select findings from the enactment of this curriculum online during the 2020-2021 academic year, where students investigated socioscientific issues relevant to their local community, such as food (in)justice, greenspace equity, environmental pollution, and health inequities, among others. Using a FOK theoretical framework (Moll et al. 1992) to investigate how students use their FOK as tools to integrate th eir everyday ways of knowing in disciplinary learning, when conceptualizing socioscientific issues in their community, I tracked the ways students explain these issues in the third space to bridge canonical concepts we discussed in class with their everyday life experiences. I used constant comparative analysis (Strauss & Corbin, 1998) in three rounds of coding to iteratively code field notes/classroom observations (five days per week for nine months) and student work artifacts, including work samples, final projects, reflections, and digital interactive notebooks.

## Findings

Here, I explore one conceptualization students demo nstrated, community health concerns, during a nutrition and food (in)justice unit, to show the ways they expressed community socioscientific issues in the third space. In this unit, students often interrogated the causes and negati ve effects of prominent community health concerns. For example, students drew upon their science knowledge and comm unity FOK to explain a lack of healthy food options in their local grocery stores. One student stated, “There are a lot of places that sell unhealthy food, like the markets that we usually go to. Those places have a lot of stuff that is unhealthy, like chips, cookies, bread, and some of the meats are unhealthy too.” In this example, the student drew upon their science knowledge of nutrition to determine what is and is not considered a healthy food, subsequently labeling the foods available in their local market as unhealthy. They, then, used their community FOK to draw conclusions about the i naccessibility to healthy food in their community, noting that their community market has a variety of unhealthy food options, even within food groups that we would not consider unhea lthy, such as “meat.” This student’s FOK around different types of meat allowed them to label which meats wer e considered healthy versus unhealthy, and categori ze the meats in their market as unhealthy. This conceptualization in the third space, later supported this student  in drawing conclusions as to how their community was living in a “food desert” that lacked many healthy and fresh food options.

Further, students correlated the presence of numerous fast food restaurants to  prominent and persistent health issues in the ir community by, specifically, alluding to how unhealthy food sources contribute to the perpetuation of health issues, such as obesity, diabetes, and high blood pressure in Black and Brown communities. For example, one student wrote, “when people live in a food desert, they are at risk of obesity, which can cause a lot of health issues like diabetes and high blood p ressure.” Another student supported this comment st ating, “families [who] only eat fast food... can give y ou problems like obesity and diabetes.” In these statements, the students used their scientific knowledge to draw a relationship between unhealthy food and negative health effects on the body, and used the community FOK around the large presence of fast food establishments in the community as a reason for the high rates of health issues in communities of color.

## Significance

Students’ use of FOK in the third space is a powerful knowledge tool that demonstrates disciplinary understanding in relevant contexts. In this work, I  present indicators of student FOK use in the third space to account for when determining if/how students demonstrate disciplinary learning  when connecting daily life to school science learning (Barton & Tan, 2009). Given the push for science learning environments th at acknowledge the value of students’ everyday experiences in learning contexts, this work contributes to our und erstanding of how to recognize moments of knowledge construction and demonstration when it is fashioned within multiple worlds.
