# Knowledge in New Pieces (KiNP): Exploring Diverse Areas of Contemporary Youths’ Intuitive Technosocial Knowledge

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Since Knowledge in Pieces, diSessa’s influential conceptual change framework, was introduced 35 years ago, the worlds that youth inhabit, navigate, and author have changed dramatically. New computing technologies have gained widespread use in daily personal, professional, and academic life. Social norms have broadened and shifted, making previously taboo topics a part of daily headlines and conversations. Advances in science have changed our understanding of global phenomena. Given the centrality of intuitive knowledge to the Knowledge in Piece framework, we propose that these changes to youth’s daily lives could result in similar changes to their thinking and reasoning that merit documentation. Moreover, should new intuitive knowledge pieces prove to be widespread, formal education and curricula could benefit from reflecting them. This symposium brings together researchers studying youth’s intuitive knowledge across a range of fields to share findings and organize toward a future research agenda.

Symposium overview Roughly 35 years ago, diSessa published a foundatio nal article, Knowledge in Pieces (1988), creating a  new paradigm in the field of conceptual change, especially in science education. In contrast to traditions that paralleled children’s development of scientific ideas with the  history of science (Wiser & Carey, 1983) or that d escribed learners as progressing through series of coherent theories as trained scientists do (McCloskey, 1983b, 1983a), diSessa drew on empirical studies of physics educat ion to posit  that learners have varied intuitive knowledge pieces (phenomenological primitives or p-prims) about how the world works that they pick up in the course of everyday life (e.g., “more force implies more effec t,” “forces act in balance”). The Knowledge in Piec es (KiP) framework suggests that these p-prims are highly contextual and persist through efforts to change or replace them (Smith III et al., 1994). Since its introduction, K iP has informed many subsequent studies on conceptu al change (diSessa & Sherin, 1998), including within mathematics (“Transfer in P ieces,” Wagner, 2006) and social justice (“Ideology in Pieces,” Philip, 2011). More recently, researchers have argued that KiP paired with inte raction analysis reframes competence and counters “conventi onal characterizations of learners as systematically deficit” (diSessa et al., 2016, p. 18) Given the centrality of everyday knowledge in formi ng the foundational elements of reasoning, and the radical changes in the contexts and technologies children are exposed to daily, scholars (Blikstein & Rosenbaum,

2023) have called for considerations of how the intuitive  knowledge that youth develop in the course of thei r

contemporary techno-social lives may differ from that of the youth with  whom diSessa worked 30+ years ago. How might youth’s everyday interactions with new me dia and sources of information outside of school sh ape their thinking and learning? How might such thinkin g and learning be augmented by the fact that today,  many youth spend more time on digital devices than interacting with peers “in real life”? In other words, what intuitive knowledge pieces are generated by youth in their contempo rary techno-social lives, in the multiple contexts in which they live, and how do these pieces guide conceptual development? Understanding the intuitive knowledge youth draw on as they learn new conceptual  domains and exploring the everyday experiences from  which such knowledge pieces emerge would build on complementar ities in recent KiP and interaction analysis approa ches (diSessa, et al., 2016). Should such knowledge in n ew pieces (KiNP) be identified and  validated through coordinated research programs, they could have implications for at least three audiences: educators and curriculum developers in better understanding and building on students' prior knowledge, researchers in examining educational contexts in more precise and culturally  relevant ways, and the field as a whole in concept ualizing dynamic learning spaces and practices that are attuned to students’ lived experiences. Though researchers have investigated potential new forms of knowledge across diverse disciplines, there have not yet been efforts to unite that research in  a symposium, to share findings, and to possibly or ganize a common research agenda. This symposium aims to do j ust that. Its panelists include researchers whose w ork evaluates potential new forms of knowledge from the areas of (1) sustainability, (2) computer science and machine learning, and (3) complex systems and climate scien ce. Additionally, our discussant is part of the ori ginal Knowledge in Pieces research team; his synthesis and discussion contributions will both assess propose d new knowledge pieces against the criteria of the KiP fr amework and expound on the theoretical implications  of potential updates to that framework. Candidates for KiNP in research on environmental education Emily Oswald on behalf of EMPOWER This commentary draws on an ongoing review of resea rch on environmental education and education for sustainable development, conducted with the aim of informing pedagogical design work for upper seconda ry students. The pedagogical designs deal with interdi sciplinary perspectives on sustainability and batte ries in Norway and are being developed as part of a univers ity-based interdisciplinary research project. At stake in the design work is what young people might already know  about sustainability broadly con ceived, as well as their experiences with electrified transport and their vi sions for Norway’s future energy systems. “Prior” “ naive” or “everyday” knowledge about these topics, is, in other words, highly pertinent. In response to Blikstein and Rosenbaum’s (2023) call to explore knowledge in new pieces, we offer three observations about environmental education research. First, research on environmental and climate chan ge education explicitly engages with the conceptual ch ange literature which, as the organizers of this sy mposium observe, is informed by knowledge-in-pieces as a theoretical model of knowledge (diSessa et al., 2016). Empirical studies have, for example, explored “differences be tween everyday and scientifi c conceptions of environmental issues in pricing” (Ignell et al., 2017, p. 68). En vironmental education researchers have also highlig hted the lack of–and need for–research that provides accounts of “change in conce ptions about what is socially, economicall y and environmentally desirable” (Lundholm, 2019, p. 1431), such as shifts in the desirability of inexpe nsive clothing, from desirable (lower costs to consumer) to undesirable (the result of exploitative labor pr actices). Second, studies of climate change education in formal settings have used ideology in pieces (Philip, 2011) to explore the intuitive knowledge students bring to bear in these contexts. Zummo (2022), for example, argues that American high school students “brought intuitive un derstandings related to divisiveness in society to their discussions of climate change” (p. 8), suggesting t hat students’ understanding of divisiveness reflect s “a broader ideological category of binarism” (p. 7). Third, some research on environmental education which does not draw on the conceptual change literature or refer to knowledge-in-pieces might nevertheless be explored in terms of pprims. For example, an interview study from 2012 do cumented a disconnect between young peoples’ understanding of the causes of climate change and their proposals for addressing it. Participants readily identified “the structural organization of society as the root  cause of climate change” (Kenis & Mathijs, 2012, p. 54), with references to “the growth economy” and “capitalism”. When asked about strategies for addressing climate change, however, interviewees proposed solutions centered primarily on change which c ould be affected by individuals. Kenis and Mathijs observed “if one asks about solutions, the first spontaneous answer one tends to get is not about the transformation of these structures, but individual behavior change” (2012, p. 54). These “spontane ous” and contradictory responses might warrant investigation  of p-prims related to the systemic causes of and individual responsibility for climate change.

Decoupling words and meanings: Secondary students’ sense-making of text classification models Jie Chao and Shiyan Jiang This commentary is situated in our research project  that aimed to engage and support secondary student s in learning artificial intelligence (AI) concepts and practices in the context of text classification, a type of languagebased AI model. A key component in AI education is introducing students to machine learning, the moder n approach to AI development (AI4K12, n.d.). Departing from traditional programming, machine learning does not require developers to write explicit step-by-step instructions. Instead, developers present trai ning data, or examples of the problem and solution, to a machine learning algorithm, which will build a computer model using the examples (Jordan & Mitchell, 2015). Take text c lassification models as an example. Instead of writ ing instructions on how each word should be interpreted, developers gather a large amount of text data and  let the machine learning algorithms search for patterns in the data and build models that describe how the words shoul d be interpreted (Mirończuk & Protasiewicz, 2018). This new way of comput ing requires students to shift away from the traditional programming paradigm and embrace a new paradigm of computational thinking. In our work with secondary students, we observed so me ideas that they commonly had about the development of language-based computer programs. In one of our studies, as an introduction to text classification, students were engaged in the testing and sense-making of a sentiment analysis model (StoryQ Team, n.d. -a). They were presented with a prebuilt text classifica tion model that could sort positive and negative re views for frozen dessert businesses (e.g., ice cream and frozen yogurt shops). They tested the model with their own reviews or other testing cases and speculated about how the  model worked. Our preliminary analysis showed that  many students thought that the model looked at keywords such as “love,” “slow,” “cheap,” and “good.” One student explained, “... if someone said ‘loved’ in their review, the n the model might take that and sort it into the po sitive category.” The students were right about the role o f words in developing text classification models. T his model they explored was trained on unique words extracted from the training text. However, many of them seemed to think that words ha d intrinsic meanings, and in this case, words had intrinsic positivity, negativity, or neutrality. Fo r instance, one student explained, “All of these wo rds (e.g., good, variety, and satisfied) have a positive meaning, and I think the computer is picking up on it.” Some students came up with a more sophisticated theory that the model sorted keywords into positive and negative groups a nd compared which group had more. Later, when they saw the words and their weights in the model, they found that while many words’ weights confirmed their ideas, so me words took them by surprise (StoryQ Team, n.d. -b). Many apparently neutral words like “flavors” and “p lace” had large positive weights, and some words su ch as “like” should have a positive weight actually had a lar ge negative weight. One student wrote, “I find ‘one ’ surprising because it doesn't show any clues indicating that the word is negative, also ‘came,’ how is that negative?

## Especially in the context of her review.”

Developmentally, it takes years for children to dev elop the coupling between words and meanings and the ability to decouple and recouple swiftly based on the context. For digital natives, computers migh t have become an integral part of this coupling, as they s ee words displayed on and transmitted through compu ters all the time. This word-meaning coupling is a great starting point for students to conceptualize how text classification models work. In fact, some students’ naive models, such as comparing the nu mbers of positive and negative words, mirror some classic NLP approaches that focu s on normative emotional ratings of English words (e.g., Bradley et al., 1999).

However, the modern NLP increasingly employs machin e learning techniques to deal with uncertainty and evolving natural languages. To develop a new wa y of thinking in terms of machine learning, student s must first decouple meanings from words and other symbol s used in language (e.g., punctuation, emoji, etc.). In the subsequent learning activities, students were guided to examine the words with surprising weights in their original text and came to understand that the words were mer ely symbols and their meani ngs were fluid and changeable depending on the context (StoryQ Team, n.d. -c). This decoupling appeared to be an important fou ndation for subsequent instruction on how machine learning algorithms work.

In conclusion, the coupling between symbols and meanings is a prevalent phenomenon in human society. In the digital age, it is possible that digital nat ives also incorporate the computer into the couplin g, seeing words in the digital interface as coupled w ith their meanings. It might be a new “knowledge pi ece” that AI educators should pay attention to and design learning experiences to address. High school youths’ everyday understanding of machine learning application from a knowledge in pieces perspective Luis Morales-Navarro and Yasmin B. Kafai Despite recent calls for promoting artificial intelligence literacy in K-12 education, not enough attention has been paid to studying children and youths’ everyday unde rstandings of machine learning (ML). This is partic ularly important since youth already have rich experiences with machine learning-powered apps (MLPAs) in their daily lives, utilizing them for school, gaming, music, and socializing with peers (Chang et al., 2022). As L ong and Magerko (2020) discuss in a recent scoping review, there is insuff icient empirical data to derive "a robust and accurate understanding of what existing pre-conceptions non-programmers have about AI" (p. 10). Existing research has centered either on (1) “misconceptions ” novices have about MLPAs and their development of inaccurate or simplified mental models that studies argue can be difficult to overcome in instruction (e.g., Hitron et al., 2019, Große-Bölting & Mühling, 2020), (2) on intelligence attribution addressing how young people assess MLPAs for intelligence and human-like traits (e.g., Festerling & Siraj, 2020), or (3) how youth build hypotheses with regards to how MLPAs work (e.g., Druga & Ko, 2 021) following conceptual change research (Vosniado u, 2019).

In this paper we contribute to research on youth ev eryday understandings of MLPAs by offering a knowledge-in-pieces perspective. As diSessa et al. (2004) argue,  everyday knowledge may not be coherently organized, existing “in-pieces” that can be productive for learners to understand complex phenomena such as ML. For diSessa (2018), everyday knowledge is rich and goes beyond “simple characterizations (e.g., as iso lated “misconceptions,” simple false beliefs)” (p. 68). S ince learning involves building on and rearranging existing knowledge elements, learners cannot reject their ev eryday understandings, instead, they can build from  them to develop normative understandings.

We conducted an exploratory study with 19 teens (ag es 14-16 years) in Spring 2022 at a science center located in the Northeastern United States. During the study youths in groups interacted with four MLPAs (TikTok filters, Alexa, a “smart” plushie, and hat  that recognized specific sounds) and were asked to explain how they thought these worked on big papers (Woodward, 2018). We analyzed recordings and artifacts by looking a t common themes across the data (Braun & Clarke, 2012) into rounds. A researcher who was not involved in  the study then audited the analysis (Lincoln & Guba, 19 95), making inquiries and noting areas of disagreem ent. We reached consensus on our findings through social moderation and discussion. Through our analysis we observed that youths demons trated an understanding of two important knowledge pieces: (a) that MLPAs learn from trainin g data or examples, and (b) that recognize patterns  in input data and depending on the patterns provide different outputs. Youths talked about training data or examples being used for MLPAs to “learn” and described the feature s or properties of data they thought were used. The y demonstrated some understanding of the complexity of ML systems acknowledging that a model's behavior could be attributed to the training data. This challenges  research on “misconceptions” that argue children a scribe little complexity to ML. At the same time, youth expressed  a nuanced understanding of MLPAs: being designed b y humans who provide data for the "system" to learn; this contrasts with previous research that characte rizes children as either thinking ML learns by itself or that humans create all the rules. While youth did n ot talk about models, they described that MLPAs recognized patterns derived from training data and that when the applications run data inputs are used in pattern recognition pro cedures that give outputs. These pattern recognitio n functions are an incipient idea of a model.

Our findings suggest that KiP provides a more nuanc ed perspective on what youth understand about MLPAs in their everyday lives and that this approach may be helpful to identify ways in which learning activities can build on what youth may already know. For  instance, youths’ incipient ideas of a model could be leveraged when introducing ML models in a classroom setting. The KiP perspective moves beyond studies which center on everyday theories and “misconceptions” novices have  about MLPAs (e.g., Hitron et al., 2019; Große-Bölting & Mühling, 2020; Long & Magerko, 2020) or studies which have focused on intelligence attribution focuses on how young people assess MLPAs for intelligence and huma n-like traits (e.g., Williams et al., 2019) highlight ing knowledge-pieces that can be productive for further knowledge construction. Knowledge in New Pieces (KiNP): Patchwork learning in youth’s technosocial lives Paulo Blikstein and Leah Rosenbaum Youth’s exposure tonew types of systems, natural p henomena, and forms of social interaction has incre ased dramatically in the last few decades. Some of these  new systems are technological, such as networked, internetenabled, real-time processing devices that are an extricable part  of youth’s physical and social spaces. Other complex systems arise in today’s discourse; conversations about resource use, climate change, global economics, and systemic inequity and bias, to name a few examples, are increasingly “in the air.” Youth today inhabit, explore, and reshape worlds that are increasingly large, det ailed, and interconnected. Based on these experienc es, we propose that today’s youth may have intuitive knowl edge and skills that differ radically from those o f youth 2030 years ago, on whom the foundational Knowledge in Pieces (KiP; diSessa, 1988) framework was built. We also suggest that these differences may matter for curriculum and instruction (“KiNP,” Blikstein & Rosenbaum, 2023). In addition to diSessa’s KiP framework, we explore youth’s modern technosocial lives through the lenses of science and technology studies and connected lea rning. We draw on research that studies of how peop le in general (Turkle, 1984) and children in particular think with and about tec hnology (e.g., Danovitch & Severson, 2021), including such topics as children’s understanding  of the internet (e.g., Brodsky et al., 2021), their use of voice assistants (e.g., Oranç & Ruggeri, 2021), and their trust in the authority of search engine results (e.g., Tong et al., 2022). We also draw on the construct of connected learning in which, through community-supported pursuit of personal interests, youth learn knowledge and sk ills to support their academic, civic, or career de velopment (Ito et al., 2013).

To date, we have conducted several rounds of semi-structured interviews with seven youth aged 8-14. Thematic analysis of these interviews suggest that youth develop nuanced if incomplete knowledge piece s in the course of their modern techno-social lives. For example, participants identified a range of variables that affect the growth rate of viral social media content: number of followers, influence (“famous” or “regular” person), metrics (views or reactions), and timeliness (“in the news”  or “just random”). At the same time, most participants had incomplete models of the phenomenon, stating that p osts continue to gain views indefinitely, achieving  any number of view thresholds given sufficient time. Participants also demonstrated sophisticated awareness of issues of social, economic, and ethical dynamics that shape global labor supply, with one participant describing clothing production as happening “in places like China, India” because American workers would not be willing to do that type of work. The ensuing conversation addressed issues of cost of living (“maybe not good work, but it’s a work that can provide enough”), wage theft (“there’s som e places that don’t even pay their people”), labor relations (“the people who are actually in control in those countries are often the ones with more money who are less willing to pay [their workers] five times more.”), and glob al economic dependence (“The work then gets shipped  off to different countries… The country is completely depe ndent on that.”). Another participant positioned in dustrial agriculture within historical hunting practices (“we have developed technology, whether they became outdated or are still used today, like spears … We made mammoths go extinct during the Ice Age, but they were such a reliable source of material”) and recognized trade-offs of various food production techniques (“Pasture-raised, it’s just … They're more comfortable since they're used to gras slands … and pasture-raised is more expensive because they spend so much time raising and taking care of and spending all these resources on the animals”). This participant credited YouTube ‘commentary videos’ as the main kn owledge source through which she developed these understandings.

Based on these pilot studies, we argue that, up to a certain limit, youth do know some advanced knowledge pieces even without formal instruction on them. The topics in mathematics, global trade, and industrial agriculture addressed above are typically not yet t aught in formal education to children of our participants’ ages, suggesting participants gained these intuitive knowledge pieces through first-hand experience with online content or through immersion in local or global public conv ersations, as some of  our participants report. There is also great potential for educational designs and curricu la to precisely address the missing knowledge piece s that are harder to glean from everyday experiences (e.g. balancing the efficiency of global production networks with their environmental and human impacts; the relative press ures of human hunting and climate change in causing mammoth extinction). Should ideas such as those presented above prove to be widespread among modern youth, curricula and educational programming could better respect their existing knowledge by taking these in tuitive pieces into consideration.
