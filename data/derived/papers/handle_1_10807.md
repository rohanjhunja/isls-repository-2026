# The Roles of Students in Technology-Rich Environments and the Effectiveness of Student Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how students take an active role in technology-enhanced environments, serving as co-creators and active participants in their learning, and how this involvement affects their learning outcomes. A total of fifty-one 5th-grade students participated in this study. The participants were divided into two groups: the experimental group engaged in creating multimedia resources using technology during social studies class, whereas the students in the control group learned from existing multimedia resources. In the experimental groups, participants assumed roles as active co-creators and collaborative contributors to the process of knowledge construction and curriculum development. In contrast, those in the control group functioned as consumers of multimedia resources. The involvement in creating content promoted student learning outcomes in social studies.

## Introduction

In student-centered learning, the conventional role of student s shifts from passive participants to active learne rs engaged in 'learning by doing' (Reigeluth et al., 2 016). Students are encouraged to assume the roles o f teachers, necessitating active collaboration between students  and teachers in the transformation of pedagogy (Fi elding, 2004). Bovill and colleagues (2011) assert the sign ificance of the roles of students to actively contr ibute to both curriculum design and classroom implementation, emp hasizing their roles as simultaneously active learn ers and teachers. The teacher-student relationship needs to be reevaluated, adopt ing a pedagogical partnership that positions students as teachers and is associated with high engagement and active participation (Pate et al., 2023). To enhance students’ participation, technology play s a pivotal role. The digital skills of students ca n enhance learning and knowledge construction, enabli ng them to be active co-creators (Gros & López, 2016). Despite the increasing integration of technology in  classrooms, variations exist in how teachers and s tudents interact with technology. Given the diverse approac hes to technology integration and interactions betw een students and technology, it is crucial to examine h ow students actively engage with technology as co-creators in their learning. Therefore, this study aims to inves tigate the active role of students in technology-enhanced environments, exploring how their involvement as co-creators and active participants influences their l earning outcomes.

## Theoretical background

Numerous researchers have emphasized that students go beyond being passive consumers of information and customers in the classroom. Students require critic al thinking and collaborative skills that can contr ibute to constructing knowledge and enhancing the learning environment (Bovill et al., 2011; Carle et  al., 2021; Peled et al., 2020). Classroom dynamics should involve diver se stakeholders, including actively participating s tudents in designing and implementing the curriculum, not limited to teachers alone (Pate et al., 2023). This eng agement in the co-creation of the curriculum can enhance students' se nse of ownership and responsibility (Cook-Sather & Matthews, 2021). Additionally, participating as con tent creators helps students' decision-making and prom otes their motivation (Walker & Lazar, 2022). Students need to transcend the passive information consumers and they should actively contribute to collectively shaping the curriculum content (Gray et al., 2014; Kovalain en & Kumpulainen, 2007). Moreover, teachers should estab lish partnerships with students as co-creators at the beginning stage when designing curricula (Blau & Sh amir-Inbal, 2017, 2018; Bovill et al., 2011). Bovill et al.

(2011) classified student engagement into three rol es: (1) students as co-creators of teaching approaches, (2)

students as co-creators of course design, and (3) students as co-creators of curricula. The concept of students as co-creators of teaching approaches involves students p laying dual roles as both learners and teachers (Bovill et al., 2011).

In addition, several studies have underscored the i mportance of technology in broadening students’ participation to actively engage in their learning process, playing a vital role in student-centered classrooms (Gros & López, 2016; Reigeluth et al., 2016).  However, technology does not simply contribute to h igh participation uniformly, as the dynamic and interaction between t echnology and students can be diverse. In the techn ologyenhanced environments, Romero et al. (2016) classif ied student engagement and roles. They introduced t he Passive-Participatory model, a spectrum from simple to comp lex, reflecting the level of students' engagement. This model proposes five hierarchical lev els of ICT usage: (1) passive consumer ICT usage, (2) interactive consumer ICT usage, (3) individual content creation  usage, (4) collaborative content co-creation, and (5) participatory knowledge co-creation usage oriented toward problem understanding within learning communities. The first two levels, passive and interactive usage, are considered "multimedia consumerism," where students interact with technology based on predefined option s. The fourth and fifth levels require more active roles and participation for students in the collaborative process by bu ilding collective knowledge and creating content (Romero et al., 2016).

With the Passive-Participation model by Romero et al. (2016) as the theoretical framework, we compared two classes where students participated in collabor ative content co-creation while another group of participants was consumers of extant resources. The goal of this study is to investigate how the co llaborative process of creating instructional resources using technology i mpacts students’ learning outcomes. In other words, the study addresses the following research question: Does the  process and production  of multimedia resources created by students through technology-enhanced collaboration foster academic achievement for students?

## Participants and context

This study involved fifty-one fifth-grade students. The participants were divided into two groups: the experimental group (25 students) engaged in creating multimedia resources using technology during social studies class, while the control group (26 stude nts) learned from ready-made videos, such as history documentaries and hist orical movie trailers available on YouTube and various fre e educational websites. In the experimental group, students played active roles as co-creators and collaborative contributors to knowledg e construction and curriculum development, while the control group predominantly served as consumers of multimedia resources.

## Procedure

The subject of the research was social studies, particularly history class. In the experimental group, students were divided into small teams of four or five members, w ith the freedom to choose historical events through  group discussions. The selection of video genres was dive rsified based on students' interests, including sho rt drama, documentary, and talk show formats. To create these videos, students initiated a comprehensive research process, exploring the historical background, insights, and relevance of selected events to modern society. The y then developed scripts and practiced before filming. The  filming was done using smartphones or tablets. Onc e the students completed their video creation, they shared these multimedia resources. Every week, one group presented their short videos as instructional resources, contributing to the understanding of social studies content. The class collectively watched the student-created content as part of the learning process throughout the entire semester. On the other hand, the control group of students watched extant videos through YouTube and educational websites as their learning resources.

Data sources and analysis To investigate students’ academic achievement, seve n subject experts and experienced teachers collabor atively developed assessments. The assessments were piloted and then revised for the final version. These tests consisted of twenty items, comprising both multiple-choice and open-ended questions, with a total possible score of 100 points each. Students took pre-and post-assessments. To compare the experimental and control groups, ANCOVA was employed for the data analysis.

## Implications and conclusion

This study examined the effectiveness of classroom resources created by learners using technology as co-creators of the curriculum by comparing students who simply consumed traditional videos through educational platforms. Student involvement in content creation influenced learning outcomes. The study reinforces the positiv e impact of student-created resources on academic success. Merely watching videos and utilizing multimedia resources do not guarantee the effectiveness of student learning. Students are more likely to engage in multimedia content and internalize it when the content is related to them.  It highlights the significance of considering stud ents' characteristics and interests for effective engagem ent and learning. When technology is integrated in to student learning, teachers need to encourage students to  be active producers of knowledge in the technology-enhanced learning environment rather than being passive ICT consumers (Morellato, 2017). As highlighted by Cook-Sather and Matthews (2021), taking part in the co-creation of the curriculum enhances students' sense  of responsibility and ownership. Consequently, the sense of ownership and high engagement positively impact students’ learning. Hence, educators should motivate students to contri bute to curriculum content creation, encouraging th eir active involvement in the learning process.

However, the study acknowledges limitations, such a s different teachers in each group. Future research should explore the impact of a single teacher instructin g multiple groups and assess the approach's effecti veness across various subjects and grade levels. Additionally, the study's findings are limited by relatively small sample sizes for different academic performance levels, warranting larger and more diverse samples for a comprehensive examination of student-created multimedia resource effectiveness.
