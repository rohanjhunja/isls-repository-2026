# Shame in Motion: Emotional Mimicry and the Influence of Perfectionism and Imposter Phenomenon

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigated how dispositional shame proneness, in conjunction with related traits of perfectionism and imposter phenomenon modulates emotional mimicry of positive and negative facial expressions. Emotional mimicry is central to social functioning, and self-conscious emotions like shame were hypothesized to influence mimicry responses. Participants completed three trait scales and viewed 46 static images (26 negative, 16 positive, and 4 neutral) while iMotions recorded mimicry. Contrary to the hypothesis, shame proneness was not related to differences in emotional mimicry between positive and negative stimuli. This lack of effect is discussed within the social-functional mimicry framework. We argue that static, non-interactive stimuli fa il to engage the social-regulatory system that shame proneness typically influences.

Understanding the mechanisms underlying emotional mimicry is essential for advancing our knowledge of how individuals perceive, interpret, and respond to others’ emotions in social contexts. Emotional mimicry – the automatic and often unconscious imitation of another person’s facial expressions – plays a central role in empathy, affiliation, and social cohesion (Hess & Fischer, 2013; Neidenthal, 2007). However, emerging evidence suggests that individual differences in self-conscious emotions, such as shame, may modulate these processes. Individuals high in shame proneness tend to experience heightened sensitivity to social evaluation and negative affect (Tangney & Dearing, 2002), which may bias their attention toward, and increase mimicry of negatively valanced emotions (Tangney & Robins, 2004). Investigating this relationship offers a critical opportunity to elucidate how maladaptive self-conscious emotions influence interpersonal functioning and emotional contagion. These emotional processes are particularl y relevant in academic learning environments, where students continuously rely on social-emotional cues to guide participation, collaboration, and motivation (Pekrun & Linnenbrink-Garcia, 2012). Disruptions in emotional mimicry may hinder cooperative learning and classroom engagement by impairing social adaptation and rapport, which are central to effective peer and instructor interaction (Wentzel, 2014).

## Theoretical framework

Chameleon effect The chameleon effect (Chartrand & Bargh, 1999) refers to the unconscious mimicry of another person’s posture, mannerisms, facial expressions, and other behaviors during social interactions. This automatic imitation occurs without deliberate intention or awareness. The effect is largely reflective and not dependent on emotional meaning. The underlying mechanism is thought to involve perception-behavior links, in which perceiving an action activates a corresponding representation in the observer’s motor syste m, making them more likely to reproduce that behavior without intention or awareness (Lakin et al., 2003). Social-functional emotional mimicry According to Hess and Fischer (2013), emotional mimicry serves a social-functional purpose, facilitating affiliation, empathy, and social understanding. Individuals unconsciously mimic other’s emotional expressions not merely as a reflexive motor response but as a communicative signal that fosters social connection. Mimicry acts as a form of emotional alignment, helping individuals coordinate affective states within social interactions (Hess & Fischer, 2014). When a person mirrors another’s smile, frown, or  look of concern, it communicates understanding and solidarity, reinforcing social bonds and mutual trust. This process is context-dependent, people are more likely to mimic those they identify with or whose approval they seek, highlighting mimicry’s role in group cohesion and relationship maintenance (Hess & Fischer 2013). Hess and colleagues emphasize that mimicry 1562 is not a universal or automatic reaction but is modulated by social motives and context (Hess & Fischer, 2017). For example, individuals may suppress mimicry when interacting with out-group members or during competitive situations, as emotional alignment w ould not serve the underlying social goal. Thus, Hess’ theory reframes emotional mimicry from a purely perceptual-motor phenomenon into a strategic, socially-embedded process, one that underscores emotion’s central role in human communication and social coordination. While both the chameleon effect and Hess’ theory of social emotion mimicry involve behavioral imitation, Hess’ model differs in the intentionality, emotional specificity, and social function. The chameleon effect describes automatic behavioral mimicry that facilitates general social cohesion, whereas Hess and Fisher’s (2013; 2014) framework emphasizes emotionally meaningful mimicry that serves communicative purposes. Emotional mimicry, in Hess’ view, is context sensitive and modulated by social goals. In contrast, the chameleon effect operates more universally, occurring across diverse contexts. Thus, which both theories underscore mimicry’s role in social connection, Hess’ theory situates mimicry within the domain of emotion communication and social regulation, whereas the chameleon effect reflects a broader automatic process. Sample The sample consisted of 73 undergraduate students from a private, liberal arts university located in the southern United States. Participants were recruited from introductory  behavioral sciences courses using non-probability sampling and were given extra credit for their participants. Instrumentation Multidimensional perfectionism scale The Multidimensional Perfectionism Scale (MPS) is a widely used psychological assessment designed to measure perfectionism as a multifaceted personality trait rather than a single construct. Developed by Hewitt and Flett (1991), the scale assesses both adaptive and maladaptive aspects of perfectionism across multiple dimensions. The Hewitt and Flett version focuses on self-oriented perfectionism (setting high personal standards), other-oriented perfectionism (holding unrealistically high expectations of others), and socially prescribed perfectionism (perceiving that others demand perfection). The MPS and its three subscales have adequate levels of reliability and validity (Hewitt et al., 1991).

Clance IP scale The Clance Imposter Phenomenon Scale “was developed to measure the concept that individuals are successful by external standards but have an illusion of personal incompetence” (Clance, 1985). The IP scale consisted of 20 5-point Likert scale items with response options ranging from 5 (strongly agree) to 1 (strongly disagree). The Clance IP scale has been shown to have high internal consistency reliability, discriminant validity, and construct validity (Chrisman et al., 1995). An example question is: “I can give the impression that I’m more competent than I really am.” TOSCA-3 The TOSCA-3 (Tangney & Dearing, 2002) was developed to measure guilt-proneness, shame-proneness, proneness to externalization, and proneness to unconcern. The TOSCA-3 consists of 15 scenario-based situations that test takers may encounter daily. Following each scenario, test takers are asked to rate the likelihood of reacting to each option on a five-point scale. The TOSCA-3 has demonstrated acceptable reliability for both the shame-proneness (α = 0.76) and guilt-proneness (α = 0.66) scales. iMotions iMotions (2023) is a multimodal biometric research platform that integrates data from a wide range of sensors to capture real-time emotional, cognitive, and physiological responses. The software synchronizes inputs such as eye tracking, facial expression analysis (Affectiva or FACET), galvanic skin response (GSR), heart rate, electroencephalography (EEG), and behavioral metrics, providing a comprehensive understanding of participant engagement and affective states. iMotions offers a powerful tool for examining implicit emotional responses that traditional self-report measures may overlook, making it highly suitable for advancing affective and behavioral science research. For the purpose of the current study, iMotions includes a powerful emotion recognition module that uses automated facial expression analysis to detect and classify emotional responses in real time. Through 1563 integrated computer vision and machine learning algorithms (i.e., Affectiva), the platform maps subtle facial muscle movements to scientifically validated emotional categories such as joy, anger, fear, disgust, sadness, and surprise, as well as valence and arousal metrics. This allowed us to capture dynamic, nonverbal emotional reactions.

## Procedure

Following the informed consent, participants completed the following three assessments, Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale, Multidimensional Perfectionism Scale, and the Test of Self-Conscious Affect. Participants were then read the following instructions, “During this portion of the experiment you will view a presentation containing images of different emotional expressions. Please pay attention during the entirety of the presentation. Your task will be to decide whether you think the emotional is positive or negative. Please rest your hands on the desk while holding one clicker in each hand and indicate your selection using the labeled clickers, “negative” and “positive.” The entire presentation takes approximately six minutes. Please press the call button at the conclusion of the presentation. Thank you again for your participants. Please let me know if you have any questions before we begin.” The stimulus presentation utilized a visual array comprising 46 images categorized by emotional valence (26 negative, 16 positive, 4 neutral). Presentation time was rigidly controlled across all trials and participants to ensure uniform exposure duration. To minimize carry-over effects and isolate each stimulus, a blank inter-stimulus interval slide separated every emotional image. The total  presentation time for the experimental phase was approximately six  minutes. Participants were then debriefed and permitted to leave. All audio and video was recorded for subsequent analysis.

To explore if any differences existed with emotional mimicry between individuals with high and low shame proneness, a series of independent sample t-tests was conducted. No significant differences were found in the amount of positively valenced expressions displayed by low shame-prone individuals (M = 21.86) compared to high shame-prone individuals (M = 22.22) while viewing positively valenced emotional slides, t (72) =.037, p =.971. Furthermore, no significant differences were found in the amount of negatively valenced expressions displayed by low shame-prone individuals (M = 94.99) compared to individuals with high shame proneness (M = 54.36) while viewing negatively valenced emotional slides, t (72) = -.727, p =.46 (See Figure 1). According to Hess and Fischer’s (2013; 2014) social-functional framework, emotional mimicry is not an automatic motor response but a goal-directed social behavior that depends on contextual cues, social relevance, and interactional intent. Because static photographs lack social interaction and dynamic emotional context, they do not communicate social intent, relational goals, or opportunities for affiliation. Consequently, the absence of real-time social feedback means there is no motivation for emotional alignment, empathy signaling, impression management—the functions that would normally modulate mimicry in Hess’ model. Thus, both high and low shame-prone individuals showed similar, minimal mimicry while viewing the static, non-interactive stimuli because the social regulatory system that governs emotional mimicry is simply not engaged. From the perspective of the chameleon effect (Chartrand & Bargh, 1999), mimicry occurs largely automatically and nonconsciously, driven by perception-behavior links. However, even within this model, the presence of an interaction partner is critical. The chameleon is strongest in live, reciprocal exchanges, where behavioral mirroring prompts rapport (Chartrand & Bargh, 1999). Static photographs, by contrast, lack the interpersonal contingency that elicits mimicry. Therefore, differences in disposition, such as shame proneness, did not meaningfully modulate responses; the perceptual system received insufficient social input to trigger mimicry.

Both theories converge on the idea that contextual and social engagement cues are essential for emotional mimicry. The absence of dynamic, reciprocal interaction likely suppressed the social function that differentiates high and low-shame-prone individuals. In live or evaluative contexts—where shame-prone participants might fear negative judgment —Hess’ model would predict reduced mimicry as a self-protective strategy. But, in static, impersonal settings, the social regulation system is offline, making mim icry uniformly low across individuals. Lastly, the multiple regression analysis strongly supported the theoretical links among these self-conscious traits, with the overall model significantly predicting 41% of the variance in shame proneness (R 2 =.41, p<.001). Impostor phenomenon emerged as the dominant positive predictor (β  =.68, p<.001), underscoring the powerful relationship between chronic feelings of intellectual fraudulence and global self-condemnation. Furthermore, while the impostor phenomenon link was robust, the significant negative predictive relationship observed for socially-prescribed perfectionism (β = -.26, p =.021) suggests that its unique contribution to shame vulnerability is more nuanced than a simple direct path and warrants further investigation into potential mediating mechanisms.
