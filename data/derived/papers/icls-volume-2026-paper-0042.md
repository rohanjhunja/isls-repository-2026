# Making First Graders’ Reasoning in Life Science Visible: Design Considerations for NGSS-Aligned Formative Assessments

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how first graders reason about key life science concepts (structure–function and biomimicry, behavior, and inheritance) through NGSS-aligned formative assessment tasks and how design features support or hinder their reasoning. Guided by Learning Progressions and Evidence-Centered Design frameworks, we developed nine formative assessment tasks and rubrics to capture first graders’ reasoning and understanding across developmental levels. Two experienced teachers implemented the tasks in individual accountability and small-group formats. Results revealed that most first graders demonstrated emerging causal reasoning when supported by multimodal representations and collaborative settings, though linguistic and visual demands sometimes constrained performance. Results also indicate that task design features, such as use of videos, familiar contexts, and oral or drawing-based responses, can enhance accessibility and reveal reasoning otherwise masked by literacy limitations. Findings contribute preliminary design considerations for developmentally and linguistically responsive formative assessments in early life science learning contexts.

Life science concepts are among the most important domains of science for developing scientific literacy, as they enable individuals to make informed decisions about socio-scientific issues such as genetically modified foods and antimicrobial resistance (Berendonk et al., 2015; Knott & Doudna 2018). Recognizing their importance, the Next Generation Science Standards  (NGSS; NGSS Lead States, 2013) include key life science concepts even in early elementary grades. In first grade, the NGSS performance expectations (PEs) emphasize three major conceptual areas: (1) structure-function and biomimicry relationships, (2) animal behaviors related to survival, and (3) inheritance and variation of traits. These PEs not only address disciplinary core ideas  (DCIs) but also require students to engage in science and engineering practices  (SEPs) and apply crosscutting concepts  (CCCs), reflecting a shift toward three-dimensional learning. This shift introduces additional complexity--and opportunity-in how young students demonstrate their reasoning about life science phenomena. Concepts like biomimicry, animal behavior, and inheritance are inherently abstract for first graders. Most research on early elementary science learning has centered on earth science topics --such as celestial movement and daylight patterns —which have guided instruction and assessment design (Plummer, 2009; Vosniadou & Skopeliti, 2017). In contrast, studies on young children’s reasoning about life science ideas like inheritance or biomimicry largely involve older students, especially in third grade and beyond (Cisterna et al., 2019; Coban & Costu, 2023; Kibuka-Sebitosi, 2007; Zudaire et al., 2024). Few studies have examined first graders’ understanding of these concepts or, from a learning progressions perspective (Duncan & Rivet, 2018), how their ideas align with NGSS PEs.

Understanding first graders’ reasoning poses unique challenges because of their developing language and literacy skills, which can limit how they express and demonstrate scientific understanding (Brown & Ryoo, 2008; Lee et al., 2013). Therefore, assessments for this age group must be carefully designed to elicit valid evidence of reasoning. Billman et al. (2021) argue that high-quality assessments for young science learners must recognize first graders as novice learners—children who are just beginning formal schooling, developing general literacy skills, and learning to use scientific language to make sense of the world. At this stage, physical, social, and linguistic growth all influence how students demonstrate proficiency in the PEs. Because science and literacy are interwoven and synergistic, NGSS-aligned assessments must address both (Billman et al., 2021). Assessment tasks that rely heavily on independent reading or writing may underestimate students’ conceptual understanding, conflating literacy skills with science knowledge (Hobbs et al., 2012). From a learning progressions perspective, students’ understanding develops along a continuum rather than being simply right or wrong (Corcoran et al., 2009; Duncan & Rivet, 2018). Collecting evidence across this continuum is critical for targeted instruction and assessment. Nobes et al. (2023) highlight that scaffolds in assessments are essential for children to show their full knowledge and skills; without scaffolds, interpretations 330 of student responses may be incomplete. In life science, assessment design must therefore account for language, representations, and response formats (e.g., verbal, gestural, or drawn), as well as item features such as the organism type used (Nehm & Ha, 2009), which can influence how students respond.

In this paper, using NGSS-aligned formative assessment tasks, we explore how first-grade students demonstrate conceptual reasoning about life science phenomena and which task design features enable or hinder that reasoning. Findings from this study aim to inform the design of developmentally and linguistically appropriate formative assessments for early science learning. The following research questions guide the study:

1. What do first graders demonstrate about their understanding of key life science concepts (i.e., structure-function-biomimicry, animal behaviors, and inheritance) through NGSS-aligned formative assessment tasks, and what challenges do they face in articulating their reasoning?

2. What design features of formative assessment tasks support or hinder students’ ability to reason about key

life science concepts?

## Theoretical perspectives

This study is grounded in theories of learning progressions and an evidence-centered design framework for developing assessments. Together, these perspectives conceptualize learning as a developmental process and assessment as a tool for making that understanding and reasoning as parts of learning visible through carefully designed evidence structures.

Learning progressions and early life science reasoning Learning progressions  frame science learning as a developmental process in which students’ reasoning and understanding of core ideas and practices grow more sophisticated through experience and instruction (NRC, 2007; Duschl et al., 2011; Duncan & Rivet, 2018). From this view, learning is gradual, coherent, and shaped by instruction and assessment (NRC, 2007). Learning progressions  help interpret students’ responses not as right or wrong but as points along a continuum from initial to canonical conceptions (Corcoran et al., 2009; Duncan & Rivet, 2018). This continu um is “mediated by targeted instruction and curriculum and are not developmentally inevitable” (Duncan & Rivet, 2018, p. 423). Thus, learning progressions  form a foundation for formative assessment (Black & Wiliam, 1998), particularly in bottom-up approaches that iteratively design instruction and assessments based on evidence of students’ reasoning (Duschl et al., 2011). In early science learning, young students reason about phenomena in concrete, emergent ways, often using drawings, gestures, or oral talk rather than abstract or written explanations (Metz, 1995; Lemke, 1990; Brown & Ryoo, 2008). Learning progressions  offer a framework for tracing this reasoning as it develops from descriptive to relational and causal forms. Linguistic and representational demands can obscure  children’s conceptual understanding, as their scientific ideas often exceed their expressive language abilities (Brown & Ryoo, 2008; Lee et al., 2013). Within a lens of learning progressions, these challenges are viewed as developmental expressions of reasoning, highlighting the importance of multimodal assessments that let children show understanding through varied modes (drawing, manipulation, oral explanation). This study examines  how design features—visuals, multimodal prompts, and collaboration—make young learners’ reasoning visible along developmental trajectories in life science.  Particularly, in this study, learning progressions were used as a conceptual lens to frame rubric levels developmentally (e.g., emerging to more sophisticated reasoning) instead of as empirically validated trajectories of student learning. Designing assessments through an evidence-centered design framework We coupled the learning progressions framework with the Evidence-Centered Design (ECD) framework (Mislevy & Haertel, 2006; Riconscente et al., 2015) to guide the design and development of NGSS-aligned formative assessments for early life science concepts—specifically, structure-function and biomimicry relationships, animal behaviors, and inheritance. The central tenet of the ECD framework is that assessment design should begin with a clear specification of the knowledge and practices to be assessed, as well as the types of eviden ce that would demonstrate students’ understanding. This process involves unpacking the three NGSS life science PEs to identify not only their three-dimensional components —DCIs, SEPs, and CCCs —but also the linguistic and literacy demands expected for students at this developmental level. The unpacking process involves deconstructing each PE into smaller, more measurable components known as learning performances (LPs) and developing corresponding task templates that make assessment design decisions explicit. These design templates help ensure that assessment developers deliberately select representations, scaffolds, and response formats that allow for students to express their understanding and what they can or cannot do in multiple ways. In our development, these  decisions include incorporating multimodal representations—such as videos, images, and drawing tasks —to reduce language barriers and using small-group 331 task formats to promote idea sharing and scaffold oral reasoning. These design choices, in turn, may support alignment between assessment tasks and students’ cognitive, linguistic, and socioemotional development. The ECD framework has been effectively adapted in several science assessment initiatives, including the Next Generation Science Assessments for Young Scientists (NGSA-YS; Billman et al., 2021; Harris et al., 2019). Building on this work, we emphasize the importance of using familiar, engaging contexts that connect to students’ lived experiences and highlight relationships between nature and the real world. Cultur al sensitivity is also essential to ensure assessment content is accessible and meaningful for all learners. To address this, Billman et al.

(2021) further recommend using everyday language and familiar visuals to help young children express their

understanding and engage with tasks. However, while such design elements can support learning, research shows they can also constrain performance (NRC, 2014; Brown & Ryoo, 2008; Billman et al., 2021). Poorly chosen visuals or tasks with heavy literacy or numeracy demands may obscure rather than reveal students’ reasoning. This study therefore examines both supportive and constraining des ign features to refine design considerations for formative assessments that better capture and scaffold young learners’ developmental understanding of life science concepts.

Formative assessment tasks We applied the ECD framework to develop nine  formative assessment tasks used to measure first graders’ understanding of life science concepts aligned with three NGSS PEs. 1-LS1-1: Use materials to design a solution to a human problem by mimicking how plants and/or animals use their external parts to help them survive, grow, and meet their needs. 1-LS1-2: Read texts and use media to determine patterns in behavior of parents and offspring that help offspring survive. 1-LS3-1: Make observations to construct an evidence-based account that young plants and animals are like, but not exactly like, their parents. The articulated LPs guided the development of formative assessment design patterns —blueprints describing how to build an assessment task that gathers specific evidence about what students know and cando. These design patterns specify the focal knowledge, skills, and abilities (FKSAs) to be measured, the observable evidence demonstrating KSAs, and the task features that enable students to produce such evidence. Each design pattern also identifies non-focal KSAs, such as reading comprehension, that might influence student performance, and included guidance on adjusting task demands, defining expected products, and establishing rubrics. Design patterns incorporated language and content considerations to ensure developmental and linguistic accessibility. These included controlling video or text length, simplifying language and vocabulary, and using visuals that support reasoning. To promote cultural sensitivity, tasks incorporated supports for any phenomena that might be novel to some students to prevent unfamiliarity from masking understanding. These components aimed to measure intended constructs rather than literacy or background knowledge. Following these specifications, we developed nine formative assessment tasks—f ive individual accountability tasks (students work individually) and four small-group (students work in small groups)  formats (Llosa et al., 2021) —along with 332 teacher guides for consistent implementation and rubrics described in the Data Analysis section. ## Participants

Two first-grade teachers (pseudonyms Cindy and Mariam) —from two different school districts in two different U.S. states—administer ed nine unique formative assessment tasks  (see Table 2 for administered tasks). Of the nine, Cindy administered four tasks and Mariam implemented seven tasks, and two of these tasks overlapped in the two teachers’ administrations. Teachers selected tasks that best aligned with their instructional contexts and ongoing science topics. Both were experienced educators with over a decade of teaching experience (11 and 25 years, respectively).

Cindy taught science twice a week (approximately 90 minutes total) and typically used formative assessments informally without assigning scores to students. Mariam taught science daily (about 160 minutes per week) and typically integrated formative assessment as part of regular instruction without assigning individual scores. Cindy administered two small-group and two individual accountability tasks, with group sizes ranging from 4 to 15 students. Mariam administered three small-group and four individual accountability tasks, with group sizes ranging from 3 to 28 students. Table 2 summarizes the tasks and the number of students who participated in each. We were not able to collect student level demographic data; therefore we could not look into how individual student characteristics may have related to their responses. ## Data analysis

We developed rubrics for each question within every formative assessment task. Each rubric categorized student responses into three levels of understanding—Green, Yellow, and Orange—reflecting FKSAs and increasingly sophisticated reasoning rather thandichotomous  right/wrong judgments. Green response category demonstrates explicit and accurate causal connections aligned with the F KSAs in the PE. Yellow response category demonstrates partial or implicit connections (e.g., correct identification of features but incomplete  structure333 function reasoning). Orange response category  shows an emerging understanding, such as descriptive observations without explicit connections required by the FKSAs in the PE. Therefore, the rubric levels captured qualitative differences in reasoning along a developmental continuum. To address the first research question,  we conducted descriptive analyses of students’ responses to identify patterns in their understanding and reasoning challenges. To address the second research question, we examined the task characteristics to determine which design features supported or limited students’ opportunities to demonstrate their reasoning and conceptual understanding. This section presents findings addressing the two RQs, organized according to the three NGSS PEs that guided the design of our formative assessment tasks. We use these PEs—1-LS1-1 (Structure and Function: Biomimicry), 1-LS1-2 (Behavior: Patterns of Survival), and 1-LS3-1 (Inheritance: Similarities and Differences between Parents and Offspring)—as anchors for reporting student understanding and reasoning challenges (RQ1), as well as task design features that supported or hindered reasoning (RQ2). Within each PE, we describe (a) how students 334 demonstrated understanding of the targeted life science concepts, (b) common conceptual challenges observed, and (c) features of task design influenced reasoning. This organization highlights how task design features grounded in the ECD framework elicited evidence of student reasoning about life science phenomena. RQ1. First graders’ understanding of key life science concepts Students were able to demonstrate understanding of concepts related to structure and function, biomimicry, survival patterns, and similarities and differences between parents and offspring. We found that there were some differences between student behavior based on the students’ knowledge of the animal or plant used in the task. 1-LS1-1 — Understanding of biomimicry in designing solutions to human problems.  In the Wings and Things task we found that nearly all students were able to recognize human-made objects that serve functions similar to animal parts—especially in the context of a bird (96%) and even outside that context (89%). However, subsequently when students were shown a picture of a turtle and asked to draw or describe a human-made object that works like a turtle shell, only about 25% succeeded. Half of the students (50%) produced structures with correct forms but unclear or mismatched functions, while the remaining one-fourth (25%) struggled to connect the turtle’s parts with human-made analogs. This demonstrates that while students are able to recognize and match functions to animal parts they may struggle to come up with examples on their own. 1-LS1-2 — Understanding animal survival patterns. Across two tasks we found that most students were able to accurately gather information from both video and text to describe how animal parents respond to their offspring’s needs (e.g., protection, feeding). This was consistent in both small-group and individual settings. For example, in the individual task Help the Baby, 93% of students successfully identified parental responses. However, performance dropped slightly (to 79-86%) when students were asked to identify, from video and text, the behavioral signals animal offspring give to parents. Some students (14-21%) appeared to struggle with recognizing relevant signal patterns from animal offspring. Instead, they sometimes focused on offspring behaviors that did not prompt a parental response (e.g., sleeping in the ne st). Despite these challenges, students generally demonstrated a strong understanding that offspring communicate their needs (e.g., chirping, moving to the center of a group) and that such signal s trigger specific parental behaviors. One major take-away from these tasks was that students were able to express their understanding through drawings  and oral explanation. In the Baby Survival task, for instance, students accurately drew and described how buffalo parents respond to danger by circling around their offspring to protect them (Figure 3). Providing students with a method other than writing may allow students who struggle with writing to demonstrate their understanding. Figure 3 Examples of Students’ Drawings of Buffalo Parents’ Reactions to Dangers, with Descriptions Written by Teachers from Students’ Verbal Explanations 1-LS1-3 — Observing similarities and differences between parents and offspring. Across tasks, students were generally successful in identifying both similarities and differences between parents and offspring. They frequently noted observable features such as size, color, fur or markings, and flower or leaf patterns. Students showed a higher rate of success in recognizing similarities in animals (rabbits 61%, dogs 73%, and cats 100%) than in plants (peas 67%, marigolds 53%). However, between 27% and 40% of students struggled to base their answers on the images provided and had difficulty distinguishing which features were truly similar versus different. Instead, some relied on inference or prior knowledge. For example, when shown a rabbit parent and its offspring and asked to describe similarities in the picture, 28% of students responded with “they both eat carrots,” an answer not supported by the visual evidence. This demonstrated that students still struggle to separate out their prior knowledge with what is provided to them in the task. Additionally, while most students demonstrated an understanding that offspring resemble but are not identical to their parents, many struggled to clearly articulate this reasoning in writing or when selecting accurate explanations in individual tasks. Although the multiple-choice options included correct explanations, only about 335 half of the students (53% which is still higher than performance in writing task) demonstrated an understanding that parents and offspring are alike but not identical. The remaining students either selected incorrect responses or did not provide accurate reasoning, indicating that this higher-level reasoning was challenging for them. RQ2. Design features of formative assessment tasks that support or hinder students’ reasoning about life science concepts We found that the features of the task might impact student’s ability to engage with it. Features that may enhance accessibility and reveal reasoning otherwise masked by literacy limitations  included use of videos, familiar contexts, and oral or drawing-based responses.

1-LS1-1 — Identifying structure and function in plants and animals. Across the three tasks we found that most first graders were able to identify the external parts of plants (92%) and animals, such as birds (79%), especially when supported with a video or static image. Visual representations also helped students recognize the functions of these external structures. For example, in the My Strong House task (Figure 1), all students were able to identify the functions of different types of roots after watching a video. While student engaging in the Wings and Things task were also mostly (82%) able to identify the functions of a bird’s external parts after observing an image, there was a difference in performance between these two tasks. This may be because the plant task included a video and was conducted in small groups, allowing for discussion, whereas the animal  tasks used pictures and were implemented in an individual accountability  setting. Overall, these findings highlight how visual representations is a task design feature that supports students’ ability to identify structure-function relationships in plants and animals.

1-LS1-1 — Understanding of biomimicry in designing solutions to human problems.   As mentioned above, students struggled to identify a function for an object inspired by a turtle shell when writ ing their explanations and working independently. However, in another individual accountability task—the Rabbits in the Garden task —most students (82%) correctly selected how a solution inspired by cactus spines addressed the problem of garden pests  when students were not asked to write but instead to select explanations. Near ly all students (92%) also chose the correct explanation for why designing a solution based on the function of plant parts was an appropriate approach.

Figure 4 Students’ Drawings of a House with Pillars/Foundations/Basements Rooted in the Soil When students were given opportunities to discuss their designs in small groups, their reasoning improved noticeably. After learning about the relationship between structure and function in deep roots, students were asked to design a house with pillars, foundations, or basem ents extending into the soil. Nearly all students (92%) completed this task successfully (Figure 4). It is also possible that b eyond the benefits of small-group collaboration, designing a house with pillars might have been a more familiar and concrete task for students thandesigning an object inspired by a turtle shell.

1-LS1-3 — Observing similarities and differences between parents and offspring. I n the tasks where students were identifying similarities and differences, the quality of the images may also have influenced students’ ability to identify similarities. In particular, around 40% of students struggled to identify similarities between a mature marigold plant and its young counterpart, likely because the image of the young plant (Figure 5) was less clear than that of the mature plant, with a contrasting background  that made it difficult for students to discern shared features. However, these differences did not appear when students were asked to identify differences using these same images. For example, in the Dog and Marigold task, 94% of students identified at least one of the two differences asked about. In small group tasks, such as Cat Families and Peas, all students were able to successfully identify differences.

We also explored how formative assessment design features support or constrain the visibility of students’ reasoning. Findings suggest that, within  the context of these tasks,  first graders were capable of engaging meaningfully with aspects of the three NGSS dimensions, specifically the DCIs, albeit with varying levels of sophistication. Consistent with the learning progressions framework (Duncan & Rivet, 2018), students’ reasoning appeared along a developmental continuum that was mediated by task design and representational supports rather than by conceptual difficulty alone. However, students’ demonstrations  of their reasoning and understanding were sometimes limited by linguistic and representational constraints. In these tasks, performance differences appeared to stem more from task design features or contextual elements (e.g., visuals, organism type, modality, small group/individual accountability format) than  from the conceptual difficulty itself. This aligns with prior work showing that differences in task features can lead to different patterns of problem-solving success due to variations in abstraction or internal problem representation (Chi et al., 1981; Nehm & Ha, 2010). Along this line, Sabella and Redish (2007) note that task cues shape  internal representations and influence how knowledge is elicited. Together, these findings highlight the need for design-based refinement of formative assessment tasks that balance cognitive challenge with developmental accessibility.

From our analyses, several formative assessment design considerations for first-grade life science learning emerged. The first one is to u se dynamic visuals such as  videos to represent processes and patterns, as these visuals may help young learners observe  change over time and make causal  relationships more salient. Dynamic representations thus support reasoning about otherwise abstract phenomena that static images alone may not convey. Second is to reduce literacy load through multimodal expression. Allowing students to demonstrate reasoning through drawing, labeling, or oral explanation provide s rich evidence of understanding. This is also consistent with prior research showing that young learners reason concretely and expressively through multiple modes (Metz, 1995; Lemke, 1990; Brown & Ryoo, 2008).  The t hird one is to incorporate small-group oral reasoning tasks before written tasks. These discussions support students in verbalizing and practicing scientific vocabulary, which may enhance their subsequent reasoning. Fourth is to use familiar and relatable organisms as contexts. Students demonstrated strong reasoning when tasks featured familiar animals  or plants, suggesting that familiarity can support cognitive engagement and facilitate analogical reasoning. This aligns with Jaun-Holderegger et al. (2022), who found that organism choice shapes children’s attention to particular features (e.g., body size, color, structure), underscoring the importance of thoughtful context selection in designing developmentally appropriate assessments. Last is to include high-quality, age-appropriate visuals for comparison tasks. Clear, comparable images are essential for accurately capturing students’ reasoning. Careful curation or design of visuals substantially scaffolds students and can help improve the interpretability of students’ responses. In summary, our findings underscore that well-designed, developmentally appropriate assessments can reveal young learners’ emerging scientific reasoning. By integrating dynamic visuals, multimodal response formats, familiar contexts, and high-quality representations, for mative assessments can better align with first graders’ cognitive and linguistic development while maintaining the conceptual rigor envisioned in the NGSS. This study has some limitations. The  tasks were implemented in two classrooms with a relatively small sample size, and we did not collect student-level demographic data. Analyses were primarily descriptive and focused on patterns within specific tasks contexts. Therefore, findings should be interpreted as design-based insights into formative assessment features in these classrooms rather  than as broad generalizations about first-grade life science learning.
