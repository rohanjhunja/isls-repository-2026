# Co-Designing and Teaching Science in Students’ Heritage Language

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Through university-community partnerships (UCPs), educators and practitioners identify practices that leverage students’ assets to enhance science learning. We study Nuestra Ciencia, a UCP where undergraduates teach microbiology in Spanish to elementary students in bilingual schools in the United States. We describe how the program’s design transformations impacted elementary and undergraduate students’ perceptions of science, scientists, and their heritage language. Design-based framing enabled ongoing programmatic adjustments while identifying best practices for asset-based science learning. From analyzing 200 pre-post questionnaires, we found elementary students learned microbiology content and felt more like scientists after activities. We also analyzed focus groups with 34 Spanish-speaking undergraduates, revealing three themes related to science identity: using Spanish as a tool, reframing science as relevant, and healing their younger self. We describe how participants’ perceptions of science and science education helped us understand the impact of the program and ways to improve it.

## Introduction

University-community partnerships (UCPs) can address inequitie s in science education and transform science learning for students from minoritized populations,  like multilingual learners (Coburn & Penuel, 2016;  Penuel, 2017). Through UCPs, educators and pra ctitioners explore and identify instructional practices that leverage students’ assets to enhance their science learning.  A new, promising asset-based approach for enhancing science learning is leveraging heritage language skills of multilingual students (Infante & Licona, 2018). For  this paper, we focus on the implementation of Nuestra Ciencia (NC), a UCP where undergraduate students from a 4-year, public university in the United States teach microb iology lessons in Spanish to elementary students in  bilingual schools. We describe how the program’s ongoing desi gn transformations impacted elementary students and undergraduates’ perceptions of science, scientists,  and their heritage language. Design-based framing (Barab & Squire, 2004) enabled ongoing programmatic adjustments while identifying best practices for asset-based science learning. To understand the program’s iterations and outcomes, we examined the features of the learnin g environment and relationships among design componen ts by analyzing data collected during different pha ses of program implementation. Participants’ perceptions o f science and science teaching helped us understand  the impact of the program and ways to improve it.

Problem of practice Latinx youth make up 29% of the student population in the United States, but Latinx people account for less than 10% of the STEM workforce (NCES, 2022; Santiago et al., 2024). Research that addresses the underrepresentation of Latinx students in STEM often  takes on a deficit approach that focuses on the ski lls and engagement that students “lack” (i.e. Taningco et al., 2008). Criti cal scholars have called for shifting away from thi s deficit lens and highlighting assets and lived experiences of La tinx students and their families and communities (i.e., Yosso, 2005). For the Latinx community in the United State s, the Spanish language can be a valuable asset for  the classroom as the majority of Latinx people speak Sp anish at home (Krogstad et al., 2005). However,  in K-12 settings, Latinx students experience linguistic dis crimination that discourages them from speaking Spa nish in school (Garza Ayala, 2023). Even in bilingual schools, Spanish is seen as a transitional language and a means for students to learn English and not as an asset for t heir subject learning (Palmer, 2011). Utilizing Spa nish in the science classroom can enhance Latinx student engage ment with scientific practices and support science identity formation (Infante & Licona, 2018).

## Program context

NC addresses the critical issue of underrepresentat ion of Latinxs in STEM through an asset-based approach of leveraging Spanish to promote students’ interest and learning in science. In NC, undergraduates and pr eservice teachers develop and teach microbiol ogy activities in Spanish and English through analo gies, storytelling, and scientific experiments that provide simple yet accu rate descriptions for K-6 students. NC aims to expose young learners to science and scientific role models in S panish, while benef iting undergraduate students engaging in science communication and service-learning. Undergraduates and preservice teachers play a central role in every aspect of the program, including topic selection, lesson development, teaching, and research. The interdisciplinary team of undergraduates, preservice teachers, and fa culty develop materials that contribute to the prac tice of education, such as bilingual activities for K-6 students, lesson plans, and science communication resources.

## Methodology

We employed a participatory design-based approach to collect and analyze data in collaboration with researchers and participants (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Followin g guidelines for DBR, we collected pre-post-surveys from elementary and undergraduate student s, recordings of instruction, focus group interview s and individual field notes from undergraduate facilitators, student work, and lesson plans. The research group —two undergraduates, two preservice teachers, two professors, and a post doctoral scholar— met weekly to discuss observations, which informed changes to program design and data collection. Multiple data sources provided a variety of perspectives to tackle the challenge of implementing a program i n a complex, dynamic environment while maintaining “empirical control” (Sandoval & Bell, 2004). We exami ned the designed learning environment, program processes, and products through conjecture mapping,  an analytic technique that articulates design feat ures, how they relate to each other, and how they influence program outcomes (Sandoval, 2014). Program implementation Our conjecture map outlines learning points as we c ollaboratively co-designed, refining over three iterations (Table 1). Our initial design conjectured that lear ning through contextualized experiments would count er misconceptions in microbiology for youth,  based on experiences of the microbiology Principal Investigator (PI) volunteering at a bilingual elementary school and addressing misconceptions of biology majors. We proposed that using scientific tools and conducting multi-day experiments would help students engage as scientists and develop interest and confidence in science. We focused on utilizing the scientific method as they worked in small groups. In the mediating process, we saw that students as young as five learned microbiology concepts, while conducting activities in Spanish expanded views of who could d o science. In particular, the lead microbiology PI was impacted by a first grader who commented after the activity that he “didn’t know that Spanish-speaking moms could be scientists.” In our second iteration, we conjectured that elementary students engaging in science activities in Spanish would see science as not having a set language, and  view Spanish as an academic asset (Table 1). We ex panded our leadership team and included Spanish-speaking undergraduates as science role models. To make the program more accessible for undergraduates’ scheduling and reach more elementary learners, we shifted from two-day microbiology experiments to one-session activities focused on storytelling. For example, in one lesson, fourth graders see how vaccines work through acting out and creating their own stories using an analogy where the virus is represented as a robber, the immune system is a superhero, and the vaccine is a “most wanted” poster. We found elementary students could still learn key microbiol ogy concepts even without directly engaging in the scientific method or seeing microbial growth. We also document ed how teaching science in Spanish mediated undergraduates’ views of self, where they viewed Spanish as an acad emic asset as they engaged in service-learning. To gain deeper insights into program dyna mics and learning, we added pre-post questionnaires for elementary students and pre-post surveys and focus group interviews for undergr aduates. Un dergraduate involvement in providing feedback on programmatic design was critical to program evolution and success. In our third and current implementation (Table 1), we hypothesize that undergraduate students reframe previous negative experiences with science and Spanish language in school. ## Data analysis

Analysis is based on focus groups with 34 Spanish-speaking undergraduates and 200 pre-post questionnaires from elementary participants. Undergraduates discussed p erceptions of science, experiences learning and tea ching in English and Spanish, and key progr am activities such as designing lessons and conduct ing research. We applied structural codes from Carlone and Johnson’s STEM Id entity Framework (2007) and added Teacher Identity, coding for Performance, Competence, and Recognition  in both domains. We also performed emergent coding (Saldaña, 2013), revised iteratively on a subset of transcripts. Aft er establishing over 85% inter-coder reliability between four researchers, all remaining transcripts  were coded using the final coding scheme.  Observed patterns were refined into themes through group discussion. We also analyzed elementary students’ pre-post surveys to assess microbiology knowledge and scientist identit y. We calculated how many felt like scientists and  shifts in understanding of how vaccines work and identificati on of food safety steps (clean, cook, chill, separa te) before and after lessons.

Undergraduate impact Undergraduates came away with an integrated and emp owered sense of self as a scientist, teacher, and S panish speaker. One student stated, “I definitely do see m yself as a scientist and I think I can take that and teach it.” Students attributed their experi ences designing and teaching lessons in Spanish as key to helping them realize “it’s something I cando and I like doing”, with ma ny echoing, “taking this class showed me that I can.” When analyzing how these shifts came about, three themes  emerged related  to feelings on science identity for undergraduate facilitators: using Spanish as a tool, reframing science as relevant, and healing their younger self.

Using Spanish to teach everyday science was transformative for developing science identities. Views on Spanish as a home language for “day-to-day things” shifted to valuing as “a tool” for expr essing self, being a role model, and connecting with family. Students expressed how it was empowering to teach science in Spanish and increase their own scientific vo cabulary and confidence in their language skills. T hrough microbiology discussions, developing the lesson, and teaching others in Spanish, students dispelled worries that their Spanish was inadequate or too colloquial.

Additionally, due to participating in the process o f data analysis and presentations about youth engagement, undergraduates saw science as a collaborative, real-life process. Undergraduates reported the impact of seeing individual contributions making a di fference as their ideas about communicating science  content were not just for college but had real-world implications. Undergraduates also described h ow they reframed previous negative experiences in science through providing w hat they would have appreciated as younger learners. Many felt disconnected from or discouraged in K-12 science or had trouble asking their parents questions due to t he language barrier and the way science was taught. On e student reported that teaching science in Spanish, “really felt like I was doing a service to my younger self. I would have loved to have this experience as a child and literally be able to show my parents what I did and not having me translate.” In addition to feeling positively about helping the next generation, teaching in this way improved undergraduates’ own relationship with science. One student reported “it healed the little girl in me that didn't get science.”

## Discussion

We documented lessons learned from shifting our program context, structure, and participants across various iterations. From the first iteration, students successfully engaged in scientific inquiry and understood microbes in daily life. However, changing to a 1-day visit and shifting to activities that did not involve microbial growth simplified scheduling and removed safety concerns. Inviting elementary classrooms to the college campus in our current iteration allowed for using the tools of scientists in an authentic research lab setting and expanded program access to more elementary and university students. Importantly, we learned the value of positioning Spanish-speaking undergraduates as co-designers and as “near peer” mentors (Tenenbaum et al., 2014). Including pre-service teachers and undergraduates from diverse majors in the lesson design and implementation process strengthened our final products. Still challenging is understanding elementary students’ comprehension of content knowledge and construction of STEM identities, beyond relying on pre-post questionnaires. In our next iteration, we will analyze video data to better understand how elementary learners construct scientific identities through performing and being recognized as a science person. While we recognize the limitation in only studying one UCP, we encourage other UCPs to include undergraduates in program design and implementation, as u ndergraduates came away with integrated and empowered identities as scientists, teachers, and Spanish speakers. Our program reframed Spanish as an academic asset, however, we see possibilities worldwide as o ther minoritized languages are unfortunately taug ht in ways that are subtractive or transitional rather than he lping learners of all ages view and employ  them “as a tool.” We propose that other UCPs employing asset-based science instruction could become powerful spa ces for reframing science as relevant, using heritage languages as a tool, and healing undergraduates’ previous negative experiences in science.
