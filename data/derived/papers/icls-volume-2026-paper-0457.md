# Participatory Narratives for AI Learning: Integrating Self-Efficacy, Agency, and Ethical Reflection

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This work presents a theory-driven design framework grounded in Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) and informed by participatory learning perspectives to support middle school students’ AI literacy and career interests. Implemented in a narrative-centered game-based learning environment, the framework connects SCT’s motivational constructs (self-efficacy, outcome expectations, and goals) with participatory mechanisms such as role-based engagement, consequential decision-making, and ethical reflection. Findings from a pilot study with middle school students illustrate how narrative participation supports the development of self-efficacy, AI understanding, and emerging career awareness.

Preparing students for an AI-infused society requires learning experiences that are meaningful, contextually rich, and connected to students’ developing interests and goals. Whereas many K –12 AI initiatives emphasize conceptual understanding of data, algorithms, and model training, they often provide limited opportunities for students to connect these ideas to personal agency, ethical reasoning, or future career pathways. As a result, AI literacy instruction may overlook motivational processes  that influence long-term engagement with computing fields. This work explores how Social Cognitive Theory (SCT; Bandura, 1986) and its career-focused extension, Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT; Lent et al., 2002), can inform the design of narrative-centered learning environments that foster motivation, ethica l reflection, and career interest. We further draw on participatory and situated learning perspectives to elaborate how these motivational processes are enacted. Theoretical integration Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura, 1986) emphasizes the role of self-efficacy, beliefs about one’s capabilities, in guiding motivation and action, while highlighting the reciprocal interactions among personal, behavioral, and environmental factors. Extending this framework, Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT) explains how self-efficacy, outcome expectations, and goals jointly shape interest development and career choice (Lent et al., 2002; Lent & Brown, 2019). These processes are further influenced by contextual supports and barriers (Byars-Winston & Fouad, 2008), underscoring the importance of learning environments in shaping motivation. Participatory and situated learning perspectives complement this view by emphasizing how learning and motivation are enacted through engagement in socially, materially, and culturally grounded practices (Greeno, 2006; Lave & Wenger, 1991). From this perspective, learners develop agency and identity through participation in authentic roles and activities (Nasir & Hand, 2008), with opportunities for meaningful contribution supporting sustained engagement (Engle & Conant, 2002).  T his work treats participatory learning as elaborating the mechanisms through which SCT and SCCT constructs are experienced and developed in practice. In particular, role-based participation, social interaction, and consequential decision-making provide contexts in which self-efficacy, outcome expectations, and goals are actively constructed and negotiated. Narrative-centered learning environments (Rowe et al., 2011) offer a powerful context for this integration by embedding learners in meaningful story-driven experiences  (Barab et al., 2010) that situate disciplinary practices within meaningful roles. Within these environments, learners engage in decision-making, receive feedback on their actions, and reflect on consequences, creating opportunities to connect conceptual understanding with motivation, as highlighted in recent research (Wang et al., 2023).

## Design and study context

Building on these t heoretical perspectives, AI4HEALTH is a narrative-centered learning environment  in which learners assume the role of a medical intern investigating virtual patient cases using AI diagnostic tools. Guided by a virtual mentor, students analyze AI models trained on X-ray data from varied datasets, prompting reflection on bias, fairness, and reliability in AI-assisted diagnosis. Through narrative feedback, diagnostic consequences, 2800 and mentor dialogue about the role of AI in medical careers, the environment operationalizes SCCT constructs of self-efficacy, outcome expectations, and goals.

An exploratory pilot study involved 25 middle school students (ages 12– 14). Students completed a 30minute gameplay episode followed by structured focus groups. Data sources included gameplay logs (capturing diagnostic decisions and dialogue selections), researcher observations, and focus group discussions (four groups, approximately 20 minutes each). Data were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis to identify patterns related to self-efficacy, ethical reasoning, and career-oriented thinking. participation supported students’ learning and motivation.

1. Enacting Self-Efficacy through Role-Based Participation. Students reported increased confidence in

using and understanding AI through enactment of the intern role. Performing diagnostic tasks and receiving narrative feedback allowed learners to experience competence in context. These experiences functioned as ma stery-oriented episodes, a key source of self-efficacy development (Ozer & Bandura, 1990), linking students’ actions to meaningful outcomes.

2. Negotiating AI Understanding through Reflective Ethical Reasoning. Ethical dilemmas embedded

in the narrative prompted students to reflect on issues of bias, fairness, and model reliability. Students discussed trade-offs between technical accuracy and social impact, demonstrating how ethical tension supported deeper engagement with core AI concepts. These moments illustrate how narrative dilemmas can mediate both conceptual understanding and ethical reasoning.

3. Projecting Possible Selves through Narrative Trajectories. Through role immersion and interaction

with virtual mentors, students began to envision themselves in future AI or health-related careers. These projections extended beyond gameplay, with students articulating emerging interests and aspirations. Narrative consequences provided a structure for exploring and internalizing possible future selves, aligning with SCCT’s emphasis on goal formation and interest development. Collectively, these findings highlight how narrative participation can operationalize motivational constructs within contextually rich learning environments, transforming abstract beliefs into lived experiences that support AI learning and career awareness.

This work advances a design conjecture: W hen students engage in narrative role-play featuring consequential decision-making and ethical reflection centered on AI, they may develop self-efficacy and interest while reasoning about core AI concepts, including data bias, model reliability, and societal impact. Together, these processes may support deeper AI literacy. Grounded in SCT and SCCT and informed by participatory learning perspectives, this work conceptualizes motivation as both individually experienced and socially situated, emerging through engagement in meaningful activity (Hickey & McCaslin, 2001). Narrative-centered environments provide structured opportunities for learners to enact roles, make decisions, and reflect on consequences, thereby connecting motivation, identity, and conceptual understanding. These findings contribute to theory-building at the intersection of motivation and participation by illustrating how participatory narrative design can serve as a bridge between psychological and sociocultural perspectives on learning. They also suggest that early, context-rich exposure to AI applications, particularly in socially relevant domains such as health, may support broader and more equitable participation in emerging STEM pathways.

Implications of this work include: ● For theory: Articulating participatory mechanisms through which motivational constructs consistent with SCCT are developed in practice.

● For design: Leveraging narrative-centered environments to embed self-efficacy, ethical reflection, and career exploration within AI learning experiences.

● For practice: Supporting early engagement with AI in meaningful contexts to foster interest and broaden participation in computing and health-related fields.
