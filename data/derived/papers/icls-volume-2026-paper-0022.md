# Everyday Science, Everyday Bodies: A Situated Framework for Scientific Sensemaking

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Everyday life is saturated with encounters that demand scientific sensemaking: interpreting a fever, adjusting a recipe, or managing a power fluctuation. Yet such sensemaking rarely draws solely on formal knowledge. This conceptual paper proposes a framework of Embodied and Adaptive Scientific Sensemaking in Everyday Life, which explains how people make sense of science through bodily, social, and material engagements that ext end beyond school learning, often initiated through sensory and embodied interaction with phenomena. Using a two-part methodology, we first develop a conceptual synthesis integrating scholarship on the bodily, cultural, affective, technologically mediated, and intuitive-adaptive dimensions of everyday sensemaking. We then employ fictional ethnography to refine and illustrate the framework’s generative logic, alongside three real-life ethnographic cases that demonstrate its functioning in everyday contexts. The framework positions everyday scientific sensemaking as an emergent, recursive system in which the body, as the site of experience, mediates the interaction of multiple dimensions to produce locally meaningful understandings of science, reframing science as a lived, interpretive, and adaptive process.

Neel wakes with a throbbing headache, unsure if it’s cold weather, peanuts, or too much screen time. He tries soda, an eye mask, and a short walk, while his spouse suggests seeing a doctor. Between embodied sensations, household routines, and quick online advice, the family gradually narrows down possible causes. Science education has historically framed its central objective as cultivating scientifically literate citizens, with formal knowledge as essential for rationality  (Crowell & Schunn, 2015; Yacoubian, 2017). This emphasis is reflected in policy statements and curriculum reforms that highlight the importance of a scientifically literate public for democratic participation in science and technology (Levinson, 2010). Yet real-world sensemaking relies on more than abstract facts, intertwining with the body of the person and their personal values, cultural beliefs, and emotional responses (Von Winterfeldt, 2013). Empirical studies even show that most people retain little of their learnt science content (Custers, 2010) yet competently navigate science in daily life. This raises the question of how the body, in a socially situated world, coordinates epistemic resources when deciding whether to rest or take medicine for a headache, figure out how much battery power to allocate during a blackout, or adjust cooking times based on how dough looks and smells. The argument this paper makes is that everyday sensemaking emerges through embodied engagements, where science is enacted in indirect, improvisational, and situated forms. For example, a headache is read through food intake, weather changes, and screen fatigue. Taking this view requires centering the body as the site  where everyday sensemaking unfolds.  Headaches, fevers, broken appliances, or changing weather all become meaningful as people register them bodily and weave them into cultural practices and shared routines. By foregrounding these embodied processes, we can  rightfully emphasise the value of scientific sensemaking to  its lived, interpreted, and adapted forms in daily life. This conceptual paper develops a framework of embodied and adaptive sensemaking, drawing on existing theoretical and empirical scholarship to highlight the intersecting processes by which households live with and make sense of  science. The paper proceeds by first outlining the premise of everyday life and scientific sensemaking, then discussing the theoretical foundations of the framework, before introducing the framework itself and demonstrating three real life, everyday cases that narrate its key ideas. Everyday scientific sensemaking: Background Everyday scientific sensemaking is crucial for interpreting and applying concepts that shape decisions and scientific literacy (Golumbic et al., 2022), especially in a world where science permeates media, conversations, and routines. Much of this sensemaking however occurs outside the boundaries of institutions. Children spend limited hours learning formal science, where most of their day involves informal engagement of everyday things 141 like touching hot surfaces, hearing accounts of floods, or rare events of observing meteor showers, where science becomes tangible and directly relevant to their everyday lives  (Schweingruber & Fenichel, 2010). As Feinstein (2011, p. 180) observes, people do not approach science as insiders but “remain anchored outside of science, reaching in for bits and pieces that enrich their understanding of their own lives.” A persistent challenge is the gap between informal, everyday science and school-based instruction. While young children are naturally adept at reasoning about everyday phenomena, school science often separates concepts from lived experience (Tunnicliffe & Shahrin, 2023; Pienta, 2014). As a result, many adults struggle with formal scientific ideas despite having been skilled informal reasoners earlier in life (Shtulman & Walker, 2020). Recognizing everyday principles fosters connections (Pienta, 2014) and reframes science education as cultivating competent outsiders who notice when science matters and engage expertise for personal purposes (Feinstein, 2011). Science thus becomes meaningful within the communities where people live, work, and care (Birmingham & Calabrese Barton, 2014). Kelly (1955) in his psychology of personal constructs s martly argued with the “man-the-scientist” metaphor that a human has  a universal capacity for observation, inference, and reasoning.

Sensemaking, as we know it now, is shaped by vari ous forms of knowledge. Folk knowledge, for example in biology, structures how people classify and reason about living kinds through cognitively grounded taxonomies that coexist with formal science, as seen in cross-cultural similarities across Maya communities, American laypeople, and trained scientists operating in different epistemic registers (Atran, 1998). The funds-ofknowledge approach and studies of family-school interactions illustrate how learners co-construct “third spaces” where domestic expertise meets classroom science (Adler et al., 2024). Eslami (n.d.) notes that mythic narratives and molecular accounts leverage shared embodied conceptual mappings across imaginative and analytical modes. As Schweingruber & Fenichel (2010, p. 31), emphasize, everyday learning includes diverse experiences, from walks in the woods to surfing the Web where the “experiences are very much selected and shaped by the learners themselves and may vary greatly across families, communities, and cultures.” These perspectives show that sensemaking extends far beyond formal learning, embedding interpretation in socio-cultural practices where people seek causal links and evaluate plausibility, with common-sense inferences often anticipating formal explanations.  In navigating everyday problems, non-experts integrate observations, plausibility judgments, and pragmatic utility, often drawing on folk physics and folk biology as causal schemas (Golumbic et al., 2022; Klein, 2023; Fragaszy & Mangalam, 2020; Atran, 1998). Such reasoning guides behavior across domains, including health, where patient experience informs decisions (Britten & Maguire, 2016). Existing frameworks highlight relationships between lay and formal knowledge, with phenomenological approaches identifying multiple sensemaking modes embedded in embodied, temporal, and linguistic practices (Warren et al., 2001).  Tools such as the Everyday Scientific Reasoning Scale (Golumbic et al., 2022) and plausibility-based accounts (Klein, 2023) operationalize these processes, linking everyday reasoning to real-world scientific engagement. Despite these advances, research often remains thematically segmented or outcomefocused, emphasizing literacy or decision-making rather than the sensemaking process itself. A systematic, integrative account is therefore needed to capture how embodied and contextually grounded practices combine to produce scientific understanding in everyday life, motivating the multi-dimensional framework proposed here. Although the framework aims to capture everyday scientific sensemaking, “everyday” is not uniform.  Experiences vary widely across populations, influenced by socioeconomic status, geography, literacy, and contexts of conflict or identity threats. Despite these differences, the human body provides a shared foundation for engaging with the scientific phenomena. The framework therefore seeks to guide understanding of general patterns of everyday sensemaking while remaining adaptable to context-specific nuances. When I use “we,” I intend to emphasize this shared human experience rather than exclusion. I come from a middle-class family (households in my country that earn between $5.6K and $33.8K USD annually (2020-21 prices-reported by PRICE)) on the eastern coast of India. I am the third and second generation on my paternal side and  maternal side respectively, to pursue higher education in STEM. My family has long valued formal science education for both ideological and economic reasons, and I have carried forward these values through my own training up to the doctoral level. Growing up, I engaged in frequent scientific discussions with my parents and continue to do so with my spouse, who shares a similar background. Even while reasoning critically about scientific concepts, I rely heavily on heuristics and intuitive judgment. Acknowledging my positionality clarifies the lens through which this paper approaches everyday scientific sensemaking. It situates the work within lived experience, grounding the framework in embodied, cultural, and social contexts while remaining adaptable to other populations and contexts.

142 This paper employs a multi-stage methodological strategy that combines conceptual synthesis with ethnographic exemplars. The goal is to develop and illustrate a framework for everyday scientific sensemaking, rather than to produce empirical generalizations.

First, the framework was grounded systematically in literature spanning learning sciences, science education, anthropology, psychology, cultural studies, STS, and cognitive science. This conceptual synthesis (Tricco et al., 2016) brings together diverse theoretical insights to construct an original analytic model. Literature was selected through an iterative, purposive process, focusing on foundational and contemporary works that address embodiment, everyday reasoning, informal learning, and sensemaking in real-world contexts. Selection was guided by relevance to everyday scientific sensemaking and the ability of sources to illuminate interactions among bodily, social, and material processes, rather than by exhaustive coverage.  In parallel, fictional ethnography served as a heuristic tool (Rinehart, 1998), using thought experiments (Aguinis, 2023) grounded in personal and documented experiences to identify and refine the framework’s core dimensions. These narratives were used as analytic devices to explore how different dimensions of sensemaking interact, enabling iterative refinement of the framework by testing coherence, relationships, and boundary conditions across imagined everyday scenarios. Fictional ethnography was chosen because it allows the integration of patterns from multiple contexts while protecting participant privacy. This approach makes it possible to illustrate the different dimensions of sensemaking in coherent narratives, which alternative methods like surveys or single-site ethnography coul d not capture as effectively.

Next, the framework’s explanatory power was illustrated through three ethnographic cases: a domestic episode managing sudden illness, a household dilemma involving cooking technology, and a mother’s conversation with her daughter about world issues and shadows. These serve as ethnographic exemplars (Højer & Bandak, 2015), analyzed to highlight recursive interactions among the framework’s dimensions in situated practice. The cases were selected to reflect variation in context, timescale, and epistemic orient ation, allowing demonstration of how the framework operates across diverse everyday situations. Theoretical basis and conceptual synthesis Theoretical lens: Embodied, situated and distributed cognition The paper situates everyday scientific sensemaking within embodied, situated, and distributed approaches to cognition. These perspectives challenge purely individualistic accounts of learning by foregrounding how knowledge emerges through interactions among people, tools, bodies, and cultural environments (Hutchins, 1995; Pea, 1993; Rogoff, 2003; Vygotsky, 1978).

The body here is understood as both a sensor and mediator, registering sensations, engaging with material objects, and participating in culturally patterned practices. Importantly, embodiment is not universal or purely biological. B odily perception and engagement are shaped through culturally learned practices, norms, and histories that influence what is sensed, attended to, and considered meaningful. Within this perspective, intuition, affect, and identity are treated as integral dimensions of cognition. Here, i ntuitive judgments are understood as embodied, experience-based ways of knowing shaped through prior interactions with the world. Affective responses influence how phenomena are noticed, interpreted, and valued, while identity reflects how individuals position themselves, and are positioned, within social and cultural practices of meaning-making. Cognition here is distributed across social, technological, and environmental networks, highlighting how reasoning and meaningmaking are shared, extended, and contextually shaped. These perspectives together frame the body as inherently situated within social, cultural, and material worlds, guiding the analysis of how everyday scientific phenomena are perceived, interpreted, and negotiated. The conceptual synthesis of related work across domains is presented across the following five themes.

Embodiment Embodiment and embodied cognition have emerged as important perspectives for understanding how people engage with the world. Embodiment highlights the central role of bodily experience in scientific sensemaking. Physical, phenomenological, ecological, and interactionist senses of embodiment show how perception, action, and interaction support understanding (Kersting et al., 2021). Bodily sensations  such as noticing a sour smell, rising temperature, or physical discomfort  serve as epistemic cues that trigger attention and interpretation (Montesi, 2023). In museums, gestures, talk, and interactions with exhibits guide how phenomena are recognized and reasoned about (Schweingruber & Fenichel, 2010). In everyday life, local and experiential knowledge interact with formal information. For example,  during the COVID-19 pandemic, bodily experience and situated 143 observations were combined with official guidance to produce “thick knowledge” or, at times, divergence and critique, emphasizing the epistemic role of sensation (Montesi, 2023). Sensory trials, gestures, and material encounters constitute primary modes of engagement with phenomena (Otrel-Cass, 2017), while metaphorical mappings and embodied social representations link physical sensation to conceptual understanding (Ni ebert, 2015). Enactivist perspectives further situate cognition in continuous organism-environment interaction, making sensemaking inseparable from embodied life (Manca, 2025). Hence, everyday scientific sensemaking is grounded in embodied experience. Here, sensory perception, gesture, and material interaction guide attention, reasoning, and interpretation, providing the first epistemic entry points into phenomena and shaping how knowledge is constructed in context. Cultural practices, situatedness, communities The next theme that we want to highlight is perhaps the strongest and prominent aspect of the everyday life. It is the role of culture, situatedness, and community in sensemaking. Learning emerges through participation in shared practices involving language, tools, and knowledge, with families acting as primary educators (Campbell, 2024; McClain et al., 2022; Schweingruber & Fenichel, 2010). These routines draw on “funds of knowledge,” consisting of skills and experiences accumulated across generations (Moll et al., 1992). People interpret phenomena through culturally situated repertoires. Indigenous and place-based practices, for instance, use sensory-driven narratives to guide collective understanding and action (Cameron, 2022). Families often blend formal and everyday knowledge, as seen in “scientific patchworking” in public health or co-construction of “third spaces” connecting lived experience and scientific concepts (Gunnarsson & Wemrell, 2023; Adler et al., 202 4). Decisions around food, health, and resources reflect cultural norms, hierarchies, and embodied practices (Taylor, 2023; Kane, 2023). Integrating diverse knowledge sources, including emotional and experiential cues, enables sensemaking in crises (Smith et al., 2025; Boe‐Lillegraven et al., 2024). Across domains of food (Polkinghorne, 2021), environment (Strand et al., 2022), and myth-biology intersections (Eslami, n.d.), culture, embodiment, and knowledge converge, with households serving as sites where hist orical, material, and intergenerational resources produce locally meaningful scientific understanding. Hence, the practises of the body take charge of sensemaking.

Identity and affective dimensions Everyday science sensemaking is inseparable from identity and affect, which infuse practices with meaning and moral orientation. Generational experiences of limited healthcare access, infrastructural unevenness, or financial precarity foster habits of “first trying at home” through remedies, observation, and collective reasoning, practices transmitted as responsibility and self-reliance (Yogith, n.d). Affective responses  like concern, trust, pride, fear, guide what is noticed, which explanations are considered, and how they are judged as plausible (Weichold, 2023; Tabak, 2023). Interpretation is always embodied and situated, ethically charged because decisions carry relational and practical consequences. For example, personal experience and emotion shape mea ning-making in texts (Marsh, 2018) just as they influence the uptake of health information. Identity and affect also operate collectively. Community resilience during crises depends on shared cues, synchronized practices, and belonging (Georgiou & Murillo, 2023), echoing accounts of sensemaking as grounded in place, loss, and uncertainty (Boe‐Lillegraven et al., 2024). Children’s pretend play similarly rehearses sociocultural contingencies, testing identity and affective roles (Weichold, 2023). Power and epistemic stance further structure these dynamics. Schildt (2019) identifies automatic, improvisational, algorithmic, and reflective processes, showing how systemic forces determine whether sensemaking is habitual, provisional, or deliberate. Across households, schools, and workplaces, identity and affect permeate science talk, practices, and participation (Callanan et al., 2011). These accounts highlight how cultural memory, self-conceptions, and emotions shape the credibility, trust, and enactment of science. Sensemaking here is meaningful with the body that is  embodied, relational, and value-laden.

Technology and media mediation In the evolving digital ecosystem, technology and media increasingly drive everyday scientific sensemaking. Mobile applications and cross-platform engagement shape daily practices (Krause et al., 2025), while social media mediates public perception of health and influences behaviors. Mediatization of science has shifted knowledge production and consumption: search engines, LLMs, and social platforms provide rapid access and interactive communication (Cinque, 2024). These mediated sources are important  sites of scientific literacy, but they also amplify risks of misinformation and conspiracy theories (Lobnikar, 2025). Digital tools also support participation and knowledge creation. Youth navigate hybrid analog-digital worlds (Rosenbaum et al., 2024) where games, online communities, and social platforms act as learning infrastructures. Studies in climate change education and 144 urban forestry show how multimodal storytelling and perspective-taking mediate connections between science, equity, and lived experience (Smith et al., 2025). Households integrate government, media, and local knowledge, producing “thick knowledge” when sources converge and prompting critique when they diverge (Montesi, 2023). Smartphones and citizen science apps enable ecological observation (Ayers, 202 4), while self-tracking tools link data to bodily sensations, producing “situated health” (Algera, 202 3). These processes depend on “digital capital”, the skills, access, and critical evaluation capacities that determine how people navigate abundant and conflicting information (Leguina, 2021). Age and context shape engagement where some older adults become “digital seniors,” while others remain excluded (Choi, 2022). Across health, environmental, educational, and domestic contexts, technology and media extend the body, mediating scientific reasoning and connecting embodied experience to abstract concepts and the larger community. Intuition and adaptation People often rely on fast, experience-based cognition: heuristics and intuition, to make sense of events and guide decisions. Intuition is tacit, rapid, and felt with certainty, enabling decisions without deliberate reasoning (Brock, 2015; Bas, 2019). Though sometimes counterintuitive to science, intuitive knowledge forms a foundation for scientific thought and is reinforced through metaphors, imagery, and narratives that render abstract concepts graspable (Blancke, 2018). Experts also develop strong intuitions through long-term engagement (Kryjevskaia, 2021). Research on folk knowledge illustrates these dynamics where folk biology organizes reasoning about living kinds (Atran, 1998), while folk physics supports everyday causal judgments about force and motion (Fragaszy & Mangalam, 2020). These domains combine with pragmatic reasoning, where judgments of plausibility anchor everyday interpretations (Klein, 2023).

Complementing intuition, adaptive cognition describes the flexible integration of new information with prior knowledge across lived experience (Hartley, 2022). It combines perception, memory, and reasoning to support decision-making in non-expert contexts with partial or uncertain scientific knowledge (Bromme & Goldman, 2014; Dubovi & Vizel, 2025). Informal learning contexts show that adaptive cognition thrives under non-didactic, collaborative, and interest-driven conditions (Callanan et al., 2011). Citiz en science provides an example, where participants merge evidence with local knowledge to generate meaningful understandings without formal expertise (Golumbic  et al., 2022; Giardullo, 2023). Adaptive cognition supports emergent sensemaking, enabling people to navigate uncertainty, reconcile counterintuitive findings, and form actionable interpretations of science in daily life (Cannady, 2019).

Building on the theoretical traditions reviewed above, we now bring these strands together into an integrative framework. The purpose of this framework is to synthesize the  strands into a situated account of everyday scientific sensemaking. The five themes identified through the conceptual synthesis were treated as overlapping dimensions of everyday scientific sensemaking. Through iterative comparison across the literature and the fictional ethnographic cases, these themes were reorganized analytically to foreground the role of the body as a central integrating axis. This process involved examining how each theme contributed to perception, interpretation, and action in everyday contexts, and how they interacted dynamically rather than linearly. The resulting framework therefore represents a reconfiguration of these themes into interdependent dimensions that capture the recursive and situated nature of sensemaking. Framework: Embodied and adaptive scientific sensemaking in everyday life We propose a framework for understanding how people make sense of scientific phenomena in everyday life, foregrounding the body as a central mediator of knowledge, practice, and identity. By placing the body at the center of interaction, this framework (see Figure 1) highlights six interrelated dimensions through which embodied experiences, cultural practices, affective identities, and technological mediation are mobilized to interpret and act upon scientific phenomena. These dimensions interact recursively, as the outcomes of sensemaking continually shape subsequent perception, interpretation, and action.

1. Sensorimotor Embodiment (The Body as Sensor):  The body is the primary interface for detecting

scientific phenomena via interoception (warmth, pain, nausea), exteroception (sight, sound, smell)  and proprioception (position, effort). These sensory inputs act as epistemic cues that direct attention, prompt interpretation, and trigger small-scale trials (touching, sniffing, toggling) that produce experiential heuristics for rapid sensemaking (Kersting et al., 2021; Montesi, 2023).  What is sensed and attended to is shaped by culturally learned repertoires of noticing and interpretation.

2. Cultural Practices & Routines (The Body as Practice):  Habitual, collective routines (cooking,

monitoring water, observing weather, following lunar cycles) frame which bodily sensations are noticed and how they are translated into socially legible meanings. Intergenerational “funds of knowledge” and 145 demonstrations scaffold attention, causal inference, and locally coherent heuristics (Schweingruber & Fenichel, 2010; Moll et al., 1992; Campbell, 2024).

3. Identity (The Body as Social Position): Identities which are shaped by histories, roles, and affiliations,

bias what people attend to, which interpretations count as plausible, and who is authorized to speak or act. I dentity mediates whether sensemaking is automatic or reflective and channels values such as responsibility and self-reliance (Yogith, n.d; Weichold, 2023; Schildt, 2019).

4. Affect & Experience (The Body as Feeling): Emotions and prior experiences (fear, curiosity, distrust;

episodes of illness, accidents, crises) prioritize certain cues, calibrate risk judgments, and shape trust in sources. Collective affect (shared concern or pride) organizes household and community heuristics and directs vigilance or complacency (Weichold, 2023; Boe‐Lillegraven et al., 2024).

5. Technological Mediation (The Body Extended): Digital and media ecologies (smartphones, apps, social

media, citizen science platforms, AI) extend perception and reasoning by amplifying, verifying, or reinterpreting embodied experience. These tools provide mediated observation and act as reasoning partners (cameras, health apps, weather platforms), while social identity shapes which technologies and sources are trusted (Manca, 2025; Rosenbaum et al., 2024; Montesi, 2023).

6. Adaptive Cognition (Emergent Sensemaking): Adaptive cognition integrates intuition, heuristics, and

reflective reasoning to reconcile new inputs with prior knowledge and produce provisional explanations and practical actions. I t co mbines sensorimotor cues, cultural schemata, identity, affect, and technological inputs into context-sensitive, time-evolving understandings (Brock, 2015; Bas, 2019; Hartley, 2022; Cannady, 2019; Dubovi & Vizel, 2025).

Various mechanisms within the framework The movement across the dimensions in the framework are dictated by certain mechanisms as follows:

• Sensemaking relies on continuous feedback across all dimensions.

The six dimensions operate as interdependent processes rather than functioning as discrete stages in a linear model. Sensorimotor events produce immediate epistemic cues; cultural routines and identity interpret those cues; affect and experience modulate attention and evaluative stance; technologies amplify or contest interpretations; and adaptive cognition integrates the ensemble and issues new behaviors or beliefs. Those outcomes then reshape subsequent sensing, routines, identities, affects, and technology use. In short  sensemaking follows a process like: perception → interpretation → action → recalibration, across multiple interacting layers and timescales.

• Different cues dominate depending on context and urgency.

Different pathways candominate in different contexts. A rapidly unfolding physical cue (e.g., sudden breathlessness) will rely heavily on sensorimotor embodiment and heuristic intuition. A  slower, ambiguous phenomenon (e.g., gradual air-quality decline) recruits cultural knowledge, identity-based 146 credibility assessments, and technological confirmation. Affect (fear, concern) can accelerate reliance on heuristics or prompt reflective checking (e.g., consulting an app or asking a parent). Technologies can both speed up feedback (real-time data) and introduce new delays or distortions (curated feeds), changing the tempo of recalibration.

• Updates occur over short and long-term timescales.

Recursive updates occur over nested timescales. Micro-level cycles (seconds-hours) involve sensory trials and immediate adjustments (open the window, test the stove). Meso-level cycles (days-months) integrate routines and social heuristics (household practices after a flood season). Macro-level sedimentation (years-generations) embeds funds of knowledge and identity narratives that bias future attention and inference. Each loop leaves residual structures through heuristics, moral scripts, technology habits that make some interpretations more likely in later episodes.

• Interpretations are shaped collectively through social and technological networks.

Sensemaking is often distributed across people and artifacts: households, communities, and digital networks co-produce interpretations. A single embodied cue becomes socially validated (or contested) through conversation, demonstration, or app-driven evidence. Collective calibration occurs when social peers and trusted technologies align, producing stable community heuristics; misalignment can produce contested meanings, experimentation, or epistemic change.

• Adaptive cognition integrates inputs and drives sensemaking forward.

Adaptive cognition both emerges from and drives the system  during sensemaking. I t is the integrative process that creates provisional explanations and then uses their outcomes to update sensors, routines, identities, and technology choices. It balances speed and accuracy, using heuristics when rapid action matters and reflective integration when stakes, ambiguity, or social consequences demand deliberation. Let us take three cases to see how this framework manifests. Cases Case 1: The Throbbing Headache: Neel, a Ph.D. employee in chemical engineering, returns home after a long day and experiences a sharp headache. His body registers throbbing pain, dizziness, and pressure, prompting him to press his forehead, walk, and try an eye mask. These sensorimotor c ues act as immediate epistemic feedback, guiding his next actions. He draws on cultural practices, recalling dietary associations (sweets and peanuts) and local knowledge linking fatigue to headaches, while past bodily experiences color his interpretation (affect & experience). Seeking further cues, he steps onto the chilly balcony, noticing how his body reacts to temperature changes, and consults his phone for weather data (technological mediation). Household negotiation brings identity into play: Neel prefers self-diagnosis, while his spouse, embodying a caretaker role, urges medical consultation. Emotional responses like chest-tightening anxiety or visible concern highlight affective cues driving attention. Together, they evaluate remedies (digestive soda, over-the-counter medicine, eye mask), integrating bodily sensations, cultural knowledge, technology, social roles, and emotions into a provisional explanation that frames the headache as manageable (adaptive cognition). This working heuristic, grounded in the body’s sensory and affective feedback, will influence how future bodily signals are attended to, interpreted, and acted upon, exemplifying recursive everyday scientific sensemaking. Case 2: The Oven Dilemma: Khushi, preparing to bake a cake, considers using an old electric oven. She actively inspects the metal tin and exposed heating coil (sensorimotor embodiment), forming an initial judgment. She asks her partner Anand about safety, and his reference to a past incident (smoke from a leaking pastry) introduces affective and experiential knowledge, heightening concern. Household negotiation engages identity and cultural practices, highlighting norms around who makes safety decisions. Khushi consults online guidance (technological mediation), which is ambiguous, leaving her uncertain. She chooses to relight the gas oven, noting afterward that the electric oven would likely have been safe, prompting discussion with Anand. He clarifies that past risk was specific t o certain items, embedding shared experiential heuristics. Together they establish a new household rule  to use electric oven for small items, gas oven for larger bakes, thus showing how embodied perception, cultural routines, identity, affect, and technology combine into a provisional solution (adaptive cognition). This episode updates routines, guides future technology use, and calibrates attention to risk cues, illustrating the recursive dynamics of everyday scientific sensemaking. Case 3: Chakra alignment and shadows:   Over two evening phone calls, Radha, a yoga practitioner in her fifties, and her daughter Diya, a scientist, negotiate different ways of making sense of recent environmental and personal changes. In the first conversation, Radha  worries about rising mental distress and erratic weather, describing them as signs of “disturbed chakra alignment” in people and nature. Her view draws from long-held yogic philosophy and embodied experience  where she feels calmer and more “balanced” after meditation, illustrating sensorimotor embodiment and affective resonance as starting points for sensemaking. Radha ’s 147 reasoning also reflects cultural practice and identity: yoga is a moral and epistemic framework that defines her way of knowing. She supplements these beliefs through technological mediation, following online podcasts and YouTube talks that reinforce her interpretation. Diya, grounding her response in ecological science, explains how reduced nature contact disrupts human neurophysiology, referencing adaptive cognition to connect embodied regulation with environmental systems. Their exchange becomes a space of interpretive negotiation rather than correction, an example of distributed sensemaking, where affective trust and shared care allow differing epistemologies to coexist. Later that night, Ra dha calls again. She sounds anxious, she has noticed two shadows of the bathroom light and wonders if it signals failing eyesight or a supernatural sign. Diya reassures her, explaining that two shadows can occur when light reflects from multiple surfaces. Radha accepts the explanation with relief, noting how easily perception can mislead. This moment exemplifies embodied noticing followed by adaptive recalibration, where immediate sensory confusion is resolved through social reasoning and trust.  These exchanges show how s ensemaking moves from cosmological interpretation to immediate perception, demonstrating how everyday reasoning flexibly spans scales. This paper advances a situated framework for understanding everyday scientific sensemaking, grounded in the embodied, cultural, affective, and technological dimensions of daily life. By centering the body as sensation, practice, position, feeling, extensio n, and adaptation, the framework presents how households and communities interpret science as lived meaning-making. It contributes to the learning sciences by bridging embodiment, informal learning, and sociocultural practice with contemporary concerns around technology, trust, and equity. This reorientation shifts inquiry away from individual cognition or deficit perspectives and toward the recursive, value-laden, and embodied processes through which knowledge is experienced in daily life. The framework also offers methodological and practical pathways for tracing learning. Ethnographic approaches, including fictional ethnography, can be used to create rich cases that surface how different dimensions guide sensemaking over time. Researchers may choose to focus on all six dimensions, or selectively emphasize those most salient to the phenomena or context under study, recognizing that dimensions often overlap in practice. Learning traced through this framework can be understood as evolving across multiple timescales, from momentto-moment sensorimotor adjustments, to household routines over days and months, to generational sedimentation of heuristics, values, and epistemic repertoires, capturing how meaning develops in situ and in relation to social and material contexts. It also has implications for practice. Educators and curriculum designers can connect school science to the embodied and cultural sensemaking children bring from home. In the same manner, health and science communicators candesign campaigns around vaccination, nutrition, pollution, or digital literacy that resonate with household routines, anxieties, and aspirations. Like any conceptual model, it has limitations. The framework primarily leans on  narratives of middleclass households, which may not accurately reflect sensemaking in marginalized or resource-limited contexts. Prior scientific knowledge, socioeconomic status, and geography also shape how the dimensions manifest. It focuses on interpretive processes rather than material infrastructures. While the six dimensions are analytically distinct, in practice they often overlap, with affect and identity frequently co-constituting one another.  Future research should test and refine the framework through comparative studies across socioeconomic, cultural, and geographic contexts. Linking the framework to equity and justice, and examining whose knowledge is recognized or marginalized, can offer critical insights for the learning sciences. In closing, this framework provokes a broader reflection: D o science and technology, as experienced in everyday life, make us happier, healthier, or more just as a human race? If so, by what measures? And if not, what does that imply for how we study, teach, and practice science in partnership with the communities  most affected by it?
