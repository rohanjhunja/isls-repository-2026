# Exploring How Dispositions Toward Navigating Scientific Uncertainty (DSUN) Influence Epistemic Curiosity and Learning Achievement

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Students’ Dispositions toward Scientific Uncertainty Navigation (DSUN) can play a pivotal role in impacting student learning achievement. However, understanding of the underlying mechanism of DSUN influencing learning achievement is limited. Drawing from related research, this study investigates the roles of epistemic curiosity and learning engagement in mediating the relationship between DSUN and learning achievement. A survey study design was employed, involving 1,137 middle school students who participated in an uncertainty-driven learning environment when learning solar energy. A sequential mediation model was tested using data collected through validated measures assessing DSUN, epistemic curiosity, learning engagement, and learning achievement. Results revealed that while DSUN positively predicts learning achievement, this relationship is sequentially mediated by epistemic curiosity and learning engagement, with epistemic curiosity not serving as a significant mediator on its own. These findings underscore the importance of fostering both epistemic curiosity and engagement in science classroom activities when students encounter scientific uncertainty.

## Introduction

Navigating scientific uncertainty is a crucial aspect of scientific inquiry (Chen, Park et al., 2024; Ha et al., 2024) and can enhance student learning when done producti vely (Watkins et al., 2018). P roductive navigation of scientific uncertainty may require students to acknowledge and engage with it (Jordan, 2015; Watkins et al., 2018), regulate their emotions (Hillen et al., 2017), demo nstrate confidence (Ilgen et al., 2019), and adopt strategic approaches (Ko & Luna, 2024) when responding to enc ountered scientific uncertainty. Thus, individual habitual patterns in these aspects —students’ dispositions toward scientific uncertaint y navigation (DSUN) —determine how they navigate encountered scientific uncertainty. Students with higher DSUN are more likely to navigat e scientific uncertainty proactively, using it as a resource for knowledge de velopment (Chen, Jordan et al., 2024), which may lead to greater learning gains. However, to the authors’ best knowledge, little research is available to understand the relationship between DSUN and learning achievement. Despite the expected influence of DSUN on learning achievement, it is not yet fully understood how these dispositions shape students' learning process es and, consequently, enhance learning outcomes. The distal relationship between DSUN and learning achievement requires further investigation, particularly regard ing potential mediating variables. This study proposes two variables—epistemic curiosity and learning engagement— that are expected to mediate the relationship between DSUN and learning achievement. DSUN, epistemic curiosity, and learning engagement Research in science education, psychology, and rela ted fields suggests how DSUN enhances student learn ing, mediated by curiosity and learning engagement. Unce rtainty is recognized as a primary source of episte mic curiosity, defined as the desire to acquire  knowledge (Litman, 2008; Loewenstein, 1994). When epistemic curiosity arises in response to scientific uncertai nty, students engage in exploratory activities, suc h as seeking information to address the uncertainty (Lamnina & C hase, 2019). Students' responses to epi stemic curiosity are likely influenced by their level of DSUN. For insta nce, students disposed to proactively embrace scien tific uncertainty are also likely to experience heightene d epistemic curiosity. Supporting this idea, Muis e t al., (2015) identified a positive mediating role of curiosity in the relationship between epistemic beliefs (e.g., "knowledge is uncertain") and learning achievement.

Learning engagement, defined as cognitive, emotional, behavioral, and agentic involvement in classroom activities (Jang et al., 2016), is anothe r potential mediating variable that may explain how  DSUN enhances student learning. Researchers have highlig hted that uncertainty is central to students’ engag ement in scientific practices, such as developing conceptual  ideas (cognitive engagement; Manz, 2015), experien cing puzzlement and confusion (emotional engagement; Jaber & Hammer, 2016), asking questions of peers or teachers (behavioral engagement; Watkins et al., 2018), and pro blematizing a problem (agentic engagement; Phillips  et al., 2018). This body of research has shown that le arning engagement  stimulated by uncertainty contributes to advancing learning achievement. Furthermore, findings from both empirical (e.g., Tu et al., 2024) and conceptual (e.g., Arone et al., 2011; Jirout, 2020) research s uggest a sequential mediation between epistemic curiosity and learning engagement, with epistemic curiosity preceding learning engagement. For instance, Tu and colleagues

(2024) found that epistemic curiosity triggered by uncertainty predicted emotional engagement in a virtual game-based learning environment focused on evaporation and condensation. The current study This study aims to explore the underlying mechanism s through which DSUN positively influences learning achievement in the context of science education. Specifically, it investigates how epistemic curiosity and learning engagement mediate the relationship between DSUN and learning achievement, as suggested by prior resea rch. To achieve this, a sequential mediation model will be tested. The study addresses the following research questions and hypotheses:

## Context and participants

A survey study design, combined with an instruction al intervention —the uncertainty-driven learning environment—was implemented across an academic year to collect data on DSUN, epistemic curiosity, learning engagement, and learning achievement. This learning  environment was developed using an innovative appr oach called the SUPeR approach. This learning environment allows students to engage  in scientific inquiries and support them to productively navigate their scienti fic uncertainties. Sixteen teachers from twelve schools in a metropolitan area in the southwestern U.S. collabor ated with the research team to deliver the interven tion on a common solar energy unit. To ensure consistency, th e research team provided all teachers with the same curriculum, inquiry materials, and consultations.

Following the solar energy unit, an achievement tes t and three separate surveys —measuring DSUN, epistemic curiosity, and learning engagement—were administered over a week. A total of 1,137 students (grades 6–9) participated with signed consent forms, as appro ved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB). Table 1 presents participant gender and grade information.

Among the participants, 696 students completed all three surveys and the achievement test, while 441 missed at least one measure. Missing data analysis compared the complete (n = 696) and incomplete (n =  441) groups to examine differences in DSUN, epistemic cu riosity, learning engagement, and learning achievem ent. The results supported the Missing at Random (MAR) a ssumption, permitting the use of Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) estimation for mediation analysis in Mplus 8.8. ## Measures

Dispositions toward Scientific Uncertainty Navigation (DSUN) DSUN was measured using a previously validated 25-item scale (Park et al., 202 4). Sample items include, “Uncertainty encourages me to read through my class  notes or textbooks to find key information” and “W hen I experience uncertainty during group work, I ask ano ther student for different ideas.” The scale utiliz ed a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Strongly disagree” (1) to “Stron gly agree” (5). The average score was used to creat e the DSUN variable for mediation analyses. Reliability was assessed through Cronbach’s alpha, Omega total, and Coefficient H, each of which has unique strengths and limitations (McNeish, 2018). All three reliability estimates were 0.94, indicating high reliability.

Epistemic curiosity The 10-item Epistemic Curiosity measure, developed and val idated by Litman (2008), assessed students’ epistemic curiosity. The scale comprises two subconstructs (five items each): I-type (curiosity driven by interest) and D-type (curiosity driven by deprivation sensitivity). Sample items include, “I enjoy exploring new ideas” (Itype) and “I can spend hours on a single problem be cause I can't rest without knowing the answer” (D-type). Responses were captured on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from “Does not describe me at all” (1) to “Completely describes me” (7). The average score was used to cr eate the epistemic curiosity variable for analysis.  Reliability estimates were high, with Cronbach’s alpha, Omega total, and Coefficient H all at 0.91. Learning engagement Learning engagement was measured using a validated 19-item scale assessing cognitive, emotional, behavior al, and agentic engagement (Jang et al., 2016). Sample items include, “When I’m in this class, I listen very carefully” and “When reading for this class, I try to explain the key concepts in my own words.” Responses were r ecorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Strongly disagree” (1) to “Strongly agree” (5). An average score was used to create the learning engagement variable for anal ysis. Reliability was high, with Cronbach’s alpha a nd Omega total at 0.95 and Coefficient H at 0.94.

Learning achievement An achievement test comprising 20 multiple-choice items was developed by the research team to assess students’ understanding of solar energy taught during the int ervention. The test covered four content areas: the  type of energy a solar panel uses to gener ate electricity, the conversion of sunlight to elec tricity, factors affecting the efficiency of solar panels in producing electricity, and the calculation of power using voltage and current. Sample items include, “Solar panels work better in sunny areas than cloudy areas. This is true because solar panels absorb more (blank) from the sun.” Each correct response was awarded 1 point, and the sum score was used for learning achievement. Reliability was high, with Cronbach’s alpha at 0.86, Omega total at 0.87, and Coefficient H at 0.84.

## Discussion

The results of this study contribute to a nuanced understanding of how DSUN and learning achievement. Although DSUN itself positively predicts learning achievement, this distal relationship can be disaggregated into sequential mediation effects of epistemic curiosity and learning engagement. Notably, the direct effect of DSUN on learning achievement becomes insignificant after accounting for these mediating variables, suggesting that stud ents with higher DSUN may not achieve greater learning outcom es unless  they also demonstrate heightened epistemic curiosity and learning engagement.

The findings extend theoretical discussions on the mediating roles of epistemic curiosity and learning engagement. Contrary to prior research (e.g., Muis et al., 2015), which highlighted epistemic curiosit y as a mediator, this study suggests that epistemic curiosity alone does not significantly mediate the relationship between DSUN and learning achievement unless further mediat ed by learning engagement. This discrepancy may ste m from differences in measurement scales. For example, Muis et al.’s scale assessed curiosity as an emotional state, while this study’s scale focused on assessing students’ habitual responses to interest or information depri vation. This implies that students’ interest-driven or deprivation-driven epistemic curiosity may not directly enhance achievement unless it fosters deeper classroom enga gement. This aligns with Jirout’s (2020) argument t hat cognitive engagement mediates the effect of curiosity on learning achievement. Practically, these findings inform  teachers how to better support students’ scientific practice s, particularly when navigating scientific uncertainties. Given the mediating roles of epistemic curiosity and learning engagement, students’ navigation of scientific uncertainties can be better supported in a way that it increases their epistemic curiosity and, in turn, enhances their le arning engagement. Without such engagement, navigat ing scientific uncertainties may impede rather than enhance learning, even if students recognize the potential benefits of navigating scientific uncertainties. Therefore, supporting students in scientific uncertainty navigation should involve facilitating engagement in classroom activities that serve as pathways to acqu iring information and addressing uncertainties productively.
