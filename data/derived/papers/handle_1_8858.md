# Restorying Toward Justice and Anti-Racism in ELA Classrooms

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Storytelling has been used as a vehicle to center diverse perspectives in classrooms; however, in the U.S., the most prevalent stories in schools continue to be those that reflect narratives of white supremacist systems. As educators search new ways to implement culturally sustaining pedagogies, storytelling can become a vehicle to allow students to voice their own funds of knowledge and act as experts on their own experiences. This participatory-design study examined ways that storytelling in 9th grade ELA classrooms supported students in restorying their classrooms and communities. Using jazz as a set of methodological tools, this study was codesigned with a U.S. mountain-west English teacher and her students as a digital storytelling project to disrupt dominant narratives in a rural school. Findings demonstrate the potential for historically marginalized youth to identify as agents of change when offered opportunities to restorying in historically white spaces.

## Introduction

Sociocultural perspectives in education have long s uggested that learning is deeply intertwined with t he lived experiences that contribute to individual and collective identities and the practices and tools found in communities (e.g., Holland et al., 1998; Wertsch, 1991). For decades, the research community has agreed that there should be more space for learners to bring their identities a nd cultures—their stories—into learning environment s (e.g., Dutro, 2009; González, Moll, & Amanti, 2005; Thomas  & Stornaiuolo, 2016). However, recommendations for how educators can include learners’ home practices, tools, and knowledges vary greatly. While critical scholars argue that learners, educators, and researchers sho uld treat stories as legitimate forms of knowledge, understandings of how to design spaces that account for learner stories, and the success or failure of communities in enacting liberatory pedagogies around stories, continue to be needed (e.g., González et al., 2005; Kumashiro, 2000).

This participatory-design study used jazz tools (e. g., improvisation, trading fours, and harmonic substitution) to examine ways that storytelling in 9th grade English Language Arts (ELA) classrooms supported students in a process of disrupting dominant narrat ives by (re)storying (Stornaiuolo & Thomas, 2018) a nd critically reinventing (Morel et al., 2019) their classrooms and communities. The study took place in collaboration with Elizabeth Murray (all names are pseudonyms) and her 9th grade students during the 2018-2019 school year in a rural, mostly white community. Elizabeth and I co-designed and implemented a year-long curriculum based upon the Throughlines digital storytelling project (www.thethroughlinesproject.com) to support her 9th grade ELA students in understanding the ways their personal narratives intersected with or were missing from the dominant narratives of their communities. In this study, we sought to acknowledge the ways that whiteness has s haped institutional spaces and examine the ways that priv ilege might be leveraged and disrupted to shift educational spaces. While this study included students from a number of backgrounds, in this paper, I focus on the ways that students who self-identified as coming from histori cally marginalized backgrounds—for example, as peop le of color, LGBTQIIA+, neurodivergent, differently abled, and/or from lower socioeconomic classes—in order to explore the ways that these students described thei r experiences of restorying in a predominantly whit e space. Given the ongoing violence perpetuated against hist orically marginalized communities by white supremac ist systems, there continues to be need for youth from these communities to have opportunities to see themselves as powerful and agentic historical actors (Gutierrez et al., 2019). While jazz has previously been used as a metaphor for education, this study explored the ways that jazz tools can support researchers in better understanding the classroom as a dialogic third space (Gutiérrez, 2008). As a discipline, jazz has a rich history of disrupting  white supremacy to restory the ways musicians and audiences interpret music. By drawing from these tools as part of my conceptual framework, I follow calls for engaging with new tools in learning sciences research to re-envision how researchers and teachers might work together to better understand how whiteness shapes classrooms, and what we cando to disrupt those systems and create space for students as epistemic agents. To do this, I explore d the following questions: 1) How do storytelling p rojects in classrooms support students to practice restorying in their ELA classrooms? and 2) How can jazz tools support the ways researchers observe and understand students’ and teachers moves toward restorying in classrooms?

## Jazz as Conceptual Framework

Jazz music bridges Western harmonies with African r hythms. Like the interactions in classrooms, the mu sic moves between structure and improvisation, which has long been associated with storytelling. Jazz offers a rich history and tools through which to make sense of th e complexity of learning and identity building that can take place when young people are given opportunities to examine their own stories. For example, as musician s improvise to come up with new melodies to complimen t a composed song, they simultaneously recall the i deas put forth by the players who have come before and recreate the genre by engaging with new ideas from the world around them (Berliner, 1994). Miles Davis (1962), the jazz trumpet player, said that every time he played he had something to say, but it was up to the audience to be listening. Classroom interactions hold similar improvisations (e.g., Philip, 2019; Sawyer, 2004). Schooling, like jazz music, is steeped in structures defined by wh iteness and connected to the oppression of historically marginalized people. However, amidst these structures exists a wealth of counterstories that allow us to speculate toward future worlds. Jazz could be a tenuous framework for a study that takes place in a mostly white, rural school by white researchers and teachers. However, as a discipline,  jazz is built upon a rich history of disrupting in teractions, educational spaces, and practices that were historically defined or usurped by whiteness. As a learning scientist, I see jazz as an essential blending of cultural tools  and practices to reimagine the world (Solis, 2009). Jazz in the U.S. was initially created by Black musicians as an  assertion of agency, dismantling the chordal patte rns and melodic phrasing of Western classical music (for an example of early 20th century jazz, hear Armstrong’s (1962) “Mack the Knife”). The discipline, and its musicians, have continued to dismantle structures of whiteness in music by continuing to posit reconceptualizations of harm ony and rhythm, playing within a group, and improvi sing outside the confines of traditional song structure (Gioia, 2011). As a musician, I see jazz as an entry point intone w ways of disrupting white supremacy in myself and in my work. North American jazz music grew out of acts of restorying, melding multiple cultures in the decades after slavery was abolished. Drawing from African call-and-response, improvisation, and polyrhythm, as well as Western harmonies and dance-based compositions, the  music carries multiple stories to diverse audience s simultaneously (Gioia, 2011). Jazz offers us a set of tools for analyzing the ways that cultural stories are leveraged, engaged, and restoried in classrooms and a way to t hink about students and teachers as improvisers wit hin the constraints of traditional schooling. In Elizabeth’s classroom, jazz emerged as a set of tools through which I could better understand students’ interactions. As I listened to Elizabeth’s students assert their stories, I was reminded of the ways that jazz musicians trade themes, improvise new ideas and perspectives, and reimagine the world. Restorying Restorying has deep roots in traditions of storytelling, particularly as a practice of counter-storying through which individuals with historically marginalized identiti es can speak back to systems and structures of powe r. In education, counter-stories can serve to question th e deficit perspectives and assumptions of what coun ts as knowledge found in traditional classrooms (Thomas & Stornaiuolo, 2016). Recent scholarship on “(re)storying” has emerged as a digital storytelling practice. “[A ] process by which young people reshape narratives to reflect perspectives and experiences that have been routinely marginalized or silenced” (Stornaiuolo & Thomas, 2018, p. 346). Stornaiuolo and Thomas (2018) theorized re storying as “a way young people use digital tools t o write themselves into existence, first narrating and anal yzing their lived experiences and then synthesizing  and recontextualizing those stories to represent a dive rsity of perspectives and reshape dominant narrativ es.” These authors have pointed to the potential of restorying  with youth to “reconfigure school spaces” (p. 346). They suggested that when youth are offered opportunities  to “re-author themselves and the institutional spa ces they encounter,” they are also supported in historicizin g the self, particularly with regard to “intersecti onal forms of systemic inequity and oppression” (p. 346). This form of restorying with students becomes a practice of supporting students in “selfmaking” and suggests that restorying with students holds potential as a rich practice to shift toward justice-oriented pedagogies and perspectives within classrooms. Jazz as Tools for Restorying Jazz as a dialogic music (Santi & Zorzi, 2016) offers a means through which to understand dialogue in education as part of the inherent structure of learning to communicate together (Bakhtin, 1981). Scroggins (2019) and Philip

(2019) have each written about the potential of critical improvisation to inform teacher’s justice-oriented practices.

Though they draw primarily from improvisatory theat re, these scholars add critical lenses to jazz-orie nted perspectives that improvisation within teaching lea ds to more creative support of students by attendin g to the positionality of the classroom participants (Solis,  2009). In improvisation, each participants’ ideas hold possibility; the history and structure of the artform dictates how these ideas either act productively or antithetically within the collaborative whole. Structures can be changed, but must be done so with purpose (Berliner, 1994). As ICLS2022 Proceedings 688 © ISLS Philip (2019) acknowledges, in education, improvisa tion must be “purposefully oriented toward justice”  to “renegotiate power” toward more humanizing perspectives (p. 4-5). In classrooms, youth often draw on improvisatory mo ves—trading and extending each other’s ideas through theme and variation—to establish third spaces (Gutiérrez, 2008) that honor their own funds of knowledge and agency as historical actors (Gutiérrez et al., 2019). The multivoicedness of these educational env ironments can be theorized as forms of polyphony and polyrhythm, what Kuby and Christ (2018) describe as “the ways that a number of ideas and perspectives compose, harmonize, and play simultaneously without loss” (p. 294). In this paper, I take up these tools from jazz to better un derstand the process that young people engage in wh en they assert ideas about themselves and their communities with one another, using dialogue to build shared knowledge (Kinloch & San Pedro, 2014). As Gutiérrez et al. (2 019) describe, this dialogic process is incomplete without engaging in acts of historicizing that support young people in seeing themselves as historical actors whose actions and interactions are part of a broader historical landscape. Building on a foundation of historicizing, Thomas and Stornaiuolo (2016) examined the ways that young people can “write themselves into existence” (p. 346) through acts of restorying that offer opportunities to reimagine how their stories are inscribed in communities. In this study, restorying appeared as an act of resistance for young people from historically marginalized communities as they engaged with the world around them and reasserted their own perspectives into dominant narratives.

## Methods

This study took place at Williams High, a high school in the mountain west United States, over the course of the 2018-2019 school year across three 9th grade English language arts classrooms. Elizabeth Murray, who identified as white and female, had been teaching for 14 years at the beginning of our study. Williams High served nearly 1,500 students, 20% of whom identified as people of  color and 30% of whom were identified as qualifyin g for free and reduced lunch. The 58 students who participated in this study resembled the demographics of the school. This study was surrounded by violent political rhet oric leading up to the 2020 U.S. presidential elect ion. More than once, I entered the building to hear small groups of students discussing the benefits of a border wall. In the years after Trump was elected, Elizabeth reported r esponding to increased bullying and worried about h ow to offer her students a safe school environment.

Elizabeth and I drew from participatory design rese arch and feminist ethnography because of their explicit attention to power, caring, and emotionality. Elizabeth and I designed curriculum and also shared meals, folded laundry, and weeded her garden. I see these interactions as part of our improvisations together  as we designed our study. In her classroom, Elizabeth led students to engage with a number of writing experiences that we called the Throughlines project. Students read excerpts from Freire and other critical scholars, engaged with their own choice texts, analyzed social and news me dia pieces developed in their community, and wrote about their own experiences and the experiences of their communities. Throughout these experiences we asked students to consider “what stories are missing from the narr atives you see every day in your school and communi ty?” Students were then tasked with developing digital n arratives that would begin to fill the gaps they no ted in the stories they encountered every day.

Using ethnographic methods of data collection and analysis, we looked for patterns in her classroom. I collected classroom and design artifacts, 67.5 hours of observation recordings, fieldnotes, reflective memos, and at least two interviews with a sample of 22 students and Elizabeth. Following jazz as method, I also wrote short pieces of fiction as part of my analysis. I treated these pieces as improvisations, giving myself room to understand my data without immediately pinning my analysis to the people within this study. After this improvisatory exercise, I returned to the data and looked for counterpoints to deepen my understanding of the students’ and El izabeth’s experiences.

I began my data analysis in concert with examining jazz as an emerging framework. I reviewed weekly audio recordings and fieldnotes, looking for examples of jazz tools being used by students in the classroom. This process began by analyzing student interactions that resembled trading, a dialogic practice of improvisation that constrains musicians to developing new ideas in a limited time frame. Discussions with Elizabeth about the ways her students’ “off-topic” conversations might instead be framed as improvisatory moves shifted the ways that we designed daily lessons. Other tools such as theme and variation, polyphony and polyrhythm, and swing became codes that both emerged inductively that I applied deductively to guide me to jazz and restorying as a framework. Together, Elizabeth and I worked to hear the stories students told and the ways their restorying resembled jazz.

## Findings and Discussion

The students included in this paper are examples of  the ways that many of the students with identities  that had been “shattered by persistent structural inequality” (Stornaiuolo & Thomas, 2018, p. 352) restoried themselves as powerful agents able to disrupt injustice. Theme and Variation: Playing until you find what to say Players restory in jazz by identifying musical motifs within a composition and then using improvisation to play variations on those themes. Elizabeth’s students al so restoried around themes throughout the year. Whi le identifying thematic ideas is a practice of ELA, in Elizabeth’s classroom, we not only asked students to identify themes they read in literature, but how they might take up those themes and shift them for their own p urposes. Students identified themes as they read and wrote, and then worked together to create variations on those themes, some of which included stories of trauma, creativity, family, and justice. Leaf, a student who identified as Transgender (capi talized in accordance with Leaf’s preferences) and part of the LGBTQIIA+ community, drew upon his gend er identity and the bullying he had faced as the th eme around which he improvised across his projects. This began with a profile he wrote about himself at the beginning of the year, where he described a “complicated’ relationship with his father, which he explained was because his father “struggled to accept me.” In his profile, Le af also discussed his respect for Justin Blake, the Transgender YouTube star who had spoken out about his own mental health and coping with bullying when he was in school. “I respect Justin Blake a lot. He’s gotten so much crap and gotten discriminated against, but he still continues to put out so much positivity. That’s really hard to d o.” In a reflection on his first project, Leaf wrot e, “I want to raise awareness about Bullying. Yeah, there’s signs saying no bullying or whatever but when it happens  no one does anything. I hate that because I know how it feels and words hurt when they build up.” In his narrative project, Leaf again drew from his own experiences being bullied because of his gender identity. He wrote an essay entitled “Mental Health Awareness,” in which he described interviewing his friends to ask, “Is mental health talked about enough?” Lea f reported his findings expertly, weaving quotes fr om interviews into his prose, offering examples of his  own experiences, and calling for increased awarene ss in the community. However, he also chose to have his story  password protected, explaining “I only want those I give the password to see it.” Although Leaf repeatedly wrote in reflections about the importance of creating awareness about the connections between mental health, identity, and bullying, he was not yet ready to present that message to his peers beyond a small group he selected. In a n interview he explained, “I kind of have a support  network, you could say, but not really, my family doesn’t really understand me…I don’t really have anybody now, I guess you could say.” Leaf explained that he felt isolate d because “there aren’t really people like me here. ” He also emphasized how important it was to him to feel like he was giving to others and noted, “Bonding with new people feels amazing” and that he really wanted to find ways to talk to people about the bullying he had experienced. Throughout the year, we observed that as Leaf share d his narratives with small groups of peers, he appeared to grow more confident. When Leaf began a new project in which students were prompted to iden tify “what it means to be human,” he also began to vary his theme of mental health to include perspectives on diversity as a benefit to society. In this iteration of his t heme and variation, he also began to articulate exc itement about being able to create a message that he could present “especially to teachers and counselors here [at Williams and at Dover Middle School], I think they really need to know more about diversity.” In a shift from previous projects, Leaf actively sought feedback on his messaging from  his teacher and new groups of peers. As he refined  his presentation, he began naming gender identity as part of the diversity that defined humanity. He created a digital presentation to use as a resource for his classmates and the school’s counselors. While he was creating his presentation, protests ag ainst a Drag Queen Story Hour at the local library began. While Elizabeth and I were concerned about the ways Leaf might feel his story was being placed in public view, in a final interview he acknowledged the prot ests and then explained that he knew people “were c losed minded” but focused on the fact that nearly 300 peo ple had also joined the counterprotest. In his fina l project, Leaf affirmed his own identities, acknowledging that his feelings of difference and diversity were exa ctly what made him valuable and necessary. In it he wrote, “Being different is a way to spread that difference. Transfer your experiences with others so that they can learn and maybe feel more valid and accepted.” By improvising around themes of identity, mental health, and diversity, h e found a variation in messaging that allowed him t o restory himself into the narratives in his community.

Stornaiuolo and Thomas (2018) “theorized restorying as a way young people use digital tools to write themselves into existence, first narrating and anal yzing their lived experiences and then synthesizing  and recontextualizing those stories to represent a diversity of perspectives and restory dominant narratives.” They also explore the ways acts of restorying can “reconfigur e school spaces” (p. 346). For Leaf, this reconfigu ring took place over the year as he examined and improvised t o find the ways that his own identities could be pa rt of the dominant narratives he heard each day in Dover. Lea f shifted from explaining the importance of talking  about ICLS2022 Proceedings 690 © ISLS diversity and mental health to just a few of his trusted peers, to speaking about his own experiences and advocating for others like him by sharing his experiences and stories. In an interview at the end of the year, I asked how he decided to speak about his own diversity and identity. He said, [This year] I started changing and understanding myself better. It’s kind of like, well, I’ve been in that spot before, depression and everything, and so I’ll do everything I can, no matter if it’s a person I hate, I don’t want anybody to go through that because it’s a lot to go through. Not a lot of people pay attention…it’s just really important to me. For Leaf, talking about his identities became a form of activism, a way to help others by restorying the narratives around him to acknowledge differences and be more i nclusive. Like a jazz musician, exploring variation s on a theme, it took Leaf time to recognize his own story  and then to find the ways that he would feel empow ered to share that story and assert his own humanity.

Polyphony & Polyrhythm: Centering family as counterstory In jazz, polyphony and polyrhythm refer to many sou nds and rhythms simultaneously. Jazz theorists ofte n use these terms to describe the layering of instruments  that creates unique textures and voicings within a  piece of music (Berliner, 1994). Together, these layered sounds allow for dialogue between musicians, a kind of musical heteroglossia (Bakhtin, 1981; Wertsch, 1991), where  multiple voices emerge using different discourses over different time frames. In Elizabeth’s classroom, polyphony and polyrhythm acted as tools to observe the multiple discourses and timeframes that young people used as they added to narratives in their communities. For example, some students restoried through a numb er of separate counterstories across the year to disrupt dominant narratives in her family, school, and community. Jazmine (who identified as Latina), for example, appeared to restory slowly and differently across each project, writing her way into the perspectives she wanted to share. On the other hand, Truc (who identified as South Asian and male) appeared to know immediately what kind of story was missing from the community. Truc seemed a popular student, friendly with nearly everyone in Elizabeth’s second hour classroom. Although he was quiet in class, he articulated his ideas clearly; o n a survey that he saw himself as capable, even if he “didn’t always like English.” When Elizabeth and I presente d the narrative project, most students had questions about what it meant to identify the dominant narratives they engaged with each day. Truc, however, immediately began to write, sitting in the back of the classroom, ignoring most of the interactions of his peers. In my fieldnotes, I recorded a moment in the middle of the project wher e I was concerned that Truc was lost because he had been so quiet. I approached him and asked how it was going. “Good.” He said, staring at his screen. Not wanting to disrupt him, I walked behind him, expecting to see a game many of the peers he sat with had downloaded. Truc had pages of writing and images already chosen for his webpage. Truc’s story was about his family, a memory that he described in his reflection as “ordinary,” though he acknowledged that it was “really different than any thing that would happen here.” In his story, Truc d escribed visiting Chicago’s Chinatown with his parents and m embers of his extended family. His writing was extr emely descriptive, with “streaks of shallow brightness” a nd “doors clanging continuously,” as well as the sm ells of “perfume and plastic,” “black coffee and green tea” making his experience “even more nostalgic.” Truc described his disappointment with what he had ordered, writing, “I requested whatever my mom had, so I was given a bowl of rice, no more than two handfuls, and a piece of bread on the side.” He described his disgust at the  bread, alluding to how much he had wished to have tried some of the other foods that made this “one of the most saturated memories I have.” Elizabeth and I both celebrated Truc’s writing. In his audio reflection, he explained that he told this story because although his family made him move to Dover from a larger city, “family is of course important. Family is one of the main factors in molding the person you are.” For Truc, this story seemed to be a way to assert his heritage and one of his identities as a person who really valued his family. He later reflected that he missed the diversity of the city, and that diversity was something he was trying to portray with his story. Similarly, in his interview at the end of the year, Truc continued to elaborate, explaining, I really liked the story project. I could write abo ut it and keep going as long as I wanted…I chose this moment because I just remembered it real ly well. I moved to [this state] because there were better jobs for my family, but Dover is just really different from there. Just how people talk, the attitude of people. I don’t know how to explain it, there’s less diversity, it’s fine, I guess, I just feel like I wrote it for myself.

Like a musician who d raws on personal and ancestral histories to invoke polyrhythmic polyphony, Truc drew from his past and his family to add his story to the largely white narratives that emphasized different values and experiences from his own in Dover. In doing so, he also recognized himself as the most important audience for his restorying. Improvising and Extending: Restorying as choice As jazz performers grow in expertise, they also build new ways to extend improvisations, sometimes even playing outside of the written chord changes to reimagine a  composed piece into something entirely new (Gioia, 2011). For Stornaiuolo and Thomas (2018), this kind of cre ative innovation is central to restorying, particul arly with historically marginalized youth. In Elizabeth’s classroom, we saw several students make moves to quest ion the dominant narratives they were observing by improvising around the structures we offered for telling their stories throughout the year. In some instances, these impro visations became full of extensions of the projects. For example, Levi and Brandon (who both identified as w hite and male), shifted their project on the second amendment to hosting gun safety classes when they r ealized the correlation between accidental death and gun ownership. While these actions were still aligned w ith the project goals, they went much farther than Elizabeth had required and created meaningful possibilities for learning. Bailey was another student who took up the projects  that Elizabeth presented and adapted them, restorying her English class assignment by assignme nt to meet her own aims. Bailey (who identified as white, female, and physically disabled) was an extremely q uiet student who spent the entire year in Elizabeth ’s fourth period class. She had been born with a congenital l imb difference and in my fieldnotes, I noted that s he carried the same denim coat nearly every day and would pull the jacket over her right arm if other students glanced in her direction. While I never recorded bullying, Bailey emerged as someone I paid attention to, first to en sure her safety, and then as I noted the ways she had reframed the projects to fit her own ideas throughout the year. In her survey at the beginning of the year, Bailey noted that she had “never been in an English class before.” However, this seemed to refer to “high school English class,” as she had attended middle school with a number of the other students in her class. She also identified herself as “very creative” and “a writer,” and noted that she felt she “mattered a tremendous amount” and “completely belonged” in Elizabeth’s classroom. Throughout much of the year, she sat at the front o f the classroom, often with two other students who also identified as white, female, and neurodivergent. Throughout the projects, Bailey voiced her commitment to being creative and adapted nearly every assignment to wor k on fantasy and fan-fiction narratives in which sh e reimagined the world as a place of “magic and adven ture” as well as “sometimes a really bad place.” Al though Bailey’s writing did not, at first glance, critique  the structures of society, her commitment to writi ng what she wanted seemed to be a way of restorying her ELA cla ss and each of the stories she constructed focused on a character who had a physical difference from their peers and families. For example, in “Pies,” these differences were punished until the character discovered a way to escape or change the situation in which they fou nd themselves. (In this story, the main character is locked in a basement because of her ability to do magic, escaping only when she learns to use her powers.) In others, such as the fan-fiction piece she wrote entitled “The Legend of Zelda Day and Night,” the main character’s differences are mentioned in the opening, but do not seem to impact the character as they move throughout the story. Al though Bailey did not reference these characteristi cs in her analyses of her writing, she did explain that she felt it was important to “have a lot of people represented.” When asked in an interview how she had approached t he class, Bailey answered, “I was creative…I wrote what I wanted to write.” As she evaluated her work using the standards Elizabeth had asked her students to meet, Bailey also explained that she thought she could meet all of them through her fiction, so she had chosen to work in that genre. For Bailey, restorying was an act of questioning the ways that English language arts class was set up and then refusing that structure to engage in the kinds of writing that would further her own goals. Jazz moves forward and expands because of the innovations of musicians who improvise outside of the boundaries and traditions of the music; however, this only occurs when they know the rules they’re breaking. Like jazz musicians in their genre, Bailey was still working within the genre of ELA, she reframed the boundaries of Elizabeth’s assignments and projects, adding her own “sound” and to get what she wanted from the class. As she ICLS2022 Proceedings 692 © ISLS explained, “The most important thing to me is creat ivity. Being original. Like, making your own things  and sometimes they can be inspired by other people, their creativity can inspire your creativity.” While Bailey’s stories did not directly critique the structures of schooling as did the stories of some of her peers, I saw her commitment to her own development as a writer as an act of res torying because she asserted a different set of val ues into a school system that had defined learning as a pursuit of the standards. Thomas and Stornaiuolo (2016) described restorying as “a participatory textual practice” (p. 313) that becomes a dialogue between two ways of being. For Bailey, restorying ELA participation meant disrupting power structures of the classroom to define her experience. Across Elizabeth’s classes, students like Leaf, Tru c, and Bailey “write themselves into existence” (Stornaiuolo & Thomas, 2018, p. 345) by restorying the narratives that they were allowed to claim with in their families, classrooms, and communities. For students like Leaf, restorying took place over the course of the entire year, as he solidified the ways he wanted to claim his own stories and then decided upon the variations that would most honor his identities. Other students knew the stories they wanted to add to their community almos t immediately. Students like Truc restoried quickly, adding his own stories to Dover’s narratives to ass ert that families like his deserved representation, even outside of the “big city.” Then there were students like Bailey, who not only decided to write fanfiction to make her own story visible in her favorite games, but also decided that the entire ELA classroom needed to be restoried in order to meet her needs.

## Significance

Jazz is deeply intertwined with justice. It has bee n the soundtrack of protests and the continuing fig ht for civil rights. As a music, its very existence is dependent on the restorying of white supremacist narratives to illustrate the multiplicity of stories and voices that have existed throughout the United States’ history. As these narratives are perpetuated insidiously in this country, jazz too continues to evolve, its artists extending the music intonew forms, adding new variations to compositions and playing outside the changes, catching their audience off-guard while pushing the music forward. In Elizabeth’s classroom, the students we observed restorying were mostly those whose identities had been historically marginalized, whether in terms of race, culture, socioeconomic status, gender identity, or ability. Restorying for these s tudents was an act of justice, claiming their right  to tell their stories told in the spaces where they lived. As these students became more confident in the themes of their stories and the variations they wanted to tell, they also b egan to push the boundaries of their classroom, tak ing up new ways of interacting and producing content in order to honor their own ways of knowing. As Elizabeth said at the end of the year, isn’t that what we want for our students? As Elizabeth’s students were supported to tell thei r stories, we also saw them make moves toward epistemic agency. I think of epistemic agency as the power to map one’s stories onto the learning that takes place in school. In the push for inclusive and culturally  sustaining schooling, teachers have often been tasked with determining how to include students’ cultural knowledge in the classroom. However, in this study, we aimed to support students in determining how their own cultural knowledge could shift classroom learning. When students are supported with justice-oriented practices, they  have the potential to reconsider the world and the  ways that they move through it. For students from historically marginalized backgrounds this is often an act of restorying. For students from dominant backgrounds, it is often  an act of questioning, beginning to discuss the hi stories of their privilege in order to shift perspectives. In an era characterized by a constant questioning of truth, it is more pivotal than ever that English teachers work to sup port their students in developing the skills to rea d the world and see themselves as powerful with potential to assert more humanizing forms of knowledge. Jazz as Methodological Toolkit Design research results not only in empirical findi ngs, but also in the development of methods and the ory. By structuring this study around jazz, I found that jazz became not only a metaphor, but a set of methodological tools through which I could make sense of the moves that students and Elizabeth made. Questions of structure  and agency are enduring in education and anthropology; jazz as a tool kit provides a way to look at these questions from new perspectives in relation to human learning. For me, jazz is about joy. White supremacy lands heavily on all of us, even those of us whom it offers privilege. Music is ultimately about joy, and jazz is a direct expression of Black joy in the United States. Even in a mostly  white, rural setting, and perhaps specifically because of this context, tools that speak to creativity and joy and  triumph are exactly the counterpoint necessary to reimagine what learning can look like in these spaces. As we continue to encounter violence against Black people, while allowing education in highly segregated spaces, then we will continue to see white supremacy proliferate. Students need support to question their own histories and narratives. In our current reality—where police killings of Black men and boys perpetuate, where the COVID-19 pandemic continues to take the lives of people of color and people living in poverty at alarming rates, and where syst emic oppression means that many people in the U.S. do not have enough to eat—helping students to reflect on their stories to disrupt inequity carries specific urgency. ICLS2022 Proceedings 693 © ISLS Taking up new ideologies and ways of knowing in education has the potential to disrupt this cycle. Especially when we draw from tools that have succes sfully disrupted these perspectives in other genres  and disciplines. Jazz tools offer a means of observing classrooms as holistic ecologies where every move h as ramifications and improvisations can be traced. The se tools also offer a way toward refusal (Tuck & Ya ng, 2014)— a means of reframing the world and refusing the framework of white supremacy. These tools offer researchers and educators new possibilities for “world making” (Solis, 2009), not only as a set of analytic tools, but also in the future as a toolkit for design, a w ay to listen deeply to the stories of our participa nts as we build new kinds of learning environments. In studies that claim criticality, making space for these new worlds based on the stories of teachers and students should be our top priority.
