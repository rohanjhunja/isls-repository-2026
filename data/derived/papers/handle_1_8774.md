# Emergent Social and Emotional Learning Outcomes Through Collaborative Reasoning Discussions

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Social emotional learning (SEL) has been identified as critical to meet students’ needs. This study examined the potential of a small group discussion approach, Collaborative Reasoning (CR), on supporting students’ SEL. Transcripts of debriefings after CR discussions and students’ post-study interviews were analyzed for indicators of interpersonal competencies. Findings suggested students benefited in social awareness and relationship building skills after taking part in a series of CR discussions.

## Introduction

The global experience of the Covid-19 pandemic has drawn increased attention to the social and emotion al learning (SEL) needs of students. Yoder et al. (2020) found that more than two-thirds of states in the United States have identified SEL as a top priority and that more  than four-fifths of states said that SEL has incre ased in importance during the pandemic. At the same time, schools are addressing concerns about academic learning gaps that have arisen even in well thought out pandemic responses (Engzell et al., 2021). It is clear that schools need to consider and implement practices that simultaneously address both academic and SEL needs. The Collaborative for Academic Social and Emotional  Learning (CASEL) has worked to identify programs that best promote the SEL competencies. Th ey recognize that programs may take on different fo rms: explicit instruction in social and emotional skills  and attitudes, interactive teaching practices such  as class discussions, and integration of SEL with academic learning (CASEL, 2020). Among the fifty elementary school programs identified by CASEL only five utilized academic integration a means of supporting SEL. Further, only two of those five tracked and found significantly improved academic outcomes alongside SEL outcomes (CASEL, n.d.). These findings suggest that there is a lack of evidence-based programs that use academic integr ation and interactive pedagogy.

Small group collaborative discussions are one prom ising option for addressing the cognitive and affective learning needs of students through interactive pedagogy. Social constructivist theory (Vygotsky, 1934) suggests that communities of students that learn from and wi th each other create positive social structures to support academic learning while also promoting prosocial be haviors. Collaborative Reasoning (CR) is one model of structuring small group class discussions aligned with both academic and SEL goals. CR is “an instructional method designed to engage elementary school children in group discussions about controversial issues raised in their readings” (Reznitzskaya, et al., 2009). In CR, children read a story and engage in peer-led and teacher-supported interactive argumentation on a Big Question arising from the text. The majority of prior research on CR has demonstrated the positive cognitive outcomes for students experiencing CR, such as enhanced argumentation skills. It is less known, however, whether CR can lead to SEL benefits. Li et al. (2007) identified children’s emergent leadership during CR, which is potentially linked to some SEL skills such as social awareness. Lin et al. (2021) found that CR students exhibited greater peer acceptance and less aggression towards others. This study aimed to expand on this developing body of research around the benefits of CR discussions on children’s SEL competencies. More specifically, we asked: Can CR support students’ development of interpersonal SEL competencies that include social awareness and relationship skills?

## Methods

76 fourth-grade children and their three teachers from an elementary school from northwestern U.S. participated in this six-month study. Two groups of five to eigh t students were randomly selected from each class t o be videotaped during the eight CR discussions. The stories used in the CR discussions were deliberately selected to present multiple perspectives for students to consi der. This included both fictional scenarios (Should a mouse dentist help a fox in pain?) and scenarios about children they might relate to  (Should Tyler be allowed to climb Mt. Everest?). Transcripts of debriefings after each CR discuss ion as well as a post-study decision making interview (n = 68), were coded using the indicators of interpersonal SEL competencies identified in the CASEL literature (CASEL, 2021). ## Results

The discussion debriefings and student interviews r evealed that CR discussions helped students recogni ze different perspectives, one essential component of social awareness. For example, a more talkative student shared in the first debriefing: “…and not interrupting other people even if you're excited about your idea. Wait for the other person to end their idea.” This quote illustrates the student’s developing so cial awareness and the recognition of the need to exercise self-management. As shown in the example in Table 1, students gained greater awareness of their peers’ ideas and strengths by actively listening to each other. Meanwhile, students suggested and implemented prac tices to foster better group interactions, a critic al element of relationship skills. In the second example in Table 1, a student transferred their learning about working as a group to a hypothetical group that was “stuck. ” Other interviews and debriefings included ideas a round effective communication, offering help and support when needed, standing up for the rights of others, practicing teamwork and collaborative problem-solving, and resisting negative peer pressure. One student shared: “I think they should like put it together and see what the ideas are together. Maybe they can work together to make it all work.”  Students that experienced CR were able to move from a largely “I” perspective to greater peer recognition.

## Discussion

The findings of this study support and expand on prior work examining the benefits of CR on students’ SEL competencies. Although still at the preliminary stage, our analyses revealed a promising confirmative answer to our research question, that is, students are lik ely to further develop social awareness and relatio nship skills during CR discussions. This finding is aligned with  previous research on emergent leadership during CR  (Li et al., 2007) where students successfully managed the group discussion, as well as research on the benefits of CR on students’ social relationship with peers (Lin et al., 2021). This finding might particularly interest school administrators and state stakeholders who have been searching for an interactive approach to integrate SEL within academics.
