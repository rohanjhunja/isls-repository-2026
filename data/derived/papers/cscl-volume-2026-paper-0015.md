# Collaboration and Competition in Educational Gameplay: Strategic Student Decision -Making

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how middle school students engaged in strategic decision-making when collaboration and competition were tightly intertwined in an educational gameplay setting. Drawing from a design-based research study of an afterschool program, six students played Isles of Ilkmaar, a multiplayer game designed to support data science learning. Using Activity Theory as an analytic lens, we traced how students mobilized tools, interpreted rules, and negotiated roles in the game’s diner competition. Analysis of three gameplay episodes showed students adapting in real time, moving from tool learning to contested claims and finally to rule negotiation. Findings highlight that collaboration and competition were not separate modes of activity but operated together, with conflicts often catalyzing adaptation and role change. This work contributes to CSCL by showing how collaborative learning can be sustained under competitive pressures, offering implications for the design of educational environments that integrate both cooperative and adversarial elements.

Real-time educational gameplay often requires students to engage in both collaboration and competition. When balanced, these dynamics can strengthen participation and learning (Qiao et al., 2024; Wang & Huang, 2023). Collaboration supports problem-solving and shared progress, while competition drives persistence and performance (Plass et al., 2013). Rather than opposites, prior research shows that collaboration and competition often intersect, shaping how students approach shared tasks (Pareto et al., 2012). In this study, we examine collaboration and competition in an educational multiplayer game world. We draw from a design-based research study in which middle school girls in an afterschool club played Isles of Ilkmaar, a newly developed educational game to support data science learning. Isles of Ilkmaar is a multiplayer game where players generate, analyze, and share data about their own gameplay while building relationships with creature inhabitants and solving mysteries across interconnected islands. The game was developed through a codesign process involving youth, researchers, and a gaming studio to create a socially engaging data science experience (Radke et al., 2024).

In Isles of Ilkmaar, players have multiple paths to success. Collaboration is not required, but gameplay is often more efficient when players coordinate their efforts. For instance, players can trade foraged items or divide responsibilities into a diner space where they prepare food for creatures, combining strengths to speed up progress. At the same time, players can view their own and others’ data in the game, enabling competition across objectives. This design meant collaboration offered advantages, while competition introduced urgency that shaped decisions.

This paper investigates how a group of middle school students (n = 6) participating in a STEM afterschool program managed strategic decision-making under collaborative and competitive pressures during a two-hour after-school gameplay session of Isles of Ilkmaar. In the study session, three groups joined an informal team-based competition at Isles of Ilkmaar’s diner (Figure 1). In the diner, players prepared and served meals for creatures, requiring teammates to coordinate while simultaneously competing with rivals. Competition was guided by a leaderboard data display that showed the foods produced and their ratings from creatures. The leaderboard was not a fixed mechanic, but an improvised system created by students using the game’s data displays, which turned available information into a running tally of performance. ## Literature review: Collaboration and competition in educational gameplay

Collaboration in serious games supports shared problem-solving and knowledge construction through cognitive and socioemotional interactions, shared artifacts, and the interplay of technology and pedagogy (Xia et al., 2025). In game-based learning, collaboration can reduce cognitive load, enhance reasoning, and deepen understanding, but these benefits depend on peer interactions being intentionally structured around shared knowledge goals (Moon & Ke, 2020). It also requires regulation of cognitive, motivational, and emotional processes, which tools such as scripts and awareness systems can support (Miller & Hadwin, 2015). Left unstructured, collaboration often leads to superficial exchanges; however, defining roles and incorporating conflict-based tasks can f oster deeper interaction and improve outcomes without reducing motivation (Vander Meij et al., 2011). Competition in educational games can motivate but also introduce challenges. When framed constructively, it encourages persistence and can improve coordination and group performance (Chen & Hwang, 2017). At the same time, it can create uneven outcomes and generate anxiety for lower-performing students (Cagiltay et al., 2015; ter Vrugte et al., 2015). It may also strain peer relationships and limit cooperative problemsolving, making facilitation critical to prevent negative effects (Yu, 2001; Almås & Giæver, 2024). These dynamics are often embedded together in digital game-based environments, where collaboration supports teamwork and competition creates urgency and shapes resource management (Qiao et al., 2024). Related work on gamification and collaborative learning systems shows that structured game mechanics and shared digital environments can scaffold coordination and participation across learners (Deterding et al., 2011). When both are present, student players not only coordinate tasks but also balance pressures from rival groups, producing complex patterns of participation (Moon & Ke, 2020).

Beyond structured roles, other social interactions can reshape collaborative and competitive dynamics and impact learning. Humor, peer-led negotiation, and creative tool use can transform how tasks unfold, often extending beyond curricular goals (Kim & Ho, 2018). Such practices include revisiting goals, adjusting strategies on the fly, and introducing playful exchanges such as spontaneous challenges or shared jokes that sustain engagement and foster ethical reflection (Pervez & Orchard, 2025). These adaptations highlight collaboration and competition as evolving features of gameplay rather than fixed conditions. Taken together, prior research shows that collaboration and competition interact fluidly in digital gamebased learning environments. They shape how students pursue goals and interpret rules, tools, and social dynamics. This backdrop frames our analysis of how these dynamics unfolded in our gameplay setting, where students engaged in data practices such as analyzing and making inferences from datasets and displays. Our study examines how collaboration and competition drive decision-making that supports social, personally meaningful learning with data. By situating these dynamics within data science learning, our research extends prior work by showing how they shape participation and mediate how students generate, interpret, and apply data in real time.

## Theoretical framework: Activity theory

Given the role of rules, tools, and social dynamics in shaping interactions and learning experiences in multiplayer educational game environment s, this study adopts Activity Theory to analyze strategic decision-making under collaborative and competitive pressures. Activity Theory, developed by Engeström (1987) and extended to digital learning contexts (Karanasios et al., 2021), views purposeful activity as the unit of analysis. In CSCL environments, digital tools and shared representations mediate collaboration, providing a lens for examining how artifacts shape interaction  (Suthers, 2006). Other CSCL research has illustrated the interdependence of tools, 52 goals, rules, and roles in structuring human action (Fischer et al., 2013; Danish et al., 2020), making Activity Theory well-suited for analyzing student decision-making in game-based environments. Figure 2 presents the Activity Theory framework used in this study, adapted from Engeström (1987). Applied to the Isles of Ilkmaar  game world and informal club context, the subject is middle school students engaged in gameplay, with the object defined as serving creatures efficiently within the diner setting. In-game tools such as recipe log, icebox, heat lamp, data tables, and leaderboard mediate student action, while rules include both formal game mechanics and informal social norms. The community compris es peers, teachers, researchers, and rival teams, and roles are flexibly distributed. Within Activity Theory, contradictions are systemic tensions that arise within or between these elements (Engeström, 1987). Here, contradictions refer to moments when collaborative and competitive goals conflict or overlap, prompting students to reorganize strategies and roles. Such tensions are productive rather thandisruptive, marking points where collective activity transforms through negotiation and adaptation.

In the following findings, we analyze selected gameplay episodes to show how talk, action, and data practices were intertwined, offering a micro-level view of strategic decision-making under the dual pressures of cooperation and rivalry.

The larger project took place during an eight-week afterschool program with five African American girls ages 11–12 who played Isles of Ilkmaar  together in researcher-facilitated sessions. This analysis focuses on the final two-hour session, which included solo, paired, and team play. Students accessed the game individually on laptops using their own avatars before forming pairs (one student team ed with the club teacher) for a timed diner competition. Due to one participant leaving early, our analysis focuses on one team of two students and on one student–teacher team. The one-hour rule required players to prepare and serve as many accurate dishes as possible, with winners determined by speed and creature ratings (ranging from worst to perfect, though only good, great, and perfect were scored, weighted 1–3) shown on the in-game leaderboard (Figure 3). Students coordinated tasks for accuracy and efficiency while competing for rankings. All participants had prior experience with Isles of Ilkmaar from earlier sessions, which involved interpreting data tables, managing ingredients and tools, and responding to creature preferences.

Episode 3: Adapting through conflict and rule enforcement In one of the more contested diner moments, Riley and the teacher adapted their strategy after Cookie and Brownie repeatedly used the shared icebox for their advantage (Excerpt 3). Riley tried to block competitors by moving all available ingredients into her personal inventory (Turn 1), seizing control of a key tool. Brownie countered by dismissing the move and asserting her team’s control over progress (Turn 2), while the teacher asked Riley to redistribute items to keep their team coordinated (Turn 3), invoking the division of labor. Instead, Riley suggested a trade (Turn 4), treating resource exchange as a new rule for fairness. Cookie reinforced her team’s d ominance with a reminder of their earlier lead (Turn 5), positioning herself within the community hierarchy. As tension grew, the teacher clarified the rule that items had to be placed in the icebox before cooking (Turn 6). Unaware of this constraint, Riley attempted to cook directly from her inventory (Turn 7), prompting the teacher to correct her and reassert procedural rules (Turn 8), explaining the proper steps (Turn 8). Afterwards, Riley and the teacher began timing their actions more carefully, placing ingredients in the icebox and quickly retrieving them to finish tasks while minimizing further interference from the other teams. This study contributes to CSCL by showing how collaborative and competitive dynamics function as intertwined forms of learning and participation in game-based environments. In the Isles of Ilkmaar  diner setting, students coordinated responsibilities while monitoring how their group compared against rivals on the leaderboard.  This dual focus made decision-making both cooperative and competitive, requiring players to maintain efficiency. Consistent with prior CSCL and game-based learning research on the co-occurrence of collaboration and competition (Qiao et al., 2024; Wang & Huang, 2023), the analysis extends CSCL research by showing that collaboration and competition can be intertwined, prompting participation under layered pressures. From an Activity Theory perspective, the findings illustrate how contradictions among rules, tools, and roles shaped student choices and adaptations. The diner’s tools (recipe log, icebox, heat lamp, and leaderboard) created overlapping demands: rules of efficiency pressed players to keep meals moving, while competitive comparison heightened the urgency of each action. Students’ talk about standings, and their adjustments to who served versus who prepared, illustrated how roles were reconfigured as contradi ctions emerged. This reinforces Engeström’s view that contradictions are productive sites of activity, while showing how they operate in fast-paced digital settings where cooperation and rivalry intersect.

The study further contributes to CSCL and the learning sciences by illustrating how micro-level transcripts of gameplay can illuminate the mechanisms through which students balance collective goals with external competition. Methodologically, integrating transcript excerpts with Activity Theory coding demonstrates how fine-grained interaction analysis can reveal strategic decision-making processes. The findings show that students used adaptive, context-sensitive strategies as they negotiated tools, rules, a nd roles to manage both cooperation and rivalry during gameplay.

This paper examines how middle school students engaged in strategic decision-making when collaboration and competition were tightly intertwined in an educational gameplay setting. From  an Activity Theory perspective, the analysis showed how tools, rules, and shifting responsibilities dynamically shaped student participation. Several limitations should be noted. While our cases support a broader understanding of the nature of competitive and collaborative learning in  CSCL environments, our claims are specific to our setting’s  activity system. In addition, one team included the teacher as a participant, reflecting an interaction structure with distinct power and knowledge relationships  compared to only student-student dynamics (Allen & Hill, 2024), shaping how roles, coordination, and decision-making unfolded during gameplay. Collaboration and competition did not operate as separate modes of activity; instead, they worked together, each shaping the conditions of the other. Students sustained participation by asserting claims, legitimizing actions, and renegotiating responsibili ties, with moments of conflict often serving as catalysts for adaptation rather than breakdowns. This aligns with prior research on productive tension and participation in collaborative learning environments (Engle & Conant, 2002; Engle, 2012). In this way, the analysis shows that cooperative work in game-based environments is inseparable from the competitive pressures that shape it. These results also build on prior work on gamification and collaborative learning in game-based environments (Qiao et al., 2024) by suggesting that competition can be framed as a productive tension, alongside prior work that has documented its potential ne gative effects (Cagiltay et al., 2015; ter Vrugte et al., 2015), that pushes students tonegotiate fairness, accountability, and efficiency under real-time constraints. When balanced with collaborative structures, such tensions can mirror authentic problem-solving environments where learners must manage shared goals while navigating external pressures. This connects to broader learning sciences perspectives on productive struggle, including productive failure in game contexts (Anderson et al., 2018) and productive disciplinary engagement in collaborative communities (Engle & Conant, 2002; Engle, 2012).  Taken together, our study highlights how participation is sustained under layered social and structural demands in gamebased learning environments.
