# Supporting K-12 Science Teachers in Equitable Science Teaching through Co-design

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
My research explores how to support K-12 science teachers’ professional development toward equitable and justice-centered teaching. Positioning teachers and students as those who decide what matters to them in their local contexts, I leverage design-based research to co-design, test, and co-refine pedagogical tools with practitioners. Through this process, I study teacher learning by drawing upon a professional identity development lens. In my most recent work, I study how to support middle school science teachers in customizing high-quality instructional materials and investigate how curriculum customization catalyzes teacher teaching. Looking forward, I hope to explore how community-based curriculum design and customization help science teachers create equitable learning experiences for their students.

My research agenda focuses on K-12 science teachers’ professional development (PD) as equity-centered practitioners. In my work, I use design-based research (DBR) (Cobb et al., 2003) to partner with teachers to design, test, and refine pedagogical tools to support them in developing equity-and justice-centered practices. Through this process, I draw upon learning as identity development as an analytic lens to study teacher learning (e.g., Holland et al., 1998; Wenger, 1998). Instead of lecturing teachers about the practices they should enact, my work starts with learning about teachers’ and their students’ needs in their local contexts, positioning them as the authoritative figures who decide what is important to them. From there, I collaborate with teachers to figure out how to achieve their pedagogical goals in the service of their students’ needs. Equity is central to my work. In my most recent work, I mainly draw upon Philip and Azevedo’s (2017) four equity discourses, whether when I work with teachers to unpack what equity means or when I operationalize it in data analysis. Instead of using this framework to evaluate the criticality of teachers’ work from a deficit perspective, I focus on helping teachers move towards the discourses that are more critical, namely, expanding what counts as science and positioning science as a tool for social transformation. Methodologically, I adopt a variety of qualitative methods to develop rich accounts of teachers’ lived experiences to inform future PD design. Research trajectory My scholarship on understanding how science teachers develop a professional identity as justice-centered practitioners began in graduate school. Throughout my tenure as a doctoral student, I worked extensively with pre-service science teachers (PSTs) in informal settings to support them in enacting justice-centered, ambitious science teaching (JuST) practices (Luehmann et al., 2024). My dissertation employed a phenomenological narrative design to examine how a group of six PSTs’ engagement in designing and implementing culturally sustaining units anchored in a local rural community contributed to their professional identity development as JuST practitioners. In this work, I drew upon the figured world (Holland et al., 1998) to understand how the PSTs improvised their JuST identities by examining the cultural artifacts they created and their reflections on the design and implementation of the units. Meanwhile, I identified the significant encounters the PSTs recognized as instrumental to their learning across cases. My analysis reveals that centering the cultural relevance in their units allowed the PSTs to develop an expansive understanding of science and their roles as science teachers. It sheds light on the challenges the PSTs experienced and the support they required when they negotiated their JuST identities through their process of becoming.

During my post-doctoral training, my research has expanded to investigate how curriculum-based professional learning (CBPL) supports science teachers in making sense of equity and the pedagogical features embedded in high-quality instructional materials (HQIM). Theorizing curriculum customization as a professional learning site, my post-doctoral work has explored how middle school science teachers customize HQIM guided by their equity goals. Teachers’ interaction with curricular materials and equity goals throughout the customization process potentially yields a more sophisticated teacher learning about both the curriculum and their approach to equity (Remillard, 2005). In the Customize project funded by the National Science Foundation, we employed the DBR approach to collaborate with science teachers from a local school district. I led the efforts at the local research site to engage the participating teachers in professional learning communities (PLCs) to customize an open-source c urriculum that employs a storyline instructional model to support students’ equitable sensemaking. The major research question I pursue in this work is “How does curriculum customization catalyze teacher teaching?” In a first-author manuscript from Customize, I foreground teachers’ work of increasing curricular relevance to students. Bringing together a humanistic science lens (Aikenhead, 2005) and Philip and Azevedo’s

(2017) four equity discourses, the article investigates how the participating teachers grappled with the idea of

“making the curriculum more relevant to students” by focusing on “who decides what is relevant for what equity purpose”. Informed by Remillard’s (2005) framework on the participatory relationship between teachers and the curriculum, I attended to the new or deeper knowledge teachers developed through their navigation of the customized lesson and the original lesson. Using thematic coding, I analyzed the video recordings of the PLC sessions, audio recordings of teachers’ reflections on their enactment, and the teacher-generated artifacts. Findings highlight the complexity of “being relevant to students” within the equity discourses and reveal the participatory relationship the teachers navigated among themselves, their equity goals, and the curriculum (Remillard, 2005). Alongside my post-doctoral project on teacher learning through curriculum customization, I continued collaborating with The JuST Lab to develop and refine the JuST framework and investigate how to support teachers’ professional development as JuST practitioners. The JuST lab is a cross-site collaboration between the University of Rochester, the University of Connecticut, and Islandwood Environmental Center. This collaboration originated from our research team’s efforts to co-design a cultural-setting unit with in-practice science teachers in the Greater Rochester area during the COVID-19 pandemic. In the unit, students investigated an anchoring phenomenon of how the pandemic disproportionally impacted people of color. They explored not only the science behind COVID-19 but also the social determinants of health that contributed to health inequities experienced by different population groups. The implementation of the initial unit allowed us to identify the design elements that the teachers recognized as helpful to create a safe space for students to share and discuss critical issues in science classrooms at the beginning of the school year. This initiative later developed into a DRK-12 project across the three research sites. Drawing upon DBR as the primary design, we sought to understand how teachers developed and revised JuST practices in networked PLCs (Thompson et al., 2019). Grounding our work in culturally sustaining pedagogy, we published the Justice-centering Ambitious Science Teaching framework of core practices in 2024 (Luehmann et al., 2024).

Future work In the future, I will continue exploring how to support K-12 science teachers in equitable and justice-centered teaching by conducting DBR research. In particular, I will focus on engaging teachers, family members, and community members in the process of curriculum design, adaptation, and enactment, and study how this process catalyzes teachers’ professional learning toward equity-and justice-centered science teaching. Ultimately, I aim to engage different stakeholders in participatory research design (Bang & Vosoughi, 2016) to investigate what new knowledge will be generated through community-based collaboration on curricular design and customization. For teacher learning, I am especially interested in 1) identifying design principles to support teachers in customizing HQIM to create more equitable learning experiences for their students through that type of collaboration, and 2) exploring how that collaboration will impact teachers’ professional learning through a professional identity development lens.
