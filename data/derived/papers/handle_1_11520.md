# Understanding How Thinking Routines Mediate Student Thinking and Interaction: Insights from a Design-Based Research

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This design-based research explores how to develop curriculum-level instructional design principles integrating thinking routines. Based on our findings from earlier iterations, we revised the conjecture map and implemented the revised instruction in a university English reading class in Taiwan. We examined how thinking routines mediate student thinking and interaction, with the aim of refining the design principles. Research results showed that thinking routines promoted interaction, deeper thinking, and created a thinking classroom ethos.

## Introduction

Although the integration of teaching thinking into instruction has been widely explored (e.g., Ennis, 2018; Ritchhart et al., 2011), effectively embedding it i nto curriculum remains a challenge. To address this, we set out to develop a thinking-centered curriculum. We adopted the Visible Thinking approach (Ritchhart et al., 2011), which promotes thinking through structured routines  that guide students to engage in specific thinking  moves. While prior studies have shown that thinking routines foster thinking abilities and cultures of thinking (Ritchhart,

2015) and that dialogic interaction supports learning (Alexander, 2020), few have examined how they can inform

curriculum-level instructional design.  To address the above, we conducted a multi-year design-based research project to develop Thinking Routines–Embedded Instructional Design Principles in two uni versity-level English courses. In the first three iterations (Chen & Jan, 2024), we identified and refined three design principles (DPs): DP1 focuses on aligning learning objectives, thinki ng moves, thinking routines, and assessment; DP2 in volves adapting and repeating the use of thinking routines; and DP3 emphasizes providing opportunities for gr oup collaboration and discussion using thinking routines. An additional principle, DP4, emerged in the third iteration: creating a classroom ethos where thinking is valued  and supported.  However, the dialogic patterns of student interaction and their engagement in specific thinki ng moves need more in-depth exploration. In addition,  more was to be discovered about how  classroom ethos was built and supported learning. Accordingly, this iteration focused on refining DP3 and DP4 in a new course con text. Our research question is: In what ways do thinking routines mediate learning through group interaction and the development of a thinking classroom ethos?

## Context and method

We employed a design-based research (DBR) methodology  (McKenney & Reeves, 2012)  to investigate how thinking routines mediate student learning and to d evelop curriculum-level instructional design principles. This study represents the fourth iteration, in which we implemented a revised set of design principles in a  university English course, Critical Reading and Discussion. Th e 18-week, 2-credit course enrolled 31 students with intermediate English proficiency. The course aimed to enhance students’ critical reading and discussion of social issues. For example, Circle of Viewpoints guided students in analyzing issues from multiple perspe ctives, while See-Think-Wonder helped them build explanations, connect ideas to personal view and questions. We initially adopted conjecture mapping (Sandoval, 2014) to connect design principles, embodied design, mediation, and learning outcomes. However, the original structure it did not fully capture how our design principles—particularly those concerning group collaboration a nd classroom ethos —evolved or mediated learning. To address this, we drew on the curricular spider w eb (vanden Akker, 2013) to propose a revised conjecture map in order to enable an analysis that captured coherence among curricular design components. We added curricular alignment to the embodiment sectio n to capture coherence across course objectives, co ntent, assessments, materials, and tasks. We also unified task structures, participant structures, and discur sive practices into a single category—learning tasks—to reflect their interdependent nature in classroom interaction. Finally, we added resources and environment to account for the physical setting, instructional materials, and classroom ethos. The adapted conjecture map also served as a framework f or data collection. To examine how DP3 was reified in learning tasks and shaped observable int eraction, we audio-recorded four student groups weekly and selected four representative episodes  from two thinking routine tasks (Circle of Viewpoints and See-ThinkWonder) for in-depth analysis. Group interactions were coded for d ialogic moves and thinking moves, which revealed how TRs-guided group work facilitated group interaction and  assisted students in build ing on each other’s thoughts. For dialogic moves, we used a cod ing scheme adapted from Hennessy et al. (2016), whi ch included expressing ideas (E), building on thoughts (B), positioning (P), reasoning (R), and connecting (C). Four thinking moves were coded drawn from Ritchhart et al. (2011): building explanations and interpretations  (EI), making connections (CO), considering viewpoints (POV), and reasoning with evidence (EV). To investigate how DP4 is embodied and shapes the ethos, we collected 27 student reflections at the end of the semester and conducted thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2012) to examine their perceptions of classroom ethos.

## Findings

Our analysis of student interaction and reflection revealed that thinking routines contributed meaningfully to both collaborative discourse and the development of a supportive classroom ethos. In what follows, we present findings related to DP3 and DP4 to illustrate how these outc omes were embodied in practice. To evaluate how DP3  was embodied in group interaction and thinking, we analyzed discussions from four student groups using two distinct coding schemes: dialogic moves, adapted from Hennessy et al. (2016), and thinking moves, drawn from Ritchhart et al. (2011). Due to space constraints, we highlight two groups from the Circle of Viewpoints task on food waste, which required students to consider multiple stakeh older perspectives. As shown in Table 1, Group 1 showed stronger dialogic engagement: 44% of their turns involved building on others’ ideas, and 31% involved providing reasoning or evidence. Their most frequent thinking  moves were building explanations (39%) and conside ring different viewpoints (29%). In contrast, Group 4 bu ilt on peers’ ideas less frequently (28%) but had a  higher proportion of reasoning or evidence (42%). Their dominant thinking moves were also building explanations (35%) and considering viewpoints (28%). These patterns suggest that the thinking routine tasks fostered student thinking, facilitated peer interaction, and enhanced th e dialogic quality of group discussions, thereby co nfirming our conjectures about DP3.

## Conclusion and contribution

Our analysis extends current understanding of how l earning is mediated in thinking-rich classrooms by highlighting the interplay between structured routines, peer dialogue, and a supportive classroom ethos, while also validating the refinement the instructional design principles. These findings align with Ritchhart’s (2015) view of how making thinking visible enhances understanding, and Alexander’s (2020) sociocultural view of how dialogic interaction and classroom culture co-construct knowledge. By revising the conjecture map through a curricular lens, we made visible how learning unfolded through  embodied design, interaction, and ethos. This methodological refinement deepened our understanding of TR-mediated learning and how it is shaped by design.
