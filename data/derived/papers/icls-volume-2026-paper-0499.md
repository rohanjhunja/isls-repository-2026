# When Code Shapes Scientific Language: Speaking Photosynthesis in Code

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Helping students reason about mechanisms in biology is a persistent challenge in science education. This study examines a sixth-grade curricular unit where students engaged in multiple representational practices: natural language, visual model drawings, a nd block-based coding. Students created drawings and explanations before and after using a computational modeling environment. Analyses of students’ drawings and their descriptions revealed a shift from everyday terms to disciplinary vocabulary, as students incorporated molecular-level entities (CO₂, glucose) and mechanistic processes. These changes suggest that coding created a new commognitive space, blending computational and scientific discourse with code serving as a representational scaffold and a medium for scientific reasoning.

We examine how students, while designing computer models, adopt computationally inspired language as they reason about and discuss the scientific phenomena they are modeling, in this case, photosynthesis.  We explore how coding serves as both a linguistic and conceptual scaffold for engaging with scientific ideas. Using n-gram terms from the modeling environment surface in students’ representations and texts. Language plays a central role in shaping how students develop scientific understanding. Vygotsky (1978) conceptualized language as a cultural tool that mediates thought, allowing learners to internalize and coordinate new ways of reasoning. Building on this view, Sfard (2008) proposes that thinking is a form of communication and introduces the term commognition, which combines communication and cognition. Drawing on the commognitive perspective (Sfard, 2008) and mechanistic reasoning (Russ et al., 2008), students develop fluency in using specialized language and representations to describe mechanisms by naming entities, processes, and transformations. Unlike descriptive explanations, mechanistic reasoning explains how and why processes occur, oft en at unseen levels (Russ et al., 2008; Krist et al., 2019). Agent-based modeling (ABM) embeds mechanistic language directly into the modeling process. As students program scientific phenomena, they must define how individual entities act and interact to produce system-level behavior.

## Context and participants

The computational modeling environment, MoDa (Fuhrmann et al., 2022; Wagh et al., 2022), is a block-based, agent-based tool designed for middle school science classrooms. It combines domain-specific modeling blocks (Wilkerson et al., 2015) with the Bifocal Modeling framework (Blikstein, 2014; Fuhrmann et al., 2018), allowing students to compare simulations with real-world data.

In this study, students used photosynthesis-specific blocks to create computational models of chloroplasts that simulate how molecules interact to produce energy, according to their coded rules. Participants were 26 sixth graders from a public school in California. This study focuses on two representative students. Their teacher, Ms. K., has collaborated with the project team for four years. During the unit, students investigated the role of light in photosynthesis, built physical models, and iteratively developed computational models using MoDa. The unit emphasized multiple representations of photosynthesis through discussion, drawings, and computational code, with students creating drawings and written explanations throughout. Before and after the unit, students drew, labeled, and described their models of what happens inside a leaf during photosynthesis. Their explanations were analyzed using comparative n-gram frequency analysis. 22 of the 26 students had complete data. After removing common stop words, we generated a unified vocabulary and transformed pre-and post-unit texts into document-term matrices, binarizing each n-gram (1 = used, 0 = not used). This allowed us to track which words/phrases students adopted or dropped in their explanations. In data displays, we used a cut-off of at least 5 students to indicate a shift in unigram/bigram distributions. 2925 In the next section, we examine how a focal student’s representations of photosynthesis changed after using MoDa.

In our n-gram frequency analysis, we found shifts in students’ language in how they describe photosynthesis after coding their models. The number associated with each term is the difference in the number of students who used that word in their explanation from pre-to post-modeling. After modeling, students used more disciplinary terms, such as energy (+12), CO₂ (+12), oxygen (+10), molecules (+10), glucose (+8), and chloroplasts (+7), many of which were previously uncommon or absent. For example, CO₂ appeared in no student’s pre-explanation but appeared in 12 of their post-explanations. In contrast, everyday or lab-related words like lamp (-9), desk (-8), food (-7), and plant(-5) declined, as expected, given the shift from lab activities to modelbased reasoning. Interestingly, the term cell  saw a shift of-8, with 13 students using the term in their pre-explanation to just 5 in the post.

Overall, these changes reflect a move from informal, context-specific language toward scientific and mechanistic vocabulary. Building on this finding, we examine two students' pre-and post-unit drawings to see how this appears in their representations of photosynthesis.

## Case study: Rina’s drawings

Rina’s pre-instruction drawing depicts a plant cell under a lamp, focusing on structures without molecular detail. Her post-instruction drawing represents photosynthesis using scientific terms and symbols, labeling inputs (CO₂, light) and outputs (glucose, O₂) with arrows. This shift reflects a move from structural to mechanistic thinking across levels.

Students’ drawings, written explanations, and code reveal a linguistic and conceptual shift: students moved from using everyday words such as lamp or plant to employing mechanistic and disciplinary terms such as CO₂, H₂O, oxygen, and glucose. To some degree, the shift at the class level was expected, as students learned through the unit; the analysis of the drawing reveals how the computational modeling space scaffolded students' thinking about photosynthesis. Coding with MoDa created what we describe as a new commognitive space, a learning environment where communication and cognition evolved together through computational syntax stemming from MoDa. Within this space, students expressed scientific ideas using both the logic of code and the discourse of science.  The programming structures mirrored scientific explanations, pushing students to draw and describe causal mechanisms and name the entities involved. In doing so, coding became a linguistic and conceptual scaffold that supported more mechanistic ways of thinking. In summary, the finding is not that students learned more science, but how they learned it, by communicating through a form of talk that fuses computational processes with scientific meaning.
