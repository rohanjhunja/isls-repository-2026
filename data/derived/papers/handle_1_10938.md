# Teaching Data for Power: Exploring Mathematics Teachers’ Perspectives on Data Literacy

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores experienced mathematics teachers' views on data science in classrooms, acknowledging complexities from the social and cultural dimensions of data. While the push for data literacy aligns with democratic participation and economic success, teachers' perspectives are understudied. Grounded in a situative view of teacher learning, this exploratory case of two focal teachers suggests teachers see data literacy not only as a means of making mathematics relevant but also as a tool for critical awareness, empowering students to challenge, critique, and access power.

## Introduction

Data has become an integral part of our society and  culture, and the ubiquity of data presents opportu nities for relevant, meaningful, and empowering contextual eng agement of mathematics using data (Rosenberg et al.,

2022) and has prompted calls to modernize mathematics cur ricula to support students’ data literacy across K-16

mathematics education contexts. However, introducin g data into the mathematics classroom also invites additional complexity. Data is a social and cultura l product, both in how data is used and applied as well as how data is collected and constructed (Van Wart et al., 2020), with historical and political dimensions that imp acts the way teachers and students engage with data (Wil kerson & Polman, 2020). Despite many implications for teachers and their practice (Philip et al., 2016), teachers' perspectives, experiences, and knowledg e are not prominently centered in research.

## Theoretical perspectives

This study rests on a theoretical assumption that c ontext matters for teaching and learning. Therefore, I take a situative view of teacher learning (Horn & Garner, 2022): teacher learning is necessarily complex, and thei r contexts are fundamental to an analysis of their le arning. Of particular relevance to teachers’ learni ng and knowledge around data literacy is teachers’ pedagogical responsibility – a deeply contextual and personal aspect of teacher knowledge, pedagogical responsibility de scribes teachers’ institutional, legal, and ethical  obligations and commitments. This study aims to understand teachers’ conceptualizations of and motivations for data.

## Methods

This study is an extension of a four-year research-practice partnership investigating experienced math ematics teacher learning around ambitious and equitable tea ching practices. The partnering organization is a w ellregarded professional development organization (PDO). This study centers two experienced mathematics teachers, Nadine and Keith as an exploratory case t o study teachers' conceptualizations of data litera cy – especially as they relate to equitable teaching. Fr om September 2022 through May 2023, we met monthly for 60 minutes as a working group where we discussed their  teaching goals, interests and challenges with data  literacy, and shared lessons and teaching resources. In March  of 2023, they each participated in a 60 minute sem istructured interview. I took a grounded analytic approach (Charmaz, 2014), with rounds of constant comparative line-by-line and focused coding.

## Findings: Data literacy as consequential to developing critical awareness

Teachers in this study viewed data literacy as impo rtant, useful, and relevant to students’ lives, how ever openly discussed critical dimensions of data that were con nected to their understandings of and motivations f or teaching data literacy. As data become increasingly  prevalent, the closeness of data can be a resource  for students to learn from and about and to question th eir world. Keith described data reasoning as a way for students to see mathematics as “a tool for you to m ake sense of what's going on”  (November Working Group). For Keith, data literacy was a way to make mathemat ics not only relevant, but a “tool” that students could use to make sense of their world. This connection to the ‘ real world’ was not simply to pique students’ inter ests, but important to equipping them for their lives beyond school. Nadine also viewed data literacy as necessa ry to engaging students in ambitious and authentic mathem atics: “If we actually want students to do meaningf ul work, they have to work with data [...] I thin k any meaningful real-world work they do has to be supported by data” (September Working Group). This quote suggest ed that data literacy has the potential to move bey ond relevance – to make the mathematics with which students engage matter. Holding Nadine’s and Keith’s ideas together, data literacy can indeed modernize school  mathematics (Boaler & Levitt, 2019)  and connect mathematics to students’ lives to engage them in di sciplinary work that has meaning and consequence to  their lives (Wilkerson & Polman, 2020).  However, in order for students to engage with data in this way, Nadine and Keith also pointed to aspects of critical data lite racy. Students need a deeper, contextual understand ing of data. Nadine and Keith framed data as something that is p roduced and manipulated. That is, data is a sociall y situated tool for meaning making (Stornaiuolo, 2020).

By invoking the social construction and use of data, Nadine and Keith began to unravel the inherent political and power dimensions of data. They freque ntly described their motivations for teaching data literacy as a means to equip students to challenge data. In tur n, this pedagogical framing challenged the position  of data as a neutral, objective, arbiter of truth. While Nadin e and Keith framed data as a mathematical tool at s tudents’ disposal, it was also a tool available to those wit h power – a “tool” that can be “bad” and “weaponized” (Keith, November Working Group). They repeatedly described data as something that can be manipulated to tell a story. These understandings of data suggest that Ke ith and Nadine challenged narrow understandings of data as objective. Furthermore, teaching data literacy was important so that their students could “understand everything has an agenda, now you can be  more cautious about what you're seeing, what you're reading and make yo ur own judgments based on that” (Keith, March Intervie w). Their desire to support students' data literacy  was tied to their desire for students to see, challenge, and  access power in and through data. While power is i nherent in the social production of data (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2 020), it won’t necessarily be foregrounded as stude nts engage in data practices – this takes substantial effort on behalf of teachers to raise these issues and support their students’ engagement and sensemaking around them.

## Significance

The findings of this study have implications concer ning teacher learning and integrating data literacy  on a larger scale, especially in working towards more culturall y relevant mathematics experiences. Centering teach ers’ perspectives and experiences highlights the deeply personal and social nature of teaching. We can learn from the way that Nadine and Keith raised tensions and critical concerns that should be attended to in supporti ng teachers in addressing data literacy. Nadine and Keith’s con ceptualizations of data literacy point to the promi se and necessity of data literacy, but do so through a cau tious, critical lens that is important to consider when enacting larger scale mathematics reform to address data literacy.
