# SciStory: A Demo of AI-Supported Socioscientific Learning through Visual Novels

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Our tool demo will explore intersections between narrative-centered learning, AI agents as inquiry scaffolds, and the interdisciplinary exploration of socioscientific issues. We will share our current design of SciStory: Pollinators, an interactive visual novel designed to support middle grades students in developing persuasive arguments to solve socioscientific problems in their community. Discussion will explore how we balance ethical issues around generative AI in our agent design, leverage co-design practices to iteratively improve collaborative game features, and draw on principles from problem-based learning to engage learners in developing solutions for complex socioscientific issues.

This session will demonstrate SciStory: Pollinators, an interactive visual novel that is designed to teach middle grades students about socioscientific issues, persuasive argumentation, and problem-solving for complex community issues (Humburg et al., in press). Visual novels are a type of interactive story game that use player choice and branching dialogues to engage players as a participant in an unfolding narrative. While there is a dearth of research on the specific impacts of visual novels on learning (Camingue et al., 2020), a handful of educational designers have leveraged the choice-based, exploratory structure of this storytelling medium to engage learners with topics such as endangered species protection (Câmara Olim et al., 2025), empowerment in healthcare settings (Yin et al., 2012), and English conversational practices (Faizal, 2016). More broadly, narrative-centered learning environments that use stories to engage students in disciplinary learning have demonstrated significant benefits for both learning and motivation (Lester et al., 2012; Naul & Liu, 2020). The goal of this design was to leverage the known benefits of digital story games with the emerging capabilities of generative AI to create an adaptive, student-centered narrative learning experience.

To maximize student engagement with the SciStory narrative, we have designed and embedded AIdriven conversational agents as story characters who can adaptively respond to students’ ideas and questions as they play through the visual novel. Our agent design builds on previous studies that have explored the effectiveness of intelligent agents acting as a teachable peer (Biswas et al., 2005), a tutor (Graesser et al., 2018), a knowledge building partner (Gerard et al., 2024), and a conversational facilitator (Dyke et al., 2013). This research has shown that well-designed agents can support students’ metacognitive practices during collaborative science learning as well as their scientific inquiry skills (White et al., 1999; Biswas et al., 2005). However, throughout our design process, we have grappled with a variety of ethical concerns in the ways that generative AI can impact student learning and overall community wellness, including the risk of unreliable or inaccurate responses (e.g., “hallucinations”), the negative impact of AI data centers on environmental resources, and concerns about how third-party AI models handle and store student learning data (Kasneci et al., 2023; Selwyn, 2022). As a part of our visual novel demo, we will engage with attendees about our AI agent design process and the ways that our team works to mitigate ethical concerns through cross-disciplinary design conversations as well as teacher and student co-design.

Tool and story overview The SciStory: Pollinators narrative draws on design principles from problem-based learning (HmeloSilver, 2004) by presenting students with an ill-structured, complex community problem that has multiple potential solutions. Students work in pairs in front of a single laptop tonegotiate ideas, collect evidence, and determine a solution to present to the town mayor as a team. Players are introduced to local community members throughout the game’s narrative and are tasked with helping the community decide what to do with an empty plot o f land. Some characters want to build a community garden, while others want to build a parking lot to attract more businesses to the area. The story is presented to students through a series of 2D backgrounds, much like a digital storybook, and each location the player visits has different selectable characters that, when clicked, trigger a conversation with that character. Students can select responses in the conversation from a list of pre-scripted choices, which lead them down branching conversational pathways. Several tools are built into the story structure to scaffold students’ inquiry, including a digital notebook where students can write down ideas, tag notes with different categories, and sort their notes to find key ideas to include in their argument draft (see Fig. 1). Students also have access to a dialogue history to review any conversations they have had. There are also a variety of opportunities for students to type out their own questions and ideas to AI-infused characters in order to receive tailored responses and feedback from AI agents.

Figure 1 Screenshots From SciStory Showing the Notebook and the AI Agent Interface Agents are designed to fill three key pedagogical roles: 1) Answering content questions (about pollinators, food justice, and plants), 2) Answering help questions (about how to play the game or overcome roadblocks during gameplay), and 3) Providing supportive feedback on students’ draft arguments for what to do with the empty lot. Each agent draws on carefully crafted knowledge bases written by learning scientists to cover key scientific and game knowledge as well as examples of effective feedback. The agents also have specific prompts embedded to prevent students from derailing the conversation off-topic or discussing inappropriate or offensive topics. The agent prompts were designed and improved over multiple testing iterations with teachers, students, and interdisciplinary research teams to check for hallucinations, the effectiveness of the safety guardrails, and the overall usefulness of the agent feedback for student learning. We also tried out and compared three different large language models (LLMs) in our agent development and focus group testing: Flan-T5-small (Chung et al., 2022), Llama-3.1-8 billion (Grattafiori et al., 2024), and GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2023). As a part of the demo, we will discuss with attendees how we weighed the pros and cons of different LLMs and tested their effectiveness in student and teacher co-design sessions to iteratively improve the SciStory agent design. Students can explore the visual novel in any order by visiting different locations, choosing which characters to speak with, and recording digital notes in their in-game notebook. After gathering sufficient evidence, students draft a persuasive argument to convince the town mayor to support their solution for the empty lot. Through this argument drafting, the students engage with concepts around pollinators, ecosystem health, and how human food systems are intertwined with the health of pollinators and our larger environment. Based on whether students successfully convince the mayor to support a garden or a parking lot, the ending of the game changes.

Demo session overview Our team will present two versions of SciStory: Pollinators for attendees to explore in our demo. The studentfacing tool will allow players to move through the story that students see, talking with game characters, gathering notes in their digital notebooks, and drafting a persuasive argument for the Mayor (see Fig. 2). Attendees can also explore the teacher demo version of SciStory (see Fig. 3), which walks the player through the narrative at a high l evel, explains the core learning goals, and allows the player to iteratively test out how the AI agents behave with different large language models (LLMs) plugged into the game characters. The student version was created for use in classrooms as a student learning activity, while the teacher version was created to gather teacher feedback during focus groups in order to improve the design of the student game. The tools will require a Wi-Fi internet connection and outlets to plug in laptops. In the case of a virtual or hybrid session, the demo link can be shared with virtual attendees for them to open and load the games on their own computer browsers. In addition to playtesting the tools, we will provide attendees with information about the SciStory narrative development, our AI agent design approach, and our pilot tests with students and teachers. For example, we candiscuss how our team shifted from developing for a 3D video game environment to a 2D visual novel design in response to student playtesting and feedback. We can also share details about our approach to improving accessibility for a variety of student reading and writing levels through the integration of automated speech recognition and text-to-speech technologies. Our hope is that demo attendees will find value in learning about several different aspects of the design, including 1) the use of visual novels as a playful way for students to engage in scientific inquiry, 2) the use of AI agents to scaffold openended inquiry while promoting student agency and metacognition, and 3) the integration of persuasive writing and scientific inquiry skills to engage students in socioscientific issues relevant to their communities.
