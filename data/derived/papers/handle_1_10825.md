# Integrating Arguments: Bottom-Up vs. Top-Down Processes

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To construct arguments from multiple documents, learners need to identify macro-propositions that reflect broad claims that the documents agree or disagree with. This task is highly challenging for learners, yet it is essential for developing a coherent understanding of the documents and weighing their positions. So far, little is known about how learners construct macro-propositions while integrating multiple documents. In this study, forty ninth-grade students read a set of seven online documents on the topic of bottled mineral water and created integrative document maps. Recordings of mapping processes, cued retrospective recalls, and interviews revealed that learners engaged in a variety of top-down and bottom-up integration processes, while some students failed to construct macro-propositions. We discuss how macro-propositions emerge and what this reveals about learners’ integration processes.

## Introduction

Multiple document integration is an essential skill  for making sense of information stemming from a va riety of sources on current science topics, such as people’s health and the environment. According to the Documents Model Framework (Britt & Rouet, 2012), to develop integrative arguments based on multiple documents, learners need to construct an integrated mental model of the contents that incorporates and connects contrasting claims, reasons, and evidence across the documents. Further, to successfully integrate multiple documents, learners alsoneed to develop an intertext model that includes a representation of the sources of the documents, the connections between sources and contents (who-said-what), and the relations among the sources (e.g. if they agree or disagree). To construct an integrated model of the contents, l earners need to organize and connect propositions from the documents and identify their relationships (Spivey & King, 1989). An essential step in this pr ocess is identifying macro-propositions that reflect cross-cutting themes or broad claims that sources might a gree or disagree about. As Kelly and Takao (2002) have described, rich argumen ts encompass a hierarchy of epistemic levels ranging from explicit references to data thr ough specific propositions to more general proposit ions. Yet, students often fail to identify macro-propositions that integrate diverse ideas and concepts  stemming from different sources (Segev-Miller, 2007), and the processes that support construction of su ch intertextual macro-propositions are not well understood.

Several recent studies have examined learners ’ integration processes of multiple documents (e.g., Barzilai et al., 2023; Tarchi et al., 2023). These studies have identified the importance of recursivity in integration processes, that is, the value of making multiple lateral transitions between sources and learners’ texts. Ho wever, it is unclear how students construct macro-propositions, that is, how they make horizontal transitions between epistemic levels (Kelly & Takao, 2002) as they construct arguments from multiple documents. Hence, in this study, we utilized an online document mapping tool to gain a better understanding of how learners construct arguments from multiple scientific documents. More specifically, our aims were to investigate the representation of argument elements and levels in learners’ maps (RQ1) and to describe learners’ b ottom-up and top-down integration processes to understand how they c onstruct complex arguments (RQ2).

## Method

Forty 9 th-grade students met individually twice with an interviewer to perform two tasks  which required developing arguments based on multiple scientific documents. Learners used a document mapping scaffold that enables visually mapping sources, identifying key contents (e.g., evidence or claims)  from these sources, and mapping connections among sources and contents. See examples in the results section. In these maps, sources are represented using circles (the color of the circles  indicate s their trustworthiness); and the blue boxes include content identified or added by the learners. Green lines re present connections of agreement or support, and re d lines represent connections of disagreement or opposition. Before the first task, students watched a ten-minute video tutorial about how to use the scaffold. In this paper, we focus on the second task, which addressed  the question: “Is drinking bottled mineral water h ealthier thandrinking tap water?” The task included seven a dapted online documents (ranging from Facebook post s to adapted scientific articles) that had varied positions, source trustworthiness, and evidence quality. Students were asked to evaluate the sources, map the documents, and then write argumentative essays that addressed the driving question. The integration process was recorded using screen c aptures. After the students completed the task, we interviewed them using a cued retrospective recall and follow-up interview questions. We coded all of the argument elements in the maps, including sources, evidence, specific propositions, and macro-propositions (see coding scheme in Table 1). By closely watching all of the videos, along with the retrospective recalls, we identified four categories of integration process es: (a) Top-down processes occurred when learners started by identifying macro-propositions and then proceeded to elaborate them with specifi c propositions; (b) Bottom-up processes occurred when learners began by identifying specific propositions and then gradually identified macro-propositions; (c) Upper-level processes occurred when students created only macropropositions; (d) Lower-level processes occurred when students created only specific propositions. In contrast, 30.0% of the students started by mapping macro-propositions. Most of these students,  but not all, went on to elaborate their maps with specific propositions. ## Theoretical and educational significance

Previous studies  have found that learners  can find it  difficult to construct complex arguments from multiple scientific documents. In this study, we identified two approaches that learners can use to develop multi-level arguments: a bottom-up approach and a top-down approach. Learners who employed the bottom-up approach started by constructing specific propositions from each document. About half of these students  managed then to identify similarities and differences between specific propositions and to construct macro-propositions that reflected those relations. The iterative reading and writing of claims from the documents appeared to help learners to gradually identify intertextual relations. However, some students wrote specific propositions only and  did not construct macro-propositions, suggesting they might have found it difficult to identify connections between lower-level propositions. In contrast, learners who employed a top-down approach started by constructing macro-propositions. These learners appeared to be more aware of argument schemas and use d these schemas to extrapolate macro-propositions from the task instructions and from the documents. Learners’ grasp of the macro-propositions helped them t o integrate the documents in the light of the se propositions Learners who used top-down approaches elaborated their maps to varied extents: Some focused mainly on macro-propositions, while others used the macropropositions as starting points for elaboration. Previous studies suggest that both macro-structures and elaboration are important for argument development (e.g., Barzilai et al., 2023; Kelly & Takao, 2002). Both approaches could help learners overcome the challenge of developing complex scientific arguments form multiple documents. Teachers might introduce both approaches and design opportunities for practice. Some learners might benefit from support in identifying macro-propositions, whereas others might need to be reminded of the importance of elaborating macro-propositions to develop richer arguments. Cognitive and metacognitive scaffolds and prompts might be designed to facilitate these goals. More studies are needed to better understand learners’ bottom-up and top-down integration processes and to how these processes might be supported.
