# Teacher Development of Programming Skills and Computational Thinking in the Context of Creating Interactive Educational Applications with Scratch

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The study examined the development of coding skills and computational thinking of in-service teachers in the context of an e-learning Master-level course. Analysis of teacher Scratch projects and interviews showed that teachers became able to apply several computer science concepts and processes. The e-learning format of the course did not present additional learning challenges, which has significant practical implications because online programs require fewer resources, are more flexible, and are easier to scale up.

## Introduction

Knowing programming  is considered an essential competence for navigating the challenges of the  modern, increasingly digitalized, world (Guzdial, 2022; Vuorikari et al., 2022). Also, it can facilitate the development of computational thinking (CT), which is considered an important transversal competence (Wing, 2006). While there is no agreement about the definition of CT (Denning, 2017), a widely adopted  framework is the one developed by Brennan and Resnick (2012) in the context of pro gramming with Scratch, which  includes three key CT dimensions: a) computational concepts that include Sequences, Loops, Events, Parallelism, Conditionals, Operators, and Data, b) computational practices such as Being incremental and iterative, Testing and debugging, Reusing and remixing, and Abstracting and modularizing, and c) computational perspectives that designers form about the world and about them, including Expressin g, Connecting, and Questioning. Both coding and CT skills are important for educators whoneed to be digitall y literate themselves but are also expected to supp ort skill development in their own students.

Research on in-service teachers with no background in CS has been limited (Tang et al., 2020; Yang, 2025). Professional development (PD) interventions have typically used block-based programming environments such as Scratch (e.g., Kong et al., 2020), where commands are provided as color-coded building blocks, to address the difficulties of novices. With few exceptions (e.g. Lazarinis et al., 2019; Marcelino et al., 2018) most PD efforts involved face-to-face meetings, which are not flexible enough  and are difficult to scale up, while less is known about the impact of online programs. Also, the effectiveness of PD programs has largely been assessed using selfevaluation surveys on teacher satisfaction, attitudes, self-efficacy, and knowledge perceptions (e.g., Kong et al., 2023; Lazarinis et al., 2019; Rich et al., 2021). Only some studies (e.g., Dong et al., 2019, Marcelino et al., 2018) analyzed teachers’ programming projects, although these can provide useful insights about areas of both growth and difficulty, especially when combined with interviews (Brennan & Resncik, 2012). The present study addresses this gap by a) focusing on  in-service teachers who learned programming with  Scratch in an e-learning Masterslevel course and b) analyzing data from both teacher Scratch projects and teacher interviews on their experience. The present study The e-learning Masters-level course is attended  primarily by educators working in K-12 and  in non-formal educational settings who have  no prior programming experience. Coursework focuses on producing digital content and  culminates in the creation of an educational application using Scratch. After approval had been obtained, 25 Scratch projects were included for analysis and 5 teacher volunteers were selected, using purposive (heterogenous) sampling, to participate in interviews. The research questions were: a) Which CT concepts were the most and least developed based on teacher products? b) What are teachers’ views regarding their learning? Teachers’ Scratch projects were analyzed  using the first key dimension of the Brennan and Resnick

(2012) framework, which includes computational concepts. The output of each project analysis includes the

number of different blocks/commands that appeared (at least once) in the project from each Scratch block category (Sequences, Loops, Events, Conditionals, Operators, and Data), which was used as an indicator of teachers’ ability to use the relevant programming concept. Parallelism was a ssessed by examining whether a project had scripts that ran simultaneously. Analysis also e valuated the complexity of the projects in terms of the n umber of programmable objects (sprites and backdrops) included. After interviews were transcribed, they were analyzed using thematic analysis, aiming at identifying and categorizing themes that related to the following questions: a) what did teachers learn by coding a Scratch application? b) which aspects of the course affected their learning?

## Main findings

Analysis of Scratch projects showed that teachers applied CT concepts from almost each category of the Brennan and Resnick (2012) framework. Fewer blocks were used from the Operations, Loops, and Data categories, which indicates that these concepts are more difficult for teachers, as also shown in other studies (e.g., Rich at el., 2021). Teachers developed various types of applications. S imulations and games, which involved more complex user interactivity, and tutorials, which had to include effective ways to present information,  were more complex  as they used more diverse Scratch features and twice as many programmable objects compared to drill-and-practice applications.

As stated earlier, analysis of teacher interview tr anscripts focused on teachers’  views on their own learning and on related aspects of the course. When referring to their own learning, t eachers addressed cognitive and emotional aspects as well as the process of learning. All appreciated that they “demystified programming” (P2) and developed coding skills at a time when “ everybody talks about AI (P5), “technology is part of students’ life”(P3), and as teachers they need to integrate technology, programming and/or computational thinking in the classroom. Also, all teachers expressed positive emotions while acknowledging that learning Scratch and creating their projects took a lot of time and effort, e.g. “I didn't realize it took me so long. It was somethi ng I was happy to do... a lot of times I couldn't wait to get home, to turn on the computer, to continue what I had built the day before” (P3), and some of them develop ed an interest in programming, e.g., “it gave me the impetus to think, to do some programming courses in general, not just Scratch, to learn programming” (P1).  Finally, their discussion of their learning and project development addressed aspects of the process that were relevant to comput ational practices and perspectives as conceptualized in the  Brennan and Resnick (2012)  framework. For example, they referred to Being incremental and iterative (e.g. “I was scared, to be honest, to build an educational app ourselves, but along the way I could kind of split the steps in my mind and dissect the process. I think it worked better” P4), to Testing and Debugging (e.g., “At the end of course there was always a test and also during the process.....via the iterations I go back to the code, see what is w rong, recognize it and correct it ” P1), and to Reusing and remixing as they studied and borrowed from other examples (e.g., “I was also going to the Internet and exploring, to see what other information I can find or to see something else that was made”, P3). In addition, teachers thought that it was highly motivating and empowering for them that they had to create an original educational application (Expressing) that they could use with their own students (Connecting). When discussing the aspects of the course that affected their learning, teachers referred to course organization, the learning materials, the final assignment  (Scratch project), and the e-learning format of the course. All participants were satisfied with course organization and stressed the importance of the video tutorials as they could watch them several times and try out the worked examples, e.g., “The videos alone are very helpful, but they are not enough if one does not sit down to  apply all that they see. And that's why I was tryi ng to have Scratch open at the same time and do the same on so me level” (P5). The Scratch project was also important because it was perceived as authentic and creative, and, therefore, highly motivating, as well as enabled teachers to assess their understanding and deepen their lear ning, e.g., “…a finished product that is usable, which is very attractive to children” (P4), “…that left a lot of freedom of thought and imagina tion and all of that” (P5), “This is when I realized what I knew, what I didn’t know and what weaknesses I had”  (P2). Teachers did not mention difficulties arising from  the e-learning format of the course, e.g. “For me, the flexibility and convenience of a distance learning course is an advantage.… I could watch the videos over and over and at the same time, on a second computer next to me, try to do what I was wa tching on the video”  (P2), “A characteristic of modern education is that the students themselves have to search and find information on their own” (P3).

## Conclusions

Although engagement  with Scratch was relatively brief, teachers developed an understanding of many computational concepts and processes and improved their confidence in their computing skills  and their interest in programming. Based on teacher interviews, working on an authenti c project that was relevant to their practice and studying worked examples via video tutorials were two important aspects of the course that supported teacher learning. An important finding of the study is that, although learning programming took time and effort, the online format of the course did not seem to present additional learning challenges. The generalizability of this finding is limited due to the small number of study participan ts; however, it needs further exploration as it  may have significant practical implications for future PD efforts because online interventions require fewer resources, are easier to scale up, and can involve participants from more geographical areas compared to face-to-face programs.
