# Amplifying the Voices of High School Graduates with Special Education and Neurodiversity Experiences to Shape GenAI Practices in Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This qualitative study explores how GenAI Use Case Scenarios spark critical dialogue about GenAI in education among high school graduates with special education and neurodiverse experiences. Drawing on Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (Engeström & Sannino, 2010), justice-driven inclusive education (Waitoller & Artiles, 2013), and DisCrit (Annamma et al., 2013), this study positions youth as philosophers of technology (Vakil & McKinney de Royston, 2022) and co-designers of AI policy. Findings reveal the critical insights marginalized voices bring to conversations about responsible GenAI use in schools.

Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) tools like ChatGPT are rapidly reshaping educational practice. Yet their potential to support inclusion for young adults with special education and neurodiverse experiences remains underexplored. These young adults, often marginalized by rigid academic and literacy norms, offer vital perspectives in ongoing debates about GenAI's role in schools. This study frames GenAI as a runaway object (Engeström, 2009), a sociotechnical phenomenon that expands rapidly across institutional boundaries, escapes the control of any single group, and carries disruptive and emancipatory potential. Responding to it me aningfully requires collective, cross-boundary action that draws in voices from across educational communities. Yet young adults historically excluded from shaping the tools that affect their lives remain largely absent from these conversations (Rice & Dun n, 2023). Theoretical framing Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT; Engeström & Sannino, 2010) is used in this study to frame GenAI in education as a sociotechnical activity system in which tools, rules, community, and division of labor mediate learning and decision-making. From this perspective, GenAI is not a neutral instrument but a historically and institutionally shaped component of a system that can either reproduce or disrupt existing inequities, making visible why those most affected by that power must be part of shaping it. Justice-driven inclusive education (Waitoller & Artiles, 2013) calls for the redistribution of learning opportunities, the recognition of diverse literacies, and the active inclusion of marginalized voices in shaping educational practice as a dialogic, ongoing process. Strengthened by DisCrit (Annamma et al., 2013), this framing directly names how young adults at the intersections of race, disability, class, and language are not only excluded from educational decision-making but actively surveilled within i t, their behaviors and learning needs read through deficit and carceral logics (Beneke et al., 2024). Many of these young adults have been surveilled and disciplined because of how they communicate, behave, and learn; asking them to openly discuss a technology already used to police academic integrity requires building real conditions of trust and safety. This study therefore extends human-in-the-loop (HITL; UNESCO, 2023) frameworks to center marginalized voices and judgment in determining how GenAI is used, positioning youth as philosophers of technology (Vakil & McKinney de Royston, 2022) whose lived experiences and critical insights are precisely the human intelligence that must be in the loop.

This qualitative design-based research study (Cobb et al., 2003) used an iterative, participatory approach to explore how young adults with special education and neurodiverse experiences think about GenAI in educational contexts. The study involved two cohorts totaling 12 young adults aged 19 to 29, engaging entirely via Zoom. Group A included eight participants, young adult interns and one educator, in a neurodiversity-focused technology internship program. Group B included four young adults with experienc e in self-contained special education classrooms, all former students of the researcher. A small group of participants from each cohort served as co-designers, shaping both the content and delivery of data collection methods. Data were collected through focus groups and surveys anchored in GenAI Use Case Scenarios (Carroll, 1997; McDonald et al., 2022), fictional but plausible situations depicting how students, educators, and school leaders might use GenAI. Scenarios were developed iteratively through pilot work and refined with feedback from ChatGPT, an advisor, a research group, and co-designers from Group A. Based on co-designer input, surveys 2551 were adapted into collaborative Padlet activities for Group A; for Group B, they were restructured as verbal survey interviews prioritizing accessibility and relational comfort. Transcripts and survey responses were analyzed using inductive and deductive coding (Saldaña, 2016), with member checking conducted across both cohorts. Reflexivity was central throughout, as the researcher's prior relationships with Group B participants and positionality as a former special educator shaped data collection and interpretation. practice, and participant perspectives on educator use of GenAI. Empowerment. For young adults whose communication styles have long been read through deficit frameworks in school, ChatGPT offered a space to find their voice. Dylan (Group A) described how ChatGPT helped him manage stress and anxiety, communicate with doctors and teachers, and ultimately "know myself better." Rather than generating work for him, Dylandrafted his ideas first and then used ChatGPT to refine them, telling it to "rewrite my words." Lucia (Group B) used it similarly, describing ChatGPT as "kin d of like going to a writing center," helping her sound more professional while preserving her authorship. Yet both Dylan and Lucia also described the fear of being punished for this use. Lucia shared, "I fear getting in trouble for using it for school or work even though I write everything out first." Dylan was made to redo assignments after honestly disclosing his process to teachers. These stories reflect what justice-driven inclusive education (Waitoller & Artiles, 2013) and DisCrit (Annamma et al., 201 3) make visible: that students surveilled and disciplined because of their communication styles carry that history into every interaction with technology. Boundary Setting.  Where media narratives frame young people as passive or irresponsible users of GenAI, the participants in this study tell a different story. Diego (Group B) described a boundary rooted in distrust of data systems, noting "I feel like it's not truly delete d," and responding by choosing to use ChatGPT anonymously without logging in, an act of digital self-determination rather than naivety. Angel (Group B) drew his boundary from relational experience, describing how a friend "got so wrapped u p in it, it led him toneglect other parts of his life," adding, "it's scary to think that could happen to anyone." Together, these reflections reveal young adults engaged in what invited guest Anya described as "mental gymnastics," the ongoing cognitive a nd emotional labor of navigating GenAI ethically, carefully, and on their own terms. Viewed through Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (Engeström & Sannino, 2010), these self-authored boundaries reveal how institutional rules, histories of surveillance, and sociotechnical contexts shape the exhausting labor required to engage with AI responsibly.

Flipping the Script. When GenAI Use Case Scenarios turned the lens toward educator and school district use, participants became policy critics. Ten out of twelve participants said teachers should disclose when they use GenAI, framing transparency as a matter of trust. Diego (Group B) offered a piercing critique of predictive systems, warning that basing curricula on flawed IEP data means "you're now going to make a curriculum for me with information that isn't true," and that algorithmic bias could "cause m ental health stress and suicide risk for students." Javier (Group A) argued that teachers must have final say over GenAI-generated content, "especially when dealing with sensitive topics around identity, culture, and inclusivity." These reflections make the case that genuine human-in-the-loop practice (UNESCO, 2023) requires expanding the loop itself, centering young adults as philosophers of technology (Vakil & McKinney de Royston, 2022) whose lived expertise must shape the policies and practices that govern how they learn.

Viewed through Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (Engeström & Sannino, 2010), justice-driven inclusive education (Waitoller & Artiles, 2013), and DisCrit (Annamma et al., 2013), this study demonstrates that young adults with special education and neurodiverse experiences bring sophisticated ethical reasoning, lived wisdom, and critical insight to conversations about GenAI in education. This work extends HITL frameworks to center those who have historically held the least power in shaping educational technology, positioning them not as subjects of policy but as philosophers of technology (Vakil & McKinney de Royston, 2022) whose expertise is essential to designing GenAI futures that are equitable, inclusive, and truly human. practices, and scenario-based dialogue can surface the tensions and opportunities needed to design ethical and equitable policy.
