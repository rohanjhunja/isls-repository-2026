# Research Practice Partnership Model for Non-Western Education Settings: A Case Study from Rural India

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Theories and methods in the Learning Sciences have pointed out that building research-practice partnerships is an impactful pathway to designing equitable learning environments. However, most of the research on building research-practice partnerships is being conducted in Western contexts. In this paper, we describe a research practice partnership model that is being built in rural India. Researchers from Learning Sciences and educators/practitioners who work with a rural school that serves Tribal and Rural students in western India started to work together three years ago to think about how to teach critical thinking and collaboration skills to students from Tribal and rural parts of India. This paper focuses on the process of how the partnership was built and contributes to the theories of design-based implementation research and participatory design research.

## Introduction

In the last decade, there has been a movement in the learning sciences where there is recognition that for designing impactful learning environments, different stakehol ders need to build sustainable partnerships with ea ch other (Riedy & Penuel, 2024).  Research-practice partnerships are pivotal for addressing global crises through education (Soland et. al, 2024).  By integrating diverse perspectives, these partnerships enrich our understanding of complex problems and promote sustainable, contextually rele vant solutions that empower communities to take act ion (Karve, 1963). By co-creating curricula that resonate with local needs, educators can enhance student engagement and foster a sense of agency among learners (Coburn & Penuel, 2016). As educators gain access to cuttin g-edge research and professional development, they are better prepared to implement effective practices that address both educational and societal challenges (Ferrell et. al., 2017). Even though it has been established that research-practice partnerships are important to build and su stain impactful learning, we alsoneed to acknowledge that the partnership-building process is not easy to conduct. For exampl e, one of the simplest tenets  of how to build a research-practice partnership between stakeholders approaching the problem at hand from different points of view is that stakeholders having more power need to “show up”. Although the conceptual idea of “showing up” sounds simple, in reality, it may meandifferent things to different ind ividuals (Brislin, 2003). How one can show up to build a partnership can vary according to race, cul ture, gender, nationality, and many other identity-related markers (Kleinsmith, De Silva, & Bianchi-Berthouze, 2006). Thus, the socio-cultural and political context within which the partnership is being built is an importan t factor that needs to be taken into consideration (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). There are many models of research  practice partnerships that are documented within t he National Science Foundation, Institute of Education  Sciences (IES), Spencer Foundation and W T Grant foundation funded projects (e.g. Design-based research collaboratory: http://researchandpractice.org/) and many journals in education/Learning Sciences have docume nted several case studies and research papers about  how research-practice partnerships can be built in the United States and other Western nations. Other cultures in nations outside of the United States might have their own versions of a research-practice partnership, and those partnership-building processes need to be grounded in the liter ature and contexts of those particular cultures (Kumar, 2022). Although we are not the first ones to make this argument that cultu re and context matter in education, we are making the argument that the partnership-building process is a cultural process that is very much integral to the context in which the partnership is being built. For example, the pa rtnershipbuilding process requires stakeholders to build tru st and trust-building, and is a context and culture-specific activity (Hakimaa, 2021). Trust building in communities where relationality is important can require stakeholders to engage in activities that may be different than other communities, which are more individualistic. Given these context-specific aspects that come into play while building a partne rship, we believe that it is important to document and discuss how one might go about building a partnership between Learning Sciences researchers and educators/practitioners serving Indigenous/tribal a nd Rural students in India. We hope that this prese ntation will also shed light on how learning sciences researchers in the Western contexts (the US, Europe, and other Western nations) can initiate partnerships with communities  in non-Western contexts across the world. We believe that knowledge exchange between researchers and practiti oners around the world is an important element of h ow global problems and issues can be addressed through education, but we emphasize that this knowledge exchange must be equitable and non-extractive. In this paper, we describe a partnership in progress that uses cul turally relevant, non-extractive approaches to building a research practice partnership in India. Building a research practice partnership in rural India In 2020, as the pandemic shut down schools across the world, it quickly became apparent that most students (68%) across rural India did not have adequate access to technology that would support remote learning (Azim  Premji Foundation, 2020). Schools serving economically upp er classes in urban areas have made some progress t oward helping students become proficient in ICT/CS related topics, but the lack of framework has left individual schools to create their pathways (M Van Mechelen et al, 202 3). Thus, in Dec/Jan of 2021 we started building a research practice partnership that brought together practiti oners, researchers and community members who live and work in rural/indigenous/tribal/refugee communities in India, along with researchers and practitioners from the United States to understand-what socio-cultural, political and economic values need to be addressed to design a curriculum framework for K-16 ICT/CS education in schools that serve rural/ind igenous/tribal/refugee students in India.

From the outset, the partnership process was ground ed in the understanding that educational research i s relational. Many Indigenous scholars advocate for u sing relationality as a framework for research cond ucted within communities where the interconnected ness of people and the land is paramount (Kovach, 2 010; Wilson, 2009). While India has adopted capitalism as a means of achieving economic prosperity, its society remains deeply relational. In Indian culture, extended families —such as aunts, uncles, cousin s—and close friends form strong networks that support an individual’s well-being and success. Given this, we chose the framework of relationality to ground our partnership-building process. This approach differs significant ly from Western models of partnership building.  The relationality framework encourages a rethinking  of professional and personal boundaries, requiring partners to blur these lines.  In India, many work-related decisions are made in informal settings—during meals, tea breaks, or casual “hangouts” afte r work. In rural areas, such gatherings often include work colleagues, and important decisions are freque ntly discussed in these third spaces. Researchers e ngaged in the partnership-building process would benefit from participating in these i nformal settings, as doing so fosters trust and strengthens relationships with educators.

In addition to the relationality framework, there are several other important considerations when building partnerships in non-Western contexts. These considerations extend beyond researcher positionality to include the complex power dynamics within the practitioner comm unity. Understanding who holds power and whoneeds to act to ensure equitable collaboration is crucial. Power Between Researchers and Educators: There is an inherent power imbalance between researchers from large U.S. universities and educators in rural Indian schools. Not only must this power differential be acknowledged, but U.S. researchers must also actively work to ensure t hat decision-making is equitable. In the context of this partnership, a ll researchers and practitioners were of South Asian origins; however,  three of the researchers were trained in Western contexts and worked for a large US research University. Thus, time needed to be spen t by the US-based South Asian researchers to understand the local ways of knowing and thinking. Power Dynamics Within the Educator Community:  Power and privilege in India operate differently than in Western settings. When working in South Asia, it is essential to consider not only caste and class differences but also gender dynamics (Paik, 2014). While our work is grounded in the relationality framework, the relationships between educators from  different castes, classes, and genders require nua nced approaches, intentional effort, and sustained commitment. As we progress with this RPP model, there are many new things we are learning that can advance learning sciences research as it expands into different cultures across the world.  As this partnership model grows, we hop e to discuss the ‘nitty gritty’ of what it means to  build trust across people who are working toward a common goal but are from different backgrounds in terms of race, class, caste, gender, and cultural settings.  With this paper, we hope to start a conversation in the learning sciences about how to build equitable partnerships in cultures across the world.
