# An Evaluation Framework for Pedagogical Large Language Models

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly adopted as AI tutors, providing scalable, interactive assistance across a range of topics. Yet there remains a lack of a unified, comprehensive framework for evaluating the pedagogical behaviors these models exhibit across multiple tutoring sessions and varying learning scenarios. This workshop addresses this gap by introducing a two-layer evaluation framework that distinguishes between (1) foundational behaviors that LLMs must demonstrate to be considered pedagogically qualified, and (2) stylistic instructional behaviors that shape their effectiveness as tutors. Participants will refine and apply this framework through hands-on interactions with an LLM-powered tutoring system, engaging in a five-session sequence of introductory Python programming modules. Through this workshop, we aim to advance the application of generative AI tools in education settings in a safe and pedagogically effective manner. His work focuses on large language models and AI safety with applications in education, and his research agenda is centered on building pedagogically effective and reliable AI tutors. Workshop theoretical background Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as AI tutors, offering personalized explanations, adaptive feedback, and conversational guidance across a wide range of learning contexts. Their capacity to generate interactive, context-aware instruction positions them as promising tools for expanding access to high-quality tutoring, particularly in domains such as programming, where LLMs have demonstrated considerable competence. However, despite their growing use in classrooms and learning platforms, the learning sciences community lacks a principled understanding of how these models teach and which pedagogical behaviors support or hinder learning. Prior evaluations of AI tutors have typically focused on behavioral metrics that do not clearly distinguish between pedagogically harmful behaviors and those that are pedagogically effective (LearnLM Team et al., 2025; Macina et al., 2025; Maurya et al., 2025). While valuable, these metrics overlook a central insight from contemporary pedagogical theory: not all instructional traits are equally suitable across learning situations, nor do all teaching styles promote learning in the same way. Friesen & Su (2023) argue that pedagogy is not a fixed set of methods or universally “correct” practices, but rather a context-dependent negotiation between competing pedagogical tensions, such as constraint versus freedom, proximity versus distance, and protection versus exposure. Pedagogy, in this view, “is structured not so much by processes, methods, and outcomes, but by i rresolvable oppositions and the tensions between them”. This perspective highlights that effective teaching cannot be reduced to a single optimal style; instead, instructional behaviors must flexibly align with learners’ needs, contexts, and developmental stages. Bringing this lens to AI tutors underscores the need for an evaluation framework that differentiates essential pedagogical requirements from stylistic instructional variations, ensuring models are assessed not only for pedagogically correct behavior but also for their alignment with pedagogically effective behavior. Moreover, due to increasing domain expertise of frontier LLMs, this can create an illusion of overestimation in their pedagogical performance (e.g. strong performance on programming tasks or reasoning benchmarks may not necessarily translate to an LLM’s ability to teach effectively). A lack of a concrete analysis of LLMs’ pedagogical behaviors risks the misuse of LLMs, and prevents researchers and practitioners from meaningfully developing the instructional quality and reliability of AI tutors. To address this gap, we identified pedagogically harmful behaviors in LLM tutors, such as conceptual inconsistencies, topic drift, and failures to detect misconceptions, and formalize these issues as forms of pedagogical misalignment. Ensuring the absence of such behaviors represents a minimal requirement for safe and trustworthy AI tutoring. Building on this foundation, we introduce a two-layer evaluation framework for pedagogical alignment. The first layer, pedagogical qualification, captures whether an LLM satisfies baseline standards of instructional safety. The second layer, pedagogical personality, characterizes stylistic instructional traits, such as scaffolding depth, adaptivity, metacognitive prompting, and instructional tone, that shape the learning experience and likely modulate learning gains. This workshop aims to refine and operationalize this framework, deepen our shared understanding of pedagogical traits in AI tutors, and collaboratively develop metrics for evaluating them. Through hands-on interaction with an LLM-powered tutoring system, participants will explore how pedagogical safety and pedagogical style intersect, and how these dimensions can inform the design of reliable, aligned, and learning-effective AI-based educational tools. Intended audience and logistics This workshop is designed for researchers and practitioners in educational technology, the learning sciences, AI/ML, and NLP who are interested in the reliable and effective use of generative AI for education. It is particularly relevant for scholars studying AI safety, AI in education, human-AI interaction, or instructional design, as well as developers building LLM-powered educational tools. The workshop is planned as a full-day event to allow sufficient time for hands-on interaction with the LLM-based tutoring system across multiple sessions, group analysis and annotations of pedagogical behaviors, and structured discussions aimed at refining the proposed evaluation framework. To support effective collaboration, we request a room equipped with round tables suitable for small-group work. If possible, we also request basic writing materials, such as sticky notes, paper, pens, and colored markers. Participants will be asked to bring their own laptops to interact with the AI tutor. The workshop is designed for a minimum of 8 and a maximum of 30 participants, ensuring meaningful interaction, hands-on guidance, and productive group collaboration. Outline of planned activities The workshop is organized as a full-day, highly interactive session combining hands-on experience with an AI tutoring system, individual analysis, and structured group discussions. Participants will engage in five short introductory Python programming sessions using an LLM-powered tutor, evaluate the tutor’s pedagogical behaviors, and collaboratively refine the proposed two-layer evaluation framework. Expected outcomes and contributions This workshop will equip participants with a refined and practically grounded framework for evaluating the pedagogical behaviors of LLM-powered tutors. Through hands-on interaction with an AI tutoring system, individual and collaborative annotation exercises, and structured group discussions, participants will collaboratively sharpen the distinction between pedagogical qualification, the foundational behaviors required for safe and trustworthy tutoring, and pedagogical personality, the stylistic instructional traits that influence learning effectiveness. The workshop will produce a consolidated set of pedagogical metrics, including clarified definitions, improved categorizations, and a consensus-based ranking of behaviors along a harmful–to–stylistic spectrum. This refined framework and metric set will support future empirical research and systematic tool development practices. Beyond methodological contributions, the workshop will foster a shared vocabulary and conceptual grounding for analyzing how LLM tutors behave across different learning scenarios. Participants will gain insight into the strengths and limitations of current AI tutoring systems, enabling them to better identify problematic behaviors, understand instructional variability, and connect model behaviors to established learning sciences theory. Thus, this workshop also aims to strengthen the research community at the intersection of AI education and AI safety by identifying open challenges and catalyzing collaborations across the learning sciences, AI/ML, and educational technology domains.
