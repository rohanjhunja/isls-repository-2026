# Fostering Community for Current and Future Latinx Science Teachers Through Testimonios and Co-Design

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how a network of current and future Latinx secondary science teachers engaged in co-design activities to explore and attend to the issue of Latinx underrepresentation among US science teachers. Using testimonios as data to inform the design of an infographic report, the co-design process yielded insights into the Latinx science teacher shortage and provided a valued community space for participants to find support.

## Introduction

Despite evidence of the benefits of having a more d iverse teacher workforce (e.g., Dee, 2005), minorit ized demographic groups in the US remain underrepresente d in the teacher workforce. In science education at  the secondary level, only 8% percent of science teachers in the US identify as “Hispanic” (Taie & Goldring, 2020), although nearly 1 of 4 US students identify similar ly (Fry & Lopez, 2012). While several studies have broadly examined the “pipeline” issue for Latinx teachers (Ocasio, 2014), the challenges faced by Latinx students as they seek to become science teachers remains underemphasized (Basile & Murrey, 2015). For this study, we recruited a network of current and future Latinx science teachers to engage in collaborative design (co-design; Voogt et al.,

2011) focused on exploring and developing an action to address the Latinx science teacher shortage. Our research

questions were: (Q1) How well can co-design activities surface insights into the Latinx science teacher shortage?; and (Q2) How might current and future Latinx science teachers’ benefit from engaging in co-design activities?

## Design Methodology

Situated learning, particularly the notion of “communities of practice,” informed our organization of the co-design process. We purposefully recruited participants wit h differing levels of participation in becoming sci ence educators. Participants included two female undergraduates with no science teaching experience, three graduate masters students (two female, one male) working to receive their science teaching credential, and one early career male science teacher. Participants in this study al l identified as Latinx. Undergraduate and graduate students attended the same western US university. The science teacher received his masters and teaching credential from there as well. Co-design occurred in three phases (see Figure 1) over eight 3 hour sessions spread over 6 months. Figure 1 All activity took place online via Zoom during the 2020-21 school year. Researchers played an important role in facilitating each session. Of note, participants’ personal testimonios served as the main source of data within the co-design process. Testimonio is a knowledge sharing genre common to Latin America where presenters seek to contextualize their experiences by seeking “to bring to light a wrong, a point of view, or an urgent call for action” (Reyes & Curry Rodríguez, 2012, p.525). Participant s shared their story (so far) of becoming a science  teacher and gave special emphasis to the academic, familial, and societal challenges they personally faced. Pa rticipants then analyzed each other’s testimonios to identify and discuss patterns of challenges – as well as pro mising supports – they deemed significant. With researcher  assistance, participants chose to develop an infog raphic highlighting the patterns they identified. This infographic has begun to be shared via social media with members of the university and local community and has already been viewed several thousand times.

## Claim 2: Co-design provided a valuable community space for participants

All but one participant explicitly noted how engaging with other Latinx science teachers (current or future) in the co-design space provided them with a supportive community. For example, the current science teacher noted how his participation provided community he had previously lacked, “Like I've said before, I didn't realize how much I needed to talk to other Latinx teachers before th is… I would really like this to continue and see wh ere I can help.” Similarly, Graduate Student 2 shared how she  felt she found community within the co-design work: “[w]hen you're looking for community, like you're definitely gonna stay if you end up finding it. I kn ow, like, I look forward to these meetings.”

## Discussion and Conclusion

This study provides a promising example of how researchers can utilize co-design to work closely in partnership with minoritized teacher populations to surface not  only new insights into longstanding issues of equi ty in education, such as the underrepresentation of Latinx science teachers in the US, but how to foster muc h needed support and community for participants. Notably, the use of testimonio in this study provided an innovative and wholly effective anchor for co-design activity, providing highly contextualized data as seen in previous testimonio work (Reyes & Curry Rodríguez, 2012), which sparked  rich discussions among participants that mediated the development of community as well as ownership of pr oducts like the infographic. Additionally, this stu dy demonstrates the promise of organizing co-design spaces to include participants at differing levels of participation in becoming teachers, and points to possible future directions for participatory design research methodologies.
