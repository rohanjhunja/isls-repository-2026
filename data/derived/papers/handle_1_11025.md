# Prison Industrial Complex Abolition Pedagogies: A Conceptual Framework for Uprooting Carceral Logics in Teacher Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
PIC abolition pedagogies is a flexible framework—conceptualizing PIC abolition as a pedagogy and tool for understanding learning—that is rooted in PIC abolition and pedagogies of joint activity. The possibilities of prison industrial complex (PIC) abolition pedagogies are explored through a community-engaged approach to teacher education, in which ethnic studies teachers and community members participated in monthly workshops to develop PIC abolition pedagogies. Implications for teacher (un)learning are also examined through this analysis.

The logic of control is steeped so deeply in schools that “[i]solation, humiliation, and exclusion are commonplace school responses to student misbehavior” (Shalaby, 2017, p. xxii). Indeed, punitive responses to misbehavior are presented as good approaches to classroom management in teacher education programs across the United States (Shalaby, 2017). Teacher education programs can be a valuable site for upending a culture of disposability but are often constrained by a multitude of factors, such as the individualistic orientation to teaching (Daniel et al.,

learning opportunities. Simply put, teacher education programs need to be redesigned to cultivate more expansive ways for educators to learn in community and about the school community to develop tools and strategies to disrupt carceral logics.

In this study, I examine how to support the dismantling of carceral logics in an ethnic studies teacher education program through prison industrial complex (PIC) abolition pedagogies and a community-engaged approach. Even though ethnic studies as a discipline is rooted in critical theory, ethnic studies educators need reorganized and sustained learning opportunities that foreground PIC abolition due to the insidious nature o f carceral logics. PIC abolition is “a political vision with the goal of eliminating imprisonment, policing, and surveillance and creating lasting alternatives to punishment and imprisonment…abolition is both a practical organizing tool and a long-term goal” (Critical Resistance, 20 22, para. 3 & 5). PIC “describe[s] the overlapping interests of government and industry that use surveillance, policing, and imprisonment as solutions to economic, social and political problems” (Critical Resistance, 20 22, para. 1). PIC abolition has clear commitments to dismantling carceral logics —by prioritizing PIC abolition in teacher education, ethnic studies teachers may be better positioned to engage with students and their communities to address their needs, disrupt oppression, and work towards liberation. Therefore, this study focuses on the following timely research questions, especially in the context of many American states’ contemporary pushes to expand ethnic studies teaching and learning: 1) How do ethnic studies teachers and preservice teachers grapple with and incorporate PIC abolitionist principles into their pedagogy? 2) What are the  limits and possibilities of ethnic studies teachers and teacher candidates incorporating PIC abolitionist principles, methods, and frameworks into their pedagogy? 3) How does the co-design of teacher education shape teachers’ pedagogy?

## Conceptual framework

PIC abolition pedagogies (see Figure 1), a new theoretical framework that has been refined through data analysis, conceptualizes PIC abolition as a pedagogy and provides a tool for understanding learning that is rooted in PIC abolition principles and pedagogical approaches and pedagogies of joint activity (Vossoughi et al., 2021). Learning is “the transformation of participation over time (Rogoff, 2003 as cited in Vossoughi et al., 2021, p. 4) and core principles of PIC abolition are shared with PIC abolition pedagogies. Pedagogies of joint activity also support the operationalization of core aspects of PIC abolition. Pedagogies of joint activity “privilege[e] the dynamic and mutual learning that can emerge through intergenerational collaboration (Wertsch, 1998; Rogoff, 2003 as cited in Vossoughi et al., 2021)…activities ‘with purposes connected explicitly with the history and current practices of the community’” (Rogoff, 1999, p. 211 as cited in Vossoughi et al., 2021, p. 4). Similar to Vossoughi et al.’s (2021) study, pedagogical talk, listening, and embodied assistance may have shaped ethnic studies teachers’ learning about PIC abolition political commitments, but there are also expansive possibilities for the emergence of more forms of collective engagement that support teacher learning. During PIC abolition workshops, teachers interacted in a myriad of ways, including inquiry groups, community circles, and whole 2377 group dis cussions. Pedagogies of joint activity also provide language and tools for operationalizing the social context beyond the adult-and child-centered education binary —positioning all co-designers as learners and teachers while also creating space for community practices to emerge. ## Study design

The analysis I present for this poster is part of my dissertation study, which is in its final stage of data collection at the time of the writing of this poster. My study is situated in a Teacher Residency Ethnic Studies Program (TRESP) at a large urban u niversity on the West Coast of the United States where community members participated in PIC abolition workshops during monthly professional development (PD) meetings.  TRESP community members co-designed the PIC abolition workshops with me as the workshop facilitator and lead designer. Because it offers an invaluable approach to research that contests the deprofessionalization of educators and leverages teachers’ assets through sustained joint activity (Philip et al., 2022), teacher solidarity co-design informed my approach to PD workshop design. Similar to PIC abolition, teacher solidarity co-design strives for transformation and partnerships anchored in solidarity. Data collection started in July 2023 and will conclude in June 2024 and includes interviews, questionnaires, artifacts, video recordings of workshops, and field notes from workshops and classroom observations.

Expected contributions and future directions Preliminary findings showcase how joint activity created openings for teachers and community members to resist and transition towards dismantling a culture of productivity stemming from racial capitalism and how a designed teacher learning space that foreg rounded PIC abolition pedagogies supported teacher and community members’ well-being and in some cases healing. I conjecture that what will emerge from further triangulation of analysis of data will yield insights about how PIC abolition workshops supported and hindered teachers’ development of their abolitionist praxis. In the poster session, I will discuss the possibilities of PIC abolition pedagogies for examining teacher healing through joint activity.
