# Cultivating Feedback Loops: Factors and Contradictions Impacting Groups Developing Emerging Technologies

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Feedback loops bring together participants from multiple contributing groups in purposeful, multi-directional, iterative, and transformative interactions to design or develop an innovation. We present the idea of three-way feedback loops between researchers, educators, and product teams. Using cultural-historical activity theory, we examine the cultural, historical, and contextual factors impacting each group’s participation in a feedback loop and the contradictions that arise within and between the systems. As research increasingly aims to center the voices of individuals who are most impacted by its outcomes and ensure innovations best serve the communities for whom they are created, feedback loops are a promising collaboration strategy. However, cultivating a productive feedback loop requires careful navigation of the different contexts and the contradictions between the groups.

## Introduction and theoretical framework

The theme for ISLS 2024 is “Learning as a cornerstone of healing, resilience, and community" and in it  we see the importance of community in learning and inclusi on in learning design. This paper explores how thre e communities can come together in service of co-designing new learning technologies to serve divers e groups of students. For decades, researchers have called for the inclus ion of interested parties and the use of participat ory and co-design strategies to democratize the design process  and develop more equitable innovations (Muller & Kuhn, 1993). This has led to co-design projects that include practitioners, students, and parents (e.g., Coenraad et al., 2021), and partnership models such as research practice partnerships (RPPs; e.g., Coburn et al., 2013), research practice industry partnerships (RPIPs; e.g., Pautz Stephenson et al., 2022), and research centers (e.g., Martin et al., 2023). These collaborative design models have led to positive outcomes including gains in student knowledge, improved student engagement, increased practitioner ownership, and positive teacher  experiences (Penuel et al., 2007; Zhang  et al., 2010). However, they have surfaced challenges for participating teachers such as lack of teacher ownership early in a project, slow-moving timelines, and significant time investment (Penuel et al., 2007). We note, the length and depth of partners’ contribu tions differs significantly depending on the innovation. While some projects integrate design pa rtner voice at multiple points or continuously thro ughout the process, others include the voices of interested pe rsons at only one or two points, typically to gathe r early input or test an innovation (Druin, 2002). Feedback loops  are structures and approaches that are purposeful, multidirectional, iterative, and transformative to ensur e the ideas and voices of multiple contributing gro ups are included throughout the design process (Baker et al., 2022). We study feedback loops as a generative collaboration strategy in the development of emerging technologies due to their focus on multi-directionality and transformation of knowledge, actions, and goals for all participan ts. In this paper, we use cultural-historical activity theory (CHAT), specifically third generation CHAT, as our theoretical framework (Engeström, 2001, 2020) to examine the cultural, historical, and contextual factors and contradictions that impact feedback loops between researchers, educators, and product teams. Identifying contradictions and tensions in a CHAT system is an essential component of the work and provides crucial understanding for future improvements as “contradictions lead to transformation” (Roth et al., 2009 p. 144). Additionally, in the fr amework, it is possible to consider multiple “plane s of focus” from individuals to collectives (Rogoff, 2008). Con sidering the different planes of focus allows you t o identify different contradictions. In this paper, we discuss  the activity of each group, researchers, educators, and product teams, at the collective plane of focus, rather than on individuals. In this project, we sought to answer the research question: What are the cultural and contextual factors and contradictions, both within-group and between-group, impacting the participation of researchers, educators, and product teams in feedback loops around emerging  technologies?  Third generation CHAT allowed us to investigate the different perspectives in the three  interdependent systems around the activity of part icipating in a feedback loop to develop emerging technologies. Thi s framework is especially valuable as it gives insi ght to situated activities of multiple systems, illuminates processes and structures not normally visible, and shows issues that arise within and across the systems. This, in turn, can help us see expansive possibilities for n ew systemic connections and transformations in activities and then into society (Roth et al., 2009).

## Methods

We analyzed surveys, focus groups, interviews, field notes, and convening artifacts co llected from researchers (6), educators (60-80 in convenings, 6 in focus groups), and product teams (7 teams, 17 individuals) with an interest in feedback loops around emerging educational techn ologies (e.g., artificial intelligence, adaptive tutors; exact technologies differed depending on participan t and design). Data were collected sequentially between October 2022 and September 2023 such that data from each group could impact the next convening, focus group, or set of interviews (Figure 1). Survey data, field notes, and convening artifacts were collected at each convening and all focus groups/interviews were recorded and t ranscribed. The first author led analysis by review ing data from each convening or set of focus groups/intervie ws and developing analytical memos outlining findin gs. Quantitative survey data were analyzed through desc riptive statistics and qualitative survey data, fie ld notes, convening artifacts, and focus groups/interviews we re analyzed using thematic inductive coding. Themes  were presented to the team members who planned and atten ded the convenings and conducted the interviews or focus groups to discuss and critique  the emerging themes. Following the first two phase s of data collection (i.e., educator convenings #1 and 2, researcher interviews rounds #1 and 2, and product team focus groups rounds #1 and 2), the two researchers, after the second author suggested it, developed an initial CHAT system based on the collected data. The system was shared with the project team members who planned  and attended the convenings and and/or conducted the in terviews/focus groups with a focus on the system with which they m ost commonly interact (e.g., team members who work regularly with product teams reviewed the product t eam system). Based on their feedback, the system was updated. Finally, the system was shared at the joint convening s o the educators, researchers, and product team could member-check it. Five participants from each of the groups (i.e., educators, researchers, product team members) collaborated in-person to critique and verify the inclusions within each of the CHAT components (e.g., subject, mediating tools, rules, community, divisio n of labor, objectives). Following the final conven ing, the system was updated. The system following these rounds of work is presented in the findings.

## Findings

While educators, researchers, and product teams all demonstrated significant interest in participating in a feedback loop around developing emerging technologies, parti cipants identified challenges stemming from both th eir position or organization and the dynamics of working between the groups. Using CHAT (Figure 2), we examined the cultural and contextual factors and contradicti ons that arose within each system and between syste ms when discussing working in this feedback loop. In genera l, the three groups discussed different mediating t ools, rules, community, and division of labor, highlighting diff erences between the groups, but also assets they wo uld bring to the development process. While their individual group objectives showed little overlap, the groups were able to consider and agree on overlapping objectives that could exist when working with other groups in the feedback loop. Finally, we found that working between groups  would be possible, but require d alleviating contradictions related to timeline, scale, motivations, outputs, a nd priorities. All three group’s largest concern was time and timeline, but it was seen in different ways. Educators needed time outside of their classroom duties; product teams needed to work on tight production timelines; resea rchers wanted time for rigorous testing and research to understand the impacts and outcomes of the work.

Developing the details for each component of the activity system was an iterative process. Due to space constraints, we are not able to go into detail abou t the contradictions, but they have been key to inc reasing our understanding of next steps. ## Discussion

Feedback loops between researchers, educators, and product teams offer a promising opportunity for collaborative development of emerging technologies, but creating a feedback loop between these three groups is not w ithout challenges. This paper outlines how we created the system and also the preliminary exploration of contextual and historical factors and contradictions that have led to difficulties. We will continue to explore how conditions might be designed for feedback loop interactions that wou ld lead to the successful development of new learni ng technologies. Participants in all three groups sugg ested that a successful feedback loop will need to handle the difference in incentives, priorities, regulations, and timelines across the three groups. As discussed in the findings section, time was an issue for all groups, and iden tification of the differences could lead to changes  that help the groups work together. From all three groups, it was  clear that buy-in is needed from leadership to ensure a successful feedback loop, particularly at schools o r districts and product companies. All three groups  identified or agreed with shared objectives and positive possibilities, like making research more accessible to teachers, as a result of the feedback loop work. They suggested th at feedback loops across researchers, educators, and product teams could be used not only in the creation of new  emerging technologies, but also in considering pol icy recommendations around emerging technologies in education. While this paper presents initial work about feedba ck loops for the development of emerging technologies, to fully understand a system with edu cators, researchers, and product teams will require  going beyond the people involved so far. Future work shou ld involve additional researchers, educators, and p roduct teams to build out the activity system and work through contradictions. Additionally, exploring what new systems (e.g., families or students) should be added is an important direction. We note conversations of power  were not raised by participants in this early study, however, power dynamics between the groups, how power materializes in a Feedback Loop, and power between designers and  participants all are key considerations needed in future work. In addi tion, an in-depth study of the activity of feedback loops at th e individual plane of focus with an educator, researcher, and product team that were su ccessful versus not will provide further insights a nd ideas for future feedback loop connections.
