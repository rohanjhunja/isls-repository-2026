# Exploring Sequential Patterns in Academic Help-Seeking: Transitions and Performance in a College Science Course

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Academic help-seeking is a critical self-regulated learning strategy that supports academic performance, particularly in complex learning environments like flipped classrooms. Previous researchers highlight the role of diverse help-seeking sources, yet little is known about how students transition between these sources over time. In this study, we examined academic help-seeking behaviors in an introductory science course throughout a semester. Undergraduates relied heavily on system-embedded help and generally remained within the same help category. Higher-performing students favored system-embedded and personalized support sources, whereas lower-performing students relied more on structured supplemental sessions and information search forums. These findings underscore the importance of understanding dynamic help-seeking patterns and their relationships to academic outcomes. This work advances the study of help-seeking behaviors in real-world educational settings.

## Introduction

Academic help-seeking (AHS) is a crucial self-regulated learning (SRL) strategy for college students (Karabenick & Gonida, 2017). SRL includes loosely sequenced pha ses of learning such as forethought, performance, a nd reflection (Zimmerman, 2002). Like SRL, AHS is sequential. The sequential characteristics  of help-seeking described in the Nelson-Le Gall’s 5-stage model (1981), start with (1) an awareness of the need for help, (2) a decision to seek help, (3) identification of help sources, (4) use of strategies to elicit help, and (5) processing the help requested. In that third stage, Nelson-Le Gall theorized that learners select a single sou rce, which they may continue with or replace based on its effectiveness. Although a single source may suffice for smaller problems, complex tasks in college science courses often require learners to seek help from multiple sources, such as forums, instructors, and peers (Wirtz et al., 2018).

In college introductory science courses, learners h ave access to different categories of help-seeking sources, including System-Embedded (SE), Structured Supplemental (SS), Inform ational Search (IS), and Personalized Support (PS). SE help includes embedded supports like hints in problem-solving tasks included by designers of digital platforms like I ntelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS; Vaessen et al., 2014). SE often requires minimal effort from learners to access help but lim its their ability to ask questions because learners  can only use pre-defined hints. SS consists of  optional, weekly group learning sessions, such as s upplemental instruction (Dawson et al., 2014). SS requires moderate effort to attend and participate, and learners have some a gency in asking questions, though the interaction is often m ediated by a session leader. IS help allows learners to phrase questions in their own words on platforms like asyn chronous course forums (Williams-Dobosz et al., 2021). IS requires high effort from learners to formulate and post questions and allows high agency in asking questions and negotiating responses through ongoing discussions a nd searches. PS help involves meetings where learne rs receive tailored support from instructors, teaching assistants, and peers (Ko & Stephens-Martinez, 2023). PS often demands the most effort for learners to attend and engage, providing the highest degree of agency in directing the conversation and negotiating immediate, tailored responses. These categories move beyond the binary framework proposed by Makara and Karabenick (2013), which exa mined help sources based on their role  (formal vs. informal), the relationship with the help-seeker (personal vs. impersonal), the communication channel (mediated vs. face-to-face), and adaptability (dynamic vs. static). Instead, this integrated approach considers both the effort learners exert and the agency they have when seeking help from different sources. Transitions between help-seeking sources Learners can transition between use of help source categories, such as moving from SS to PS, or can use multiple sources within a single category (e.g., a type of SE to ano ther type of SE). Research suggests that students often begin with informal sources and progress to formal ones (Wirtz et al., 2018), yet transition patterns remain largely unexplored. Higher-performing students are more likely to seek help (K arabenick & Gonida, 2017) and tend to favor in-person AHS, whereas lower-performing students prefer mediated AHS (Reeves & S perling, 2015). However, the link between help-seeking source choice and performance across more t han two binary categories is under-researched. In addition, little is known about how students move between different help-seeking sources and how these transitions  relate to their academic performance.  Understanding these patterns will not only contribute to theory by deepening our knowledge of self-regulated learning processes, but also offer practi cal implications for promoting more effective academic support systems. Thus, in this study, we aimed to address three research questions:

1. What are common sequential patterns in college students’ transitions between help-seeking sources?

2. How do higher-performing students and lower-performing students differ in their help-seeking source

usage across the four categories of help-seeking sources?

3. How do higher-performing students and lower-performing students differ in their help-seeking source

transition patterns among the four categories of help-seeking sources?

## Participants

This study involved 488 undergraduate students enrolled in a first-year undergraduate introductory biology course at a Southeastern public university in the U.S. in Fall 2019. Participants consented to share their digital trace data on course-related electronic platforms and their usage of cam pus resources, in addition to their academic performances. Course grade was a composite score that included ho mework performance (18%), class participation (7 %), and three exams (75%) (M = 82. 711, SD = 9.737). Students were divided into four q uartiles based on their course performance; higher-performing students were those in the first quartile (n=121, M = 93.223, SD = 2.521), and lower-performing students were those in the fourth quartile (n=121, M = 69.510, SD = 7.954). We chose groups at the extremes of our continuum wi th the hope of showing the most important differenc es in AHS transition behaviors and how they related to learning performance. Data sources of AHS behaviors This course followed a flipped learning format, inc orporating an active learning approach during face-to-face sessions and providing practice opportunities through home exercises. Both the course and the university offered numerous help-seeking opportunities. We categorized 12 help-seeking events into four types (SE, SS, IS, PS) based on the effort that learners need to exert and the agency they have when seeking help from different sources. ## Data analysis

For RQ1, we used sequence mining to examine 2-event, 3-event, and 4-event patterns across all students who had any help-seeking events, identifying common sequences. Analyzing sequences of varying lengths separately helps capture both simple and complex help-seeking patterns and balances cases with consecutive dominant events. For example, if SE is dominant, then top sequences may be SE SE, SE SESE, and so forth, which can overshadow other less frequent but potentially meaningful patterns that involve transitions to other forms of helpseeking. By analyzing sequences of different length s separately, researchers can gain a more nuanced understanding of the dynamics of help-seeking, identifying both straightforward repeated actions and more complex transitions across different categories of help sources. For RQ2, we compared the two groups’ category counts in SE, SS, IS, and PS using the Mann-Whitney U test due to non-normal data distributions. For RQ3, we applied differential sequence pattern mining to compare the groups ’ transitions among help-seeking events, focusing on the top five 2-event, 3-event, and 4-event sequences. One important note is that for both RQ1 and RQ3, we  considered consecutive help-seeking events. Although previous studies (e.g., Zhou, 2023) have combined consecutive events into a single event (e.g., SESE SE into just SE), we chose to retain consecutive help-seeking events. This decision was made because we used aggregated categories, and even within the same bro ader category, learners were engaged in distinct ev ents. Moreover, consecutive help-seeking events may reflect learners’ academic help-seeking strategies.

## Discussion, limitations, and future directions

In this study, we found that undergraduate students  in an introductory science course tend to rely hea vily on system-embedded help. This highlights the importance of on-demand support when learners work in computerized learning environments outside of clas s. Additionally, learners’ tendency to remain within the same help source aligns with broader self-regulated learning research, which indicates that l earners often persist with the same strategy regardless of its effectiveness (Dembo & Seli, 2004). Higher-performing students used more system-embedded help, such as homework hints, aligning with research underscoring the importance of before-class preparation in flipped classrooms (Rahman et al., 2015). In contrast, lower-performing students, used structured supplemental h elp (e.g., group review sessions) and used informational search  (e.g., course forums) more frequently. Their preference for structured supplemental (SS) help may reflect SS’ role in reinforcing content and strategy exposure (Martin & Arendale, 1992). Their reliance on Informational Search (e.g., course forums) aligns with findings that that lower-performing students tend to favor mediated help-seeking (Reeves & Sperling, 2015).

Regarding help source transitions, higher-performing students exhibited more frequent transitions within personalized support (e.g., one-on-one tutoring with peers and instructors). This might suggest that personalized supports provide cultural capital in addition to kn owledge that could contribute to students’ higher course performance (Richards, 2022).  H igher performing students also had more transitions from system-embedded support to personalized support, whereas lower-performing students remained within structured supplemental and informational search sources. This might suggest that higher-performing students are more adaptable in their helpseeking strategies, similar to findings in SRL research (Foong et al., 2021). This study has several limitations. First, the sequence mining results here only demonstrated the top five sequences of varying lengths in each group and do n ot imply statistical significance, as the sequences  were not analyzed against other predictors (Bannert et al., 2014). Second, we limited analysis to sequences of up to four events to avoid data sparsity; examining longer chains could yield insights into understanding help-seeking. Third, the groups were retrospectively defined based on le arners’ final exam performance and included trace data from the full semester; these limit opportunities to design interventions early or during the semester. Despite these limitations, this study is among the first to empirically examine help-seeking behaviors in a real-world learning context  by analyzing transitions across multiple help sourc es over time.  The findings challenge traditional views of help sources as binary (e.g., in-person vs. mediated; formal vs. informal) and suggest the need for a more nuanced categorization. The findings also align with and elaborate the third stage of Nelson-LeGall’s help-seeking model, where learners select and either persi st with or switch help sources based on effectiveness—though our findings suggest habit may play a strong er role than effectiveness. These findings can inform instructors about the preferences and behaviors of higher-and lower-performing students in navigating different help sources. Future researchers should use methods like variable selection to identify sequences that are statistically significantly related to performance.  Researchers can also refine student grouping crite ria by incorporating prior know ledge and in-semester performance changes. Special attention should also be given to students with low initial knowledge who later achieve high performance, as their trajectories may reveal effective help-seeking strategies that can inform intervention designs.
