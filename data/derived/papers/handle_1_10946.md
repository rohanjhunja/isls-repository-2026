# Resource-rich versus Resource-poor Assessments of Multivariable Causality in Simulations-based Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study addresses resource-rich assessments, using interactive simulations, compared to traditional resource-poor assessments to detect differences in learning outcomes induced by two different learning approaches. Students' reasoning about complex relationships within scientific phenomena was assessed. 71 undergraduate students completed resource-poor pretest, exploration task using interactive simulation (before or after instruction) and resource-poor and resource-rich posttests. Results show that resource-rich assessments do not offer significant advantages over resource-poor assessments, regardless of the learning approach utilized.

Objectives Assessments are primarily intended to help educator s understand what students know and cando. However, students’ learning involves mental representations and proces ses that cannot be directly  measured. Therefore, assessment is a process of evidence-based reasoning. Assessments are tools that play a crucial role in the field of Learning Sciences. They help identify differences in learning facilita tion between different learning approaches. Traditional assessments are usually paper-and-pencil based and resource-poor, which lack access to any additional resources. They typically measure students' knowledge at a given point in time by asking them to recall facts and reproduce content knowledge or apply fixed solutions for structured problems.  However, resource-rich assessments include additional resources, such as digital resources, which enable learners to solve au thentic and complex problems by engaging in an iterative process of reasoning, problem-solving, and decision-making. This widens the range of complex constructs and other as pects of students' performance that can be measured and cannot be evaluated through resource-poor assessments. Resource-rich assessments represent a powerful shift in the focus of assessment from  simple knowledge reproduction to knowledge-in-use (Foster, 2023). In this study, we focus on exploring the sensitivity of the two ty pes of assessment used to evaluate the development of multivariable causality reasoning (MVC; Saba et al., 20 23) when learning is based on using interactive simulations. MVC reasoning focuses on the process of why and how  interacted factors impact an observed outcome when exploring complex scientific phenomena. Recently, interactive simulations have been proven to be an effective tool for learning complex phenomena (Saba et al., 2021). However, it is unclear whether and how using resource-rich assessments, employing interactive simulations, and resource-poor assessment allows the detection of differences in learning outcomes about  MVC reasoning that are promoted by different learn ing approaches, Problem-Solving before Instruction (PS-I), and Instruction before Problem-Solving (I-PS; Loibl et al., 2017). The PS-I approach is adapted to exploration phase (with simulation) befo re instruction (Explorationfirst condition), compared to  I-PS approach, which starts with  instruction, followed by exploration using simulations (Instruction-first condition). Thus, the research question of this study addresses whether and to what extent are different types of assessment, resource-rich, and resource-poor, sensitive to differences in learning outcomes regarding MVC reasoning as induced by the two different learning approaches.

## Method, materials, and data sources and analysis

Seventy-one undergraduate students were randomly assigned t o either Exploration-first condition (n= 35) or Instruction-first condition (n= 36). They completed a resource-poor pretest and intervention where they engaged in exploration task and instruction phase. Following the intervention, both groups completed resource-poor posttest and resource-rich posttest. The difference between conditions was in the order of the exploration task and the instruction: exploration before (Exploration-first) or after (Instruction-first) the instruction. Learning materials include an exploration task and direct instruction of MVC reasoning.  T he exploration task focused on investigating a virus transmission  in a population using  a “Virus Spread”  simulation (Figure 1). Data sources included: resource-poor pretest, resource-poor posttest, and resource-rich posttest. The resource-poor pretest comprised an open-ended question that aimed to assess students' prior  knowledge of MVC reasoning. The resource-poor posttest consisted of a screenshot of a "virus spread" simul ation that showcased the spread of a virus in a village. It included an open-ended question that required the students to analyze the given factors' certain values and how they impacted the number of deaths r esulting from the pandemic. The  resource-rich posttest included similar context and question as resource-poor posttest. However, it was accompanied with the "virus spread" simulation, which allowed students to explore the phenomenon and answer the q uestion. To evaluate MVC reasoning, a coding scheme was created  based on Krist et al., 2019, and students’ reasoning were scored between 0-3.

## Results

ANOVA test was conducted to compare students’ prior knowledge regarding MVC reasoning between conditions. Results of analyzing resource-poor pretest showed that both conditions have similar low prior knowledge regarding MVC reasoning, and no significant difference s were found: Exploration-first: M=0.54, SD= 0.56, Instruction-first: M= 0.39, SD=0.59; F(1.69)=1.249, p=0.268,  ηp2 = 0.018. MANCOVA test was used to compare conditions (Exploration-first versus Instruction-first) related to measuring MVC reasoning when using resourcerich and resource-poor posttests, resource-poor pretest was used as a covariate. Findings revealed no significant difference between conditions  in each type of assessment: F(1.69)= 0.296, p=0.745,  ηp2 = 0.009. In resourcepoor posttest: Exploration-first: M=1.40, SD= 1.14, Instruction-first: M= 1.50, SD=1.18. In resource-rich posttest: Exploration-first: M=1.03, SD= 1.44, Instruction-first: M= 1.08, SD=1.42. Furthermore, no significant differences in learning outcomes were revealed when using resource-rich versus resource-poor assessment in each condition.

## Scholarly significance

The focus of this study is on the sensitivity of using two types of assessment (resource-poor versus resource-rich) to detect differences in learning outcomes of MVC reasoning. It compares between two learning approaches using simulations, exploratory learning which involves exploration prior to instruction and conventional learning (I-PS approach) which involves instruction followed by ex ploration. Findings revealed no significant affordance of using a resource-rich assessment over  using a  resource-poor assessment in measuring learning about MVC reasoning, and this result is not sensitive to a certain learning approach. This result suggests that when the learning context is based on using interactive simulation, different types of assessments, whether resource-rich or resource-poor, can similarly measure students’ learning of MVC reasoning. Additionally, findings found that the sequence of exploration and instruction is not sensitive to th e type of the assessment. In other words, there is no significant difference between the two learning app roaches when outcomes are assessed using either resourcepoor or resource-rich assessments. It remains to explore whether and how resource-poor and resource-rich assessments of students’ learning bout MVC would better predict the transferability of MVC reasoning learning.
