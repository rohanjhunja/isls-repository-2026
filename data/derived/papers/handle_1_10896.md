# Facilitating Data Conversations with Generative AI

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
There exists a need to promote critical data literacy, so that individuals can critique the ethics behind data work. We present results from using ChatGPT, a generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) tool, to facilitate data discussions. Generally, AI-generated questions successfully built on participants’ ideas and prompted discussions of data practices. Participants engaged in critical data literacy ideas, although to a lesser extent. We discuss the promise of using AI tools to facilitate data conversations.

## Introduction

Descriptions of data literacy have focused on data collection, analysis, and visualization (Lee & Wilkerson, 2018). Scholars have also called for critical definitions of data literacy, t o consider the ethics and power behind data curation, processing, and representation. Data feminism framework (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020) involves principles to question data categories, recognize emotions beh ind data curation and analyses, and position data work in context. These efforts consider that individuals are active agents who leverage data (D’Ignazio, 2017). One way to facilitate critical data literacy is through community-based conversations (Lupi & Posavec, 2016). We present preliminary results from using ChatGPT, a Generative AI tool, to facilitate these discussions. We ask: RQ1: How did the AI-generated questions build off the discussions? RQ2: How did the questions facilitate data literacy?

## Methods

We analyzed the audio recordings from four dyads engaging in data conversations via Microsoft Teams (120 min of audio data). The conversations were automatically transcribed in real time. Participants were graduate students in an Instructional Design program in the Mountain West United States (five Master’s, three PhD; 37.5%  male; 62.5% female). They represented diverse experience with data (h aving taken 2.63 statistics classes on average, SD = 1.27). Participants received $10 gift cards for their participation (IRB #13497). The data conversations focused on recreation decisi ons and their environmental impacts. This context allowed for connecting personal data and data in social, economic, and ecosystem topics, to position participants as agents in urban planning. The dyads participated in two data conversations in  random orders: one with four predetermined questions (generated by ChatGPT and revised by the authors) and one with AI facilitation. Both conditions asked about data collection, analysis, and representation, but the AI facilitation condition had additional access to the unfolding conversations. Participants started both conditions by picking a recreation activity with personal significance. Then, they had 15 min to discuss how to improve the identified  activity through data use (total 30 min/dyad). We used a Wizard-of-Oz design for AI facilitation: researchers manually pasted the unfolding transcripts from Microsoft Teams into ChatGPT interface and shared the AI-generated questions one by one with participants. We inserted a question when there was a natural p ause in the conversation, or every four minutes. The ChatGPT prompt is: “You are a facilitator of a conversation between two  group members with little to no data knowledge. You will interject and ask us questions to help us understand data and data implications, including data terminology. I will submit several points of dialogue, and you can ask questions to help guide the conversation to consider topics found in the Data Feminism framework […] Be sure to mention data terminology […]” For RQ1, we qualitatively analyzed the AI questions for whether or not they (1) reflected the researchers’ instruction prompts for critical data literacy ideas and (2) built off participants’ conversations. For RQ2, we used deductive coding based on research on data practices (Lee & Wilkerson, 2018) and data feminism (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020). Data practices pertain to data collection, analysis  (e.g., filtering data; comparing data; applying statistics), and visualization. Meanwhile, data feminism attends to critical data literacy ideas, such as considering multiple knowledge forms, making the labor behind data work visible, and attending to the emotions that influence data curation and representation (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020). We added two codes emerging from the discu ssion: understand variability (data practices) and ethics/privacy (critical data literacy).

## Findings

AI facilitation questions built off the conversations, but did not always reflect prompts All of the questions ChatGPT created in real time reflected the prompts to focus on data practices. Encouragingly, 79% of the questions built off the conversation, by  quoting or referencing something that participants  had said. However, only 50% of the questions demonstrated cri tical data literacy, and 36% focused on recreationa l and environmental topics. For example, ChatGPT asked: “Baylor [pseudonym], you suggested having users fill out a profile or survey. What specific information do you think should be col lected in this profile to better tailor recommendations? How can we ensure that users provi de accurate information?” While the question mentioned ideas that Baylor had raised and prompted about data collection, it did not reflect critical data literacy perspectives.

## Participants engaged with data practices and critical data literacy

AI facilitation promoted data practices, including data collection (23 total utterances across dyads; M = 5.75, SD = 1.5), analysis like comparing data, statistical techniques, and filtering/aggregating/sorting data; 14 utterances; M = 3.50, SD = 2.58), and checking data quality  (validating sources; 5 utterances; M = 1.25, SD =.63). When asked: “Could you elaborate on how you might use geographic information systems or mapping tools to visualize the data about popular locations and community engagement?”, a participant proposed: “I could see using Google Maps they have a tool that will show you times thro ughout the day … which locations are most visited”. This example highlights data collection, analysis tools,  and visualization. We further compare the  AI facilitation and pre-determined conditions (Figure 1). Due to the small sample size, we  are not reporting statistical tests, and instead report numbers descriptively. We observe more frequent mentions of data variability and communication with AI facilitation  (variability: AI: 5 utterances; M = 1.25, SD =.63; predetermined: 0 utterances; communication: AI: 5 utterances; M = 1.25, SD =.75; predetermined: 1 utterance; M =.33, SD =.33) There were instances of critical data literacy, although to a lesser extent. With AI facilitation, participants considered data ethics and privacy, such as considering the anonymity of data collection (11 total; M = 2.75, SD = 3.20), make visible the labor  behind data collection (8 utterances; M = 2, SD = 0), and recognize emotion underlying data work (7 utterances; M = 1.75, SD = 2.22). These ideas were more present with  real-time AI facilitation (Figure 1). Consider the discussion in dyad four, who were developing a family friendly hiking app. ChatGPT prompted: “How can we make sure that our hi king app promotes fairness and inclusivity by incorporating or diverse viewpoints and addressing potential biases in the data?” Participants naturally mentioned that the data being collected would come from indiv iduals with varying levels of experience, and data collection should consider these differences: “Like I said, an  expert's going to be like, oh, this was an easy hi ke, whereas your family is going to be like, no, it was not.” T his exchange demonstrates consideration of context and acknowledgement of experiences influencing data curation. One dyad brought up the need to consult Indigenous knowledge about data representations (embrace plura lism, 3 utterances). We did not find many examples that articulated data context (1 utterance) or reconsidered data binaries and hierarchies  (1 utterance). ## Conclusion

The facilitation questions generated by ChatGPT showed promise in building on the unfolding discussions and inviting participants to engage in certain data practices and critical data literacy ideas, compared to the condition with pre-generated prompts. The conversations bridged participants’ ideas and personal experiences with tangible data talk. However, utterances showing critical data literacy occurred less frequently tha n data practices. This points to opportunities to experiment with promptin g strategies for generative AI tools  and compare the impact of different configurations (e.g., AI-generated, human-generated, hybrid human and AI) on data conversations.
