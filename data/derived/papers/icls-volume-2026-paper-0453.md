# Building Bodies, Building Minds: A Hands-On STEM Activity in Biomechanical Engineering for Elementary Students

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how 10 elementary students engaged in a biomedical engineering activity. Students built spine models, designed prosthetic discs, and tested materials through compression and bending tasks. Reflexive thematic analysis of video and survey data revealed four themes: engineering mindset development, embodied anatomical exploration, material testing through interaction, and social collaboration. The findings suggest that hands-on activities grounded in distributed cognition can support students’ engineering mindsets and understanding of anatomy and material properties.

Over the last 30 years, we have been moving toward a world where technology is not only part of our daily lives but increasingly integrated within our bodies, shaping how we live and think. Biomedical engineering, a discipline that integrates science and engineering, is leading us toward this future by developing technologies that unify human bodies and machines, such as pacemakers, AI-enhanced prosthetics, and wireless e-health systems that save lives (Baradaran Shoraka, 2024). As Hutchins (1995) noted, “The future of medicine is not just in the machine that comes to your bedside, but in the technology that comes to your body” (p. 226). STEM education, particularly engineering, supports the development of analytical thinking and an engineering mindset throu gh real-world learning experiences (Mohr-Schroeder et al., 2014; Nugent & Barker, 2014).  Preparing young people for a technology-integrated world requires skills such as applying interdisciplinary knowledge, critical thinking, creativity, and problem-solving (Kennedy & Sundberg, 2025; OECD, 2023). STEM education, particularly engineering, supports the development of analytical thinking and an engineering mindset through real-world learning experiences (Mohr-Schroeder et al., 2014; Nugent & Barker, 2014). Despite the inclusion of engineering in the Next Generation Science Standards and programs such as Engineering is Elementary (Cunningham, 2009), biomedical engineering concepts and practices remain largely absent from K-12 education. As a result, many students are unaware of biomedical engineering careers and their role in improving human health (Carter, 2020; Li et al., 2019; Melvin, 2024). Moreover, engineering fields face ongoing workforce shortages as relatively few students pursue STEM pathways, despite increasing demand for skilled workers (Deming & Noray, 2020). To address this gap, we introduced elementary students to biomedical engineering through a hands-on STEM activity using accessible materials that integrated concepts from biology, biomedical engineering, materials science, and mathematics. This study explored the following questions: (1) How does a learning activity informed by distributed cognition support elementary students’ sensemaking of biomedical engineering concepts and the development of  an engineering mindset? (2) In what ways do elementary students participate in engineering practices, and how does this participation influence their interest in biomedical engineering?

## Theoretical framework

This study draws on a distributed cognition framework, which posits that thinking and action are distributed across people and non-human artifacts, including tools, representations, and environments (Hutchins, 1995). This framework informed our  design and analysis. Biomedical engineering devices such as prostheses and implants represent this idea, because functional responsibilities are shared between the body and technological artifacts. Clark and Chalmers (1998) similarly argue that cognition extends beyond the individual, as humans form coupled systems with tools and representations integral to thought and action. Prior work has also shown how engineering problem-solving and learning are distributed (Ramey & Uttal, 2017; Stevens & Hall, 1998).

## Method

This study was conducted during a single 90-minute session of an afterschool program at a racially and socioeconomically diverse, suburban elementary school in the Midwestern United States. Twenty children participated, of whom 10 agreed to participate in research (3 girls, 7 boys; ages 8–11; grades 3–5). The workshop began with a pre-survey assessing students’ knowledge of biomedical engineering, the spine, and prosthetics. Example questions included: “What do you think biomedical engineers do?”, “Do you know what a prosthesis 2788 is?”, and “Do all materials behave the same when you touch or bend them?”. Students then received a brief lecture on biomedical engineering, covering the role of engineers, spine anatomy, and disc damage. Each student was given two kits. Kit A, used to build the spine model, included Styrofoam discs (vertebrae), playdough (intervertebral discs), flexible balloon wire (spinal cord), and a balloon (the head) for  artistic expression. Kit B included two round cardboard pieces representing the superior and inferior endplates for prosthetic design. For the testing activity, six additional materials with varying stiffness were provided, from very rigid to highly flexible: a wooden disc (hardest), a black solid 3D-printed disc (also rigid), a gray 3D-printed disc (low flexibility), a blue foam disc (more flexible), and a marshmallow (most flexible, mimicking a real intervertebral disc). Students worked in groups of three to test materials with rulers and protractors, measuring compression and bending, recording results on worksheets, and selecting the best prosthetic core material before completing post-surveys. Data sources included video recordings, pre/post surveys, and student worksheets. Video data were collected using stationary and wearable cameras worn by researchers and students. All video recordings were transcribed using Adobe Premiere. We analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2019), generating initial codes based on students’ talk, actions, gestures, and interactions with materials. Themes were refined through iterative memo-writing and revisiting the data and were defined using representative examples. The video analysis served as the primary data source, while the pre/post surveys and worksheets provided complementary evidence. Our reflexive thematic analysis of video and open-ended survey data  revealed four themes: (a) developing an engineering mindset, (b) embodied learning and anatomical exploration, (c) exploring material properties through interaction, and (d) social collaboration and peer feedback.  The first theme, developing an engineering mindset, appeared as students progressively built, adjusted, and refined their models. For example, one student instructed a peer, “Pull it as hard as you can, but without breaking it.” Others discussed dimensions (“You think that's big enough or a bit bigger?”), pressure (“You want to keep a little pressure right inside”), and performance (“It seems it doesn’t bend very well”). One student measured a height difference, saying, “Twenty-five minus twenty-four… only one?” These moments highlighted that, as students worked, they noticed that they had to think about design constraints, dimensions, and measurement. The second theme, embodied learning and anatomical exploration, was evident as students used their bodies and gestures to reason about spine movement. One  twisted their torso, saying, “Our spine lets us turn like this,” while another said, “Touch your back to feel a little lump.” and the other said, “Look, I am bendy.” A student placed a balloon with a face on the spine and said, “Oh, it’s me—with a purple face and my vertebrae!” By mapping their sense of self onto the model, anatomical structures became more meaningful and personally relevant. Consistent with pre-survey responses, this suggests that their understanding of the spine relied mainly on visual and spatial reasoning. The third theme, exploring material properties through interaction, was evident as students physically manipulated materials to evaluate stiffness and flexibility while designing their prosthetics. Students touched and pressed materials to compare their behavior. For example, one student pressed a marshmallow and said, “It’s squishy,” while another calculated, “Ninety-five minus ninety-five equals zero—very inflexible,” using math to reason about rigidity. Another student pushed the prosthetic between two hands and explained, “This is like when we squish the discs,” using analogy to make their reasoning visible. Taken together, these interactions suggest epistemic–pragmatic cycles (Kirsh & Maglio, 1994), in which students used information-revealing actions to better understand material properties and then used that information to guide design decisions. The fourth theme, social collaboration and peer feedback, appeared through students’ shared problem-solving during the activity. Students guided one another through the task (e.g., “Why don’t you start? It’s not that hard, I can help you,” and “Put Styrofoam first, then playdough”) and asked questions about the procedure and measurements (e.g., “Do we need to use the protractor for the height?”). They also supported each other physically, holding rulers or protractors while peers recorded measurements. At  times, students framed the activity playfully, with one commenting, “I’m surprised we’re doing this—it’s more like we’re playing. ” These interactions show that students’ learning was supported through collaborative problem-solving, peer guidance, and shared participation in the task.

Ultimately, this study suggests that elementary students did not learn engineering in isolation; instead, their thinking was distributed across peers and materials, contextualized by the biomedical problem they were solving.

Our findings suggest that low-cost, hands-on activities can help students learn biomedical engineering in ways that reflect the collaborative, interdisciplinary, and tool-rich nature of the field. Building on prior studies showing that engineering activities can foster collaboration, career awareness, and design thinking (Melvin, 2024), our study extends research into elementary biomedical engineering.
