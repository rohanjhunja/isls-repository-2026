# Multimodal Analysis of Productive Confusion During Virtual Reality Collaboration: An Exploratory Study

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Confusion is a common yet complex emotion in learning, which can become productive confusion that promotes deeper understanding and reflection. However, little is known about how such confusion unfolds in collaborative virtual reality (VR) environments. This exploratory study investigates how learners express and regulate productive confusion during a VR collaborative problem-solving (CPS) task. Eight undergraduate students participated in the collaborative VR game. Multimodal data, including in-game recordings, videos, and audio, were coded for verbal and gestural behaviors. Sequential pattern mining and temporal mapping were conducted to reveal multimodal patterns. Results show that confusion was jointly expressed through verbal –gestural coordination, particularly questioning paired with pointing and manipulation. Successful dyads transformed confusion in to resolution through iterative cycles of planning, execution, and reflection. Gestures played a crucial role in establishing shared confusion. This study contributes to understanding how productive confusion operates as an embodied, socially shared process in VR environments.

Confusion is often regarded as a negative affective state for learning, yet research in learning sciences has reframed it as a potentially productive emotion that can promote deeper understanding when effectively regulated and scaffolded (D’Mello et al., 20 14; Kohatsu, 2025). Productive confusion, similar in concept to productive struggle, refers to a state of cognitive disequilibrium that can stimulate effortful cognitive processes such as reflection, deliberation, and hypothesis testing to restore understanding (D’Mello et al., 2014). Prior research has examined productive confusion within individual problem-solving contexts, demonstrating that when effectively regulated, confusion can foster deeper comprehension and conceptual change (Granberg, 2016; Muis et al., 2025). In collaborative problem-solving (CPS) contexts, confusion rarely occurs in isolation; it is often socially and interactionally constructed, emerging as learners negotiate divergent perspectives and coordinate actions toward a shared goal (Graesser, 2016; Sung & Nathan, 2025). Understanding how learners jointly experience, regulate, and move past confusion can therefore provide insight into how collaborative learning unfolds. Prior studies have examined cognitive and affective confusion regulation primarily through verbal exchanges (Muis et al., 2025; Russo & Hubbard, 2024), but in immersive learning environments, communication extends beyond speech. Gestures and embodied actions become central to how learners externalize uncertainty, direct attention, and maintain coordination (Lindgren & Johnson-Glenberg, 2013). In virtua l reality (VR) environments, gestures are not merely supplementary; they constitute the interaction itself,  allowing learners to manipulate, point, and spatially reason in shared 3D environments (Planey, 2023). However, how confusion is jointly expressed and regulated through verbal and gestural modalities in VR environments is underexplored. This exploratory study addresses this gap by analyzing multimodal data from dyads collaboratively playing a VR puzzle game that naturally elicits confusion. By analyzing verbal and non-verbal (gesture) behaviors, we investigate how learners express, share, and regulate confusion through speech and gestures. Specifically, this study addresses the following research questions (RQs): RQ1: What verbal and gestural behaviors emerge during productive confusion in VR ? RQ2: How are these confusions jointly expressed across verbal and gestural modalities? RQ3: How do dyads collaboratively regulate and resolve confusion across CPS phases?

## Participants and VR CPS environment

Four dyads (N = 8 undergraduate students, aged 18– 25) participated in this exploratory study. Participants collaborated in the VR game Carly and the Reaperman, which requires two players to coordinate complementary roles and solve spatial puzzles through communication and joint action. The task naturally elicited moments of confusion, failure, and repair, as it required partners to synchronize their actions, such as timing jumps and planning paths, while maintaining effective communication.  Each session (50 mins) involved two participants 1986 wearing Meta Quest 2 headsets. A 360° camera captured body movement  and verbal exchanges, while screen-recording software documented in-game actions (Figure 1). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Figure 1 The Experiment Setting and In-game Recording All multimodal data (screen recordings, 360° video, and audio) were first synchronized and aligned along a unified timeline. Verbal interactions were transcribed and coded for CPS behaviors, including Question, Explain, Confirm, Share, Request, Direct, Execute, Disagree, Reflect, Off‑task, adapted from the CPS coding framework of Andrews & Forsyth (2020). Gestures were coded following McNeill’s (1992) typology and adjusted for the VR context, including Pointing (Referential and Directive), Iconic (Tracing, Mirroring), Beat, and Manipulating gestures (see Table 1). 1078 verbal and 562 gesture codes were generated. Reliability was ensured by two coders (Cohen ’s Kappa=0.78). Confusion episodes were identified when learners verbally or nonverbally expressed uncertainty or misunderstanding, such as statements “It’s not working,” repeated failed attempts, long pauses, or hesitation. Each episode was further categorized as productive when it led to regulatory behaviors (e.g., confirmation, or corrective action) or u nproductive when it resulted in prolonged inaction or disengagement. In this case study, all dyads exhibited productive confusion, as each episode ultimately contributed to progress toward task completion. Dyads’ collaborative performance was evaluated through task performance and collaboration quality. Task performance included completion time and task completion status (completed or not). Collaboration quality was rated using a 5-point rating scheme adapted from Meier et al. (2007). To address RQ1, descriptive statistics were used to examine the frequency and distribution of verbal and gestural behaviors during confusion. For RQ2, this study combined verbal and gesture code based on timestamp, sequential pattern mining, a data mining method to identify statistically significant and recurrent sequences of events or behaviors within time-ordered data, was conducted to explore how verbal and gestural modalities were jointly expressed and transitioned during confusion regulation. Finally, to address RQ3, temporal mapping across five CPS phases was performed to visualize how confusion evolved and was collaboratively resolved over time. Figure 4 revealed that productive confusion mostly emerged during the problem identification and representation phases. These moments initiated productive confusion cycles, where partners jointly questioned, pointed, and manipulated objects to externalize their uncertainty. The alignment of verbal questioning and gesture actions served as a form of co-regulation, allowing learners to transform initial misunderstandings into mutually visible problems or joint  confusion. After reaching joint confusion, successful dyads typically transitioned from confusion to resolution through iterative cycles of  Confusion → Plan → Execute → Reflect and Monitor. These cycles were rarely effective on the first attempt; instead, learners progressed through multiple rounds of trial and adjustment (Plan → Execute), gradually resolving one small step until the entire level was completed. During the planning phase, learners frequently used sharing, explaining, requesting, and pointing behaviors to collaboratively describe and negotiate their next steps of action. In the execution phase, directing and sharing were often accompanied by manipulating and pointing gestures, as participants jointly enacted their plans in the shared virtual space. When failure occurred, they paused, reflected, and restructured their strategies before re-engaging in new attempts. This cycle repeated several times; each failure yielded actionable insight, prompting learners to adapt their approaches and transform mistakes into constructive feedback loops. This exploratory study shows how dyads collaboratively express and regulate confusion in VR CPS through verbal and gestural behaviors. P roductive confusion emerged as a jointly constructed and multimodally expressed phenomenon. Overall, during confusion episodes, learners exhibited a strong tendency to couple questioning, sharing, and pointing gestures, reflecting that learners relied on both dialogue and embodied reference to make their thinking visible  to their partners. These findings align with prior research suggesting that gestural actions can serve as embodied strategies for regulating cognitive dissonance and facilitating shared understanding in collaborative problem solving (Johnson et al.,  2017; Planey et al., 2023; Walkington  et al., 2019). Second, successful CPS involved iterative cycles after confusion, progressing through Plan –Execute–Reflect sequences. In the VR collaborative space, gestures were not merely supportive cues but embodied mediators that helped learners maintain shared understanding, externalize misunderstanding, and reframe confusion as a collaborative opportunity, aligned with prior research (Sung & Nathan, 2025). Although this study’ small sample size and case-based analysis, the findings highlight the potential of VR as a rich context for investigating the embodied regulation of complex cognition. The affordances of virtual environments, such as shared manipulation  and spatial referencing, appear to help learners transform confusion into productive exploration. Future work should use larger samples and automated multimodal analytics to track confusion dynamics over time, and integrate real-time feedback systems to support learners in recognizing and regulating confusion during collaborative inquiry.
