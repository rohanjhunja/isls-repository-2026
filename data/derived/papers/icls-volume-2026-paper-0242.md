# More Than Digging a Hole in the Ground: Novice Enactment of Science Practice in an Archaeological Apprenticeship

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While science education emphasizes learning through authentic practice, the specific processes that transform routine participation into scientific inquiry remain underexamined. This study investigates these processes within an archaeological summer program. Through qualitative analysis of interviews with 11 undergraduate novices, we seek to understand the science practice novices enact and the experiences that facilitate this enactment. Findings suggest that students readily engaged in embodied routines throughout and began conceptual problematization when encountering new and exciting artifacts. However, their development could be constrained by their peripher al social position, which limited access to expert reasoning and decision-making. These findings highlight the challenge of moving novices from “doing” tasks to “thinking” scientifically. We argue that authentic apprenticeships could leverage expert thinking to link the embodied routines to the conceptual and epistemic work of the discipline.

Designing science learning in authentic contexts involves more than mastering disciplinary content (Stroupe, 2015; Nieminen et al., 2025). It requires a shift toward engaging in the authentic scientific practices used to address real-world problems (Sadler et al., 2010). To that end, science education reframes the goal of learning from knowing scientific facts to being able to participate in the disciplinary work of a scientific community (c.f. productive disciplinary engagement, PDE, Engle & Conant, 2002). While apprenticeships are a common pedagogical model for this transformation, and research has shown their effectiveness in producing p ositive outcomes (Charney et al., 2007), the specific processes that engage learners in an embodied and authentic experience of science practices remain less examined. Simply participating in the daily routines of scientists does not automatically guarante e learning (Hodson, 1993). This raises an interesting question: What and how do specific processes and experiences within these “daily routines” support student science learning? Past research has extensively examined students’ productive disciplinary engagement from various perspectives, e.g., epistemic diversity (Agarwal & Sengupta-Irving, 2020), epistemic affect (Jaber & Hammer, 2016). However, such understanding usually relies on the conversation-based classroom, where students are far from where scientists work, i.e., the laboratory and the field. To understand the students’ learning in the context of where scientific knowledge is produced, this study turns to archaeological fieldwork, a rich context for scientific practices that are learned and enacted at the same time. Archaeology is a highly practice-based science where knowledge is not simply “dug out” but is constructed from fragmentary material evidence through excavation, analysis, inference, and argumentation (Cobb et al., 2024). Unlike many lab-work-heavy disciplines, archaeological fieldwork immerses novices in an environment defined by uncertainties and the constant interplay between human intention and material reality, making it a rich setting to investigate the processes of becoming a scientific practitioner. This paper examines the learning experiences of 11 undergraduate students from various disciplines who joined a summer archaeolog ical excavation project. We aim to understand how archaeology is intertwined in authentic practice and learned by students who participated in these practices. Two research questions guide our study:

1) Which aspects of archaeological science practices are developed and enacted by a novice student through

engaging in authentic fieldwork?

2) What experiences (e.g., social arrangements, adoption of material tools, and embodied excavation

experiences) facilitate the student’s development of these aspects of science practices? By answering these questions, we aim to provide empirical evidence of how archaeology is learned through the interplay of social, material, and embodied science practices (Stroupe, 2015). The findings will highlight the role of engaging students in science  practices in transforming routine participation into genuine scientific inquiry, offering insights for designing learning environments that foster authentic engagement. 1692

## Theoretical perspectives

Science practice comprises two parts: the features of disciplinary work and the perspectives on how novices learn to participate in valued community activities (Stroupe, 2015). The participation in community activities implies the learning of professional disciplinary work. Prior research has realized the significance of such participation and reported various learning outcomes related to the participation, such as career aspirations, understanding of science content, and confidence in doing science (Sadler  et al., 2010). While many promising outcomes are reported, how authentic science practices are linked to these positive results remains under research (Sadler et al., 2010). Within the context of research apprenticeship, a few empirical studies have inves tigated what factors facilitate students’ learning. Charney et al. (2007) found that through participating in lab work, students developed a more sophisticated way of thinking about questions. Perin et al. (2020) noted that material resources, such as instruments, artifacts, and physical environments, are associated with learners’ affect of science and their scientific identity. Carsten Conner et al. (2021) reported that power and positionality, whether learners are positioned as authors of their research, shaped how learners participate in the science practice. That being said, merely completing the everyday tasks of scientists does not mean participating in science practice. A complicated scene of learning in science practice requires more contextual understanding. Following Stroupe’s (2015) definition of science practice, archaeology, especially archaeological fieldwork, provides a unique context through which novices learn to participate in the disciplinary community activities. While expert archaeologists lead the iterative work of conceptualizing and refining the interpretation of the human past based on excavated sites, novices can also engage in meaningful yet straightforward practices by working alongside them. Throughout archaeological inquiry, novice learners ’ daily routine involves interacting with material evidence and conceptualizing archaeology. For instance, they need to excavate archaeological sites using various tools, document context information, and measure and preserve artifacts they excavate. Moreover, such inquiry does not yield a single correct answer, which helps students recognize that scientific knowledge is iteratively constructed rather than being discovered (Stroup, 2015). The evidence for archaeological inquiry, such as sherds and multiple stratigraphic layers, is often incomplete and continuously revealed through excavation. This nature of archaeology enables novices to iteratively reason, hypothesize, debate, and refine their interpretation of the human past within multiple plausible explanations (Wylie, 2016). Therefore, archaeological fieldwork is ideal for us to unpack students’ authentic science practice experience and explore what supports their learning.

## Method

This study employs a qualitative  analysis within a larger ethnographic investigation of a 10-day archaeological excavation experiential learning program. The field school involved 11 undergraduate students from various disciplines participating in a summer excavation project in Armenia. Before joining the excavation, students received some basic training in the content knowledge, but almost none of them were exposed to archaeology science. Students’ backgrounds included digital humanities, Chinese history, art history, architecture, science education, etc. Only one of the participants (i.e., Nicole, pseudonym) majored in archaeology, but she ha d never done any excavation.

The primary data for this analysis consists of transcribed audio recordings of daily interviews and unstructured focus group discussions with the students. The focus group interviews were typically done during the afternoon lab sessions. These conversations were designed to elicit students’ in-the-moment experiences, their developing understanding of the archaeological work, and their reflections on the excavation process. The analysis of the interview transcripts was guided by our theoretical framework, integrating concepts of science practice, cognitive apprenticeship, and problematization. We conducted a thematic analysis of the transcripts, employing a coding scheme derived from our research questions and the provided data memos. Three researchers participated in the coding and discussed the codes and themes until a consensus was reached.

To address RQ1, we first identified segments where students described their engagement in the work of archaeology. These segments were assigned descriptive codes (e.g., note taking, discussion on what the bone belongs to). These initial codes were then syn thesized into broader themes of science practice observed in the fieldwork (e.g., Logging/recording process-archaeological routine, Forming hypothesis-archaeological problematization). Finally, these emergent themes were mapped onto the four dimensions of science practice (i.e., material, social, conceptual, and epistemic) as defined by Stroupe (2015) to build a rich description of the students’ enactment of the discipline.

To address RQ2, we analyzed the same interview segments, to identify the specific social arrangements, material tools, and embodied experiences students connected to their learning. These were categorized to understand how different elements of the authentic fieldwork environment supported or hindered students’ participation in the science practices identified in RQ1. 1693 RQ1: The enactment of archaeological science practices Our analysis of the student interviews reveals a complex picture of how novices begin to enact archaeological science practices. From the students’ interview results, we identified three major themes that characterize their engagement with archaeological work: (a) participating in archaeological routines, (b) engaging in archaeological problematization, and (c) navigating their peripheral involvement in the team. These themes align with and cut across science practice’s material, social, conceptual, and epistemic dimensions (Stroupe, 2015). First, students’ initial entry into archaeological practice was through the material dimension, specifically by learning and participating in the daily, often embodied  routines of excavation. One example is the practice of journaling and site recording. Wendy (pseudonym), when asked about her experience keeping the site journal on the second day, could recall the procedural details of the excavation, “today we have opened context 3, 4, 5, 6… I took the pictures… let me think… when we remove the wall.” However, the data also show that embodied routines without a clear connection to the larger conceptual work of the science practice could be perceived less enthusiastically. Sylvia (pseudonym) stated bluntly about her lab experience of taking pictures of artifacts, “It is so boring... I need to be out of that lab”. Similarly, Wendy explained that she only learned the spatial directions of the site through the repetitive task of placing east/north/west/south signs: “I feel like it’s from placing that sign too many times”. These instances suggest that while the embodied routines are a foundational part of the material practice, their value for engaging novice learners in science practices may vary.

Second, as students became familiar with the basic routines, they began to engage more deeply with archaeology’s conceptual and epistemic dimensions by problematizing (Phillips et al., 2017), such as identifying gaps in understanding, forming hypotheses, and making inferences from material evidence. This practice often occurred spontaneously when students encountered “exciting” artifacts. For instance, a discussion erupted when students examined a photo of a small bone found in the lab:  Daisy (pseudonym): “When I was in the lab today, I found a set that looks a lot like human bone.” Celvin (pseudonym): “I think it’s a mouse, or a small bird.” Daisy: “It looks a lot like a human tooth.” Daine (pseudonym): “This is definitely a human tooth.” In this exchange, students moved from observation to hypothesis (“looks a lot like a human tooth”) and considered an alternative hypothesis (“I think it’s a mouse, or a small bird.”). Although the evidence-based reasoning may be limited, this represents a shift from simply executing tasks to participating in the interpretive work of the discipline.

Third, while students show ed engagement in material and conceptual practices, their interviews reveal challenges in accessing the social dimension of the professional scientific community. They often described themselves as peripheral participants, physically present but not part of the core interpretive conversations of the expert archaeologists. As Luna  (pseudonym) noted: “Mainly, they talk too much about those professional terms that we don’t understand.” This sense of exclusion was intensified by what students perceived as opacity in the experts’ decision-making processes, which  left students feeling not  like intellectual contributors. Clara’s (pseudonym) account of the sudden destruction of a wall they had been excavating captures this feeling: “And then I thought we were going to keep it, kept the wall, and then not. And then they just rushed the entire wall. They just destroyed the wall, pretty shocked because it was like 10 minutes before we were supposed to leave.” The lack of accessing the scientific reasoning behind such a decision reinforced students’ positions on the periphery of the community, allowing them to “do” but not “think about” the science practice. RQ2: Experiences that facilitate engagement in science practice The students’ interviews highlight how specific experiences acted as catalysts or barriers to their engagement in the practices described above. First, the primary social experience  of the summer program is the opportunit y for students to learn alongside experts in the way of a cognitive apprenticeship. One of the junior archaeologists was observed explaining the complex relationship between clay chemistry, firing temperature, and color to students. This is a typical example of coaching, where an expert provides context-specific knowledge that enriches a routine task. However, the data also suggests that  social experiences could fail to support a  sustained cognitive apprenticeship. As noted earlier, students felt the experts’ conversations were inaccessible. This lack of access to the experts’ modeling of their thinking processes meant that the work often involved an easy-to-execute physical task for the students. Although these physical tasks are part of the scientific routines, without connecting to the conceptual and epistemic aspects of the science practices, the experience loses its scientific nature and creates a disconnected community where students and experts work more in parallel and less in collaboration. 1694 Second, embodied experience often is the precursor for curiosity or confusion in the inquiry process (i.e., the vexing questions, Odden & Russ al. 2017). Wendy provided an example that began while she was engaged in the embodied routine task of sieving: “It was when I was sieving that I saw that kind of pebble, and then I became very curious why this thing would be here. So I asked Henry [one of the archaeologists], who was also sieving at the time, ‘What is this?’ He said it was something shaped by the impact of water, and then he pointed to the location of the Vedi river... then I started thinking about the movement of the river.” This sequence of the conversation demonstrates a learning trajectory embedded in embodied science practice: A serendipitous discovery during an embodied experience (i.e., sieving) leads to a moment of problematization (i.e., “why this thing would be here?”). This question then prompts the learners to seek help from an expert (i.e., apprenticeship), which provides a new piece of information that allows her to engage in complex conceptual and epistemic practice (i.e., making inferences about landscape changes over time). Material tools, particularly those for documentation, also played an important role in problematization. For Wendy, being asked to do the site journal was not just a laborious task but an important mechanism for making sense of the vexing questions. “After writing this journal today, and after I took some photos, I roughly understood. Oh, so it turns out that our different contexts are divided like this.” The act of recording forced her to attend to the details that defined the practice, transforming a confusing activity into a structured, understandable process. This study examines how novice learners learned archaeology through participating in authentic archaeological fieldwork. While prior studies argued that participating in fieldwork supported the development of thinking processes in scientific questions (Charney et al., 2007; Flash-Gvili & Dodick, 2010), our results demonstrated how students participating in science practice shape such development. Specifically, participating in repetitive scientific routine (i.e., often seen as a primary engagement of science practice) without being aware of the bigger scope of science could undermine students’ agency and motivation for in-depth engagement. On the other hand, conceptual, epistemic, and social aspects of the practice could make the scientific routine more meaningful. This view echoes with the research on students’ problematization in science practice (Phillips et al. 2017). While usually prompted by the researcher in this study, students presented initial problematization, such as forming hypotheses and making inferences.

While students have demonstrated a certain level of conceptual and epistemic engagement of science practice in archaeology through participating in fieldwork, many of them consider themselves not qualified to engage in science practices because of the limitations  of their expertise. That being said, science educators need to actively connect scientific routines with authentic problems that students believe they have agency to work on (Engle & Conant, 2002; Agarwal & Sengupta-Irving, 2020). This aspect echoes with Stroupe’s (2015) question— Whose science practice? While cognitive apprenticeship is believed to be effective in a less-structured experiential learning setting, our data here suggests that the fixed social hierarchy of the team on the site could hinder students’ access to being coached by the experts.

Beyond enacting science practice for learners, archaeological fieldwork provided unique and embodied experiences that facilitated meaningful learning moments. These learning moments occurred as they encountered interesting artifacts, tried to make sense of  such artifacts, and sought evidence for the sensemaking. While such an encounter is usually serendipitous, this serendipity is inherently scientific, as uncertainty is part of scientific practice, and learners will continuously work with uncertainty in th eir pursuit of science (Kampouraki, 2018; Kirch, 2011). Meanwhile, in such embodied experiences, materials and tools mediate the problematization of science practice. While prior research suggests that materials such as scientific costumes formed part of learners’ scientific identity (Perin et al., 2019), we argue that materials and tools are associated with learners’ participation in specific processes of science practice, such as initiating vexing questions.

In conclusion, our study provided a unique disciplinary perspective on science practice, where novice archaeologists participated in archaeolog ical fieldwork and learned about archaeology. Through rich interview and focus group data, we demonstrated preliminary evidence for different dimensions of their archaeology learning and diverse embodied experiences that support such learning. While students learn the material routines of the discipline through embodied participation, their engagement in deeper conceptual and epistemic work is not automatic. We highlighted the role of problematization in this process, often arising from puzzling material encounters, but also dependent on social arrangements that grant them access to expert reasoning. To that end, for the cognitive apprenticeship model to be truly effective, it is more than placing novices alongside the experts. The invisible work  of scientific thinking needs to be made visible, transforming routine participation into genuine scientific inquiry.
