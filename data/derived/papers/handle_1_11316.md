# Adaptive Scaffolding with Generative AI Chatbot for Solving Ill-Structured Problems

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates the role of generative AI (GenAI) chatbots in supporting students’ ill-structured problem-solving. A GenAI chatbot, Probot, was developed to offer adaptive scaffolding through question prompts during problem representation, solution generation and selection, justification, and monitoring and evaluation. Sixty-one undergraduate students in Korea were divided into control and experimental groups. The experimental group solved an ill-structured problem with Probot while the control group used a non-adaptive chatbot. Interaction patterns with GenAI chatbots were analyzed using Epistemic Network Analysis. Results showed the experimental group had strong connections with orientation in problem analysis, solution analysis, and solution elaboration, suggesting that Probot supported students’ analytical and elaborative thinking. The control group showed strong connections between problem analysis, solution analysis, and solution suggestions, indicating a focus on information and idea-seeking. The findings highlight Probot’s potential in guiding ill-structured problem-solving and offer design insights for adaptive scaffolding.

## Introduction

Problem-solving skills have emerged as a critical competenc y for students in the 21st century (Partnership for 21st Century Skills, 2015). Among various types of problems, students particularly face difficulties in solving illstructured problems due to their abstract and complex nature, which requires advanced cognitive skills (Jonassen, 2000). In general, the ill-structured problem-solving process is presented as a series of phases, namely problem representation, generating and selecting solutions, making justifications, and mon itoring and evaluating (Ge & Land, 2004). Given that ill-structured problem-solving demands extensive  cognitive and metacognitive skills, scaffolding can be valuable for students who encounter difficulties in the ill-structured problem-solving process. Prior studies on the mechanism to support students in solving ill-structured problems have primarily employed fixed conceptual and metacognitive scaffol ding. Such scaffolding is provided to help  students who struggle to identify relevant information due to the lack of necessary conceptual knowledge (Kim & Lim, 2019) and to effectively utilize metacognitive skills in solving ill-structured problems (Ge & Land, 2004). For instance, Kim and Lim (2019) provided scaffolding by posting exploratory questions, hints, and visual materials  on a bulletin board for solving instructional design problems. Ouyang et al. (2021)  offered students voice-and text-based scaffolding through an online platform to solve the practical challenges instructors face in remote online education during COVID-19.

Some studies have explored the use of adaptive scaf folding in ill-structured problem-solving. For instance, Winkler et al. (2021) implemented adaptiv e scaffolding by providing feedback through a perso nal AI assistant based on students’ solutions to legal pro blems, while Zheng et al. (2022) offered adaptive s caffolding tailored to students’ performance in solving C programming p roblems. However, since adaptive scaffolding messages are often pre-designed by experts or instructors, such scaffolding is limited in its ability to fully address individual students’ needs. Additionally, these scaffolding approaches are typically confined to specific domains, such as instructional design, legal, or programming  problems. This highlights the need for further res earch into adaptive scaffolding mechanisms that dynamically resp ond to students’ specific questions or interactions, rather than relying solely on pre-designed messages or narrowly focused problem domains. Generative AI (GenAI), which leverages deep learnin g models to create diverse content such as images and text, offers significant potential for dynamica lly generating scaffolding customized based on the context of problems and student input (Lim et al., 2023). In this study, we developed a GenAI-integrated chatbot, Probot, to provide adaptive conceptual and metacognitive scaffolding to support students in solving ill-structured problems. This research investigates how students interact with Probot throughout the problem-solving process. University students who participated in the study  were divided into two groups: one with adaptive scaffolding provided by Probot and the other without  adaptive scaffolding. Specifically, the study addresses the following res earch questions: (a) What patterns emerge when students interact with Probot during the ill-structured problem-solving process? (b) How do these interaction patterns differ between the groups with and without adaptive scaffolding?

## Research context and participants

This study recruited 61 Korean undergraduate students (25 males and 36 females) through convenience sampling and assigned them to either an experimental group (31 students) or a control group (30 students). Participants first completed a one-hour pre-task a week before the experiment, which involved proposing a policy to address issues related to deepfake technology. The task required s tudents to define the problem, propose a policy, ju stify their solution, and monitor and evaluate their proposed s olution in essay format. Two independent coders gra ded the outputs using rubric, which assesses the problem and cause analysis, solution generation, so lution justification, and solution monitoring and evaluation. Cohen’s kappa coefficient was.87. Based on the pre-task scores, participants were evenly distributed between the control and exp erimental groups. A Levene test (p =.96) confirmed the homogeneity of the two groups.

The main experiment took place in a university computer lab where participants solved an ill-structured problem by proposing a policy to address issues rel ated to teenagers' use of social networking sites (SNS). Students were required to define the problem, justi fy their proposed solution, and monitor and evaluat e their approach, producing their answers in essay format. The control group us ed TalkGPT, a GenAI chatbot without adaptive scaffolding. T he experimental group used Probot, which provided adaptive s caffolding tailored to students’ interactons with Probot.

Probot: GenAI chatbot for ill-structured problems This study developed a GenAI chatbot, Probot, to provide conceptual and metacognitive scaffoldi ng in the form of question prompts to support students in solving ill-structured problems. Drawing on prior research, the scaffolding was carefully designed to address the s pecific needs of each phase of t he ill-structured problem-solving process: (a) problem representation, (b) generating and selecting solutions, (c) making justifications, and

(d) monitoring and evaluating (Ge & Land, 2004; Kim & Lim, 2019; Ouyang et al., 2021; Winkler et al., 2021).

In the problem representation phase, conceptual sca ffolding was designed to help students identify key concepts of the problem, elaborate their thoughts, and understand the relationships between different problem domains. Additionally, metacognitive scaffolding supported students in accessing their prior knowledge about the problem and monitoring and evaluating their progress in understanding and framing the issue. For the generating or selecting solutions phase, conceptual scaffoldin g was used to encourage  students to elaborate their ideas and articulate their opinions about the problem. Metacognitive scaffolding, on the other hand, guided students to refine their thoughts and reflect on the feasibility and r elevance of their proposed solutions, promoting d eeper engagement with the task. During the making justifications phase, conceptual scaffolding played a crucial role in helping students infer logical connections and justify their proposed solutions. This type of scaffolding encouraged students to develop  well-supported arguments and strengthen the validity of their solutions.  Finally, in the monitoring and evaluating phase, metacognitive scaf folding supported students in critically assessing their proposed solutions. This involved encouraging them to monitor their decision-making process and evaluate the quality and effectiveness of their solutions.

We developed Probot using prompt engineering with a Large Language Model (LLM), specifically gpt4-turbo-2024-04-09. In designing Probot, we applied principles from Bsharat et al. (2024) such as example-driven prompting, which involved assigning a specific task  and role to the chatbot. The prompts were developed to provide scaffolding without directly offering answe rs to engage students in the problem-solving process. For the user interface, we built the chatbot using Python a nd integrated LangChain, Stream lit, Streamlit Cloud, and Firebase. These tools allowed to connect to the LLM  and collect log data from the interactions  between students and Probot. Figure 1 shows the interface of Probot and sample conversation logs demonstrating how participants used Probot to solve ill-structured problems.  To investigate differences in student interactions with GenAI chatbots, the control group used TalkGPT, an identical chatbot interface without adaptive sca ffolding. TalkGPT maintains a direct response mechanism similar to ChatGPT to ensure that interactions closely resembled a typical user experience with GenAI chatbots.

## Data analysis

To analyze interaction patterns through conversational logs, we employed the Functional Category System (FCS) developed by Poole and Holmes (1995). The FCS coding scheme, shown in Table 1, includes four main categories of students’ ill-structured problem-solving process: problem representation, orientation, solut ion development, and others.  Duplicate coding was permitted for each conversatio n to ensure a comprehensive analysis. Two independent coders applied the scheme to the conver sation logs, achieving a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of.82. Any discrepancies between  the coders were resolved through discussion until c onsensus was reached.  After coding the data, we conducted an Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) to visualize interaction patterns and compare the networks between the control and experi mental groups (Shaffer et al., 2016). Using the ENA  web tool, we set the control and experimental groups as units of analysis, the students’ conve rsation logs with GenAI chatbots as the conversation source, and a stanza w indow size of 4. To evaluate differences between th e two groups, we performed a two-sample t-test to identify statistically significant differences along the axis in the ENA. ## Results

We examined the interaction between Probot and participants in ill-structured problem-solving through ENA to identify how chatbots support students ’ ill-structured problem-solving. The control and experimental groups ’ ENA projection graphs are presented in Figure 2. The model showed that MR1  (means rotation) dimensions accounted for 30.8% while SVD2 (singular value decomposition) dimensions accounted for 16.1% of the variance in the data. Along the x-axis (MR1), the two-sample t-test result showed a statistically significant difference (t (44.05)=11.89, p=0.01, Cohen’s d=3.07) between the control (mean= -0.35, SD=0.28) and experimental (mean=0.34, SD=0.15) groups. In the y-axis (SVD2), no statistically significant differences were observed. As illustrated in Figure 2., the experimental  group showed strong connections between Orientation  (OO) and problem analysis (PA) with a connection weight of 0.72. In sequence, orientation (OO) and solution el aboration (SE), orientation (OO) and solution analysis (SA) showed strong connections (connection weight=0.20 and 0.19). On the other hand, for the control group, strong connections between problem analysis (PA) and solution analysis (SA) (connection weight =0.29), problem analysis (P A) and solution suggestion (SS) (connection weight= 0.20), and solution analysis (SA) and solution suggestion (SS) (connection weight=0.20) were observed. ## Discussion and conclusion

This study used  ENA to explore how Probot, a GenAI chatbot with adaptive scaffolding, can support undergraduate students ’ ill-structured problem-solving. The main  findings include the following. First, the experimental group using Probot demonstrated strong connections between  orientation and problem analysis, indicating that students spent substantial time in problem representation. Probot also provided significant direction and guidance during problem analysis, as well as during solution analysis and solution elaboration. This pattern suggests that Probot played a crucial role in guiding students’ analytical and elaborative thinking processes. Second, the control group  showed strong connections between problem analysis, solution analysis, and solution suggestion, indicating th at students mainly sought help in problem identific ation and solution generation.  This result implies that students tend to seek informati on and solution ideas in their ill-structured problem-solving simultaneously. This pattern is similar to that observed by Krupp et al. (2024) and Yan (2023), in which students request the output generated by ChatGPT and use them for assigned task s. Additionally, the absence of strong connections with orientation implies that  TalkGPT lacks guiding ill-structured problem-solving processes. This may be due to the tendency of GenAI chatbots like ChatGPT to adopt a prescriptive approach, producing outputs that match students' requests. (Contel & Cusi, 2024). Third, the main difference between the two groups was the presence of orientation in the experimental group. It  demonstrates that students who interacted with Probot received scaffolding messages supporting more time in analyzing the problem, which  can enhance students ’ comprehension of the given problems. This interaction pattern supports Fui-Hoon Nah et al.'s (2023) claim that GenAI could facilitate learners' brainstorming in problem-solving. As the context, level of abstraction, and complexity of ill-structured problem-solving are high (Jonassen, 2000), the problem representation phase becomes crucial. For future chatbot design, segmented scaffolding in problem representation  to support a more in-depth analysis phase such as identifying key concepts, stakeholders, and constraints related to the ill-structured problem is recommended.

This study has some limitations that should be ackn owledged. First, while the groups were divided into experimental and control groups for ENA, further analysis based on students’ problem-solving performance levels could provide more insights into interaction patterns adaptive to varying skill levels. Second, the scaffolding provided by Probot was limited to cognitive scaffolding for conceptual and metacognitive understanding. Future research could expand on this by incorporating affe ctive scaffolding to address students’ socio-emotional needs during the ill-structured problem-solving process.  Despite these limitations, this study makes a valuable contribution to understanding adaptive scaffolding in problem-solving. Developing Probot using question prompts demonstrates the potential of GenAI in dynamically scaffold ing the ill-structured problem-solving process.
