# Tracking the Teacher in the Classroom, a Case Study

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Tracking the teacher’s position in real-time during the class should be able to increase the appropriateness of the advice that an orchestration system gives to teachers and students. However, such tracking needs to be done accurately without impinging on student privacy. This paper describes both our implementation of a teacher-tracking system and an evaluation of its accuracy in a mathematics middle-school classroom. The tracking system interprets data from an indoor positioning system that has the teacher wear a transmitter whose signals are detected by sensors on the walls of the classroom.

## Tracking the Teacher in the Classroom, a Case Study

Bahar Shahrokhian, Kurt VanLehn, Bahar_shahrokhian@asu.edu, Kurt_VanLehn@asu.edu Arizona State University Abstract: Tracking the teacher’s position in real-time during the class should be able to increase the appropriateness of the advice that an orchestration system gives to teach ers and students. However, such tracking needs to be done accurately without impinging on student privac y. This paper describes both our implementation of a teacher-tracking system and an evaluation of its accuracy in a mathematics middle-school classroom. The tracking s ystem interprets data from an indoor positioning system that has the teacher wear a transmitter w hose signals are detected by sensors on the walls of the classroom.

Goals and previous work Teachers’ position and movement can impact students’ motivation, learning gain, engagement, etc. (Buai Chin et al., 2017; Lim et al., 2012; McArthur, 2015). Hence, tracking and visualizing the teacher’s position during an inperson classroom can play an important role in teacher learning and development(Lim et al., 2012; MartinezMaldonado, 2019). Overall, teacher tracking, and visualization can provide valuable fee dback for teachers and help them adjust their teaching strategies, which can ultimately lead to better learning outcomes for students. Teacher tracking can also enhance classroom orchestration systems, which is the application that drives this work.  An orchestration system helps the teacher manage the flow of information and activities in a class that involves individual and group work as well as whole-class work (Dillenbourg, 2013). If a classroom is e quipped with an orchestration system, then tracking the teacher’s positions can enhance its advice to teachers and students. For instance, if the class is working in small groups and the teacher is c irculating among the groups, and if a tracking system can tell the orchestration system which groups the teache r has visited, then the orchestration system can avoid advising the teacher to visit a group that has just been visit ed.  If the students have been asked to work collaboratively in groups, and the orchestration system detects that a group has  ceased working, then a teacher tracking system is useful for determining whether the work stoppage is due to the students themselves or to the teacher visiting the group or addressing the whole class.  This may pre vent the orchestration system from advising the students to return to work when they should attend to the teacher.  The work reported here was undertaken because our FACT orchestration system (VanLehn et al., 2018) needed teacher tracking data to prevent it from giving inappropriate advice to teachers and students. Although teacher tracking for teacher development can be done with human observers, teacher tracking for orchestration systems must be done in real-time, which would be difficult for human observers.  Thus, several researchers have measured the accuracy of teacher tracking using computer vision systems(Ahuja et al., 2019) or indoor positioning systems(Martinez-Maldonado, 2019).  For example, Ahuja et al. achi eved high accuracy by using multiple 3-D cameras to determine whether a teacher was standing at the front of the room, walking through the students, or stopping somewhere among the students. Although camera systems can capture a wide range of data such as speech or raised hands, they are expensive and often raise privacy concerns among parents and others even if they convert the data into a privacy-preserving format before proces sing. Thus, researchers have started working with indoor positioning systems.  These systems have the teacher wear a tag that transmits radio signals that are received by devices placed on the classroom walls.  For exampl e,  (Martinez-Maldonado et al., 2022)achieved high performance using such a system to determine teachers’ stops, their transitioning between stops, how much time they spent at important stops such as group tables, etc. Although indoor positioning systems avoid student privacy concerns, the current system for traditional classrooms can only track the teacher, so if visits to students are to be tracked, the students’ seating should be measured and recorded in advance. The goal of this paper is two-fold. The first goal is to explore the feasibility of implementing an ad-hoc IPS in an authentic classroom setting. The second objective is to answer the  following questions about the performance of the IPS system when groups are close together, as is unfortunately common in US schools:

1. Can this IPS system accurately determine which group a teacher is visiting when multiple groups are

close together?

2. How can the performance of the IPS system be improved in this context?

The data analyzed here were collected in June 2022 in several middle sc hool math classes as part of a larger study of our orchestration system. Classroom notes, audio, screen recording, educational logs, and teacher positioning data were gathered.  Human observers recorded the teacher's position in order to evaluate the tracking CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 435 system’s accuracy.  All the classes were held in the same room.  T he tables were in fixed positions, which had been mapped the night before.  When students entered the room and were  assigned seats, each group was seated on one side of a table.  Figure 1 shows the classroom floor plan with gr oup numbers.  Although there is a large empty space in the middle of the classroom, the groups were seated close to  each other.  The proximity makes it more difficult for the tracking system to determine which group the teacher is visiting. The next section explains our teacher tracking system. We then present an evaluation of the performa nce of this system in an authentic classroom.

Figure 1 The class floor plan with anchors and group numbers is shown after calibration Technology The teacher’s position and orientation were captured using the Pozyx Creator  kit.  It uses Ultra-wideband technology, which enables devices to communicate with each other in a short-range using pulses of radio waves sent every couple of nanoseconds across a wide frequency spectrum (more  than 500MHZ). This can achieve a positioning accuracy of 10cm. With low cost, low latency, low power specificati on, and immunity to other’s device interference, this technology is the most popular choice for indoor positioning systems (IPS) in recent years (Alarifi et al., 2016; Poulose & Han, 2020). The Pozyx creator kit was developed for small-scale applications. It contains 5 anchors, 4 tags, and a master tag. The anchors are stationary and act as reference points. The tags are mobile.  The master tag is connected to a computer with Pozyx cloud/software. The master tag communicates with the mobile tags wirelessly and relays their position to the Pozyx application(Pozyx Hardware Setup, 2022). Figure 2 obtained from (UWB(Pozyx), n.d.) summarizes how the Pozyx creator kit works on the top level. Implementation We placed a mobile tag connected to a power bank inside a custom-made we arable badge and asked the teacher to wear this badge in the classroom, so we could track her position. The da y before the class trial, we created a precise floor plan for the classroom and upload it to the Pozyx application.  We  also installed all 5 anchors according to the suggestions made by the Pozyx website (Pozyx Hardware Setup, 2022).  We then tested and corrected the positions of the anchors so that they could optimally communicate with each other and the teacher’s tag.  Figure 2 shows the floor plan.  Figures 3 show the positions of some of the anchors on the walls. Accessing the classroom beforehand was vital since positioning the anchors and mapping the floor plan can take a few hours. Here are tips for installing the anchors in a room /classroom based on Pozyx’s recommendations and our experience:

• Anchors should be installed within a range of 2-200 meters of each other.  Have power banks ready

since power plugs can be out of reach for the optimal anchors’ places.

• The best practice is that all anchors always have a line of sight with the mobile tag and each other.

However, this is not always possible due to many reasons such as class layout, or sometimes a pair of anchors can temporarily lose sight of each other, due to sensor or installation malfunction. In our experience, if at the beginning of setup, the anchors’ coordinates are manually entered into the software and every anchor has a clear line of sight with at least 2 other anchors (no obstacle between them, students and teacher rarely block their line of sight), the result would also be accurate.

• Make sure to place an anchor near important places the teacher frequents and avoid placing anchors

near metal objects.

CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 436 Data processing and the relevant details We analyzed the position data gathered from the IPS and compared it to th e ground truth. Ground truth was obtained from human observers in the classroom.  One or more observer(s) annotated the teacher’s stops and movements during the classroom, and any mismatches between annotators or misse d data were resolved by listening to the teacher’s and students’ audio.  Orientation data were not used in this evaluation. Figure 2 How the Pozyx Creator kit works Figure 3 Front of the classroom (3 anchors shown inside red circles) Following research such as (Martinez-Maldonado et al., 2022; Shahrokhian Ghahfarokhi  et al., 2020), we divided the class timeline into three types of segments: 1) Whole-cla ss 2) Group Interaction 3) Other. In particular, the human observers classified the teacher’s stops as either  Whole-class, the number of the group she is talking to, or Other. Data were collected from one classroom during 5 cla sses of 50 minutes of 6 th or 8th-grade math. We had a total 137,886 of data points out of which 134,335 of them were valid. To preprocess the raw data, we performed the 2 pre-processing steps of down-sampling and interpolation as suggested in (Martinez-Maldonado et al., 2022) to end up with one data point (x, y) per second. A rectangular zone was assigned to each part of the classroom. The zone surrounding each long sid e of a table was mapped to the group seated on that side of the table. The zones surrounding the teacher’s table, the whiteboard, the projector, and the teacher’s desk were deemed Authoritative zones and as mapped to the whole-class segment label. To process the data, we gathered all the positioning data points that we had w ithin the duration of each human-annotated segment. For each human-annotated segment, we first found the stops the teacher made during that segment. If the stop(s) during the segments’ duration were inside the zone assigned to the group identified by the observer, then it was a true match, else we would consider it as a false match. Following(Lim et al., 2012; Martinez-Maldonado et al., 2022) a stop was defined as a series of points that are within 0.75 meters of each other in a 5-second duration. These numbers were selected since the teacher tended to make short visits to check in with students.

## Results and discussion

Across the 5 classes, 52 segments were labeled as group visits by the human obs ervers.  Our system was able to distinguish the correct group 42 times. We had 261 segments that the human observer s labeled as the Wholeclass. Our system was able to correctly label these segments 208 times (see Table 1). Of the segments that our system thought were group visits, about half were due to misclassifying a whole class segment as a group visit. This is the most serious performance issue to resolve. One solution to reduce the number of times the system falsely thought the teach er was visiting a group, is to use the teacher’s speech. Based on (Shahrokhian Ghahfarokhi et al., 2020) we can classify teacher’s audio with good accuracy into whole class, group talk, and other. Another solution is considering tha t one factor that can change the meaning of segments is the current class activity(Buai Chin et al., 2017). If the orchestration system knows from the lesson plan that the current class activity is whole class, then group visiting is unlikely.  Our system did not know or use the current class activity. Martinec (Martinec, 2001), introduced the Engagement System model, where he re-interpreted social distance introduced in (Hall & Hall, 1966) by taking body orientation into account. Although our IPS recorded the teacher’s orientation, our teacher tracking system did not use this information. Overall although our teacher tracking system is perhaps the first to identify w hich group the teacher is visiting, its performance could be improved by combining the teacher’s speech and or ientation, and knowledge of the current class activity. This should allow it to provide an accurate, real-time description of the state of the classroom, which is critical to our orchestration system and other applications. Thi s would be the next step for this project.

This work can serve as a low-budget hands-on guide to setting up a tracking system in a classroom for various applications, that help researchers and practitioners in the field of education technology to design and develop more efficient classroom management tools that incorporate IPS technology.
