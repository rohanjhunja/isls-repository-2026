# Let them Build their House!: Youth Developing Youth Power

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The house metaphor of low floors, high ceilings, and wide walls can be used as a lens to view youth power, and the healing of oppressive structures. As youth are building the house and enacting their power, adults must recognize their positionality and stand in solidarity with youth without manipulating their power. The backyard as another element is added to focus on the impactful change and transformation that youth make “in their own backyard.”

## Introduction

Youth are often viewed as powerless, and society intentionally constrains youth power, while youth are trying to push the boundaries of change, and what is imagined to be possible (Thomas & Stornaiuolo, 2016). This paper aims at recognizing the ways in which youth develop their power, what they do with and how they use their power, and the role of adults to support their power development. Homes and houses are viewed as places for making, creating (Resnick & Silverman, 2005), a place of resistance (Nhat Hanh, 2001), nurturing, strengthening, and healing (hooks, 1999). By framing youth power development through a house metaphor, we examine how youth develop power and build this house, enact their power within this house, and the responsibilities of adults with the house.

## Conceptual Framework

For youth, building a house means opening doors to transform and reshape dominant structures in society (Siry et. al, 2014). This means rebuilding the current narrative and storylines that portray youth as powerless, which opens up different forms of engagement, practice, and possibility (Calabrese Barton et. al, 2021). Audre Lorde

(2007) says, “For the master’s tools will never dismantle the master’s house.” By building the house, youth are

reclaiming their space, and creating a space for vulnerability, healing, and recovering, and a nurturing of their power (Tan & Calabrese Barton, 2018). By building the house, we are simultaneously dismantling the master’s home.

Inside the house, youth exercise their power and agency through restorying, bending, and reshaping existing stories, narratives, and structures to include a wider range of voices, backgrounds, and perspectives (Thomas & Stornaiuolo, 2016). Youth also engage in digital activism which refers to the use of the internet, and social media platforms to participate in social movements (Ozkula, 2021) such as the #TulsaFlop (Bandy & Diakopoulos, 2020), and the Jojo Pose and Vogue challenges (Peterson-Salahuddin, 2022). Youth use their voice and power on digital activism to call for change. Additionally, youth are encouraged to create, imagine, and innovate by critically thinking and reflecting without focusing on the object or product but the process, not for financial gain, but for community impact, and problem solving (Greenberg et. al, 2020). Our role as researchers position us to “critically be with,” which is an intentional choice to exist alongside and in solidarity with youth, which forces adults to critically examine their role and stance in youth learning (Calabrese Barton et. al, 2021). Standing in solidarity means being with youth, not having over them (Greenberg, 2020). I pose another element to the house metaphor: the backyard. The phrase, “in our own backyard,” refers to our own community and people that surround us to express a discontentment in need of change. In the house, youth innovate to solve problems that they recognize in their own backyard (Greenberg et. al, 2021). When youth are empowered, and when youth are given meaningful experiences, oppressive structures are transformed, power dynamics are shifted, and change becomes wide-scale and impactful.
