# Fake News Literacy: Intuitive vs. Analytic Processing

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Understanding the cognitive processing of fake news is essential when planning to counteract fake news effects, such as through media literacy training. In this study, we aim to provide an insight into how news recipients assess dubious information and how accurate their appraisal of exemplary news is. We conducted interviews with N = 51 participants and found evidence for the role of emotions as well as the importance of design and layout when trying to discern true from fake news. Additionally, two particular cases illustrate the different ways of assessing news credibility: through intuition or analytically. In consequence, we suggest a model of online news processing including individual traits such as social influence, attitudes (e.g., political beliefs), and media literacy.

## Introduction

Potentially everybody is exposed to huge quantities  of information: Fake news, i.e., news content that  mimics news articles while neglecting journalistic duties and aiming to mislead recipients (Pennycook & Rand,  2021), play a major role in this regard. Therefore, unders tanding and fostering fake news literacy has become  an important topic of educational research and practice. Developing intentions and forming a cognitive perspective are the results of a decision, for which informatio n needs to be processed (Greifeneder et al., 2021) either intuitively (Schwarz & Jalbert, 2021) or analytical ly, using methods that discern truth from falsehood s such as fact-checking. However, several factors can bias the processing of information, such as source credibility, social consensus or supporting evidence (Schwarz & Jalbert, 2021). Recent research has further added to the suspicion that failure to identify fake news is often rooted in familiarity and source heuristics (Pennycook & Rand, 2021).

## Research Questions

The insight in the cognitive processing of fake news is still insufficiently differentiated. Hence, in this study we aim to explore the cognitive processing routes (mai nly intuitive vs. analytic) online news recipients utilize. We examine the following research questions (RQs): RQ1: How do online news recipients assess news credibil ity? RQ2: What is the accuracy of their assessment?

## Methodology

This exploratory study was built upon a mainly basic, qualitative design with semi-structured interviews for data collection. The interview protocol included 42 questions about demographic data, usage of the internet and online media, specific experiences with dubious news articles, and reactions towards those. In addition, we presented the participants two snippets of news articles, one true and the other fake, and asked them to rate these and describe how they dealt with them. The interviews were condu cted in June 2021.The participants were a convenien ce sample of N = 51 German internet users (23 female, 27 male, 1 n/a), from which 40 were 18–30, 4 between 31– 49, and 7 between 50–60. The majority of participan ts (26) were university students, 13 of them had al ready graduated. When describing their own self-confidence in dealing with online news and its credibility, we found our sample to be very self-confident (43) with only  nine accounts of moderate confidence, and one pers on who described themselves as very insecure on this accou nt. As to whether our participants thought of thems elves as intuitive or analytical when dealing with problems, decisions, or information, 25 participants strongl y relied on their gut feelings and 27 moderately trusted their intuition. Only 10 participants described themselve s as being “head-persons”.

For this study we also chose to highlight two speci al cases. Participant A (female, 22 years old) was a communication design student born in Germany and wo rking in an information technology firm. She felt moderately self-confident (4 on a 10-point scale) in handling online news, and reported to seldom question news credibility or check sources. Participant B (female, 24) was a media and communication science  student, born and grown up in Madrid, and working in Germany. She had already taken part in media literacy training during her studies. She rated herself as highly confident (8 out of 10) when handling online news. ICLS2022 Proceedings 2110 © ISLS

## Findings

RQ1. Many participants (18) explained that they would read an article entirely before passing judgement over its credibility. Some others only read the headline (14) or specific parts of the article (14). However, a deciding factor seemed to be the source of the news content and level of interest in it (32). Throughout the three news examples, the layout and design played the most important rol e in our participants assessments (11 for participa nts’ own examples, 31 for our real news, 24 for our fake news). In contrast, the source seemed to play a less important role (8, 4, 8). Participant A named a majority opinion towards a topic or the co mments under the news as the main assessment criterion for news credibility. In absen ce of such information, she asked other people, fri ends and family, to share their beliefs and then adopted tho se of who she thought highly of. Participant B mentioned focusing on the content and source of an article wh en analyzing its credibility, with trusted sources being the established news media formats. She rated our real article as credible because of its content. She rat ed the fake article as not credible at all, again based on its content. RQ2. In terms of credibility assessments, we found some  disparities between the real and fake news articles. The real article was rated as moderately credible (18), not credible (14), or very credible (12). On the flipside, the fake news article was rated as not cr edible by a majority (26), moderately credible (14), or very credible (4). Our cases varied substantially in ter ms of their accurate credibility ratings. Participant A gave the real article a 3 out of 10 (not credible). On the other hand, she rated the fake news article as credible (7 out of 10). Participant B accurately identified both pieces of news, giving the real article a 10 out of 10 rating, i.e., very credible, and the fake news a 1 out of 10 rating, i.e., not credible at all.

## Conclusions

This study was aimed at gaining insight into the co gnitive routes utilized by news recipients when dealing with dubious online content. When assessing news credibi lity, our participants mainly focused on design and  visual factors (intuitive route), less on the content itse lf (analytic route). Furthermore, we found a tenden cy towards overconfidence in regards to dealing with online ne ws. While the participants in our sample were quite  sure of their ability to accurately detect fake news, their  results in the two exemplary tasks did not match t heir selfassessment—this was mainly associated with intuitive online news processing. The issue of overconfidence when dealing with media  effects is not new. It stands to reason that susceptibility to fake news is also rooted in third-person beliefs, i.e., thinking of oneself as not being as vulnerable towards misinformation as the average citizen (Ştefăniţă et al., 2018). Additionally, our two cases can offer some more specific insight into how recipients deal with online news. The great degree of trust participant A put into her social circle when assessing truth paints an interesting but worrying picture of recipients who simply do not think for themselves but rely solely on intuitive processing through social cues. Social consensus is a major factor when discerning truth from falsehoods (Schwarz & Jalbert, 2021) and can largely open the door to misinformation. Finally, participant B may have shown us the value of prior media literacy education through concise and accurate assessments of our exemplary news articles.

Altogether, this study suggests a classification of  online media consumers: critical thinkers, such as participant B, with a high degree of media literacy and solid so urce knowledge who do not bother investigating dubious news but simply ignore them based on their experience, and naïve non-thinkers like participant A, who solely follow social cues but not necessarily exper ts and do not evaluate credibility at all. For futu re research endeavors, it may be worth investigating the interplay between personal characteristics (e.g., a strong focus on the social environment), prior consolidated attitudes (e.g., political beliefs), and specific media literacy competencies (e.g., high degree of source knowledge) in regards to which factors really help people identify misinformation and how to foster them with targeted educational interv entions. These individual traits may be part of a c onceptual model of online news processing, which we aim to validate in future research.
