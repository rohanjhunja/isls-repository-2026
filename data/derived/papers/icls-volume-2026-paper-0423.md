# Leveraging AI-Powered Simulation for Transitional Care: A Pilot Study in Usability and Learning Design

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This pilot study explored the potential of an AI-powered simulation to support learning in nursing transitional care. Two nursing students and two nursing educators engaged with the prototype and evaluated its usability using the System Usability Scale (SUS). Findings indicated a generally positive perception, highlighting the simulation's opportunity to foster clinical reasoning and learner engagement. Participants also identified areas for improvement, such as instructional scaffolding and interface design. Insights are discussed through a lens of situated cognition, emphasizing how AI-powered simulations can advance experiential learning while underscoring the need for iterative design to optimize usability and educational effectiveness.

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a powerful technology, particularly when applied to simulation —a long-standing approach to designing hands-on, experiential learning in nursing (Chan et al., 2025). Dai and Ke (2022) argue that by incorporating AI techniques, simulation-based learning can foster active engagement and support decisionmaking through human-like interactions. AI-powered virtual agents, for example, can help address one of education’s grand challenges: providing personalized learning (Woolf et al., 2013). These data-driven modeling techniques, when combined with delicate fine-tuning, dynamically shape and mediate naturalistic learner interactions. This clearly distinguishes AI-powered simulations from other interactive educational applications, such as rule-based digital storytelling, computer-assisted quizzes, and online discussion forums. Simulation has long been central to nursing education, offering safe, controlled environments for students to practice clinical skills, critical thinking, and decision-making. Traditionally, these simulations rely on predesigned scenarios and specialized equipment to replicate real-world contexts (Chan et al., 2025). Artificial intelligence (AI), however, can elevate these experiences by introducing context-specific and persona-reflective patient responses, adaptive scenarios, and individualized feedback. Critically, the ability to simulate natural, human-like interactions allows educators to overcome inherent barriers in replicating authentic clinical environments. This carries a particularly significant implication for transitional care, the movement from hospital to home for previously hospitalized patients. High quality transitional care is a critical juncture in patient outcomes, which necessitates coordinated interventions from frontline healthcare teams. Nurses are uniquely positioned to address the holistic needs of patients due to their specialized knowledge and patient-centered approach (Micheal et al., 2025). Effective communication is central to this process, yet new nursing graduates often struggle with interprofessional communication. After interviewing thirteen new nurses, Yang et al. (2025) pointed out that there are many complexities in interprofessional communication the new nurses face, and thus, targeted education in communication skills is critical to ensure smooth collaboration across diverse healthcare teams. Thus, we prototyped a simulation for nursing students to practice conversations regarding transitional care with patients and other healthcare stakeholders. By leveraging GPT-4 and Unity game engine, the simulation allows learners to rehearse complex dialogues with various healthcare team members (Figures 1). This paper reports the preliminary results from a pilot study focused on usability and AI-agent-supported learning design.

## Method

Two pre-licensure nursing students and two nursing educators participated in the simulation, which lasted approximately 30 minutes, including both testing and a post-survey. The students had minimal clinical experience, whereas the educators each had more than 10 years of practice. Participants engaged with the simulation independently, with minimal facilitator intervention, primarily testing the hospital nurse scenario featuring John Davis, a patient with congestive heart failure. They interacted with multiple virtual agents, including a physician, pharmacist, and caregiver. All simulation sessions were recorded. Following the simulation, participants 2698 completed a post-survey that included the System Usability Scale (SUS), a widely used 10-item instrument for assessing usability (Brooke, 1996). The SUS has been shown to be a reliable and valid measure across diverse domains, including healthcare technology evaluation (Hyzy et al., 2022). This study was reviewed and determined to be exempt from IRB oversight.

SUS yielded a mean score of 74.38 out of 100 (SD = ≈ 36.6), indicating “Good” usability according to the adjective rating scale established by Bangor  et al. (2009) This suggests that, overall, participants perceived the simulation system as usable and effective for its intended purpose. However, the distribution was notably polarized. Three participants rated the system very highly (SUS = 97.5, 95, and 90), emphasizing that interacting conversationally with multiple AI-powered characters made the experience “informative” and “realistic.” In contrast, one participant, a nurse educator with extensive experience as a bedside nurse, assigned a markedly lower score (SUS = 15). This participant noted difficulty initiating the scenario due to the absence of contextual clinical materials typically available in practice (e.g., patient charts, medication records, or care plans). Other participants also reported that the simulation captured aspects of transitional care but still lacked several elements to feel fully realistic. Some also pointed to the perceived syntheticism in character voices and conversational flow. At the same t ime, participants valued the breadth of roles presented, the informative responses by the role-specific agents, the gamification features, and the option to verbalize the input. Suggested improvements included expanding the depth of patient information, preferably recorded following SBAR (i.e., nursing communication framework that includes situation, backgrounds, assessment, and recommendations), and improving the navigation and interaction mechanics within the simulation easier and more intuitive for all users. This pilot study presented preliminary findings from the development and user testing  of an AI-powered nursing simulation designed to support learning in transitional care. The results informs future design of LLM-based simulations of authentic interprofessional communication and patient interactions. The feedback on the lack of contextual clinical materials by the participant who was an experienced bedside nurse  critically highlights the importance of balancing conversational realism with information fidelity and interdisciplinary perspectives in simulation scenario design when developing AI-based virtual simulations for professional learning. Our early findings suggest that LLM-based, conversational AI agents can act as dynamic synthetic actors  that support the practice of medical communication and coordination, particularly in online or hybrid nursing programs where access to live clinical simulation is limited. This aligns with Onda (2012)’s viewpoint that situated cognition helps novice nurses grow into socially skilled professionals through experiences in high-fidelity simulation. Moving forward, our development priorities include refining the user interface and improving the fine-tuned contextual awareness in the LLM-based agent to reflect authentic patient care environments. Future iterations will incorporate structured patient data, medication lists, and an input interface of documentation to strengthen clinical realism. Ultimately, we envision scaling this platform to include a diverse library of patient cases, enabling nursing students to practice authentic, scenario-based care transitions across a range of conditions and set tings.
