# Cultivating Socioecological Care through a Speculative Approach to Science Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Socioecological crises plaguing the world demand that education strives toward otherwise futures. Unfortunately, dominant approaches to education, and science education in particular, continue to perpetuate anthropocentric and technoscientific worldviews that do more to prolong thandisrupt these crises. In this conceptual paper, I present an alternative, approach to science education centered around cultivating socioecological care. Drawing on the work of feminist and Indigenous scholars who center entanglement and interconnectedness, I conceptualize the practice of socioecological care as multidirectional flows of care, performed by all entities (human and more-than-human) to sustain collective flourishing. I offer a speculative approach comprised of paying attention, recognizing, and envisioning as a tool for designing and studying science learning toward socioecological care.

## Introduction

Current ways of life are not ecologically sustainable and youth’s awareness of ecological precarity is causing high levels of anxiety, which for some, contributes to a  sense that nothing can be done (Brophy et al., 202 3; Ojala, 2012). Therefore youth need support to feel a sense of agency and hope. However, this agency and hope must not fall intoneoliberal traps of naïve hope (Zembylas,  2014) that we might be saved by dominant status qu o approaches to environmental problems that overempha size anthropocentrism, individualism, and technoscience (Hursh et al., 2015; Swaffield, 2016). While individual behavior change and novel technologies address facets of environmental problems, e.g. curbing emissions, these approaches rarely account for sociopolitical context, often focus narrowly on short term outcomes, overlook uni ntended consequences, and privilege humandesires a nd needs over more-than-human or systemic needs. The disproportionate burde n of environmental degradation on minoritized communities makes this e specially concerning. The notion that individual ac tions or technoscience may overcome environmental injustices overlooks sys temic inequities that impede attempts to counter th e powerful interests (e.g. of technoscientific indust ries) that back the status quo. So rather than naïve hope, youth need to develop critical hope (Zembylas, 2014) root ed in critiquing dominant past and present human-nature relations and uplifting the insights of those who h ave contested these relations. This requires tools and practices that support alternative worldviews toward harmonious ways of living. Science education offers a logical space for youth to develop nuanced views of human-nature relations. However, despite efforts on the margins to engage y outh in critiquing dominant human-nature relations and uplifting minoritized perspectives (e.g. Bang et al., 2014), dominant approaches to science education perpetuate status quo approaches to environmental issues. Scie nce education often bolsters neoliberal capitalist aims by emphasizing technocratic ideologies, overlooking so cial, ecological, and politic al costs of technoscientific solutions (Feinstein & Kirchgasler, 2015; Morales-Doyle et al., 2019). For example, Morales-Doyle et al. (2019) highlight how the Next Generation Science Standards  suggest students use cost-benefit analyses to evaluate solutions to complex sociopolitical problems, such as the m anagement and use of energy and mineral resources, and explicitly suggest “developing best practices” for mining and pumping fossil fuels. The standards thereby overlook whom such practices harm and frame Earth t hrough the lens of exploitation. Relatedly, the sta ndards repeatedly separate humans from nature and sidestep  or downplay humans’ (unequal) culpability for environmental problems (Hufnagel et al., 2018). As such, the standards communicate a cultural  assumption that humans exist apart from nature (Bang & Marin, 2015), enabling ways of thinking and being that ignore t he interdependence between all life on Earth. By disre garding human complicity in ecological precarity, t he standards further minimize ou r responsibility to maintain and sustain Earth’s we ll-being. Supporting youth to develop critical hope and to acknowledge and remedy  socioecological harms will therefore require shift s in the ways we teach science. To that end, this conceptual  paper first expounds on socioecological care, then offers an approach to science education focused on cultivating it. This approach aligns with recent calls to expand the notion of equity in STEM education to incorporate heterogeneity, justice, and sustainability (NASEM, 2025). Socioecological care Drawing on the work of scholars who center entangle ment and interconnectedness, I conceptualize the pr actice of socioecological care as multidirectional flows of care, performed by all entities (human and more-than-human) to sustain collective flourishing. Within an entangled web, instances of care involve a dynamic interplay between recognizing interdependence and assuming responsibi lity for one’s role within the larger web (see Figu re 1). Below, I review relevant literature to expand on these components. Figure 1 Socioecological Care Within Entangled Webs In its most extreme formulation, the notion of enta nglement rejects the existence of individual agents. I believe that a complete rejection of individual age ncy is a risky proposition in relation to socioecol ogical care because it opens the door to apathy and complacency  born from an assumption that one cannot make any difference. However, the idea that worlds are created through entangled relations critically counters human-centric egotistical notions of saviorism. Haraway’s (2016) concept of sympoesis—“making-with” or “worlding-with, in company” (p. 58) —as the basis of collective flourishing reflects thi s. Applying this notion to care engenders humbleness and shifts the focus from autonomous age nts caring about or for others toward relations within a larger web. This is not to say that humans lack age ncy, but recognizing that no one ever acts alone, one cannot presume to fully control the outcomes of care provi sion, nor treat care-giving as something with a distinct end. Puig de la Bellacasa (2017) echoes this in her understanding of responsibility for what/whom we care for as “being involved” rather than “being in charge” (p. 90), emphasizing the importance of recognizing interdependencies in relation to assuming responsibility (the double-sided arrow in Figure 1). This illuminates how care  flows in multilateral, asymmetric ways to sustain life; our mutual vulnerability creates caring obligations. While Puig de la Bellacasa (2017) and Haraway (2016) emphasize care as a practice of engaging with others in specific contexts, they also emphasize thinking and knowing in socioecological care. Puig de la Bellacasa states, “Ways of knowing/caring reaffect objectifie d worlds, restage things in ways that generate poss ibility for other ways of relating and living” (p. 65). And Har away writes, “It matters what stories make worlds, and what worlds make stories” (p. 12). Both authors emphasiz e that storying the world through the dominant (Wes tern) narratives of capitalism and technoscience, which c enter around progress and output, forecloses multis pecies flourishing.

Figure 2 Ways of Engaging in Socioecological Care Within Indigenous Epistemologies Indigenous ontologies, epistemologies, and axiologies help to further elucidate a worldview that centers around relations and collective flourishing rather than progress and output. An ontology in which every “thing”— humans, plants, animals, rocks, water, etc.—is animate underlies Indigenous ways of knowing (Ca jete, 2000; Kimmerer, 2013; Whyte & Cuomo, 2015). Though someon e with a Western worldview may critique this as anthropomorphism, this falsely assumes that Indigen ous understandings of agency align with Western one s. Through multiple studies with Ngöbe people and US c ollege students, ojalehto mays et al. (2020) found that Ngöbe participants inferred agency mostly based on observable interactions and relationships, as a communicative capacity. Meanwhile, US participants inferred agenc y as unobservable mental states underlying actions,  as a psychological capacity. This was ev ident in a study where Ngöbe participants identifie d a plant’s chemical signaling to spur a neighboring plant’s defenses ag ainst insects as kin altruism, i.e. a form of socia l communication. On the other hand, US participants i nterpreted the signaling as a  biological byproduct, i.e. an automatic chemical process. ojalehto mays et al. (2 020) suggest that the Ngöbe people’s framework “may  offer conceptual tools for transforming fraught human-environment relationships by encouraging the percep tion of ecologies as social domains” (p. 69). Viewing more-than-human beings as social interlocutors means we must learn to speak their languages and become attentive  to the unique ways they relate with each other and  with us. From this viewpoint, recognizing interdependence is  not an academic exercise of describing relations, but a relational process of starting and sustaining conve rsations with others in our entangled socioecologic al web (see Figure 2a). These conversations become a way of getting “involved” (in Puig de la Bellacasa’s (2017) terms). Viewing larger ecologies as “social domains” and be ing in conversation with more-than-human entities also shifts perceptions of interdependence. While P uig de la Bellacasa (2017) and Haraway (2016) discu ss interdependence in terms of mutual vulnerability, v iewing more than humans as social others imbues interdependent relations with an axiological flavor  of gratitude. Kimmerer (2013) notes, “It is human perception that makes the world a gift” (p. 30). In Indigenous  cultures where more than humans are recogn ized as agentic beings, capable of caring and offering gifts, resou rces we depend on (fresh air, food, water, etc.) ma y be appreciated as gifts. Kimmerer describes the feeling of being “awash in gifts” (p. 154) when she traces the entities that surround her to their origins—kleenex from trees, toothpaste from algae, etc. Gratitude for these gifts begets an obligation to reciprocate (see Figure 2b and 2c); recognizing the ways that nature sustains us spur s responsibility to sustain other lives. Kimmerer (2013) asserts that our duty as humans then, is to know “our unique gift and how to use it in the world” (p. 134) to care for humans and more than humans alike. Reflecting on the life ways of the Rarámuri people of the Sierra Madres of Chihuahua, Mexico, Salmón

(2000) describes how reciprocal practices generate abundance. Rarámuri understand their role as caretakers of the

land. So, for example, when they harvest wild onion s, they take only the larger bulbs, leaving smaller  ones to encourage continued growth. Digging the bulbs out also disturbs the ground, which protects onions from pervasive perennials. Caretaking begets abundance; reciprocal relations nurture collective flourishing. Gift-giving and the abundance of reciprocity might be th ought of as the double-ended arrow between recognizing interdependence and assuming responsibi lity in Figure 1. Gratitude for the gifts we receiv e emphasizes the affective aspect of recognizing inte rdependence—appreciating all the ways that others support our well-being—which pulls us to the other end of the arrow, to reciprocate. But if we think of these double-ended arrows, these reciprocal appreciations for and giving of gifts, within each instance of caring in the entangled web, we can see how they extend along the entangled lines begetting more instances of caring, to create the abundance of the overall web (see Figure 2d).

Finally, caretaking toward abundance as the Rarámuri people do requires a different form of stewardship than Western versions underlain by notions of human s separate from and in control of nature. Indigenou s stewardship “refers to acknowledgement of one’s place in a web of interdependent relationships that create moral responsibilities” (Whyte & Cuomo, 2015, p. 238); staying involved by assuming responsibility for our roles within the larger entangled web (Figure 2e). Recognizing h umans as just one among many beings in dynamic rela tions with rippling effects within the larger web entails  a measure of humility. Indeed, Indigenous peoples recognize the impossibility of controlling nature because nature’s constant state of flux engenders uncertainty (Cajete, 2000). Learning how to be good stewards is thus a matter o f constant relation and reflection on relationships  with the natural world toward sustaining balance and harmony. We must continually travel along the double-ended arrow in Figure 1, between conversing with the more-than-human world to better understand interdependent relationships and acting to sustain the web. Schola rs have emphasized participation and experience as central to Indigenous epistemologies (Cajete, 2000; Salmón, 20 00). This continual pa rticipation is an enactment of socioecological care that enables collective flourishing. Speculative science education toward socioecological care Given that science education often frames science as a detached, objective endeavor (NRC, 2012; Bang & Marin, 2015), it is clear that cultivating socioecological  care through science education requires substantia l changes. Moreover, science education toward socioecological care embraces a shift in what science education is for. Rather than science education for preparing a STEM workforce (which often harms youths’ communities; Nasir & Vakil,

2017) or for mirroring Western science’s pursuit of  supposedly obj ective truths, science education toward

socioecological care is explicitly and unapologetic ally for justice and for sensemaking and relationsh ip-building to foster collective well-being. Speculative education (Garcia & Mirra, 2023) —an expansive and future-oriented approach to education free from the constraints of current systems of oppression (capitalism, white su premacy, etc.)—offers a helpful guide for this radically different  approach to science education. Building on critica l scholarship, speculative education doesn’t reach bl indly toward imagined futures. Rather, it is rooted  in critique of the harm, oppression, and disempowerment inherent in current systems of education. Imagining and designing for alternative futures grow from recognition that the status quoneeds to change (Dunne & Raby, 2013). While critique may be passive, speculation actively works  toward envisioning and enacting alternatives. The plural “alternatives” is important: speculative approaches  do not prescribe particular ends. Rather, they dis rupt the present and open up other possible ways of life. Du nne and Raby (2013) talk about this in terms of off ering “compasses not maps” (p. 44); speculation offers wayfinding tools to travel off the well-trodden path of dominant, oppressive ways of life into “the multiverse of wor lds our world could be” (p. 160); “it is a catalyst  for social dreaming” (p. 189).

Expanding the critique of sociopolitical issues emb edded in Garcia and Mirra’s (2023) speculative education to critique of socio-politico-ecological issues makes room to contest the power h ierarchies of the Anthropocene. This includes challenging assumptions  of human superiority. Becoming aware of and curiou s about more-than-human beings and the complex stories of human and more than human entanglements may open up alternative worlds to explore through speculation and reveal different ways we might exist in harmony within these worlds. A speculative approach to science education could thus be not just a catalyst for social  dreaming, but a catalyst for socioecological dreaming and car e. Building on and rephrasing Garcia and Mirra (202 3), a speculative approach to science education toward socioecological care is an expansive and visionary approach to science teaching and learning that operates beyond the bounds of current canonical scientific practice s and dominant cultural assumptions that perpetuate nature-culture binaries, environmental injustices, and related forms of oppression. Critiquing dominant approaches to sc ience education and the nature-culture binaries that underlie canonical practices and thinking creates space to a ttend to interdependence (one en d of the arrow in Figure 1); and envisioning alternatives where humans aren't se en as masters of nature creates space to assume a d ifferent role (the other end of the arrow in Figure 1) toward collective thriving. Next, I offer and describe three processes—paying attention, recognizing, and envisioning—that support this approach. The first centers around attending to the more-than-human world, which is an important precursor to critique and envisioning because we must underst and the world as it is to perceive what harms need to be addressed, to understand the multiverse of worlds t hat already exist, and to imagine alternatives. The  second focuses on critiquing and reckoning with harms, as well as uplifting more just and sustainable ways of being. And the third is future-oriented, encompassing both imagination and action. Though in some ways, paying attention is a precursor to recognizing, and recognizing is a pr ecursor to generative envisioning, these processes are al so recursive and cyclical. By weaving back and forth b etween them, learners may deepen their appreciation for and become better stewards of the entangled web we are part of. Paying attention Paying attention to others (human and more than hum an) enables one to recognize interdependence. This comprises not just becoming aware of interdependent  relations, but the affective and social connection  that pulls one to further pay attention, become concerned about others’ well-being, and feel obligated to reciprocate. Paying attention requires a balance and interplay between (a) learning how and what to pay attention to and (b) remaining open to getting swept up in the more-than-human world, freed of predetermined assumptions about how to observe and what one might discover. Socially cultivated methods of perception, or professional vision (Goodwin, 1994), and openness (Ingold, 2023) are both key.

Professional vision shapes the kinds of questions s cientists ask, the observations they make, and the conclusions they draw. Goodwin (1994) describes thr ee practices of professional vision—(1) coding phenomena in ways that are relevant to the particular p rofession, (2) highlighting salient aspects of phen omena, and (3) producing and articulating material representations. Perceptual frameworks differ between different sc ientific disciplines (Goodwin, 1995). For example, Goodwin (1995) explains how a geochem ist who measures features of the water column from a ship needs a representat ion that highlights the depth of his measuring inst rument (instrument-surface distance), while a physical oceanographer d riving the instrument needs to highlight the instrument-seafloor distance. Nonetheless, commonalities underlie different disciplines’ perceptual frameworks. Generally, Western science aims to pursue objective  truths and assumes that the best way to discover truths is by following procedures to observe phenom ena from the outside. While what they code may diff er, scientists generally code phenomena to taxonomize  and compartmentalize facts, and they highlight aspe cts of phenomena in ways that zero in on particular facets  rather than attempting to approach phenomena holis tically. These general features of Western science enable ce rtain means of paying attention, bu t foreclose opportunities to attend in other ways. In particular, objectifica tion, detachment, and tendencies to compartmentaliz e counter holistic ways of attending that might better suppor t socioecological care. As science education strive s to engage learners in professional science practices (NRC, 20 12), these assumptions and tendencies trickle down into the forms of professional vision that we cultivate in young science learners. However,  practices may be transformed in science education contexts to (re)shape professional vision (Pierson & Clark, 2019). To envision practices that might shape professional vision toward care, I draw from Native science. One key difference gleaned from research with Indigenous communities is the transformation of coding and highlighting from practices performed by humans  on an objective field to coding and highlighting a s interactional accomplishments between humans and more than humans. Marin and Bang (2018) demonstrate this in their study of an Odawa mother and son engaging in walking, reading, and storying land to make sense of their place within a complex, dynamic, and interconnected  system. The mother disciplined her son’s perceptio n by drawing his attention to “land as a relevant semiotic resource” (p. 101), or in other words as an interlocutor in the interaction. As they walked, features of the land d irected their attention, and therefore influenced w hat they read and what stories they co-constructed.

A second important shift transforms highlighting fr om a means of marking and compartmentalizing features of a phenomenon to a way of attending to more-than-human agency and relations across space and time. In Marin and Bang’s (2018) study, the mother and so n highlighted relations by engaging in temporal tog gling, connecting their observations of land in the presen t to observations they had made on prior walks to m ake sense of changes. They also attended to more-than-human agency and relations through perspective taki ng. The son storied observations of a forest path and riverbed markings by taking the perspective of a deer walking, making a trail, and getting stuck in the river (Bang & Marin, 2015). Pugh et al. (2019) and McDaid Barry et al. ’s (2023) studies of Indigenous youth in a summer camp demonstrate th e potential of designing instruction to foster thes e elements of professional vision. The researchers ex plicitly designed opportunities to engage in imagin ative embodied perspective taking, used talk moves to mod el perspective taking and recognizing plants’ agent ic capacities, and offered material representations that framed plants as persons. While these aspects were designed, it is worth noting that Indigenous youth brought in  their own socially cultivat ed practices, knowledges, and perceptive abilities too (e.g. knowledge of plants as medicines to highlight plants’ roles as helpers). For non-Indigenous youth, recognition of more-than-human personhood, or learning to read the land, may  constitute a larger shift from existing cultural ways of knowing, requi ring un-and/or re-learning. Either way, disciplining perception toward noticing more-than-human agencies and relations is key to recognizing interdependence and forming more caring relationships with the more-than-human world. Expanding on the notion that more than humans may d irect our attention, openness entails releasing expectations of discovering the truth about entitie s through detached objective observations, and ente ring into a receptive, affective relationship. Shifting learnin g to outdoor settings —not necessarily “wilderness,” but rather, mundane everyday settings—may provide opportunities to be moved by our complex, entangled relations (Taylor & Pacini-Ketchabaw, 2019; Nxumalo, 2018). For example, witne ssing the death s of animals on and near early learning centers has spurred young children’s affec tive connections, wonderings about what it might fe el like to be those animals, and inquiry into human-nature relations (Taylor & Pacini-Ketchabaw, 2019; Nxumalo, 2018). Shifting the pace of learning may offer further opportu nities to engage deeply with the more-than-human world. While stillness enables learners to become receptiv e and “to notice the entanglements or connections b etween their embodied selves and the [more-than-human world]” (Stapleton & Lynch, 2021, p. 284), mo vement can surface different experiences of places and differe nt relations with the world. Movement can open up m ore embodied and affective relations to places and their inhabitants by enabling learners to laminate different sources of information in places (e.g. layering past histor ies over present landscapes; Hall et al., 2020); and coordinating attention to more than humans and their lifeworlds (Marin, 2020). Learning outdoors, through both stil lness and movement, thus provides opportunities for learners to receive the more-than-human world in all its richness and complexity in ways that traditional science learning in a classroom may foreclose. Paying attention through professional vision and op enness is a critical first step in science educatio n toward socioecological care. By opening ourselves u p to the world, we may appreciate the multitude of beings and relations that surround us and experience affective connections. And by broadening our toolkit for perception beyond the traditional lenses of science —coding more-than-human beings as agentic, highlighting roles and relations across time and space —we may more deeply understand more-than-human lifeworlds. This sets the stage for understanding and examining our own roles and responsibilities, i.e. for recognizing. Recognizing While recognition often refers to identifying someone or something as familiar, here I refer to recogn ition as reckoning with new understandings of the world and of oneself in relation to the world. As a component  of speculative science education, recogniz ing involves challenging dominant narratives of hum an-nature relations and grappling with one’s roles/responsibilties in r elation to these larger narratives toward new under standings of interdependence and critical consciousness. Gruenewald’s (2003) critical pedagogy of place is a helpful guide for this. He describes a critical pedagogy of place as integrating critical pedagogy and place-based education through “twin objectives”: (1) decolonization, which aims to “identify and change ways of thinking that injure and exploit other people and places” (p. 9); and (2) reinhabitation, which aims to “identify, recover, and create material spaces and places that teach us how to live well in our to tal environments” (p. 9). Critical pedagogy of plac e thus aims on the one hand to develop learners’ critical consc iousness toward transforming injustices, and on the  other to support learners to appreciate and affirm ways of sustaining socioecological systems. In some instances, traditional tools of science education may support recognition of injustices. For example, Davis and Schaeffer (2019) found that elem entary students leveraged scientific domain knowled ge to make sense of water justice issues in a place-based socio-scientific unit that centered around the Flint water crisis. Students’ understandings and representations of wat er properties and water contamination, and of the h armful bodily effects of lead poisoning, supported their identification and understanding of water injustices. Furthermore, scientific facts, such as how many days people can survive without water, supported students’ construc tion of moral arguments about water as a human right. Simil arly, Dimick (2016) found that urban high schoolers  used scientific knowledge to interrogate dominant cultural and sociopolitical practices around land as they engaged in an urban park restoration project. For example, applying their scientific understanding of ecological relationships (some species’ dependence on tall grass), they critiqued legal and cultur al practices that require trimming grass. Finally, in Kayumova and Tippins (2021) study with youth in a rural Texas border community where frack ing was on the rise, building scientific models to pred ict the longevity of local oil reserves led youth to crit ique oil industry representatives’ false narratives about the potential of fracking in their community, and more broadly to understand the inherent biases of scientific data. In all of these studies, scientific domain knowledge and practices played an essential role in students’ identificatio n of phenomena and practices that harm human and mo re-thanhuman actors (i.e. Gruenewald’s (2003) decolonization). At the same time, practices and knowledges that are  typically cast out by (Western) science education are also key to the nuanced understandings and reflection needed to develop critical consciousness. In particular, engagement with personal, communal, and  historical stories is crucial for deeper sensemakin g around issues of power and positionality in relation to injustices. In Davis and Schaffer’s (2019) study, reflecting on  personal stories and experiences with water shut-offs in their own city was critical to students’ re cognition that the Flint water crisis was not just something happening to other people in another city, but rather a part of a larger systemic issue. In a study of a data science workshop on env ironmental racism, Reigh et al. (2022) found that contextualizing quantitative representations of spa tial inequities in personal, sociopolitical, and hi storical stories gave youth insight into the root causes of quantita tive patterns. Countering initial tendencies to dra w false conclusions (e.g. “places with higher pollution attract low-income people because they cost less”), this supported youth to reflect on larger systemic causes of injus tice, specify the actors and means of harm, and con sider their position in relation to the injustice.

Stories also provide important opportunities to cen ter minoritized perspectives and experiences. In hi s work with college students in environmental studies  and science courses, Valle (2021) found that shari ng narratives of relations with land helped students c hallenge dominant environmental narratives and vali dated minoritized students’ experiences. For example, som e Latinx students’ stories of food-based relationships with land offered perspectives that countered the nature-culture divide and expanded some other students’ assumptions about human-nature relationships. Davis and Schaeffer (2019) highlighted how narratives that affirm minoritized experiences provide a critical anti-deficit counterpoint to stories of oppression and i njustice, inviting learners to envision the role of science in creating more just futures. Stories may thus support both the decoloni zation and reinhabitation sides of recognizing.

In discussing decolonization and reinhabitation, it  is essential to acknowledge the danger of settlers ignoring Indigenous claims to land and erasing Indigenous futurity (Tuck et al., 2014). Settlers’ attempts to “live well” may shift into exploitation and  colonial violence if they veer toward “playing Indi an” (Deloria & Lytle, 1998 as cited in Bang et al., 2014) and fail to app roach Indigenous stories with respect and humility.  Centering Indigenous desires and experiences also nuances the  shapes that decol onization may take. Decolonization may sometimes entail critiquing and deconstructing Western, imperialist narratives of history and unsettling the ways that Indigenous social issues have been framed to r ecognize systemic oppressions rather than placing b lame on individuals. However, for Indigenous and other mino ritized peoples, it may entail refusing to particip ate, share, or be commodified, and must take into account that remembering historical and intergenerational trauma s may take a toll and must be paired with efforts toward healing, joy, and connection (Smith, 2021). In summary, recognition is a process of humble enga gement with multiple stories of places, critical reflection on what cultural practices must be resis ted and what practices support socioecological care, and introspection. The work of recognizing may look different for different people and demands care-full engagement with marginalized stories of place. For settlers es pecially, critical introspection is key. Reckoning with the ways dominant epistemologies shape one’s thinking and ho w one is implicated in harms to human and more-thanhuman beings is an important precursor to envisioni ng and enacting more harmonious relations. This is messy and tension-filled, but creating space for multiple narratives of place, especially stories of resistance, thriving, and connection, calls attention to multiple ways to enact socioecological care and aligns with speculative envisioning toward “the multiverse of worlds our world could be” (Dunne & Raby, 2013, p. 160). Envisioning Envisioning engages learners in speculating about p ossible futures toward collective thriving. This ma y occur at different scales (individual, collective, moment-by-moment, longer-term) and take multiple forms. Envisioning does not put blind faith in “salv ific futures” where technology will rescue us, but “stays with the trouble” (Haraway, 2016), contesting problematic realities and ideologies while provoking otherwise possibilities. Envisioning may be tightly entwined with paying attention. As demonstrated in research by the Learning in Places Collaborative (2022), when young children pay attention to the more-than-human world and view more than humans as agents, they may envision and enact different relationships. Their ethical deliberations about how to treat more-than-human entities may shift toward greater respect for  the entities’ lifeworlds and roles within greater ecosystems, and spur responsibility to not disrupt them. For example, a child might leave more  than humans in their natural environment rather than removing them for the sake of observation (Sherry-Wagner, 2023) or might refrain from touching a more than human to  avoid distressing them (Lees & Bang, 2022). While these actions may seem insignificant, they embody ways of thinking that offer a foundation for care-filled decisionmaking around much larger socioecological and political issues. Envisioning may also be spurred by recognizing. In McGowan and Bell’s (2022) study, coming to understand not only the ecological context, but als o the entwined sociopolitical context of sea star d ecline (e.g. lack of policies for wildlife protection, actio ns by the oil industry, etc.), contributed to stude nts’ speculative designs for otherwise futures. Students iterated on causal-loop models first to represent current relationship s that deteriorate sea star health, and then to represent alternative relationships that could support sea star health in the future. The socioecological care engendered by students’ speculative design work multiplied outward and forward to further instances —to discussions at home, other academic work, and co mmunity action projects.  Recalling Figure 2, care begets care, spurring abundance.

Rousell and colleagues’ (2017) study of youth’s speculative engagement in a program focused on climate change awareness further exemplifies the viral potential of envisioning. In their study, envisioning took the form of speculative fiction around the Anthropocene. One  youth’s fiction centered on an individual with mut ant capabilities from Earth who was on the run in an ap ocalyptic future where cyborgs surveil and hunt mut ants like them. This story spurred a flurry of activity among  other youth in the progr am who created characters modeled after the story’s protagonist and engaged in climat e change-related speculations (e.g. theorizing possible biological adaptations to climate change). While th ese youths’ speculations were darker than the envis ioning undertaken in McGowan and Bell’s (2022) study, they simil arly expressed a critique of status quo relations (e.g. of surveillance technologies and disregard for life). As climate justice writer Mary Annaïse Heglar ar gues, grief is a reasonable, healthy response t o the precarity of the Anthropocene (personal commu nication, February 20, 2024). Rather than uncritically envisioning a futur e under status quoneoliberal ideologies, the apoca lyptic envisioning of youth in Rousell and colleagues’ (20 17) study may be an important step in processing gr ief and grappling with the reality of harmful relations so that repair may follow. This is an important nuance  of envisioning work. While there are undoubtedly forms  of speculation that uphold or hyperbolize oppressi ve ideologies and do not “veer toward horizons of goodness” (G arcia & Mirra, 2023, p. 7), envisioning as I conceptualize it is not restricted to visions of fl ourishing. Indeed, in Rousell et al.’s (2017) study, grappling with apocalyptic visions contributed to collective conti nued participation with the Earth and created space  for “the emergence of new possibilities for life through res istance to the present” (Bogue, 2011, p. 88 as cite d in Rousell et al., 2017).

## Conclusion

In the context of current socioecological crises, t he stakes of science education are high. Youth need  scientific tools to understand and address the multitude of in terconnected environmental injustices that plague t he Earth. Yet teaching science without a  foundation in care risks approaches to these proble ms that perpetuate techno-centric, capitalist solutions rather than collectiv e thriving. Here, I have described an approach towa rd cultivating care through paying attention  to more-than-human beings and r elations within socioecological systems, recognizing human roles within these systems, and envisioning more caring futures. The practices I have outlined are not intended as a checklist, nor do I presume that they represent a complete list of the ways one might engage in paying attention, recognizing, or envisioning. F urthermore, though presented as a linear process, p aying attention, recognizing, and envisioning interweave and iteratively loop. For example, recognizing frau ght socioecological histories may spur new inquiries th at require paying attention to the particular ways different entities are entangled in the present, which may en gender speculations of other possibilities (envisio ning). Through (spiraling) engagement in these processes, speculative science education toward socioecologica l care aims to cultivate different ways of perceiving and relating with others. Enacting this approach to science education supports youth to lift up experiences of socioecological care and envision futures of collective well-being, offering hope for a more just, thriving world.
