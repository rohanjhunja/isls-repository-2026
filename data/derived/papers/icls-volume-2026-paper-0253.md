# Coloniality in Data Science Education: Marginalization and Absorption of Indigenous Perspectives

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how middle school students engaged with the sociopolitical dimensions of data when learning about Indigenous food sovereignty through a social studies unit integrating data science. Drawing on Lee et al. (2021) and Sabzalian (2019), we analyze interview and artifact data to explore how Indigenous perspectives were centered or erased in the enacted curriculum. We found students engaged with data through deficit-based orientations and proposed solutions positioning Indigenous communities a s problems requiring outsider intervention. Even an Indigenous student's insider perspective was reinterpreted by the teacher through narratives of individual transformation, reproducing colonial logics. These findings extend the sociopolitical layer of data engagement to address colonial dimensions and highlight the need for anticolonial frameworks in data science education to challenge erasure of Indigenous perspectives in seemingly equity-oriented learning contexts.

Learning scientists increasingly call for critical approaches to K –12 data science education that interrogate who benefits from data practices, whose voices are represented, and how data can reproduce or challenge existing power relations (Louie, 2022; Phi lip et al., 2016). Taking a critical stance means attending to the political and social forces that shape our engagements with data  (Monroe-White, 2021). Yet, how students engage with the sociopolitical dimensions of data remains one of the least examined aspects of data literacy education (Lee et al., 2021). Existing research on sociopolitical issues in data science often focuses on students analyzing public datasets related to social inequities or investigating data from their own communities (e.g., Rubel et al., 2016; Van Wart et al., 2020). We extend this work by examining middle school students’ engagements with data about food access on tribal lands in two Western states. Because tribal nations have a unique legal and political status in the United States (Wilkins & Lomawaima, 2001), working with data about Indigenous communities introduces unique sociopolitical dimensions. Indigenous studies scholars have long critiqued mainstream U.S. education for reproducing colonial logics that center Western knowledge systems, erase Indigenous sovereignty, and marginalize Indigenous ways of knowing and being (Bang & Medin, 2010; Calderón, 2014; Sabzalian, 2019). Attending to the sociopolitical dimensions of data in this context thus requires examining whose knowledge and perspectives are centered when students work with data relevant to past and contemporary issues on tribal lands. In this study, we examine how middle school students engaged with the sociopolitical dimensions of data as they learned about Indigenous food sovereignty through a social studies unit integrating data science practices. The unit was designed for educators in states with Indian Education for All (IEFA) mandates that require integrating Indigenous content across K-12 curricula, and was implemented in an eighth-grade classroom where students created data visualizations mapping food access on tribal lands and developed action plans to address food insecurity. Specifically, we ask: (1) How did students engage with the data on Indigenous food sovereignty, and (2) How are Indigenous perspectives centered or erased in their work?

## Theoretical background

To examine how students and their teacher engaged with the sociopolitical dimensions of data about Indigenous communities, we draw on Lee et al. (2021) as a conceptual framework and Sabzalian (2019) as an analytic framework. Lee et al. (2021) propose that students' engagements with data operate across personal, cultural, and sociopolitical layers. While these layers are intertwined and mutually shape one another, this study focuses on the sociopolitical layer, which examines the enduring social and politica l forces that shape how data are produced, interpreted, and valued, and who is empowered to participate in or contest those processes. We center the sociopolitical layer of students’ engagements with data because the curriculum was designed to center Indigenous sovereignty and challenge settler colonial narratives. While Lee et al. (2021) identify this dimension as important yet understudied, their framework does not specify how to recognize colonial logics within these political and 1747 social forces, particularly in Indigenous contexts. We extend Lee et al.’s (2021) framing of students’ sociopolitical engagements with data to address the colonial dimensions of knowledge systems. We argue that analyzing the sociopolitical layer in Indigenous contexts requires explicitly attending to anticolonial frameworks that can reveal sovereignty erasure and settler colonial narratives. To do so, we draw on Sabzalian's (2019) framework, which outlines six orientations that educators can attend to for anticolonial civic education: place, presence, perspective, political sovereignty, power, and partnerships. Here we focus on the perspective orientation, which emphasizes the value of Indigenous perspectives in disrupting Eurocentric curriculum. As Sabzalian (2019) writes, “Emphasizing Indigenous perspectives disrupts the pervasive pattern of positioning Indigenous peoples as the objects of study in the curriculum” (Sabzalian, 2019), positioning Indigenous peoples instead as those we can learn from (not about)” (p. 20). This orientation is particularly critical because it exposes tensions between Western epistemologies that privilege individualism and Indigenous knowledge systems grounded in relationality and collective responsibility (Tynan, 2021), what some scholars describe as “we are, therefore I am” (Burkhart, 2004, as cited in Brayboy, 2005). The perspective orientation provides the analytical tools needed to identify how students’ data work becomes a site where colonial logics are reproduced, negotiated, or contested. Building on Lee et al.’s (2021) framing of the sociopolitical dimensions of data engagement, we added Sabzalian's (2019) perspective orientation to guide our analysis, examining whe ther students working with data about Indigenous food sovereignty reflected colonial or anticolonial orientations.

## Context and methods

This study is part of a larger research project focused on data science education in social studies, aiming to teach students about the importance of treaties through the lens of food sovereignty. The unit comprises distinct sections on sovereignty and treaties, each accompanied by associated projects (Searle et al., 2023). In the sovereignty portion of the curriculum, students engaged in a food access mapping project in which they used Google Maps to identify fresh food access across seven reservations in two states, then created data visualizations of these food access patterns (see Figure 1 left). The curriculum was implemented by one social studies teacher over two consecutive academic years at a rural middle school where about 30% of the student body was Indigenous. The teacher was also accountable to Indian Education for All (IEFA) standards, which required all students in the state to learn about the Indigenous inhabitants of the state. Prior to implementing the unit, the teacher received eight hours of professional development emphasizing the technical aspects of the projects. In Fall 2024, the teacher implemented the unit in an eighth-grade social studies class over five instructional weeks. The teacher had his students complete the mapping project we designed, followed by a writing project of his own design. I n this project, students worked individually to create Public Service Community Service Action Plans (PSCSAPs) where they proposed solutions to food access issues on reservations where they identified insufficient access to fresh food. The teacher  also provided structured scaffolds that prompted students  to consider what resources a community might need, how they could provide evidence to support their claims, and whether similar initiatives had been successfully implemented elsewhere (see Figure 1 right; state names blinded). Figure 1 Left: Food Access Map Project; Right: PSCSAP Prompts Provided by the Teacher We conducted a post-implementation interview with the teacher, lasting approximately 100 minutes, to understand his reflections on the curricular implementation and students’ engagement with the projects. We also collected and scanned a selection of students’ PSCSAPs (N = 15). For analysis, we first identified interview excerpts where 1748 the teacher discussed students’ work on the food access maps and PSCSAP projects. We then used Sabzalian’s perspective orientation to deductively code these excerpts, focusing on how Indigenous perspectives were centered or marginalized, using PSCSAPs to triangulate our interpretations. Before presenting our findings, we emphasize that the teacher is not positioned as the problem. His choices were shaped by institutional and curricular constraints common in U.S. classrooms. Our curriculum design also unintentionally contributed to reproducing colonial logics. These dynamics reflect broader structural challenges of engaging students in the sociopolitical aspects of data (Philip et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2021) rather than individual shortcomings. Our research team included Indigenous and non-Indigenous scholars during design but not implementation, which shaped the perspectives we brought to this analysis. The team consisted of a white principal investigator experienced in partnerships with Indigenous communities and international graduate students with diverse cultural backgrounds. As outsiders, we approached interpretation with care, recognizing that equitable research relationships require sustained commitment (Tuck & Yang, 2012). Our analysis shows that colonial logics persisted in students’ PSCSAP projects, where data practices and interpretations often erased Indigenous perspectives despite the curriculum’s focus on sovereignty and treaty education. We present two themes: the fir st examines how students engaged with data while adopting outsider perspectives that positioned Indigenous communities through deficit lenses; the second illustrates how an Indigenous student's insider engagement was reinterpreted through the teacher's framing, absorbing her community-based perspective into personal transformation flattening its meaning and reproducing colonial logics. Engaging with data through an outsider lens to the project but was shaped by deficit-oriented assumptions and the marginalization of Indigenous perspectives. The teacher summarized the as signment’s structure, explaining that each student would select an Indigenous reservation to analyze, “You’re going to explain what’s going on, what the problem is, and what your solution is to that.” The teacher then described how he encouraged students t o analyze quantitative indicators of health and food access to identify community needs and propose solutions. He explained: They were expected to provide data and analyze that data and be able to say, you know, look at the health, look at the life expectancy, look at the obesity rates, look at the diabetes, look at all these health problems that this community is facing… and look at the lack of food access that they have.

The teacher also directed students to investigate how other communities across the country had addressed similar challenges, explaining “I had them research what other communities have done around the country to bring food sovereignty and security to communities.” Reflecting on students’ engagement, the teacher noted that they “took such pride in creating a visual presentation” and he was “very pleasantly surprised to see students who sometimes have the hardest time staying engaged in any class embrace this project wholeheartedly.” While the teacher viewed the combination of the food access map and PSCSAP as engaging, examined through Sabzalian’s (2019) perspective orientation, his framing of the projects positioned students’ data work within deficit-oriented frameworks that marginalized Indigenous perspectives. Instructions emphasizing what services Indigenous communities lacked reinforced deficit framings and invited students to act as external experts developing “service action plans” rather than as students seeking to learn from Indigenous peoples. The teacher’s repeated “food desert” language also reflected this orientation while our design emphasized food access, which is the term used by the U.S. Department of Agriculture. Student PSCSAPs echoed these narratives, centering non-Indigenous perspectives and solutions. Many compared reservation data with national averages, framing communities as problems to be fixed by outsider agents. For example, one student wrote, “Compared to the national average, people on reservations have lower life expectancy,” while others proposed, “this is how I think we can help [Reservation Name] step toward a more sustainable future.” Many proposed solutions modeled on Western development approaches, citing examples such as national nutrition programs f rom non-Indigenous contexts to justify effectiveness. These comparisons implied that what worked in cities like New York could also apply to reservations, reflecting a lack of perspective on Indigenous contexts. These patterns reveal how deficit-based reasoning legitimized Western knowledge while erasing Indigenous ways of knowing. Students rarely referenced Indigenous food sovereignty movements or community-led efforts. Although they practiced reasoning with data on sociopolitical issues, both the teacher’s discourse and student work reified deficit framings. Reification of colonial narratives despite insider lens 1749 When reflecting on student learning, the teacher highlighted one Indigenous student’s PSCSAP as being particularly meaningful. This student, who had recently moved from a reservation in the Four Corners region, asked to focus her action plan on her home community rather than on the pre-assigned reservations. In her project, she proposed reopening a closed store in her community to increase access to fresh food. The teacher described this Indigenous student’s engagement with the PSCSAP as transformative, emphasizing her initial reluctance and later enthusiasm as evidence of growth. He noted that she “loved what she was doing, and had an opportunity to reflect upon her upbringing and how it had personally impacted her community.” He praised her “taking ownership” of the project and described the experience as “empowering for her.” In the interview, the teacher framed this project primarily as a story of individual transformation and overcoming personal learning challenges. He explained that the student had come  to see “there’s more to the story than what she had perceived prior” and that “she had maybe more power to bring about positive change than just an idea of victimization.” In describing what he hoped the student learned from this experience, the teacher emphasized that people are “not defined by their past but defined by the present and their future” and encouraged “breaking the chains of past oppression.” Using the lens of perspective  (Sabzalian, 2019), the teacher’s framing reveals how Indigenous perspectives can be marginalized even when they are explicitly present. Although the student brought an insider lens, identifying the store closure through lived experience, the teacher’s interpretation reified colonial narratives. He reframed her engagement as a story of individual transformation, emphasizing that she discovered her “power to bring about positive change.” This celebration of personal empowerment a ligns with Western epistemologies that privilege individualism over the collective responsibility and relationality central to Indigenous knowledge systems (Tynan, 2021). The teacher's language of “victimization” and “past oppression” also centered Western frameworks that frame Indigenous struggles as historical grievances to be overcome, erasing both ongoing dispossession and understandings of treaty violations and sovereignty breaches as contemporary structural conditions. This framing reflects colonial assimilation logics embedded in U.S. policy toward Indigenous nations (e.g., Dawes Act) which sought to dissolve collective sovereignty by dividing communal lands into individual allotments. The teacher's rechanneling of the student's work into mainstream narratives of personal overcoming demonstrates how coloniality operates to absorb and reframe Indigenous perspectives. This study contributes to the growing scholarship in data science education within the learning sciences by showing that engaging students with sociopolitical issues through data practices is not, on its own, sufficient to challenge colonial logics. Even when the curriculum explicitly centered Indigenous sovereignty and an Indigenous student brought insider knowledge, colonial narratives persisted through the teacher’s interpretation and framing. These findings highlight that equity-oriented data science ed ucation can still reproduce colonialism if it fails to interrogate the epistemological and political foundations of data practices. Building on Lee et al.’s (2021) framing of data engagement across personal, cultural, and sociopolitical layers, this study extends the sociopolitical layer to address the colonial dimensions of knowledge systems. While Lee et al. emphasize that data are always shaped by social and political forces, our analysis demonstrates that these forces include the enduring structures of settler colonialism that define whose data, interpretations, and forms of reasoning are legitimized. Examining the sociopolitical layer in Indigenous contexts requires anticolonial frameworks that identify deficit framings and the erasure of sovereignty. Because Indigenous erasure is central to U.S. social studies education (Calderon, 2014), such frameworks are needed across contexts where students engage with data. This work extends Sabzalian's (2019) call for an anticolonial approach to civic education by grounding her framework in empirical data. It demonstrates how the tensions Sabzalian (2019) theorizes around the presence or absence of Indigenous perspectives manifest in everyday teaching and learning. This study advances anticolonial scholarship from conceptual framing toward empirical analysis, revealing how erasure of Indigenous perspectives and deficit framings can persist even within well-intentioned designs. This work has implications for curriculum design and teacher professional development. We acknowledge the limitation that our data include only the teacher’s reflections and student artifacts rather than students’ direct voices. While this constrains our understanding of students’ lived experiences with the projects, the teacher’s interview still provides valuable insights into how classroom practices reproduce or challenge colonial logics. Importantly, this analysis should not be read as a critique of the individual teacher, but as an examination of the structural and curricular tensions that shaped his enactment of the unit. The curriculum’s focus on mapping “food access gaps” oriented students toward identifying what Indigenous communities lacked, reinforcing deficit framings embedded in mainstream civic inquiry routines. Future iterations should foreground community-led food sovereignty initiatives and emphasize restoration, reciprocity, and resilience rather than lack.
