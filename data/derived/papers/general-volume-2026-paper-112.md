# Thresholds of Knowing

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract

Thresholds of Knowing Quilting connects art, craft, design, storytelling, political resistance, and cultural preservation. Coupling the fiber arts with electronics can expand opportunities for storytelling and learning. In this gallery submission, a quilter and two art-computing education researchers reflect on their distributed process of integrating quilting with electronics and present a triptych of large-format electronics quilts, each centering an unsung woman in technology: - Miriam E. Benjamin: the initiator, inventor of the initial airplane call button and hotel call button [signals] - Angela Ruiz Robles: the illuminator, inventor of one of the first e-readers [mechanisms] - Gladys Brown West: the locator, contributed to mathematically modeling earth supporting the development of GPS [mathematics] We communicate how their signals, mechanisms, and mathematics have impacted the ways in which we codify our existence, our knowledge, and our presence. We will present a 5min video documentary of our asynchronous, distributed process highlighting the opportunities for cultural learning through narratives. Kayla DesPortes, Mia Shaw, Earamichia Brown Research for Humans: Social Practice Art as Scholarly Communication Research for Humans is an interactive multimodal installation using social practice art to address a global epistemic crisis. Trust in reality wavers as traditional institutions lose their footing, while technology accelerates the need for reinvention. What is truth, how do we know it, and how might we design ways forward? Inspired by centuries of automata, the work reflects our enduring fascination with what it means to be alive and the uneasy balance between human vitality and machine logic. Visitors approach a roving cart overseen by an animatronic peddler — part mystic, part minstrel — and converse by talking or typing. Responses drawn from published research are printed in real-time on cards: one to keep, one added to a growing repository. Social practice art transforms research into communal inquiry. Here, academic knowledge becomes something felt, embodied, and carried home — a provocation toward braver thinking and social action. Emily Reardon, Lucius Von Joo, OreOluwa Badaki, Chris Moffett Mathematizing, Visualizing, and Power: A Collection of Community-Centered Data-Art by Appalachian Youth This collage poster shares photographs and artist statements of artistic data visualizations created by East Tennessean youth in Mathematizing, Visualizing, and Power (MVP): Appalachian Youth Becoming Data Artists for Community Learning, a multi-year NSF-funded project. Across five sites over three years, approximately 150 Appalachian youth participated in this project to connect data science, art, and community life. They were invited to ask questions that mattered to their communities, collect and analyze data from their communities, and transform what they found into artistic data visualizations. The featured data-art works in this poster spotlight how youth used data to tell stories about community issues they care about and to communicate those stories in creative, personal ways. By bringing together data investigation and artistic expression, this project created a meaningful and community-centered pathway for youth to engage with both data science and the arts.
