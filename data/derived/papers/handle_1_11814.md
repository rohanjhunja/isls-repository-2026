# Collaborative Sensemaking as Social Studies Teachers’ Professional Learning: Adapting to Thematic Instruction

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Educators’ ongoing engagement with questions of epistemology and pedagogy is vital in delineating the repertoire of instructional strategies they will apply in their classrooms. For in-service teachers, opportunities to think with epistemology and pedagogy can come through teacher professional learning (TPL) workshops. There, teachers’ thinking is challenged by innovative instructional strategies, new resources, and conversations with educators from different backgrounds. In the TPL analyzed here, teachers were given the opportunity to apply a novel instructional strategy to their own teaching materials. Participants leveraged their distributed expertise to collaboratively interpret and negotiate prerequisites and core principles of student-driven inquiry necessary to implement this pedagogical strategy with integrity. Using interaction analysis, this study illustrates the principled adaptation of a second-year social studies teacher’s world history unit on ancient civilizations. It is an example of distributed expertise and collaborative sensemaking with implications for the instructional design of TPL.

## Introduction

For educators and young people to reach their potential as active transformative agents, we need to let schools be places where we examine and tackle the complex issues facing society. When teachers facilitate sensemaking on the systemic level  with the instructional method of student-driven inquiry (SdI), students become aware of recurring patterns in the structures that govern their various communitie s. K-12 students are not too young to realize the complexity of different experiences and social studies are the natural home for honing the skills needed to leverage their understanding of issues related to social justice, equity, and sustainability. When they cando SdI into complex social issues  in school, young people are positioned to understand, critically reflect,  and then challenge structural inequity and unsustainable economic practices. S ocial studies teachers are instrumental in creating the learning environments where such complex work can be done. They are not free from the constraints  of formal schooling in their local contexts but of ten have leeway in the specific instructional strategies the y choose to apply —student-driven inquiry can be part of their repertoire. However, it rarely  features other than through research projects at the end of a term. E ducators’ epistemological and pedagogical views, as well as t he academic capabilities they ascribe to their stud ents play a role in how SdI is approached. These views are shaped by teacher preparation  programs, teaching experiences, work within schools’ professional learning communities, and teacher professional learning (TPL) opportunities. In an effort to promote principled adaptation of Sd I, I posed the following research question: How do social studies educators from different backgrounds collaboratively negotiate student-driven inquiry for their local learning contexts? In this analysis, I am reporting  on one episode from a TPL workshop where social studies educators collaboratively made sense of SdI by leveraging distributed expertise in their negotiations. Inquiry in social studies Inquiry is ubiquitous in K-12 education and features prominently in the standards for science and social studies proposed by U.S.-national councils (National Council for the Social Studies  [NCSS], 2010, 2013; NGSS Lead States, 2013) and the International Baccalaureate Organization. In social studies, inquiry deals with human behavior, decision-making, and their impacts on various stakeholder gr oups in the past and present. Teachers of history, government, civics, geography, economics, ethnic studies, sociology, and psychology apply it “to move to deeper levels of understanding” (International Baccalaureate Organization, 2014, p. 16) because inquiry is how young people build critical information-and media literacy that will enable and empower the m to take informed socio-political action within our pluralist democracy. In preparing young people to be “knowledgeable, thinking, and active citizens,” NCSS (2013) promotes an Inquiry Arc where students (1) develop questions and plan inquiries; (2) apply disciplinary concepts and tools; (3) evaluate sources and use ev idence; and (4) communicate conclusions and take informed action. While not a monolith across all social studies disciplines, inquiry generally includes evaluation of primary source materials from multiple perspectives to generate evidence-based arguments. What it often does not include in reality, however,  is the student agency called for in this Inquiry A rc: NCSS’s recommendation is not universally implemented  because it is perceived as not fitting in with constraints such as curricular content mandates, testing regimes, and teachers’ views of pedagogies that best support the first two (Schamberger, 2025). Because there is a premium placed on content delivery and assessment, most so-called inquiries that occur during the school year in K-12 social studies are not student-driven. Social studies teachers don’t feel like they have the time to dedicate to the kind of student-driven inquiry (SdI) the Inquiry Arc asks for while assessments are still to be had.  Hence, inquiry that centers student interest and applies critical literacy to real-world civic engagement is often relegated to low-stakes end-of-term research projects. With the TPL workshop here, I sought to generate synergies to support educators in using SdI year-round. SdI is not a free for all, it is students’ guided engagement with multiple cases of a specific topic where teachers set a corridor through which inquiry is scaffolded: Adapted from Papert (1980) and Resnick et al. (2009), SdI’s choice occurs in a space with  an elevated floor that may require an on-ramp (e.g., building up  research skills), and somewhat narrow walls with sign-posting by way of research scaffolds to guide the students along a specific trajectory. But it should have a high ceiling to invite student s’ creativity and pursuit of own interests in generating a variety of possible output products to  share their findings with peers.  Even when the topic may not be the main object of interest for rigorous research, the outcome product can be —for example, when students want to build an accurate scenario in Minecraft, build a diorama, illustrate a comic, or narrate a mini-documentary.

## Theoretical framework for teacher professional learning

For teachers to integrate student-driven inquiry (SdI) into their regular instruction, they need to see pedagogical value in it and understand how they can fit it with the academic requirements of the courses offered at their specific schools. Appreciation for SdI is a combination of epistemolo gical and pedagogical views, as well as teaching experiences within their local contexts, situated within the particular infrastructures of formal schooling (Hopkins & Woulfin, 2015; Penuel, 2019). It comes through teacher professional learning (TPL) that is not just a collection of isolated experiences. Over the course of a teacher’s career, expertise ac cumulates from different TPL experiences, including initial teacher preparation,  classroom practice, work with department colleague s, workshops, and independent study of pedagogical and  content resources. Every TPL opportunity is situat ed in a specific local learning environment and interacts with participants’ existing knowledge (Brown et al., 1993; Cole & Engeström, 1993; Lave, 1988; Lave & Wenger, 1991). Teachers’ s kills grow over time as they build on distributed expertise in TPL (cf. Brown et al., 1993; Cole & Engeström, 1993; Pea, 1993; Ramey & Uttal, 2017). According to Ramey and Uttal’s (2017) framing of distributed exp ertise (Brown & Campione, 1996), the material, social, and activity contexts impact what participants take from TPL workshops. The material context of TPL includes visuospatial representations like d iagrams or sketches as well as physical artifacts, such as laptops, whiteboards, a printed booklet containing the state social studies standards with personal an notations, existing unit plans, or lesson templates teachers can think with. The social context of TPL is created by the specific availability and contributions of other people. Whe n educators work together to use talk and gesture in their interpretation of a pedagogical strategy and design of instructional materials, they create a social system in which their sensemaking is influenced by the knowledge, skil ls, and repertoires of practice they each bring  (see also Stevens, 2000). The activity context of TPL encompasses the types of tasks in which the educators engage. In this study, social studies teachers made sense of a demonstration unit of SdI by talking through how they might implement it in their own instructional contexts. From there, educators’ main task was collaboratively adapting their own existing units to become more Sd I. In this proces s, the teachers negotiated what principled adaptation of SdI could look like to keep the integ rity of the instructional strategy in their individual context without turning planning for SdI into a checklist approach (cf. DeBar ger et al., 2017; Penuel et al., 2007, 2023; Penuel & Gallagher, 2009). The case shared here is an example of more experienced educators guiding a novice’s sensemaking (Elby, 2016).

## Methods

The design-based research study (Barab & Squire, 2004; Design-Based Research Collective, 2003; McKenney & Reeves, 2018) these data are drawn from comprises of two iterations of a TPL workshop to introduce student-driven inquiry (SdI) as a viable  year-round instructional strategy for secondary social studies. Participants were asked to examine what principled adaptation of SdI might look like in their own classrooms. In each iteration, the workshop included a form of modeling the strategy w ith an example SdI about election engineering, a decomposition of the strategy’s main features (its design principles), and time to collaboratively tur n both the demonstration unit as well as participants’ pre-existing units into SdI that would work in their sp ecific local contexts. In the process of making sense of SdI and  collaboratively negotiating what principled adaptation  of it would look like in their particular contexts (Penuel et al., 2007; Penuel & Gallagher, 2009), the educators surfaced barriers and supports for this kind of instruction.

Data for  the overall study comprise of  whole-and small-group conversations in the video-recorded workshop sessions; video-recorded, individual pre-and post-interviews; individual written reflections; and the lessons the educators created for the ir adapted units. The analysis presented here draws on the video-recorded conversations collected in the first workshop.  This iteration included two social studies classroom teachers, an instructional coach, and myself. The different expe riences and pedagogical knowledge we each brought t o the workshop were explicitly desired as socially distri buted expertise (cf. Ramey & Uttal, 2017) that woul d enable distributed collaborative sensemaking. High school history teacher Frank was only one year out of his teacher preparation program and in the process of seeking an MA part-time. As a second-year teacher, Frank was very up-to-date with what universities present as effective teaching practices and had a firm grasp on the new textbook his rural school had purchased. Rachel was an instructional coach at the generalist-taught intermediate level (grades 5 and 6) and came to the workshop with a MA  in Teaching (MAT) with endorsements for s ocial studies and middle school science, as w ell as 10+ years of experience teaching and coaching middle school teachers in one of Iowa’s largest districts. Additional assets for  Rachel lay in having received advanced TPL, engaging in independent study of instructional strategies (she brought a book on visible learning [Hattie et al., 2020]  to the workshop), and having provided various forms of TPL to her colleagues. All this positioned her as an expert in instructional design. As workshop leader with a public agenda of supporting sensemaking that would promote SdI, I was actively involved in the conversation. My 10+ years of experience teaching with inquiry in different school contexts, as well as the advanced degrees and university course work in social studies education positioned me as expert as well. 12 years prior to this study, Rachel and I had presented a bid for conceptual social studies teaching at a national practitioner conference  during the course of our joint MAT program (reference omitted to preserve Rachel’s anonymity). The second teacher, Mira, was an experienced middle school teacher who, with the exception of a brief interlude,  chose to operate as a “sponge” and preferred to listen as  the conversation analyzed here took place.

For the episode here, I applied interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995; Hal l & Stevens, 2015) to a longer conversation between Rachel, Frank, and myself. This method focuses on how knowledge and attitudes are used in and changed through verbal and nonverbal embodied interactions. It surfaces how teachers’ interestsand knowledge-in-use were enacted in micro moments of direct communication with other educators as well as interactions with the material and activity contexts (Ramey & Uttal, 2017) that comprised this TPL workshop’s learning environment. The analysis below focuses on Frank’s evolving interpretation of SdI, curricular adaptations available for it, and how Rachel and I guided this sensemaking (Elby, 2016). Distributed expertise in negotiating foundations for student-driven inquiry Over the course of 90 minutes, second-year teacher F rank reimagined an integral part of his  year-long world history course by moving from transmitting mainly textbook-selected content from the Stone Age to the present (or at least through the Cold War) chronologically to inserting a thematic approach that would allow some of the content to be covered thematically (e.g., Metro, 2020) and through inquiry. These 90 minutes included interludes about grading, the state’s social studies inquiry a nd content standards, teaching about issues in agri culture and ‘both-sidesism,’ as well as teaching through student-generated claims and multiple counterclaims. They were all tangentially related to the kind of thematic teaching Frank was coming to terms with, but are not foci here. At the start of this episode, Frank shared that he followed the newly-acquired (and expensive) textbook’s approach in sequentially teaching about ancient civilizations in Mesopotamia, Egypt (and Judaism as a parallel development), Rome, Greek city states, Chinese dynasties, and pre-colonial India. His assessments were usually 15 multiple-choice questions about facts he had previously tran smitted and 1-2 short-response questions to describe or explain an event in more detail. With that kind of a content and assessment crunch, he mainly delivered factual information drawn from the textbook to his students and felt he had no room for any American and African civilizations in the syllabus. Upon hearing that, Rachel asked a pertinent question: “How important d o you think it is for your  students to know each of these civilizations, like know them and their story?” Beyond referring to the unit test, Frank did not have an answer to this  question. With both depth and breadth in mind, I s uggested letting students work on concept-based inquiry of civilizations and have them share their findings with peers. From my experience, I knew that letting students se lect a civilization in a well-scaffolded inquiry could get learners’ buy-in to doing substantial research work  on their own. It would  also increase the amount of overall content coverage, increase learners’ understanding of development patterns in the rise and fall of civ ilizations, and do all of that in the same or less instructional time than the previous sequential teacher-led transmission. To support Frank’s instruction, Rachel suggested teaching about the rise and fall of civilizations by using a model arc that would scaffold student inquiry. It captures the recurring pattern of stages societies tend to go through (see Figure 1, left image) and it is thus applicable to different groups at different times and in differen t geographical spaces.

Teacher professional learning as distributed collaborative sensemaking The distributed expertise at play here factored int o Frank’s sensemaking of the instructional strategy and how he envisioned SdI’s pedagogical implementation in the local learning environment of his sophomore world history course. In order for Frank to  make his instruction more inquiry-focused, he needed to depart from what he had been taught in his own apprenticeship of observation (Lortie, 2002) in high school and university history classes: He loosened his insistence on chronology and considered the merit of thematic instruction focused on patterns. The expertise illustrated in this episode was distributed across the activity, material, and social contexts (Ramey & Uttal, 2017). The activity context was deliberately designed to provide time for participants tonegotiate adaptations of SdI. Over the course of several workshop hours, Frank was able to adapt some of his own units together with Rachel, Mira, and myself. In this pro cess, he also examined other alternatives to transmission and other constraints placed on his instruction through his school’s specific expectations. The material context of the physical space we were in also supported distribution of expertise: The use of the whiteboard to draw a diagram in real time allowed Rachel to express her conceptu al understanding and offer it up for other particip ants’ engagement. In talking through the creation of it, Rachel was not the sole author of the diagram that Frank came to use in his unit design. The social context of th is TPL workshop was its main strength. It brought  together teachers who were interested in more inquiry-based instruction, but unsure how to do it. It had seasoned educators who appreciate inquiry as a highly effective way to  learn and teach (Rachel and I) and novices in terms of using inquiry-based instruction year-round (Frank and Mira). Together, we functioned as active sounding board s in negotiating the principled adaptation of SdI that was emerging for Frank’s unit on civilizations. Teacher-led transmission versus student-led inquiry Frank’s administrator had expressed a desire to make social studies more inquiry-based, which emboldened Frank to look for specific TPL. With limited preparation for it through his licensure program and the content crunch he felt in world history, he had resorted to transmission through lecture in his first year of teaching. In his deliberate move towards inquiry, Frank was able to lean into Rachel’s and my expertise as he generated his own corridor in which SdI could happen for a unit on civilizations: First, he would model the stages of civilizations’ rise and fall (see Figure 1, left image), using the examples of ancient Rome and Greece. Those two contain the content Frank felt all students must get for appreciating civic develo pments in the U.S.  centuries later.  M ore importantly, however, this modeling sets parameters for students to use the diagram to structure their own examinations of other civilizations across places and time. The diagram helps to scaffold (e.g., Puntambekar & Hübscher, 2005; Reiser, 2004)  what they should look for without prescribing where they can find this information. Instead of hearing an authoritative lecture about six ancient civilizations, students now see two as exemplars of  the development pattern, learn deeply about one civiliz ation by researching it, and hear about 10-20 more from their peers—depending on class size and whether or not Frank makes this inquiry partner work. An additional perk of teaching with SdI and assessi ng for application rather than factual recall is th at it reduces the teacher’s workload for preparation of l ectures and grading. Rachel was looking to manage s tress by saying “Let’s take some things off yo ur plate. Let’s prioritize what’s most important, g ive you some tools ((pointing at the diagram)) that you can have kids do it so that you can say ‘yeah, that’s enough.’” I nstead of transmitting facts and then assessing frequently fo r content, Rachel promo ted letting students do the lifting and then assess their understanding through a specific application of the model that we generated here. Once they get over the surprise of “wow, [the teacher is] not goi ng to stand in front of us today to lecture” (Rache l was exaggerating a common instructional approach), students appreciate even limited ownership over their education that comes from choosing a civilization, interpreting the model, and sharing their work with their peers. This can create deeper learning a nd buy-in for rigorous study. The skills needed for this k ind of SdI prepare students for critical understanding and transformative involvement in their local, regional, and global communities. The unit Frank was planning did not have all the fe atures that I had introduced for designing SdI into complex social issues, but Frank and Rachel’s joint  negotiation was a form of principled adaptation in  how it allowed Frank to set a corridor for students to ope rate in. Through the distributed expertise at work here, Frank was able to do important groundwork for SdI. By neg otiating thematic instruction with Rachel, myself, and his existing views of teaching and learning, Frank bega n to see history educati on in a new light that allowed him to frame it as inquiry within a corridor (cf. Papert, 1980; Resnick et al., 2009): it has an elevated floor (research skills), a moderately high ceiling (ways of sharing student findings), and somewhat narrow walls with sign-posting in the form of the model’s stages to guide the stud ents along a specific inquiry trajectory. At one po int in this conversation, I called the generation of the model and Frank’s willingness to use it to set a corridor  for his sophomores an adaptation that can be “a gateway dru g” for moving into more independent SdI —for Frank’s students as well as the design of additional units in world history and other courses he taught.

## Conclusion and future directions

The dynamic that developed between the four educato rs in this TPL workshop was not easily replicated w ith a larger group of different educators in the second iteration. The analysis here led me to retain the design principles of bringing together distributed expertise, promoti ng distributed collaborative sensemaking, and encou raging negotiation of principled adaptations in the second  workshop and the teachers there also generated ver sions SdI to apply to their own units (see Schamberger, 2025).

To better understand and design for effective TPL i n favor of conceptual instruction  and principles adaptations of SdI, teachers’ epistemological and p edagogical views need to be examined. History educa tors are not historians and, due to the infrastructures of s chooling they operate within, often view history as  a series of events in chronological sequence. Their job then is to transmit an authoritative narrative to get students ‘the facts’ needed to succeed in tests. This is what they have experienced in their own K-16 schooling, see from colleagues, or are told by publishers of teaching materials.  Yet, if teachers think learning happens as (passive) rece ption of information, their pedagogical choices will center on authoritative lecture to transmit it. In such a framing, young people are cast as less capable learners whoneed to receive and memorize factual knowledge because they cannot be trusted to make sense of complex scenarios. Expo sure to student-centered epistemology and corresponding pedagogy can he lp build an asset-based view of learners. From a reframing of inquiry  and the student-centered approaches to learning and teaching that Rachel and I brought to the workshop, Frank could tap into the expertise distributed across the social, material, and ac tivity contexts of the workshop. When educators neg otiate epistemological and pedagogical foundations together through practical applications, their sensemaking is imbued with authority that offers a higher chance of individual implementation beyond the TPL. Designers of TPL may wish to consider the necessary activity, material, and social contexts needed to facilitate such paradigm shifts. The collaborative sensemaking of SdI and its adaptation to Frank’s specific context also surfaced barriers to implementation of SdI that require further study of the infrastructures of schooling (Penuel, 2019; Penuel et al.,

2023) and their effects on social studies education.  It also has implications for instructional coaching  and

curriculum design.  Throughout the workshop,  perceived rigidity of curricular mandates,  chronology as main organizing principle of history courses, content-focused standards and testing regimes, grading practices, and state policies recurred as potential barriers to using SdI during the schoo l year rather than only as an end-of-year lowstakes research project.  TPL is only one cog needed to position SdI as effec tive learning. Other cogs and gears necessary for sustained implementation of SdI at sc ale need to be addressed through long-term infrastructuring. The system is very stable and subject to inertia (Becker, 1995). Any design-based implementation of SdI requires sustained research-practice partnerships for TPL and changes within the broader educational system. Frank’s story is clearly not the end of the line, it is a beginning.
