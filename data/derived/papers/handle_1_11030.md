# More-than-human Empathizing for Science Identity, Ethical Accountability and Solidarity

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Here, we examine empathizing as a sociocultural process that promotes science identity development. In particular, we attend to how students learn about themselves and their science by empathizing with nonhuman others. We find that empathizing often looks very difficult, uncomfortable and risky, and that it is likely an essential process in learning environments that promotes dignity-affirming scientific solidarities.

We add to research that theorizes the mutually constitutive nature of processes of identity, emotion, and reasoning in STEM learning. Specifically, we examine processes of empathizing (e.g., Batson, 2009) —with humans and non-humans alike—in a science classroom, and how they support collective identity work (e.g., Azevedo & Mann, 2022). We are interested in empathizing as a sociocultural process in which one positions  oneself in relation to another to think and feel from the others’ perspectives (Batson, 2009). Our analysis surfaces little-studied aspects of the relationship between identity, emotions, and learning, contribut ing to the design of dignity-affirming learning environments  (Espinoza et al., 2020). We borrow from sociocultural and sociopolitical theories of learning, framing identity as lenses through which one positions oneself in social interaction and through which other practice participants position such a person relative to socially and culturally available categories (Varelas et al., 2012). Identity work is the situated and interactional process in which a person deploys these positional lenses to author themselves and how they are reflexively authored by others (Azevedo & Mann, 2022).

## Empirical context and methods

Data for this study were drawn from three iterations of a larger, ongoing design-based research project (Barab & Squire, 2004) on a semester-long course taught by the first author on the broader social and ethical impacts and consequentiality of science. O ur data corpus included over 48 whole-class forum discussions, over 66 hours of recorded video of Zoom and in-class discussions, three iterative drafts of “ethical issue investigation” papers per student, and the readings and resources students assembled and cited to frame their issue investigations. The first author–a participant observer and instructor in the course –used field notes to flag online and face-to-face classroom discussions that were relatively rich in discursive interaction. Authors independently logged video and audio data (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) for these cases, and reviewed related forum discussions and student writings. Comparing and discussing logs and notes, the authors refined a shared analytical focus on how empathizing supports science identity work, and focused on cases that revealed these processes of empathizing. Here, we present one case to illustrate our analysis. The names of all humans and nonhumans in our analysis are pseudonyms. The case focuses on a class discussion prompted by an undergraduate student named Viraj about whether urban environments contain “significant nature” for science learning. This is something about which Varaj was initially skeptical, and here we pick up the discussion at the moment when he acknowledge s knowing many environmental science students who study an urban creek near campus called Danner Creek. I n this laughter-filled exchange, students found humor in positioning Danner Creek as “significant nature.” They construct the creek as “so gross” (line 14) and “disgusting” (line 13) that one must “wear gloves” (line 17) to work with the creek, and yet the students also communicate a shared familiarity and affection for the creek, referring to it as “Good Old Danner.” We interpret that this friendly way of referring to the creek is funny to the students at least in part because it highlights an irony in their feelings of affection for the dirty, damaged, seemingly insignificant creek, a nonhuman friend. They collectively recognize and grapple with this irony, in part, through laughter. Good Old Danner Creek—here loosely anthropomorphized as something like a mutual friend-in-need— motivates and mediates a shared emotional process of reasoning about the value of the creek, helping students identify and articulate a shared relation and scientific accountability to it. Erin ultimately posits that the creek is not just worthy of scientific attention but that “it needs our attention” (lines 22 and 24) in order “to fix it” (line 26). Through this process, not only do the students articulate a position of care for the creek as salient to scientific identity and practice, but April even links this kind of care work with a specific scientific discipline: “You could be an urban hydrologist” (line 27).

## Significance

Across cases, our current analysis suggests that empathy– much like conceptual change –may be very difficult to achieve. Students may find empathizing uncomfortable and risky, for instance, when it invites a repositioning or transgression of social boundaries. Such empathizing may demand consideration of new and ethically urgent accountabilities and solidarities, for instance, between humans and creeks. We also see it is a process that is undertaken through interactions between students, as they deploy different reasoning strategies like sympathizing and anthropomorphizing to reason as if  and about human and nonhuman others. In the single case we have examined, we see how this collective process helps students work out their accountabilities and roles as scientists, and weigh issues of the value and worth of others that are consequential to how they understand their scientific community and practice. We argue that such emotional processes of empathizing are critical to STEM learning environments that affirm the dignity of diverse others, both human and nonhuman. Azevedo, F. S., & Mann, M. J.
