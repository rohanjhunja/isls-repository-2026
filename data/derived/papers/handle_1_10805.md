## Introduction

Online courses continue to be popular offerings for  students but concerns about student success within  those courses persist. One identified challenge to learni ng in an online environment is the difficulty of re plicating effective human interactions. Interaction and social presence are two theoretical perspectives that researchers have focused on related to learning outcomes and student engagement (Hostetter & Busch, 2006; Richardson & Swan, 2003). Social presence is defined in various ways but is o ften used to understand how people socially interac t in online learning environments (Whiteside et al., 201 7). A significant result of social presence is that it in creases the level of interaction in an online learning environment (Tu & McIsaac, 2002). We agree with Sung and Mayer

(2012) that fostering online social presence will l ead to increased social interaction, and therefore to improved

student outcomes.  However, we also believe that online learning "requ ires the reconstruction of student and instructor roles, relations, and practices" (Vonder well, 2004, p. 31). One example of this reconstruct ion is to reimagine the peer instructor role. Teaching assistants, specifically undergraduate teaching assistants (UTAs), can be taught to increase their social presence and facilitate im portant student interactions in the online learning environment.

Using a qualitative approach, we examined reflectio ns from undergraduate online teaching assistants to understand how they create social presence that leads to interpersonal interactions with students in the onl ine classes they support. This research was guided by the following research questions:

1. What practices are online UTAs using to create social presence in their online courses?

2. What type of interactions are UTAs having to support students in their online courses?

## Literature review

The Community of Inquiry framework (Garrison et al., 1999) states that an effective online community should have cognitive presence, social presence, and teaching  presence. Social presence was originally defined as the sense that a person is “real” and “there” in a communication medium (Short et al., 1976). A simplified definition of social presence is the measure of the feeling of community (Tu & McIsaac, 2002). Substantial social presence can improve student learning (Shin, 2002) and influence completion (Kim, 2012). Education is, at its core, essentially interactions  between content, students, and teachers (Garrison & Shale, 1990). Three of the most common forms of int eraction in online learning are student-student, student-teacher, and student-content (Moore, 1989). Teacher-student interactions positively influence learning (Oyarzun et al., 2018) and increased faculty and student satisfaction (Cole et al., 2014). Online education should be designed to facilitate targeted, intentional, and engaging i nteractions (Abrami et al., 2011), but how is this accomplished when these interactions come at such a high financial and time cost (Anderson, 2004)? Expanding the role of teaching assistants in online courses is one way to address that concern and maintain and improve interactivity. UTAs play an increasingly important role in higher education (HE) institutions and in improving student outcomes (Dempster & Dempster, 2019; Hite et al., 2 021). We believe that online UTAs can be taught to create social presence and to be proactive in offering the type of support that facilitates student interactions and promotes student success.  To effectively teach  peer educators on how to improve their interpersona l interactions, it is important to understand what the research reports are the best practices for creating and improving social presence in online environments. Abrami et al. (2011) wrote that the next generation of online education should be designed to facilitate and promote more purposeful interaction. We believe that undergraduate teaching assistants can be a part of the next generation of online educators who support those high-quality interactions.

## Research methodology

This exploratory case study deepens our understandi ng of how UTAs in online university courses can support students by using research-based interaction practices to establish a social p resence that leads to purposeful interpersonal student interactions. Case study can produce a rich and detailed account of a phenomenon  in reallife situations (Merriam, 1998). The participants of this study were 13 undergraduat e students from a large US university employed as UTAs assigned to an online university course. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with all 13 participants.  Interviews were transcribed, and the researchers an alyzed each case record to identify common themes using thematic analysis related to the research questions. Trustworthiness activities included member checking, peer debriefing, and negative case  analysis. Limiting factors, include a small sample size that may not fully represent the experience of the other UTA’s in the program. In addition, the UTA experiences were self-reported and not verified through researcher observation or student surveys.

## Results and discussion

Data analysis identified ways in which these UTAs were using some of the best practices, identified in research, to promote student interaction, including (a) establishing early connection, (b) affective communication, (c) selfdisclosure, and (d) proactivity. Establishing early connection Gunawardena (1995) believed social presence could be created or cultivated. Whiteside et al. (2017) emphasized the importance of doing so early in the process. We explored how UTAs were establishing a connection with their students, so we asked them how they introduced themselves. All but one of the interviewees mentioned that they supplemented the posting of a short bio with a personalized email sent to participants earl y in the semester. Participants shared the most important message they wanted to convey in that introductory email. “I would say that I want them to know that I want them to be successful, that they can have fun in this class, and that I'm here to help them in any way.” “Try to emphasize like they can really just come to us for anything or like any help. Like we're there. I mean, literally, our purpose is like to he lp them succeed in the class. So we try to encourage them all to reach out to us whenever they need anything or have any questions.” “Just that I am here. I'm a real person, and I do want to help you.” These UTAs seemed to innately understand what research reports as the primary reason for establishing a social presence, to communicate that there is a “ real person” behind that mediated communication (Gunawardena, 1995 p. 151). Many other researchers understand the importance of creating this realness (Kreijns et al., 2022; Short et al., 1976). This realness can combat feelings of loneliness and isolation, sometimes prevalent in online education, and help create a sense of community. Affective communication Initial research done on using computer-mediated communication (CMC) suggested that it did not have the capacity to support social and affective interaction (Short et al., 1976). Subsequent studies found CMC to, in fact, support highly affective interpersonal interactions  when used in educational settings  (Rourke et al., 1999). Affective features of online social presence are re lated to social respect, social sharing, open-mindedness, social identity, and intimacy (Sung & Mayer, 2012). These are seen in personal expressions of emotion, feelings, beliefs, and values and sharing personal stories, humor, and  information. We asked our UTAs to share an experience where they were able to help a student, and we saw examples of them offering different types of affective support. “Sometimes I send little like memes or something th at might be relatable, but like you know, keep moving forward. They need to know that they can connect with me on a personal basis.” “I always whenever I did have a student join my office hours.. I would always try and be their biggest cheerleader of you cando this.” "I mean, this is just like a little thing, but ever y time a student emails me with a question, I always like to respond and start by saying, like, thanks for reaching out; that's a great question. And then, like, I hope you have a great day at the end of the e-mail or something just to personalize it a little bit and to make them feel like I'm like someone who's there for them.” Self-disclosure Learners feel more comfortable when they believe th e instructor shares a kinship, common beliefs, and values (Sung & Mayer, 2012). This self-disclosure is understood as “conscious and deliberate disclosures about oneself, aspects of one’s professional practice, world or pe rsonal views, personal history, and responses to on going classroom events” (Rasmussen & Mishna, 2008, p. 192). UTAs are often closely connected to online students by age, experience, and a nearness to the content  and have experienced many of the same challenges and successes. Our data included examples of UTA’s connecting with students by sharing this common experience. “I think the thing that I tried to do the most is t o help my students feel like they belonged or were heard or seen. In my messages to the entire course, I would typically say something along the lines of this paper is due and kind of like som e logistical things. And then I would acknowledge I'd say, I know from personal experience that this is stressful. Like I've taken this course, I know how time-intensive it can be, and so if you're feeling that way like you aren't alone.” Proactivity Studies have shown that quality interpersonal interaction leads to better outcomes, but also of note are studies that show that a lack of them can be detrimental (Cole et al., 2014;). In an increasingly electronically connected world, students are often more disconnected from others, a nd this “crisis of connection” (Cohan, 2017, p. 1) has been prominent in HE’s, with many institutions addressing the ‘loneliness  epidemic’ (Clay, 2018). In online learning, this perceived loneliness can be exacerbated (Ali &  Smith, 2015).  Stakeholders in this online program believe online communities must be f ostered to be sustainable and successful (Palloff & Pratt, 200 7) and that loneliness can be mitigated by being proactive in communicating with students.   The UTAs described several instances where they showed evidence of proactivity, such as: “I think naturally I try to be the one to reach out to them first.” “I think that's like a big step. Like, if you take that first step, people aren’t so uncomfortable to approach you.” “Just saying throughout the semester, like if you n eed help on this specific thing or this paper that's coming up or even especially as we are getti ng towards the end of the semester saying, hey, if you're missing late assignments, come talk to me and we'll get something worked out. And I had a lot of students reach out and say, hey,  you said this in your message to the entire class, so I'm taking you up on that offer, and that worked out really well.” Job satisfaction We believe there are many things online instructors  can learn from the experience and actions of these  online UTAs. One of the most useful may be about what the UTAs li ke about their job.  Long-standing research has shown a positive correlation between job satisfacti on and job performance (Judge et al., 2001), and ma ny of the UTAs interviewed expressed that satisfaction openly, saying: “Oh, all of it, there's so much to say. I think num ber one, I love what it is, right? It's working as a teaching assistant to an awesome professor and he lping students in the class. I just love that kind of interaction with students and dealing with those kinds of relationships.” “I love my students, and I love it when they say thank you, and I love it when we find a solution that works for them. I love to see them as they're improving or they're getting used to the cadence of the course, and I really like seeing them catch up. I love seeing them learn. It just literally gets me so excited.”

## Conclusion

Creating social presence can and should be fostered  by multiple creative strategies (Billings & Halstead, 2009). We see evidence that UTAs can proactively utilize m ultiple techniques that help students be successful in online learning and can be better utilized to decrease onl ine student learning deficiencies. These techniques include establishing early connections, using affective com munication such as personalization, and expressing emotions such as encouragement, humor, and compassion. One o f the most ef fective ways the UTAs created interaction with other students was to seek and create quality communication proactively.
