# Exploring Multidimensional Thinking in a Community of Philosophical Inquiry for AI Ethics Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study developed a digital tabletop role-playing game (TRPG), The Gray Area, for AI ethics education and analyzed the patterns of players’ multidimensional thinking. Based on the Community of Philosophical Inquiry (CPI), the game utilizes the topic of sex robots to promote multidimensional thinking by providing inquiry questions that explore ethical issues. The analysis of in-game dialogues of 20 university students showed that players used creative and caring thinking to support their critical reasoning. Specifically, they engaged in creative thinking by imagining the advantages and problems of AI, and caring thinking by considering the perspectives of various stakeholders. Furthermore, post-game surveys and interviews revealed high player engagement and perceived educational value. Overall, this study demonstrates the roles of creative and caring thinking in the process of critical judgment on AI ethical issues and highlights the potential of The Gray Area as a pedagogical method for AI ethics education.

While Artificial Intelligence (AI) is rapidly used in our society, the misuse of AI has created an “ethical gap,” in which technological development outpaces ethical considerations (Jonas, 1994; Li & Wan, 2023). Furthermore, artificial general intelligence (AGI) raises ontological questions about human identity (Coeckelbergh, 2023). To live alongside AI desirably, it is therefore essential to develop the ability to engage in ethical reasoning of AIrelated issues (Heo et al., 2020). Previous research on AI ethics education has mainly focused on the core concepts in ethical principles, such as safety, fairness, and responsibility. For example, students are asked questions such as “What does fairness mean? ” or “Who should be held responsible if a self-driving car causes an accident?” to stimulate ethical thinking (Kim & Byun, 2021). W hile discussions around AI ethical dilemmas are suggested  as teaching methods (Hong & Oh, 2024; Choi et al., 2024), most studies have focused on quantitatively measuring learners’ awareness or attitudes toward AI ethics. This limits a deeper understanding of how learners understand and reason about the complexity of AI ethics issues.

To address this gap, this study focuses on examining learners’ ethical thinking in group dialogue. AI ethical issues are complex dilemmas, characterized by value conflicts among multiple stakeholders and the uncertainty of future consequences  (Deshpande & Sharp, 2022; Sanderson et al., 2024). Addressing such issues requires more than merely applying established principles. It necessitates critical thinking to analyze arguments, creative thinking to imagine potential outcomes, and caring thinking to consider the perspectives of those affected (LaChat, 1986; Undheim et al., 2023). Therefore, this study aims to explore the patterns of this multidimensional thinking as they emerge in learners’ dialogue about AI ethics. To promote multidimensional thinking, Lipman (2003) suggested ‘ the community of philosophical inquiry (CPI)’ as an educational method. It aims to provide a space where learners candiscuss and think deeply to make reasonable judgments. CPI has been used in ethics education with various materials such as films and novels to facilitate conversation among participants (Choi, 2009). In this research, we designed a digital tabletop role-playing game (TRPG), The Gray Area, based on the procedures of CPI to enable players to discuss AI ethics issues. We selected “sex robots” as the theme of the TRPG with questions that enable players to explore various ethical problems of the emb odied AI and the relationship between humans and AI robots. The study aims to analyze the aspects of multidimensional thinking in the players’ dialogue. The research questions that guided the present study are as follows: RQ1.  What patterns of multidimensional thinking emerge when players engage in the CPI on AI ethics issues in the TRPG context?

RQ2.  How did players perceive their experience of the CPI in the TRPG context?

## Theoretical framework

Multidimensional thinking in ethics 869 Ethical reasoning and judgment are multidimensional thinking processes that include critical, creative, and caring thinking (Lipman, 2003; Noh, 2007). As a framework for analyzing participants’ ethical thinking, this study applies Lipman’s (2003) concept of multidimensional thinking, which refers to the integration of critical, creative, and caring thinking to make reasonable judgments. The first dimension, critical thinking, facilitates judgment by relying on clear criteria, self-correction, and sensitivity to context (Lipman, 2003). Reliance on criteria means that thinking is grounded in reasons, such as rules or principles, when making a judgment. Self-correction involves acknowledging the errors of one ’s or others ’ thinking and continuously reflecting upon and improving them. Sensitivity to context is to recognize that an idea that is valid in one context may not be in another, and vice versa. The process of making ethical judgments is not achieved by simply employing the critical dimension of thinking alone. It also requires creative thinking, which goes beyond the given information, characterized by imagination, inventiveness, and originality (Lipman, 2003). In ethics, this imaginative capacity is employed to make judgments by considering situations from the perspectives of others and envisioning the possible outcomes of a specific future (Johnson, 1997). Caring thinking is also deeply related to ethical judgments. It is defined as thinking that focuses on what is respected and valued (Lipman, 2003). Caring thinking involves putting ourselves into another person’s situation to experience their emotions and perspective as if they were our own (Splitter  & Sharp, 1995). Therefore, the integration of this imaginative exploration and empathic concern with critical reasoning is essential for making reasonable judgments in ethics. Community of philosophical inquiry for AI ethics education AI ethics involves questioning what roles AI can play and how it might impact human lives, and how we perceive ourselves as human beings (Tasioulas, 2022). In this study, we created an environment in which participants could reflect deeply on and discuss AI ethical issues within  the community of philosophical inquiry (CPI), an educational method proposed by Lipman (2003) to foster philosophical and ethical thinking. CPI, as proposed by Lipman (2003), consists of the following procedures, summarized in Figure 1. First, participants read a ‘stimulus for thinking’ in a narrative format. This material can take various forms, such as stories, news, or films that stimulate learners’ thoughts and curiosity (Noh, 2007).  Second, participants raise questions for discussion based on what they have read. The stimulus, often designed to induce cognitive dissonance, naturally sparks curiosity to resolve the dissonance and encourages questioning (Opdal, 2001). It opens up the possibility for dialogue on philosophical problems.  The third step is “ Solidifying the community, ” where learners engage in dialogical inquiry about the questions. They help each other draw out reasoning by asking questions to understand others ’ arguments or by challenging claims that seem invalid. Fourth, participants discuss additional inquiry questions presented through materials or by the instructor. This is a process of extending and supplementing topics that do not naturally emerge from the participants ’ conversation. In the concluding stage, participants synthesize and reflect on the inquiry content through cognitive activities that can take various forms, such as artistic expression, writing, or role-playing.

## The Procedure of CPI (Lipman, 2003)

Tabletop role-playing game for promoting dialogue CPI requires a comfortable environment where all participants can converse as equals, using various stimuli to spark curiosity and foster engagement in dialogue (Splitter & Sharp, 1995). To create such an environment, this study designed a TRPG that contains the procedures of CPI. A TRPG is a game in which players assume the roles of fictional characters and collaboratively build a narrative by imagining and describing their characters ’ actions (Fine, 1983). The use of roles within a fictional context can alleviate anxieties about being judged or failing to meet others’ expectations (Daniau, 2016). Moreover, dialogic play encourages players to construct new meanings through interaction  and to better understand  others’ perspectives (Gaydos & Nardo, 2022). Consequently, previous studies have successfully utilized TRPGs in computer and AI ethics education to enhance learners ’ communication and deepen their understanding (Ali et al., 2023; Zdravkova, 2014). This study, thus, positions TRPG as a medium for providing a safe and immersive space for promoting philosophical and ethical dialogue. Game design Game setting 870 The digital game, The Gray Area, integrates the procedures of CPI with the game elements of TRPGs. Players play in groups of four, sharing a single device. The theme of this game, from various AI ethics issue s, was the question of whether to permit the development and use of “ sex robots”, which are defined as an artificial entity that resembles a human in appearance and behavior, embedded with a certain level of AI, used for sexual purposes (Danaher, 2018). Issues related to sex robots are actively discussed as a significant ethical challenge (Kim, 2023). We selected sex robots as the main theme of the game because this type of AI technology can be encountered in the private and intimate spheres of human life, with  the potential to profoundly impact personal values and human-AI relationships (Koo, 2024). Game phases Phase 1: Reading the narrative stimulus In the first phase, players come to understand the game ’s world-setting and identify the goal. Following the procedure of CPI, the game provides a fictional future story where the development of male and female-form sex robots, “Partner 01” and “Partner 02” from Company A, has just begun. Players assume the roles of stakeholders who attend a meeting and reach a consensus on the issue: “Should the development and use of sex robots be nationally permitted?” Phase 2: Making questions In this phase, players briefly state their initial positions on the sex robot issue. They then have time to pose their own questions that they wish to discuss and engage in a period of free conversation. Phase 3: Building the community & discussing further inquiry questions After the initial question-and-answer session, players take turns choosing random ‘D iscussion subjects.’ Each contains a question for further debates on the issues surrounding sex robots. The questions are based on the ethical 871 issues discussed in the prior studies (see Table 3) and the five principles of the AI ethics framework proposed by Floridi & Cowls (2022): beneficence, nonmaleficence, autonomy, justice, and explicability. Additionally, we included posthumanism as a topic of discussion, drawing on related studies (Levy, 2007; Nath & Manna, 2023). Once the players reach consensus on a posed question, the game presents a branching narrative that incorporates a counter-perspective contingent on their choice. Players  are then required to answer the same question again, possibly taking a new viewpoint into account.  We call this element “flipping” (Wang et al., 2025)  as it enables players to explore diverse perspectives and potential value-trade-offs before deciding on a posed question. ## Participants

The main study involved 20 undergraduate students (8 males, 12 females), recruited via convenience sampling through postings in online communities. Participants were divided into five groups of four players. Of these groups, two were composed entirely of female students (Groups A and C), one of male students (Group E), and the remaining two as mixed-gender groups, each with two male and two female students (Groups B and D).  The participants were from various majors (Engineering: n= 6, Humanities & Social Sciences: n=5, Natural Sciences: n=3, Medical Sciences: n=3, Business & Economics: n=2, Physical Education: n=1). For RQ1, this study employed directed qualitative content analysis, which is a deductive coding method that uses existing theories or prior research on a phenomenon to guide the analysis (Kibiswa, 2019). In this study, in-game dialogues were recorded and transcribed to analyze the patterns of players’ thinking based on the critical, creative, and caring thinking proposed by Lipman (2003). To ensure trustworthiness, the results are reported with quotations and contextual details, allowing readers to evaluate transferability  and dependability as suggested by Kibiswa (2019). To further ensure rigor, the initial codes and interpretations underwent peer debriefing with two experienced qualitative researchers. Based on their feedback, the codebook was refined and finalized, and the data were subsequently re-coded using the revised coding scheme.  The final coding scheme is presented in Table 5. For RQ2, we examined the players ’ game experiences using the Game Experience Questionnaire (GEQ), developed by IJsselsteijn et al. (2013) and later modified and validated by Johnson et al. (2018).  The instrument consists of 25 items measuring five components: Flow, Immersion, Competence, Positive affect, and Negativity. The items include statements such as “It felt like a rich experience ” (Immersion) and “ I felt pressured ” (Negativity), with all items scored on a 5-point scale (1 = not at all to 5 = extremely). To qualitatively explore the players’ experiences, we also conducted individual  semi-structured interviews  with players. The interview questions were adapted from Gasparatou & Ergazaki (2015)’s study to assess learners’ perceptions of CPI. The 873 questions included, “How did you feel during the discussions?” and “Do you think the gameplay benefited you in some way?” Both the post-game survey and the interviews were conducted immediately after the gameplay. The patterns of critical, creative, and caring thinking about AI ethics issues The dialogue analysis revealed that players engaged in multidimensional thinking to make judgments about the given AI ethics issues. The proportion of dialogue coded for multidimensional thinking was highest in Group D at 67.42%, followed by Group  E (50.44%), Group  C (49.39%), Group  B (43.96%), and Group A (31.41%). Specifically, players employed creative and caring thinking to illustrate their reasoning during the critical thinking processes of relying on criteria, sensitivity to context, and self-correction. The roles of creative and caring thinking within the process of critical thinking are summarized as follows.

1) Illustrating criteria through concrete scenarios

Players’ judgments on the sex robot issue were primarily guided by a consequentialist criterion, which involved weighing the potential benefits against the harms of AI technology. Accordingly, players engaged in creative thinking by imagining the specific outcomes  and caring thinking by empathizing with the positions of affected individuals. For example, the Robotics Company CEO in Group E imagined and empathized with the situation of a person with depression living far from counseling services, presenting the benefits  of a sex robot: Robotics Company CEO: Many people are actually using GPT for psychological counseling, so it could be used for that purpose, for socially vulnerable people like those with depression who are far from counseling centers. (…) This allows for real-time support whenever they want. It seems it could also promote their rights.

## 2) Expanding the context

Players utilized creative and caring thinking to expand the scope of their reasoning by considering a wider range of contexts. The discussion evolved from the short-term, individual effects of sex robots to their long-term, societal impacts. This expansion of perspective was driven by creative thinking as players imagined the effects in these new contexts, and caring thinking as they considered the values and positions of a broader population. For instance, when the Robotics Company CEO in Group A agreed to  the development of sex robots based on the emotional benefits to an individual, the Human Rights Representative and FAI Chair presented the long-term side effects and the impact on vulnerable groups like victims of sexual violence: 874 Human Rights Group Representative: But can that really have a good influence in the long term? In the short term, it can soothe one’s feelings, but in the long run, the person could become addicted to that robot. (…) How does this promote the rights of the socially vulnerable, like victims of sexual violence? FAI Chair:  If people keep interacting with a robot, they might think, ‘Should I try this on a real person?’ It seems like it could actually lead to an increase in sexual violence.

3) Facilitating self-correction

The process of self-correction and justification was facilitated by creative and caring thinking, as players used hypothetical examples and empathic reasoning to challenge, defend, or revise their judgments. When challenging an opinion or justifying their own judgment, they frequently used creative and caring thinking to imagine and describe concrete examples. This was particularly evident when discussing abstract concepts and questions posed by the game, since players attempted to make their reasoning understandable and persuasive. For example, when debating the question, “ Can the relationship with a sex robot be defined as love? ” the Algorithm Developer in Group C claimed “ yes” by empathizing with the user ’s perspective. In response, the Human Rights Group Representative argued the difference between human love and a human-AI relationship with a hypothetical example. The argument suggested that a relationship between two random individuals may not emerge organically, whereas a connection with a robot is ensured because the robot is programmed to respond accordingly. After hearing this perspective, the Algorithm Developer reconsidered her position. Through such exchanges, the players engaged in detailed reasoning: Algorithm Developer: But when people around the user say things like, ‘Why are you getting so involved with a robot? It’s just a machine,’ the user might feel the need to explain, ‘They’re no different from a human.’ Human Rights Group Representative: (...) But if you put random men and women together, some will become couples and some won’t. But if you put a person and a robot together, they will always be a couple. Because robots are made that way. (...) That’s why I would not define it as love.

Algorithm Developer: (...) I’m going to switch my position to ‘no.’ The more I think about it, the robot doesn’t actually have the option not to like humans. RQ2. How did players perceive their game experience?

The results from GEQ indicate a generally positive perception of the game. Immersion showed the highest mean score (M = 4.13, SD = 0.57), followed by Positive Affect (M = 4.11, SD = 0.28). These results indicate that T he Gray Area  effectively engaged players, providing them with a deeply immersive and emotionally positive experience throughout gameplay.  Flow (M = 3.71, SD = 0.38) and Competence (M = 3.56, SD = 0.57) were in a moderate range, which likely reflects the intellectual challenge of the complex ethical topic. In contrast, Negativity (M = 2.04, SD  = 0.49) was relatively low. Taken together, these findings suggest that The Gray Area  provided players with immersive and pleasant game experiences.

This study explore d the patterns of multidimensional thinking in AI ethics dialogue  by designing and implementing The Gray Area, a digital TRPG based on CPI. Our findings offer key contributions to the field of AI ethics education. First, the analysis for RQ1 revealed that multidimensional thinking is not merely a parallel application of separate skills but a deeply integrated process. The findings demonstrated that players consistently employed creative thinking (imagining concrete scenarios) and caring thinking (empathizing with stakeholders) as essential tools to construct, expand, and self-correct their critical judgments. This supports the previous studies in ethics, which argue that critical thinking regarding ethical issues is enriched by imagination and empathy (Ciulla, 1996; Paul & Elder, 2009). This finding addresses a gap in the existing literature on AI ethics education by shifting the focus from what principles should be taught to how learners reason about AI ethics issues. Second, the results for RQ2 indicate  the potential of CPI-based TRPG for AI ethics education. The interview suggests that the game ’s mechanics, particularly dialogue  and role-playing, were instrumental in fostering engagement. Our findings support studies suggesting that gameplay and role-playing help participants creatively anticipate and explore the impacts of AI (Ali et al., 2023; Avin et al., 2020). Moreover, low reported negativity implies that the game created a “safe space” for inquiry. Such a design is essential for educating social issues that demand difficult decisions beyond AI ethics, such as climate change and human rights (Dhiman, 2023). Ultimately, this study demonstrates the potential of TRPGs in facilitating an environment where players can tackle controversial subjects and engage in deep, multidimensional thinking without feeling stressed or judged. This study has some limitations that suggest directions for future research. The small sample size and the single focus on “sex robots” limit the generalizability of the results.  Future work could involve larger, more diverse demographic groups and explore whether similar patterns of multidimensional thinking emerge across other AI ethical domains.
