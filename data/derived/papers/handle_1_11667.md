# Examining Lower Secondary School Students’ Misunderstandings of Decision Trees: A Distractor Analysis

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Artificial Intelligence (AI) literacy education is in bloom and extending its reach to increase accessibility of foundational concepts to younger audiences. However, our prior research has shown that adolescents at the lower secondary school age tend to struggle with some fundamental concepts, one of which is decision trees (DTs). This paper probes into students’ struggles with DTs through a distractor analysis of 3 multiple choice survey items administered to lower secondary students (n=699) before and after a curricular intervention.

## Introduction

Understanding the mechanisms of AI algorithms—its parts, how and why it makes predictions and classifications —plays an important role in developing AI literacy (Long & Magerko, 2020; Mutawa & Sruthi, 2025), particularly when learning how AI seems to reason through automated decisions (Touretzky et al., 2023). Yet, studies of how students come to understand AI reasoning mechanisms  have found that upper secondary students (age 15-18) students with mathematics and computer science back grounds struggle to unde rstand graphical representations (Greenwald et al., 2021) and logic structures (Frie l et al., 2001, Glazer, 2011) showing how AI system s work. Further, little is known about how students, particularly those in lower secondary school (age 12-15), with limited mathematical background understand these structures.

To investigate the roots of this issue, our team ha s launched a multi-year research project that aims to establish empirical evidence in support of a decisi on tree learning trajectory, which would connect co ncepts necessary to scaffold understanding of DTs. We argue that DTs are a fundamental AI concept in part because they are often human intelligible and thus can serve as a bridge to less relatable ways of making machines act intelligently, i.e., neural networks. In this paper, we report an analysis conducted as part of this larger work, which examines response patterns from lower secondary school age respondents (n=699) across 3 multiple choice items from the AI concept inventory (AICI) (Zhang et al.,  2024) that measure understanding of AI logic struc tures specific to DTs. DTs are graph structures consisting of nodes and edges designed for classifying data based on its features. Our analysis sought to determine whether there was evidence within response patterns of effi cient distractor performance. Efficient distractors (Testa et al., 2018) are incorrect alternative options designed to target common misconceptions that attract respondents, wit h emerging understanding, as plausible answers. The y can also help prompt an examinee to reveal their thinki ng of DTs in clinical interviews; thus, creating opportunit ies for our research to identify common pitfalls on the pathway to understanding DTs.

## Method

The data used in this report draws from a project t o integrate AI literacy in lower secondary school c lassrooms using the Developing AI Literacy (DAILy) curriculum, which was developed in 2020 to introduce concepts of AI ethics, mechanisms, creative applications, and care er opportunities. The curriculum includes a 45-minute lesson on DTs, introducing a basic form of human-designed classification graphs that categorize a gi ven dataset using the activity PastaLand (Lee et al., 2021). As students build their own DTs  in this activity they learn to identify features, trace or follow the logic through a DT, c raft binary questions, and distinguish features tha t differentiate pasta types. The 3 items that are the focus of our analysis were designed to measure learning from these activities and are displayed in Figure 1.

We analyzed pre-/post-survey AICI responses from 699 lower secondary school students. The AICI was administered before and after implementation of the  DAILy curriculum during the academic years 2021-2023. Participants were majority 6th grade (54%, n=377) male (56%, n=391) students recruited from classrooms in urban regions of the Eastern and Central United Sta tes. Participants who scored above and below a standard deviation from the median on the AICI pre-survey were labeled as having high and low prior kn owledge of AI, respectively. Roughly a third of participants had high (30%, n=210) and low (34%, n=241) prior AI knowledge. Following established distractor analysis methods (Testa et al., 2018), we define an efficient distrac tor as an item option that (a) was selected with greater frequency than would be expected at random that (b) exhibits negative discrimination. Negative discrimination me ans an incorrect option (a distractor) is chosen mo re frequently by respondents who performed poorly on t he overall test compared to those who performed wel l. To determine negative discrimination, we appli ed the formula used by Testa and colleagues (2018) because it minimizes the likelihood that distractors chosen at  random were identified as efficient. ## Results and discussion

Overall distractor performance was suitable: 33% (n = 3) of the distractors met our criteria for effici ency and showed an increase in negative discrimination on th e post-survey: 1D, 2C, 3B. The most efficient distractor w as 1D, which suggests an incorrect reading of the root  node split. On the pre-survey, respondents with low to moderate prior AI knowledge tended to be attracted to 1D instead of the correct answer. Post-survey responses show that 35% of the individuals who chose 1D on th e pre-chose it agai n on the post, and 12% of those who chose the correct answer (B) on the pre-switched their answer to 1D on the post. Option 2C also proved to be an efficient distractor on the pre-and the post-for all levels of prior knowledge (low and high). T hirty five percent of respondents who chose 2C on the pre-chose it again on the post. This selection pattern may be the result of differing interpretations of features of the fruit.  Item 2 prompts comparison of a banana to a round g reen fruit, which could be seen as a lime or a mango. This emphasizes the need for careful selection of the images for assessment items, as they may elicit different inte rpretations. For respondents with low prior knowled ge, 2C proved attractive in the pre-and post-survey. It is possibl e that these choices were due to differences in interpretations of the item language; yet, it may b e that respondents struggled to understand boolean logic or the representation of binary branch logic. Further rese arch is needed to determine if and how the se alternative interpretations of DTs impact the emerging understanding of lower secondary school age learners (age 12-15).

## Concluding remarks

We examine lower secondary school students’ responses to 3 items on a survey of DT concepts. Responses offer evidence of 3 attractive distractors for respondents with emerging understanding of AI. Our analysis suggests that to develop normative understanding of DT, learners may need support not only understanding how DTs work but more importantly how to interpret the representatio ns within DTs. Findings have implications for AI li teracy frameworks and curriculum development. Conclusions are limited by the fact that analysis examines responses to a small number of survey items. In future work, we will conduct clinical interviews to test these findings.
