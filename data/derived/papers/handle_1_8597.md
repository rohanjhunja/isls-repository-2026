# Instructional Dialogues in an Inquiry-Based Classroom: Towards a Typology of Teacher Self-Positioning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Effective classroom dialogues are vital for knowledge building in inquiry-based classrooms, and the positioning of teachers and students play an essential role. This qualitative study explored the patterns of and decision-making process underpinning the dialogues through analyzing lesson recordings and conducting stimulated recall interviews. Three types of teacher self-positioning were identified, which were associated with considering the cognitive-developmental needs of the respective students and the contextual constraints.

## Introduction

The concept of dialogic teaching emerged as more comparative studies on classroom discourses were conducted. Dialogic teaching empowers students to use talk pow er to collectively construct knowledge alongside te achers' supportive layout and active engagement (Alexander,  2004). A body of literature has studied the dialog ue patterns (vande Pol et al., 2017), effects on spec ific learning skills (VanDerHeide et al., 2016) and  talk functions (Boyd & Markarian, 2015). However, the im plicit decision-making process behind the instructi onal dialogues is scarcely explored (Reznitskaya & Grego ry, 2013). Teachers constantly decide on and re-neg otiate their discursive positions, which could be understo od through the lens of positioning theory (Davies &  Harré,

1990) by considering how students' rights and dutie s are being assigned and negotiated. Their self-

(re)positioning could uncover the dilemma faced by the teachers regarding their adoption of the dialog ic approach. The current study qualitatively analyzed a classroom in-depth to explore dialogue patterns a nd accentuate the thinking process of participants. This study aims to develop a typology of teacher self-positioning in classroom inquiry processes and provide interpretative accounts of the respective pedagogical intentions. It is hoped that the current effort could sustain the scholarly discussion on positioning theory as an analytical tool of classroom discourse analysis, particularly of the i nquiry-based classroom discussion of science-relate d social issues (e.g., Bossér & Lindahl, 2017).

## Methods

Our participants included a teacher and a Secondary  5 (Year 11) class of 32 students at a secondary sc hool in Hong Kong. By far, 12 lessons have been recorded fo r identifying dialogic elements at the turn-and le ssonlevels. The lessons observed were under the subject  of Liberal Studies, which required teachers and students to co-construct interdisciplinary understandings of so cial issues under various curriculum-specified them es. The recordings of the in-class videos were analyzed by the analytical framework derived from the dialogic indicators developed by Alexander (2004) and the dialogic feat ures distilled by Mercer and Littleton (2007). To disentangle the decision-making process behind dialogues, stimulated recall interviews were conducted with the teacher and students to unearth the respective moti ves of their behaviours in various talk moves, toge ther with the perceived effects of their actions on learning and teaching. The interview data were inductively processed to permit typological frameworks of teacher self-posit ioning to emerge, with particular attention paid to  role assignment and negotiation processes.

## Findings

Three types of teachers' self-positioning have emer ged from the preliminary analysis, and they were fo und to influence the substantiation of instructional dialogues: "self-background" (Type 1), "co-construction" (Type 2), and "self-foregrounding" (Type 3). "Type 1" episode s put students in th e foreground to their arguments in extended dialogues, whereas "Type 2" episodes invol ve conversational participation opened to the whole  class alongside a foregrounded teacher-student pair. "Type 3" episodes resemble monologic teaching where dialogues are less negotiable. Students' responses are expect ed to be concise and direct, and the teacher always  provides quick evaluative remarks. Initially, the teacher declared Michael the preferred interlocutor after seemingly hearing his voice. Subsequently, at the speaking turn (8), Michael challenged the teacher's duty-assignment decision. The teacher softly reiterated that speaking turn (1) was an invitation that Micha el had not declined, which resulted in Michael's du ty to remain in the dialogue. After the negotiation, Mich ael resumed his duty again. This episode contrasts with "Type 3" episodes, in which the teacher usually gives less wait time and emphasizes the evaluation more than individual viewpoints. It is also distinct from "Type 1" episodes,  in which the teacher intentionally backgrounded one's self for the students to state their arguments independently.
