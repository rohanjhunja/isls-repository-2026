# Who Gets to Know? Children’s Epistemic Agency in Science eBook Reading with AI

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Children’s ability to act as epistemic agents not only support their understanding of concepts but also for shaping how they engage with ideas and participate in knowledge construction within their learning environment. The advancement in artificial intelligence (AI) has provided opportunities for interactive science learning, such as serving as a learning companion in dynamic and dialogic reading. While prior research has examined how children enact epistemic agency in human-to-human interaction, it is unc lear about whether, and if so how, children enact epistemic agency when interacting with AI, a non-human entity. This paper draws on video recordings of two individual children, each reading a science eBook with an AI agent. Using an interaction analysis, we examine how, through both language and embodied action, these two children claimed, evaluated, and negotiated knowledge, positioning themselves as epistemic agents in interaction with the AI agent.

Epistemic agency emphasizes that learners have the capacity to contribute to knowledge building through actions like asking questions, evaluating ideas, and taking responsibility for what and how they know (Scardamalia, 2002; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 1991).  This ability is important in science learning, where children move beyond receiving information to constructing and refining understanding (Damşa et al., 2010; Stroupe, 2014). Prior work has examined how teachers support epistemic agency by positioning students as knowledge builders (Bautista & Davis, 2024; Stroupe, 2014), and how students enact and negotiate this agency in interaction with others (Baze et al, 2025; Jung et al., 2023).

Recent advances in AI have provided opportunities for dialogic reading, where AI can respond in real time based on the context. Carefully designed AI systems can support children’s science learning by prompting them to construct responses, ask questions, and engage with feedback and scaffolding. Because epistemic agency is deeply shaped by the social and interactional context (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2010), the nature of the learning partner—whether human or non-human—may influence how children position the mselves as knowers. It is therefore important to examine whether, and if so, how children enact epistemic agency, and what this reveals about how knowledge, authority, and participation are negotiated and constructed in child– AI interactions. To explore these questions, we analyzed video recordings of two children reading a science eBook with an AI agent using an interactional analysis approach (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). This analysis shows how children, through language and embodied action, claimed, evaluated, and negotiated knowledge —positioning themselves as epistemic agents in interaction with the AI. It also revealed how they managed their epistemic stance differently with the AI and a human researcher, showing how they wanted to be perceived as knowers, and by whom.

Epistemic agency in learning Epistemic agency refers to learners’ responsibility and ability to actively participate in knowledge-building (Stroupe, 2014; Scardamalia, 2002; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 1991). Rather than passively receiving information, learners act as epistemic agents who pose questions, evaluate explanations, and negotiate ideas. In science learning, this may involve challenging received explanations, generating alternative hypotheses, or reasoning through what they have heard. Building on this, we conceptualize the enactment of epistemic agency as a relational process in which children evaluate received information and negotiate and justify knowledge claims through language and embodied actions in response to the AI’s questions and feedback. Understanding children’s epistemic agency through co-operative action This study draws on Goodwin’s theory of co-operative action (Goodwin, 2013, 2018) to examine how children enact epistemic agency in interaction with an AI agent. This notion is that, in conversation settings, action is not produced in isolation but participants collaboratively construct it using multiple semiotic resources, such as talk, gaze, gesture, and body orientation, while responding to each other (Goodwin 2000, 2012). Participants “build 459 action by laminating different kinds of meaning making resources together” (Goodwin, 2013, p. 16), allowing us to trace how ways of knowing and acting emerge through interaction. A key concept in co-operative action is “substrate,” which refers to semiotic or material resources produced in prior turns that can be reused and transformed by subsequent participants (Goodwin, 2013). As participants build on prior actions, elements are both preserved and modified, linking new contributions to earlier ones. In this process, Goodwin mentioned two things typically happen: first, the previous substrate is modified or transformed as it is reused; second, elements of the original action are pr eserved in a recognizable way, linking the new act to the earlier one. This process of reusing, modifying, and preserving what came before could show how participants collaboratively build meaning over time. For instance, in child-AI conversation, the AI a gent’s earlier feedback can become the resource or substrate for the child’s response (through language or embodied actions), making visible how this action emerged through co-operative action. Goodwin’s theory of cooperative action provides a lens for us to examine the ways children express their epistemic authority and agency with AI, as well as how they want to be perceived by the technology “AI agent”. Conceptually, it allows us to see not only where a substrate first emerges in interaction, but also how it is preserved and transformed throughout the subsequent actions. In the context of this study, we can trace how the AI’s prompts, feedback, or evaluations become substrates for children’s responses, and how children take up or resist these substrates to claim or negotiate epistemic authority moment by moment. This paper draws on data from the first week of our pilot study conducted prior to the launch of our large-scale experimental project on children’s science learning with an AI-supported eBook. The pilot period was designed to refine study procedures and test the functionality of the eBook system. Seven children (aged 6-8) participated in individual reading sessions with the AI agent. During each session, children read the same science eBook about frogs and interacted with the AI agent, which asked questions, provided feedback, and prompted discussion throughout the reading. Children were instructed to read the eBook and respond to the AI’s questions as they progressed. The two children presented here were purposely selected because their interactions highlighted the dynamics of claiming authority as knowers to AI and acted as epistemic agents. Therefore, they serve as two “telling” cases (Mitchell, 1984, p. 239), defined as instances in which particular circumstances make broader theoretical relationships visible, for examining how children enact their epistemic agency and how they want to be perceived by the AI while reading a science eBook with AI. Data generated included video recordings of each child’s individual eBook reading session with the AI agent, along with screen recordings of dialogic interaction between child and AI. Across all seven participants, recordings ranged from 16–23 minutes per session, totaling 112 minutes. These data provide a multimodal account of how children evaluate information, express understanding, and enact epistemic agency with the AI. To understand each child’s modes, bodily orientations, and interactions with the environment in relation to the AI agent’s questions and responses, we followed Erickson’s (2006) whole-to-part analytical procedure. The first author first watched all seven v ideo recordings from start to finish and noted each moment when verbal and nonverbal actions occurred in a Google Doc. Shen then rewatched each of those moments in greater detail—from the beginning to the end of the relevant eBook page —when both verbal and nonverbal actions were present. For example, if a noted moment occurred when the child and the AI agent were discussing the purpose of frogs’ voices, she rewatched the entire episode beginning from the start of that page to just before moving on. During this process, she identified what Jordan and Henderson (1995) referred to as “hot spots” or “shapes of events,” signaling shifts in epistemic authority and agency between the child and the AI agent.

Using these selected moments as focal cases, the first author transcribed them using both Jefferson’s conversation analysis conventions (2004) and Mondada’s (2018) multimodal annotations. Jefferson’s conversational analysis captures the temporal organizati on of the talk, while Mondada’s framework incorporates bodily orientations and gaze shifts with the AI agent. Using both approaches allowed us to trace how epistemic authority was negotiated not only through speech but also through the embodiment and environment. Grounded in Goodwin’s concept of substrates (2013), our analytic lens focuses on how AI agent’s prior talk (i.e. evaluation to the child’s response, re-prompt, and conclusion) become resources for subsequent turns, shaping how children position themselves epistemically and negotiate epistemic authority and meaning with the AI agent. Presented as two “telling cases,” we illustrate how children positioned themselves as knowers when interacting with the AI reading companion. In Case 1, the child asserted his epistemic authority by correcting the AI’s failure to recognize the intent behind his earlier contribution, expressing dissatisfaction, and reclaiming authority. In Case 2, the child enacted epistemic agency by evaluating the AI’s scaffolding utterances and claiming knowledge came 460 from herself rather than the AI. Across both cases, we see children are not simply responding to the AI but actively calibrating their relationship with their AI partner. These “telling cases” invite us to consider how children enact a sense of epistemic authority, shaping how they want to be perceived by AI. Case 1: Expressing epistemic authority Reading informational texts such as science books supports children’s ability to move from everyday ideas toward more systematic reasoning about the physical and biological world during the elementary years. Pedagogical questioning further enhances comprehension by encouraging children to express their thinking and reflect on or revise their understanding through feedback (Carey, 2000; Vosniadou, 1994). From sharing his ideas about frogs’ voices to humorously correcting the AI’s feedback, the focal child in Case 1 illustrates how he sustained and defended his epistemic authority in dialogic interaction with the AI reading partner. As evidenced in the transcript (see Extract 1a in the supplementary materials; the glossary of transcription symbols and conventions appears in the supplementary materials), the AI agent was narrating to the child (line 1) and posed a pedagogical question (line 3) to invite the child to think about the purposes of frogs’ voices. While listening to the question, he moved his hand toward the “press to talk” button to get ready to respond. Immediately after the AI’s question ended, he pressed the button (line 5) and gave his answer without delay or referring back to the text. He confidently shared his first response that frogs use their voices for mating, stretching the word “mating”. Then, the AI agent prompted the child to think about another reason frogs use their voice when they are scared (line 7). Like in the previous turn, the child moved his hand toward the “press to talk” button to get ready. Right after the question was narrated, he immediately pressed the button and gave extra force to “scream” (line 9) in his answer. His hand movement and ways of speaking signaled readiness and confidence in his epistemic state about this science concept, showing that he positioned himself as already knowing the answers. His answers were further confirmed by the AI’s evaluation in the later turn. However, the AI agent did not think the child had fully addressed the answer, so it prompted the child to think about another purpose (line 10). Unlike in the previous turn, this time, he was not ready to answer this question. The child paused for seven seconds while referring back to the text in search of an answer (line 11). He then responded, “I don’t really know if they use it for any other instances” (line 12). If we just look at this textual information in this sentence alone, the phrasing shows this child’s uncertainty, which is also how AI interpreted this response, as the AI agent acknowledged this uncertainty with “That’s okay!”. However, when combined with the child’s embodied actions and ways of delivery (quick tempo, playful tone, and repeated head shakes), the child’s response is not just simply expressing and signaling lack of knowledge. His response carried confidence that no additional answers existed. Based on what he knew, he thinks that there are no more other usages of frogs’ voices. Through all these cues, the child’s uncertainty is not as a sign of not knowing but as an epistemic stance that enabled him to sustain his role as a knower, even as the AI treated him as an unknower. Closer to the end of the conversation on this page (see Extract 1b in the supplementary materials), the child reclaimed his authority as a knower through both facial expression, tone and language when he realized that the AI had misevaluated his second response about purposes of frogs’ voices or had failed to recognize the underlying meaning the child intended. Because the system had reached the maximum number of turns, we predefined, after acknowledging the child’s uncertainty, the AI proceeded to provide a fuller explanation of the correct answer, ending with a closing prompt asking whether the child had further questions (line 13). When the AI was delivering feedback, the child was showing visible frustration, furrowing his brows and silently mouthing words (line 14, fig.1-2), as if pushing back against this situation that the AI’s interpretation failed to register the nuance in what he meant by frogs “scream” when frightened. In other words, the child may have wondered why the AI asked for another reason for frogs’ voices when, from his perspective, his earlier answers “scream” already covered what the AI later explained “to communicate” (line 13). His embodied response thus signaled frustration with how the AI evaluated and interpreted his contribution. Once the AI finished speaking, the child pushed back again, saying with emphasis and laughter: “I already told you that they use their voices to communicate, ha ha ha”  (line 15, fig.3). Although his earlier response (“scream”) did not explicitly mean “communicate,” he framed it as equivalent to the AI’s explanation of the correct answer. He laughed at AI and resisted the AI’s rigid interpretation of correctness and reasserted himself as the one who knew, placing strong emphasis on the word told. Case 2: Claiming authority as a knower to the AI while displaying unknowing to a human researcher In this case (see Extract 2 in the supplementary materials), the focal child was reading a page about frogs’ eyes and discussing how they help frogs see food without moving. She positioned herself as the knowledgeable one in relation to the AI by drawing on her prior understanding to explain how the eyes work, yet simultaneously 461 displayed unknowing toward the human researcher. This invites us to think about how children may express their epistemic stance depending on how they want to be viewed by the one they are talking to. When the AI invited the child to imagine what allows frogs to do when they can see all directions (line 1), the child paused for a moment (line 3) and responded “ I have no↑ idea what you just said because I didn't understand it”(line 4). Rather than simply expressing uncertainty, she shifted responsibility to the clarity of the AI’s utterance. In other words, instead of passively accepting the role of an “unknower,” this child demonstrated her epistemic agency by first evaluating  the comprehensibility of the AI’s utterance, then assessing her own understanding, and justifying her lack of ability to answer on the grounds of the AI’s lack of clarity. To give the child a hint, the AI invited the child to imagine being in the frog’s situation and to think about how being able to see in all directions might help with finding food and staying safe (line 5). The child paused to think for about 14 seconds (l ine 7). During the first half of this time, she gazed at the chat box, reread the question, and then looked to the side toward the researcher (fig. 4), as if wanting to be recognized by the human researcher that she did not know this answer. After realizing that the researcher did not respond, she turned her gaze away and continued thinking. Since the AI did not hear anything from her, it redirected her attention back to the conversation by repeating the question (line 9). This child used her gestures (fig. 5), facial expressions, and language to demonstrate how frogs catch food with their tongues, closing with: “He never said some of that, but still I know that, so yeah” (line 10).  Here, she claimed her authority as a knower by distinguishing her own knowledge from the AI’s, making clear that it came from her prior knowledge rather than from the AI’s teaching. Her explicit statement about the source of her knowledge reinforced her epistemic agency by using confident language and embodied expression (fig.6-7) to assert that the knowledge was hers, not something learned from the AI.

## Summary and discussion

The goal of this study was to examine how children enact and negotiate epistemic agency during science eBook reading with an AI partner. Rather than treating children’s responses as isolated, we approached them as socially situated within an unfolding chil d–AI interaction, where knowledge and authority —who knows, whose knowledge counts, and how authority is displayed—are negotiated turn by turn. Although the AI was designed to scaffold understanding, children did not participate passively; they evaluated, reflected on, and at times resisted the AI’s explanations, revealing a desire to be recognized as knowledgeable participants. The two cases illustrate distinct ways children asserted epistemic agency. In one case, the child resisted the AI’s explanation by asserting that his prior response was correct, signaling his expectation to be recognized as a knower. In the other, the child shifted her epistemic stance across partners—orienting uncertainty toward the human researcher while holding the AI accountable and reasserting knowledge in interaction. These moments show that epistemic authority in child–AI interaction is relational and dynamically reconstructed. Through the lens of co-operative action  (Goodwin, 2018), our analysis further shows that epistemic authority is enacted through multiple semiotic resources, including language, gaze, and embodied action. Children coordinated these resources to build on or challenge the AI’s contributions, demonstrating how they work to sustain recognition as knowledgeable participants. Attending to these micro-level interactions offers a more nuanced account of learning with AI, highlighting that participation involves not only responding to prompts but actively shaping knowledge and interactional roles. Without such attention, moments of resistance or negotiation may be misinterpreted as errors rather than meaningful expressions of agency. These findings have implications for the design and study of AI-supported learning. Children’s displays of knowing extend beyond verbal responses to include embodied and interactional cues, suggesting the need for AI systems that better recognize and respo nd to these forms of participation. Future work could examine how children negotiate epistemic authority with human versus AI partners, as well as how they themselves perceive these interactions. While based on a small number of cases, this study offers analytic insight into how children seek recognition and position themselves as knowers, informing more responsive and equitable AI designs that support children as active contributors in knowledge building.
