# Learning With and About AI: Building Capacity for Equitable Participation in an AI-Shaped Future

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract

My research investigates how learners and educators understand, design with, and collaborate with AI systems, and how these sociotechnical interactions shape equity and participation. Using design-based research, participatory design, and qualitative and computational methods, I study AI education and disciplinary integration of AI in K–12 contexts. My work aims to design equitable learning environments, develop theoretical models of learner engagement with AI, and build cross-disciplinary approaches to teaching with and about AI. Theoretical and methodological foundations that inform my work My research sits at the intersection of the learning sciences, data science and artificial intelligence (AI) education. I am particularly interested in how educators and learners understand, design with, and collaborate with AI systems, and how these sociotechnical interactions shape equity and participation in society. In my work, I frame learning as a socially and materially mediated practice, drawing on sociocultural perspectives that conceptualize knowing as participation in activity systems rather than simply the acquisition of decontextualized concepts (Vygotsky, 1987; Wertsch, 1991). Within this view, AI tools and data infrastructures function as cultural tools (Cole, 1996; Saxe, 1991) that shape what becomes visible, what is considered authoritative, and whose perspectives are deemed valid as knowledge. I also draw on constructionist (Holbert et al., 2020; Kafai & Resnick, 1996; Papert & Harel, 1991) and design-oriented traditions that treat building computational artifacts as a powerful avenue for learning. When students build or critique AI applications and data stories, they are engaging in epistemic work that reveals their assumptions about what counts as evidence, how models relate to the world, and whose experiences are encoded in data (Vakil et al., 2022). I am particularly attentive to how young learners develop both conceptual and sociotechnical understandings of the affordances and limits of AI technologies, and how educators can support these competencies in ways that foster agency, critique, and creativity. Theoretical and methodological foundations that inform my work My research sits at the intersection of the learning sciences, data science and artificial intelligence (AI) education. I am particularly interested in how educators and learners understand, design with, and collaborate with AI systems, and how these sociotechnical interactions shape equity and participation in society. In my work, I frame learning as a socially and materially mediated practice, drawing on sociocultural perspectives that conceptualize knowing as participation in activity systems rather than simply the acquisition of decontextualized concepts (Vygotsky, 1987; Wertsch, 1991). Within this view, AI tools and data infrastructures function as cultural tools (Cole, 1996; Saxe, 1991) that shape what becomes visible, what is considered authoritative, and whose perspectives are deemed valid as knowledge. I also draw on constructionist (Holbert et al., 2020; Kafai & Resnick, 1996; Papert & Harel, 1991) and design-oriented traditions that treat building computational artifacts as a powerful avenue for learning. When students build or critique AI applications and data stories, they are engaging in epistemic work that reveals their assumptions about what counts as evidence, how models relate to the world, and whose experiences are encoded in data (Vakil et al., 2022). I am particularly attentive to how young learners develop both conceptual and sociotechnical understandings of the affordances and limits of AI technologies, and how educators can support these competencies in ways that foster agency, critique, and creativity. Methodologically, my work draws on design-based research (Hoadley & Campos, 2022), participatory design (Cumbo & Selwyn, 2022), and qualitative approaches commonly used within the Learning Sciences. I employ design-based research to iteratively co-design data science and AI resources with educators and community partners, while developing mid-level theoretical constructs regarding AI literacy and learner–AI collaboration. Participatory design approaches with youth and teachers position them as co-designers of curricula, games, and AI experiences, helping surface the tensions they experience when trying to integrate AI into their local contexts and disciplinary practice. In terms of analysis, I primarily employ qualitative methods, including thematic and discourse analysis of interviews and observational data, to examine how students and teachers make sense of AI tools and concepts in situ. I complement these with quantitative and computational approaches, including epistemic network analysis (ENA) (Marquart et al., 2018; Shaffer, 2017) to explore patterns in how learners and educators engage with, reason about, and respond to AI. I am also beginning to explore LLM-assisted coding and modeling workflows that can support, but not replace, human interpretive work with complex datasets (Arastoopour Irgens & Adisa, 2024). Through this combined theoretical and methodological orientation, I aim to contribute to the Learning Sciences by conceptualizing AI and data-driven technologies as part of the social and epistemic infrastructure of learning. Past and current work My research trajectory began with questions about how young learners come to understand, apply, and critique AI systems. In my doctoral work, I collaborated with community afterschool educators to design learning environments that situate machine learning concepts in sociopolitical contexts. I led studies examining how children interrogated datasets, reasoned about model outputs, and built their own AI applications. To achieve this, I collected and analyzed a wide range of data sources, including students’ code snippets, design sketches, digital interaction logs, discourse transcripts, and survey data. I used both ENA and thematic analysis to map the connections students made across concepts as they interacted with AI systems, supportive adults, and peers. My analysis showed that when students are given opportunities to troubleshoot and design AI systems, their computational thinking practices improved and their confidence in questioning and proposing solutions to a lgorithmic limitations increased. Alongside this work with youth, I participated in several projects focused on K–12 data science and computing education. Within research–practice partnerships in elementary schools, I collaborated with teachers to design data science units that integrated students’ interests (such as music) with data modeling and interpretation (Herro et al., 2022). These projects examined how teachers understood data science, how school structures influenced their capacity to adopt new curricula, and how students engaged with data storytelling and visualization (Adisa et al., 2024). Collectively, this work contributed to emerging conversations in the Learning Sciences about how data practices can be introduced in ways that are meaningful, culturally relevant, and equitable for younger learners. During my doctoral studies, I also became increasingly interested in how teachers and students perceived the role of emerging generative AI technologies in education. I conducted survey and interview studies examining high school and middle school students’ perspectives on tools such as ChatGPT, including their hopes, concerns, and ideas about appropriate educational uses. These projects documented a range of stances, from enthusiasm to ambivalence and resistance, and underscored the need for grounded AI literacy support for both students and educators (Adisa & Adefisayo, 2025; Famaye et al., 2024). My current postdoctoral work at Stanford builds directly on these experiences and moves more centrally into the AI literacy and human–AI collaboration space within the Learning Sciences and computersupported collaborative learning (CSCL) communities. Recognizing the pivotal role teachers play in shaping learners’ critical engagement with technology, I co-design AI literacy resources across subject areas with teachers through the CRAFT (Classroom-Ready Resources About AI For Teaching) project. This includes analyzing how teachers conceptualize AI literacy within their disciplines, how they design activities that connect AI concepts to core disciplinary practices, and how school and policy contexts shape what is possible. I am particularly drawn to cases where teachers design AI lessons that stretch conventional disciplinary boundaries— for example, exploring the intersection of AI and art, AI and environmental science, or AI and statistics in ways that invite critique and imagination. These efforts allow me to study teacher learning in PD contexts, including how educators make sense of rapidly evolving AI technologies, how they negotiate district expectations and ethical concerns, and how they connect AI literacy goals to their existing pedagogical commitments. In parallel, I examine learner–AI collaboration in problem-solving contexts, an area that has become increasingly important as generative AI systems participate more directly in students’ cognitive and creative work. I conceptualize human–AI collaboration as a form of joint activity that redistributes cognitive, social, and metacognitive work across human and machine actors. To investigate this, I have designed and implemented studies examining how students negotiate meaning, agency, and communication with AI when solving problems individually and in groups. Using epistemic network analysis alongside fine-grained interaction analysis of video and interview data, I trace how cognitive moves (such as strategy generation and evaluation), social moves (such as turn-taking and justification), and metacognitive moves (such as planning and monitoring) are distributed among students and AI systems. My preliminary findings suggest that productive human–AI collaboration is characterized by students’ active regulation of the AI’s role—including engaging in independent work, questioning AI outputs, and integrating AI-generated ideas with their own reasoning. Across these strands, my past and current work contribute a line of inquiry focused on how learners and educators come to understand AI systems, how they collaborate with AI in problem solving, and how AI literacy can be integrated into disciplinary learning in ways that foreground agency, critique, and creativity. Future work My future research agenda will focus on designing and studying learning environments that enable learners to collaborate productively with AI to learn, create, and engage in STEM problem-solving. Building on my current projects, I plan to partner with educators and students to co-design AI-powered digital learning environments that promote STEM learning while cultivating critical and reflective engagement with AI. My focus here is on understanding how AI agents and human contributions jointly shape cognitive, communicative, and metacognitive outcomes, and what kinds of support help learners maintain agency in the presence of increasingly capable tools. On the design side, I plan to develop lightweight tools that encourage students to engage in productive forms of collaboration, provide transparency into AI suggestions, and capture data that can be repurposed for timely feedback and teacher insight. Methodologically, I will advance the use of epistemic network analysis and natural language processing to model how students connect disciplinary content with AI tools and practices, and to characterize the evolving interaction patterns that emerge in hybrid human–AI teams. Relatedly, I intend to continue articulating and testing cross-disciplinary frameworks for integrating AI literacy into K–12 instruction. Drawing on my experience working with teachers across AI, data science, and various disciplinary contexts, I will collaborate with educators in multiple subjects to design and study AI-integrated units that align with disciplinary epistemologies while fostering a critical understanding of AI.
