# Youth Learning Through Climate Activism: The Mediating Roles of Interaction in a School -Based Climate Course

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite the increasing call for action-oriented climate education, few studies explore youths’ experiences of participating in climate activism guided by formal school curricula. This paper examines how 6 high school students learned to engage in climate activism in an Interdisciplinary Climate Exploration course. Employing narrative analysis, we examine youths’ stories engaging in civic action projects. Focusing on their interactions with people within and outside the school, this study analyzes how interactions affect youths’ engagement and action-taking to address climate-related issues within their community. The analysis reveals three mediating roles of their interactions with people: 1) providing information that deepened youths’ understanding about the focal problem, 2) giving access to the resources, practices, and information that otherwise are difficult for youth to leverage, and 3) affirming, validating youths’ emotions, and being present with youth. Implications for designing and studying youths’ learning to engage in civic action are discussed.

Youth are increasingly tasked with engaging in complex civic dilemmas, such as rapid climate change and dilemmas on how to address it (Zummo et al., 2025). Research suggests that youths’ social interactions with people within and outside the school might be a critical component of curricula that support youth climate activism (Brown et al., 2019, Lee et al., 2021). Interacting with people from diverse backgrounds, including the impacted people, enables youth to learn from diverse perspectives and adopt a justice-oriented approach as they recognize how climate change is deeply intertwined with not just scientific, but also social dimensions (Lee et al., 2021). As such, building social consciousness can set the foundations for youth to take actions for systemic, lasting change. Despite various efforts to increase youths’ social interactions with diverse community members as part of formal education, currently, there is limited research that sheds light on youths’ experiences from their perspectives. Importantly, few studies center on youth voices when examining their experiences. Currently, we know little about which interactions, and with whom, are beneficial for inspiring and supporting youth to address climate-related challenges.

In this study, we explore how 6 youth in a high school Interdisciplinary Climate Exploration course narrate their experiences of engaging in civic actions to address climate-related issues and what role their interactions play in their civic engagement. Ex amining youths’ stories, we deepen our understanding of the challenges that they face and how they navigate those challenges with the help of adults and peers, which can serve as a resource to broaden the ways we support youth climate action. The following questions guide this study:

1. What support do youth narrate as useful for their meaningful and persistent engagement in climate civic

action?

2. How did youth narrate the roles of the support in their climate civic action project?

Framework In this study, we draw upon socio-educational ecologies as the framework for our study to analyze youths’ experiences engaging in climate action and how social interactions supported their engagement. The perspective of socio-educational ecologies calls fo r schools to expand educational boundaries and shift their focus beyond individual learners and the boundaries of school classrooms (Jornet et al., 2025). This perspective highlights the importance of connecting society with education, noting that “education and learning cannot and should not be understood as constrained within one specific context or setting but rather involve learning trajectories  across diverse and multiple contexts” (Jornet et al., 2025, p. 2). In this piece, we define ecosystem as the interconnected network of people, institutions, and contexts that are distributed across the classroom, school, and community boundaries, which youth are navigating and drawing upon while engaging in climate action. This framework 1677 directs our attention to how learning trajectories can span across ecosystems rather than remain bounded within the classroom.

This framework is useful as an analytical lens to explore youth’s interactions with people for the following reasons. First, as the context of youths’ guided engagement of civic actions, this framework draws our attention to the interactions that were instrumental in mediating youths’ experiences across socio-educational systems. Second, it allows us to attend to the roles of the interactions in youths’ developing agency and becoming empowered to enact social change in their communities.

## Study setting and participants

This study took place in an 11th and 12th-grade Interdisciplinary Climate Exploration course at a public high school located in Southern California during the 2024-2025 academic year. The school is located in a middleclass community and serves a diverse student population with a predominantly Asian population (about 60%), followed by White (17.2%), Hispanic/Latinx (9%), Filipino (2.5%), and Black (1.8%). Participants were 6 students of 16 to 18 years old who were enrolled in the climate change course. Reflective of their school’s diverse population, the participants had substantially different identifications, personal backgrounds, and histories. The participants in the study also had varying experiences engaging in civic action projects; thus, gathering such perspectives offered rich insights that deepened and strengthened the patterns across participants’ experiences, revealing how to support youth in taking action.

## Curricular context

The interdisciplinary climate change course was geared towards supporting youth to take justice-oriented climate action throughout the year. With climate civic action projects, youth addressed climate-related issues in their community. In this study, we examine youths’ experiences in one climate civic action project, the Heat Action Project.

As part of the Urban Heat Island Unit, youth conducted research about the high temperatures that they recently experienced while interacting with multiple community members for their project. In the classroom, students interacted with peers, their teacher, and guest speakers. Outside of the classroom, students interacted with peers who did not enroll in the climate course, custodians, and school administrators. In addition, they interacted with community members, such as family members and city council representatives. Based on what they learned about both the heat island phenomenon and the community’s concerns, the youth designed and implemented solutions with peers in their class. These solutions ranged from planting trees on their school’s campus to reduce surrounding temperatures, to advocating for improving the public transportation (i.e., increasing bus routes) to alleviate their school’s traffic congestion and lower carbon emissions from cars. Data sources Multiple sources of data were generated through interviews, artifact collection, and observations. The primary source of data was interview transcripts. We conducted 6 in-depth, semi-structured interviews with each student (25 minutes x 6 participants = about 150 minutes) and 2 semi-structured group interviews with five participants from this study (60 minutes x 2 interviews = 120 minutes), focusing on students’ personal background, experiences engaging in the Heat Action Project, and views on agency and justice-centered actions. In addition, we analyzed various student learning artifacts produced from the course, such as students’ reflections and presentations related to their Heat Action Project. We used video-recorded observations of the instruction to examine the pedagogical activities, the students’ implementation of the civic action project, and classroom interactions.

## Data analysis

Employing a qualitative data analysis and narrative inquiry approach (Connelly & Clandinin, 1990; Tisdell et al., 2025), we used stories as data that “make sense of our experiences, how we communicate with others, and through which we understand the world around us” (Tisdell et al., 2025, p. 41). The first stage of the data analysis involved examining youths’ stories from their interviews, developing interview memos and case profiles, and identifying supports noted by youths as useful and their roles (RQ #1 and 2). This open-coding helped us to generate a coding scheme that characterized youths’ stories with respect to: 1) interactions with peers and 2) interactions with adults. Guided by the social educational ecologies framework, we paid particular attention to the places (i.e., where youths’ learning took place) and the roles of interactions (i.e., what youths narrated about their interactions in the 1678 places). The coding scheme was revised iteratively as we conducted the data analysis. We used the revised coding scheme for all the interview transcripts, which revealed the themes and patterns across the stories of participating youths. The analysis was t riangulated and further contextualized with the analysis of the learning artifacts and videos.

RQ#1: Supports youth narrate as useful: Conceptual, structural, and emotional supports Youth narrated that interacting with a diverse group of people was valuable, noting that it provided new insights about climate-related issues and/or feedback on their climate civic action projects. The analysis revealed three kinds of support narrated by youth: 1) conceptual, 2) structural, and 3) emotional. First, conceptual support was guidance and knowledge that helped raise youths’ awareness about issues, enabling them to develop ideas and solutions in response. In addition, conceptual support referred to feedback and suggestions that aided youth to revise and improve their civic action project. For example, through interviews and surveys, students identified a shared desire among their peers to add greenery on their school’s campus to help reduce temperatures: “After getting a lot of the surveys and data and asking through interviews, I realized, although not spoken a lot, but within our school, I realized that there is actually a lot of a need of plants from a lot of students and a lot of students did want [more greenery]” (Student #6) This form of conceptual support was useful for students as it helped them make sense of complex problems affecting their local community, which further enabled them to justify and refine their response to environmental concerns.

The other kind of support narrated by multiple youths was structural support. This support helped youth navigate complex ecosystems and expand traditional boundaries of learning across diverse and multiple contexts. This included assisting youth in coordinating outreach and advocacy efforts, approving their project ideas, and providing access to restricted data that is not publicly accessible to students. For example, one group got access to attendance records and administrative data from their school’s principal, which showed the relationship between student absence and the distance students lived from the school. Access to these kinds of resources and tools helped youth, as it “solidified that [the local issue] is a widespread problem rather than just like an isolated few students problem” (Student #12).

Lastly, emotional support  inspired and motivated youth to overcome challenges they faced while engaging in climate action. By providing encouragement and validating youths’ concerns or ideas, emotional support fostered a sense of joy and accomplishment that empowered youth to be resilient and build strong, collaborative communities. This form of support alleviated youths’ concerns, such as being tokenized by adults, as one student reflected on their presentation at City Hall, “We felt that what we said did matter” (Student #12). RQ#2: The roles of supports in their climate civic action project In this section, we present the roles that each kind of support played at different stages of engaging in civic action projects–understanding the problem, identifying the focal issue to address, and taking action. At the beginning of their climate civic action projects, youth first focused on understanding and framing the problem. As youth heard diverse perspectives from adults in multiple settings and learned from their community’s diverse funds of knowledge, this raised youths’ awareness around issues within their community that they were previously unaware of. In turn, this informed youths’ project ideas and transformed their outlook and engagement with the world. For one group, they shared that: "after talking with [our school’s principal] and other people on admin and things like that, we were able to learn that there isn't really a reliable busing system or any system for students who don't have access to be able to get to school. So for that reason, we wanted to advocate for the expansion of the [city bus routes] to encompass like, to be able to cover the routes connecting to [our high school] so that students can have more access." (Student #12) After interacting with the principal, the group began researching this issue further, connecting their school’s traffic congestion to both the global climate crisis and academic inequities, finding that students at their 1679 school were missing class because they did not have reliable transportation to school. Hoping to address this issue, the group went on to present at their city’s council to request the expansion of their city’s bus routes, which they proposed would reduce their school’s traffic congestion and provide increased transportation for students. Providing such support empowered youth to take their learning beyond the classroom and advocate for system-level changes in their community, illustrating how youth candevelop agency and meaningfully transform society when given the opportunities to integrate the two domains of society and education. After framing the problem, youth grappled with taking action and implementing their project ideas across the school and community ecosystems. While conceptual support played a crucial role in helping youth design solutions, structural support, particularly  from adults, assisted them in navigating structures beyond traditional learning environments. For example, one group planted a tree on their school’s campus, aiming to lower surrounding temperatures. This was not possible without getting approval from their school’s custodian and principal. A member of the group outlined the process, explaining, “we would have to get [the proposal] approved by first the custodians and then the principal of our school too, so that we would be able to plant [the tree]” (Student #6). Their teacher also coordinated and guided their efforts by supplying tools to plant the tree. A student from the group reflected fondly, saying “[The teacher] really help us a lot. They bought the tools for us, and yeah, without them, we really ca nnot make this semester and this project” (Student #8). Working together, youth and adults fostered collective goals, collaborating as a community to transcend traditional roles and responsibilities. Ultimately, the process of engaging in climate action was one that youth described as deeply emotional. One student reflected on the challenges they experienced at City Hall and their presentation being cancelled many times, sharing that “[the cancellation] was certainly a bit demotivating and kind of made me reconsider whether I really want to do it because it can be discouraging to have that many setbacks over and over again” (Student

#19). However, youth were able to manage these challenges from the emot ional support provided by both peers

and adults. Youth reported that emotional support from peers and adults made them feel more motivated, safe, and encouraged. For instance, one of the students expressed that  “at the beginning, I felt very lost. I don't know what to do.”(Student #8), but they navigated this challenge by relying on their peers as they stated: "Because personally, I really rely on the, like, there's a chemical reaction from the team and I will feel very positive. I have very positive energy when I work with team comparing with doing project individually. Like I really don't know what to do and like very pessimistic about what is the next step. So very thanks for my teammates, like they always guide me how to do. I very appreciate that." (Student #8) In the beginning of the project, this student had reported feelings of anxiety related to the project, but eventually, he came to overcome them by working alongside supportive teammates who fostered an encouraging and collaborative environment. Later, he took on the role of reassuring his group members when they needed support, and he described that he “try to comfort our team” (Student #8), further illustrating the crucial role that emotional support plays in promoting youths’ resiliency when engaging in climate activism. The analysis of youths’ narratives reveals two key findings. First, youth interacted with a variety of people who provided three types of support: 1) conceptual, 2) structural, and 3) emotional. These diverse interactions shaped youths’ climate action, inspiring and motivating them as they raised their awareness of climat e-related issues within their community, as they facilitated their learning about the complexities of climate change and its intersecting social and scientific dimensions, such as how humans contribute to and are affected by increasing greenhouse gas emissions. Second, these supports guided youth to expand the domains of traditional learning contexts and work towards making changes within their community. Interacting with multiple people across different ecosystems, supported youth to take a human-centered approach, avoid making simplistic solutions, and devise meaningful strategies to address multifaceted problems as they made broader connections between their learning and community.

The perspectives of youth and their experiences engaging in climate activism are understudied. Thus, expanding research on this topic can lead to a better understanding of how to support youth climate action. Furthermore, exploring youth climate action facilitated in schools can address current limitations of schooling systems that often disconnect youths’ everyday lives from traditional learning spaces.
