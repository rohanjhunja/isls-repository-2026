# Zone of Playful Development: Play for Transformational Equity in Multilingual STEM Classrooms

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Play is a vital yet underutilized tool for advancing equity and engagement in multilingual STEM classrooms. While traditional frameworks like scaffolding emphasize guidance from adults or advanced peers, play offers a dynamic space where students independently or interdependently explore, learn, and create, thus transcending linguistic and systemic barriers. Grounded in Vygotsky’s sociocultural theory, this study introduces the Zone of Playful Development (ZPlayD), positioning play as central to authentic science learning, culturally responsive teaching, and transformational equity. Using qualitative video analysis of emergent multilingual students, our findings reveal how play supported engagement with Science and Engineering Practices, bridged lived experiences with academic content, enabled creative role-playing, and fostered multimodal expressions of competence. We recommend integrating play as an intentional, equity-driven practice in STEM education—one that acknowledges multilingual students’ ways of knowing as scientific, honors the cultural resources they bring, and reimagines what counts as meaningful science learning.

## Introduction

Play has long been recognized as central to humandevelopment, fostering cognitive, emotional, and social growth in early childhood stages (Nicolopoulou et al., 200 9; Piaget, 1952; Vygotsky, 1978) and across human lifespans (Duncan & Tarulli, 2003; Perone & Goncu, 2014). Sch olars argue that play bridges imagination and struc tured knowledge, allowing learners to engage with complex  concepts in ways that encourage creativity, proble msolving, and collaboration (Lillard, 2015).  Within educational contexts, play not only enriches learning (Duncan & Tarulli, 2003; Rogers, 1985), but also creates pathways for student self-expression and empowerment, educating both hearts and minds in mutually reinforcing ways (Nicolopoulou et al., 2009). For emergent multilingual learners —many of whom face systemic barriers —play offers a uniquely powerful pathway to equitable engagement in STEM classrooms. These students are often marginalized by restrictive curricula, language-focused assessments, and deficit-based perspectives that undervalue their cultural, linguistic, and multimodal assets (Delpit, 1988; Ladson-Billings, 2011; Grapin, 2023). As Ladson-Billings (2011) critiques, playful behaviors are frequently misinterpreted as off-task rather than recognized as legitimate forms of meaning-making and intellectual engagement. As we show in this study, when acknowledged and supported, such behaviors reflect students’ culturally grounded way s of knowing and doing science (Gutiérrez & Rogoff,  2003). Yet despite its potential, the role of play in supp orting multilingual learners’ engagement with in STEM spaces remains under-theorized and under-researched (Pierson et al., 2024; Ryan, 2024). Drawing from these insights, this study explores how some emergent multilingual students engage with play in an out of school STEM classroom. Specifically, it addresses the foll owing research question:  how do multilingual students engage with play in STEM lear ning environments? By examining their lived experience through the lens of our theoretical framework, this paper aims to highlight the potential of play as a transformative force for equity and inclusion in multilingual education.

## Theoretical framework

ZPD vs ZPlayD Vygotsky’s Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) descr ibes the space between what a learner cando independently and what they can achieve with guidan ce from more knowledgeable others (Vygotsky, 1978).  In this study, we introduce the Zone of Playful Develo pment (ZPlayD) as a subset of the ZPD —one that emerges when play becomes the medium through which learners  stretch their thinking, engage with others, and co nstruct new understandings. Unlike typical ZPD settings such as direct instruction or guided problem-solving, ZPlayD is characterized by voluntary, imaginative, and multim odal engagement, often initiated, and sustained by students themselves. For example, while a teacher-led science demonstration may activate the ZPD thro ugh explanation and questioning, ZPlayD might emerge when students playfully experiment with materials, role-play as scientists, or reimagine scientific phenomena through storytell ing. In this context, we define play as a dynamic a nd openended process where learners explore, express, and make meaning through interaction with tools, ideas, and peers. Equity: access vs. transformation Equity in education is often framed as providing eq ual access to resources and opportunities (Grapin, 2023). However, as Grapin  (2023) argues, equity-as-access alone fails to address hidden systemic inequities. What is needed is equity-as-transformation, which seeks to disrupt underlying power structures by validating the cultural knowledge and diverse ways of being, knowing, and l earning of marginalized learners (Gutiérrez & Rogof f, 2003). For multilingual students, this shift requires moving beyond efforts focused solely on access to classroom content, toward efforts which disrupt hegemonic, Western-oriented STEM education practices (Pierson et al., 2024). In this paper, we argue that play offers a s pace to empower multilingual learners toward these goals. This requires epistemological shifts that center the voi ces, identities, and lived experiences of multilingual learners (Kayumova & Dou, 2021; Kayumova et al., 2018; Kayumova et al., 2022). Science and engineering practices with play The Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS) positi on Science and Engineering Practices (SEPs) as acti ve processes grounded in inquiry, collaboration, and c ommunication (NGSS Lead States, 2013). Rooted in sociocultural theories, SEPs reflect learning as socially mediated and often imaginative (Vygotsky, 1978). Yet in practice, emergent multilingual learners are freque ntly marginalized by language-heavy tasks that overlook their embodied and creative competencies (García & Kleifgen, 2010; Grapin, 2023). At the sam e time, play is often excluded from STEM instruction, particularly for th ese marginalized youth (Ladson-Billings, 2011). We argue that integrating play into SEP-focused tasks offers a generative space for  multilingual learners to participate authentically, drawing on their full multimodal repertoires to dem onstrate scientific thinking, experimentation, and expression.

## Methodology

We employed an interpretive case-study (Stake, 1995) method, drawing on video and audio recordings of classroom interactions, observational notes, and se mi-structured interviews with students in Grades 5 –8. Participants were emergent multilingual or bilingua l learners from diverse linguistic backgrounds —primarily Spanish and Portuguese, with some speaking Chinese,  Vietnamese, and other languages. The classroom was  led by a teacher, Diana, who facilitated inquiry-based STEM activities during a two-week summer workshop in 2019. We used video analysis to gain insight into students’ multimodal engagement and embodied participation (Heath, 1986). Video recordings enabled close exami nation of how learners navigated tools, language, a nd gestures to engage with STEM concepts. As Pink (200 7) emphasizes, video captures the dynamic interplay between student action and learning environment, ma king it especially useful for studying multimodal meaningmaking. We followed Braun and Clarke’s (2006) frame work for thematic analysis. Initial codes  were developed inductively from the data, yielding themes of interaction, play, and scientific engagement, and then refined deductively using our theoretical framework.

Ethical guidelines were followed to protect student  privacy. Below we present four illustrative themes that showcase how students used play to engage in S TEM by: demonstrating competence aligned with Scien ce and Engineering Practices, integrating play with  classroom content, behaving like researchers using the camera, and expressing creativity while completing classroom tasks.

## Findings and discussion

Science and engineering practices during play Students in the class were learning about energy and the classroom had recording equipment lying around. Jayden, an emergent multilingual student, was particularly drawn to the  bright red ladybug-shaped microphone on his table, which he initially thought was a speaker. Curious, he called out, “Miss, is this a speaker?” while the teacher, Diana, was speaking with another student. Gio, sitting nearby, corrected him, saying, “No. It is a mic,” pointing to it with his pencil. Intrigued by this claim, Jayden leaned closer and began experimenting. Speaking dir ectly into the microphone, he attempted to communicate wi th Andrew, the cameraman, saying, “Man, man, hey bro, hey. Shaa…” Jayden’s fascination and actions reflected more than simple curiosity; it was his deliberate attempt to verify Gio’s claim. This interaction exemplifies hypothesis testing a s outlined in the Science and Engineering Practices (SEPs) of the NGSS framework, where students gather evidence to support or refute claims. In another instance, Lizzy, a student helper, noticed Jayden holding the microphone very close to his face while speaking. She remarked, “This microphone is sensitive —you don’t need to be so close,”  and gently moved it away from him. Jayden immediately tested her claim by snapping his finger s near the microphone and asking Andrew, who was across the room, “can you hear this?” By using the low intensity sound of snapping and seeking confirmation, Jayden demonstrated a keen understanding of testing and validating claims through playful behavior. His actions mirrored the SEPs of planning and car rying out investigations, illustrating how playful exploration can naturally encourage scientific practices. Jayden’s engagement with the microphone in these two inst ances highlights the scientific competence that multilingual students bring to the classroom. While traditional assessments often emphasize struc tured, measurable academic outputs, moments like this underscore how play fosters opportunities for authentic scientific practices. Jayden’s voluntary will to test and validate claims reveals that students like him are not merely passive participants but active scientists, using play as a medium to explore and make sense of the world around them. This scenario aligns with Nicolopoulou and colleagu es (2009) argument that play merges imagination and rule-based inquiry, enabling students to engage in compl ex cognitive processes easily. Similarly, Vygotsky

(1978) emphasized that play creates the zone of proximal development, allowing children to operate “a head taller

than themselves” and engage in behaviors beyond their current developmental level (p.16). As educators we often overlook such moments, failing to recognize the scientific rigor embedded in students’ playful behaviors. Jayden’s interactions with the microphone demonstrate how pl ay can serve as an intrinsic driver of  student learning, allowing multilingual students to authentically eng age in science while showcasing their competence and scientific temperament.

Integrating classroom content into play During a group activity on types of energy, Diana, the teacher, used her phone to play a ringtone, eng aging Gio and Samantha’s table. “What energy is that?” she asked, prompting Samantha to respond, “The sound energy.” Diana nodded, affirming. As this discussion unfolded, Gio picked up the phone and fed its sound into the ladybug microphone. Smiling towards Andrew, the cameraman, he asked, “Do you like that sound?”  He then turned to Diana, saying, “Miss, I can talk to here [pointing to the microphone], and he can listen over there [indicating to Andrew]. Is this energy?” This moment exemplifies how children seamlessly integrate real-world elements into their play and vice versa. While the lesson focused on types of energy (such as sound, light, or kinetic energy), Gio’s question introduced the concept of energy transmission—how energy moves from one place to another (e.g., sound waves through a medium). This  concept, though not yet formally introduced, was ex plored by Gio through his playful curiosity, demonstrating his ability to connect his environment with abstract scientific ideas. Gio’s interaction with the microphone illustrates  how playful curiosity can spark scientific reasonin g. As Nicolopoulou and colleagues (2009) emphasize, play allows children to test, question, and make sense of their surroundings, often creating learning opportunities  that transcend structured academic content. In thi s case, the microphone, initially a playful object, became a probing model for energy transmission, aligning with the Science and Engineering Practices (SEPs) outlined in the NGSS framework. Acting like researchers with play Towards the end of a group activity, Diego discovered the camera and microphone left unattended in the classroom and began exploring it. He picked up the video camera and began recording Andr ew, the cameraman, declaring, “I told you I know how to record, and now I am reco rding you.” Andrew, surprised, asked, “you are recording me?” to which Diego confidently replied, “yes.” Faiza, another member of the organizing crew like Andrew, asked, “do you know how to zoom?” Diego demonstrated the zoom feature, prompting Faiza to remark, “he knows how to do it,” as the group laughed. Diego then turned to filming his friends and acting like a researcher, asking, “hey, what’s your name?” while Andrew reminded him to handle the camera with care. These moments illustrate the power of un planned tools in fostering spontaneous discovery, creative exploration, and social learning.  Presence of tools like the camera and microphone ac ted as catalysts for meaningful engagement, encouraging Diego to experiment, manipulate, and show competence. Importantly, the student transformed these tools into objects of pla y and inquiry and acted like the researchers observing the workshop. This aligns with Vygotsky’s idea that  play emerges as a way for children to act beyond th eir current developmental limitations, often fulfilling unmet desires to take on adult roles (Vygotsky, 1978). Creative expressions during play Another student, Chang, turned an activity where students were observing seeds in jars with soil and compost into a creative performance. Assuming the role of a news  anchor, he declared, “News 5! How does this jar smell?” prompting Sophia to humorously respond, “Yikes. It is not fresh. It smells like rotten oran ge.” This imaginative play transformed a routine classroom task into a dy namic and engaging interaction, allowing Chang and Sophia to express their observations creatively while taking ownership of their roles in the activity. Meanwhile, Jackie found a different form of engagement by using a plastic bottle as a makeshift launcher. He casually developed a game by squeezing the bottle against the table and then releasing it to launch into the air. His enthusiasm spread, with Armani mimicking his actions and, eventually, Roxy, Chang, Sophia, and others joining the game. Their synchronized motions reflected shared joy and collaboration, creating a rich environment for social learning and play.

These two scenarios showcase the spectrum of imaginative play, ranging from the explicit role-playing as a news anchor to the implicit self-imposed game of bottle launching. As Vygotsky (1978) and Nicolopoulou et al. (2009) note, imaginative play is structured by an imaginary situation and the rules embedded withi n it.  Both scenarios underscore the importance of viewing play  as a legitimate and transformative aspect of learn ing. Ryan

(2024) emphasizes that children’s play blurs the boundaries between f antasy and reality, creating a dynamic

interplay where abstract concepts and lived experiences meet. Recognizing the value of both explicit and implicit forms of play encourages educators to design environments that validate these diverse ways of knowing and learning. These practices, in turn, foster transformational equity spaces that bridge structured academic tasks with the imaginative and exploratory nature of childhood.

## Implications and recommendations

Play for authentic SEPs One of the key contributions of this study is demonstrating how play enhances multilingual student engagement with SEPs by fostering and reinforcing its tenets, such as experimentation, arguing with evidence, col laboration, and communication (NGSS Lead States, 2013; Ryan, 2024). For example, Jayden’s hypothesis testing with the microphone demonstrated his ability to ask questions, use empirical evidence, and validate claims—core aspects of SEPs. Similarly, Jackie’s rocket-launching game reflected iterative learning and pro blem-solving. Thus, incorporating and allowing play in STEM classrooms ensures that emergent multilingual students can engage in meaningful, inquiry-based science learning experiences while fostering their creativity, collaboration, and scientific thinking.

Play for CRT and CRP Play also supports the goals of Culturally Responsive Teachin g (CRT) and Culturally Responsive Pedagogy (CRP) by validating students’ cultural knowledge, lived experiences, and ways of engaging with science as valid (Brown-Jeffy & Cooper, 2011; Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003; Lad son-Billings, 2011). Chang’s news anchoring illustrated how play allows students to connect aca demic tasks with their imaginative identities and c ontexts. Scholars emphasize that play enables multilingual learners to express competence in ways that transcend systemic barriers, fostering inclusive learning environments  where their being and doing are valued (García & Kleifgen, 2010). Thus, curriculums, policies, and teacher education that aim to incorporate CRT and CRP must intentionally make space for play, recognizing it as a legitimate  and powerful way for all students —especially those from marginalized communities to learn.

Play for transformational equity Our study  provides empirical evidence that play creates meani ngful opportunities for multilingual students to express agency and demonstrate competence through multimodal engagement. Yet, as Ryan (2024) cautions, play is not inherently equitable; it must be implemented intentionally, with attention to the unique needs of multilingual learners and the systemic inequities they face. Edu cators and policymakers must therefore treat play n ot as a supplemental activity, but as a vital driver of tra nsformational equity in STEM education. As Vygotsky (1967) observed, “Play is the source of development and creates the Zone of Proximal Development” (p. 16). Extending this insight, we introduce the Zone of Playful Deve lopment (ZPlayD) —a specific form of the ZPD in which learners expand their capabilities through imaginat ive, embodied, and culturally grounded engagement. ZPlayD positions play as more than engagement: it reframes  it as a powerful mechanism for equity, authentic l earning, and joyful growth.
