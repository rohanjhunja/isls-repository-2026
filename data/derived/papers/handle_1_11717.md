# Reframing Competence: Science Learning as Relational and Embodied

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The study examines how competence in science emerged as a relational and interactional process during joint inquiry between multilingual students and their teacher in a two-week STEAM program. Focusing on investigations of osmosis and road salt runoff, we explore how everyday knowledge, gesture, silence, and peer collaboration contributed to shared sense-making. Through micro-interaction analysis, we highlight how multimodal participation enabled students to be repositioned as knowers and doers of science, fostering dignity, engagement, and epistemic agency in science classrooms.

## Introduction

Multilingual youth from non-dominant communities often experience science classrooms as exclusionary spaces where their everyday knowledge and cultural practic es are rendered invisible or undervalued. These exc lusions are not accidental but reflect broader societal ine quities mirrored within educational institutions (E spinoza et al., 2020; Morales-Doyle, 2017). As Warren et al. (2001) note, youth from minoritized communities often make sense of scientific phenomena through everyday language a nd informal practices —forms of engagement frequently dismissed within traditional, monolingual conceptio ns of science learning. This study explores how multilingual youth engaged in a two-week STEAM program in Southern Massachusetts, inves tigating osmosis and the ecological impacts of road salt runoff. The learnin g environment was designed to connect students' liv ed experiences to disciplinary practices, emphasizing the integration of everyday knowledge, embodied participation, and collaborative sensemaking. Using a sociocultura l lens, we see how competence is not merely an individual attribute, but a co-constructed, relational process shaped through mult imodal interaction. Our work builds upon and contributes to ongoing conversations within the  learning sciences that challenge deficit perspecti ves and reframe what counts as engagement and knowledge in science education (McDermott & Raley, 2011; Rosebery et al., 2010). Rather than attempting to validate t he presence of competence in multilingual students through dominant standards, we seek to illuminate the practices, discourses, and pedagogical conditions that a llow competence to emerge and be recognized in its many forms.

## Theoretical framework

This study is grounded in a sociocultural theoretic al framework, which views learning as a socially me diated activity shaped by cultural, social, and historical  contexts (Rogoff, 2003; Vygotsky, 1978). From this perspective, competence is not a fixed individual t rait but an emergent attribute of participation in socially situated activities (Engeström, 1993; Greeno, 2006; Lemke, 1990). Learning is understood as relational, where participants co-construct knowledge through reciprocal interactions, assuming reversible roles as knowers and learners (Dominguez, 2019). We frame science learning environments as dynamic activity s ystems where multilingual students and teachers engage in joint sense-making and problem-solving through diverse discursive, embodied, and coordinated practice (Kayumova & Kahveci, 2023; Kayumova & Sengupta, 2022). Central to this framework is the recognition of div erse ways of knowing  and participation, including non-verbal forms such as attentive observation, gesture, and silence, which challenge traditional, speech-centered definitions of engagement (Paradise & Rogoff, 2009). This approach aligns with the concept of dignity restoration (Espinoza & Vossoughi, 20 14), emphasizing learning environments as sites where  students’ intellectual and cultural contributions are validat ed, fostering equitable and justice-oriented education. This lens informs the study’s focus on how competence is  socially constructed through classroom interaction s, repositioning multilingual learners as active knowe rs and doers of science, and challenging dominant paradigms that perpetuate educational inequities.

## Method

This study took place during a two-week summer STEAM program in Southern Massachusetts  that brought together multilingual youth from racially and lingu istically minoritized communities. Students engaged  in inquiry-based investigations of osmosis using chicken eggs and explored environmental pollution through the lens of road salt runoff and its ecological impacts.  Our data set is drawn from five consecutive days of  classroom interactions facilitated by a life science teacher,  Debora. Data include over 12 ho urs of classroom video, student artifacts (notebooks, visual documentation), and field notes. We focused on the interactions of two focal students, Graciano and Eduardo. Using micro-interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995), we  examined how competence was collaboratively constructed through student-teacher and peer interactions, with attention to both verbal and non-verbal practices.

## Findings

To explore how science concepts intersect with real-world issues, students investigated the effects of road salt runoff using two hands-on activities: one that demonstrated osmosis using eggs and vinegar, and another that tested brine shrimp egg viability in salty water. T hrough these experiences, multilingual students lik e Graciano and Eduardo engaged in sense-making practices rooted in their everyday knowledge. In the first case, Debora asked Eduardo if he wanted to set up the experiment. When he remained silent, she turned to Graciano: “Can you explain why you're doing it?” Recognizing Graciano as an “ old-timer” who had prior experience with the experiment, Debora strategically repositioned him a s a knowledgeable other. Graciano responded confidently, “You need to separate the things that are inside th e egg from the hard shell.” When Eduardo offered, “ by boiling?”—drawing on his everyday knowledge—Graciano didn’t correct hi s answer or signal that his response is wrong but extended the analogy: “You know how like eggs have babies in them, right?” This shift from cooking to biology, framed through accessible language, sho wed Graciano’s ability to bridge informal and forma l reasoning. Throughout the interaction, Eduardo remained mostly  silent but deeply engaged. He watched attentively, took notes, and participated through e mbodied practices. His written observations in his notebook demonstrated care and reflection, complementing Gra ciano’s verbal explanations. Rather than frame Eduardo’ s quietness as disengagement, we viewed it as a valid mode of participation that contributed to their shared inquiry. In a second exchange, Debora supported Graciano in reasoning through the concept of osmosis during an observation of eggs immersed in vinegar and corn sy rup. She asked, “Do you remember what I called it?” prompting Graciano to recall, “Membrane.” When she explained that “the membrane allowed for the vinega r to go inside,” Graciano’s recognition of the liquid tr ansfer supported his evolving understanding. Later,  prompted to guess why the egg was lighter, he first said, “the yolk” before landing on “the water?” and confirming, “It came out of the egg.” Debora responded emotionally, “You’re giving me goosebumps.” Her visible excitement affirmed Graciano’s scientific reasoning and helped position  him as a capable knower. A third case involved a procedural error in the brine shrimp experiment. One slide was  left covered by paper, limiting visibility. Instea d of labeling this a mistake, Debora reframed it: “That’s what scientists do… we call it a possible source of error.” She guided students to record the oversight, modeling authenti c scientific practices and inviting students to ana lyze results with awareness of variability. Eduardo and Graciano  nodded, taking the task seriously. Here, Debora po sitioned uncertainty as an expected part of inquiry and made space for students to author their own understandings. Across these moments, competence emerged not from individu al mastery or verbal fluency, but from collaborativ e efforts, embodied presence, and the weaving of pers onal-everyday experience with disciplinary knowledge. Graciano’s use of analogies and Eduardo’s quiet foc us contributed equally to the inquiry. Debora’s rol e as facilitator—at times prompting with known answers, at others cr eating room for exploration —reveals the complexity of scaffolding student agency in multilingual  classrooms.  These findings suggest that when classrooms recognize diverse forms of participation —including observation, analogy, and error —they become more inclusive of the full range of student experti se. In this setting, students were not only learnin g science but becoming recognized as scientists.

## Discussion and scholarly significance

This study reframed science learning as a relationa l, socially mediated process in which competence em erged through shared, multimodal participation. Through c lassroom interactions rooted in real-world inquiry, multilingual students demonstrated scientific reasoning by drawing on everyday knowledge, gesture, silence, and analogical thinking. Rather than proving competence  through dominant norms, we illuminated how it was coconstructed and recognized in culturally sustaining  ways. These findings pointed to the need for pedag ogical practices that honored diverse epistemic resources,  affirmed student dignity, and expanded what counte d as knowing and doing science.
