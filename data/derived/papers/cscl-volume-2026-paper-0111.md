# Complementary Roles of Collaborative Reflection and AI-generated Feedback to Support Students' Science Writing

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Automated assessment can provide timely feedback on students’ writing, but students often struggle to interpret and apply feedback effectively. This study examined the extent to which collaborative reflection activities can support students to interpret, evaluate, and act on automated feedback to improve their scientific explanations.

Complementary Roles of Collaborative Reflection and AI-generated Feedback to Support Students' Science Writing Indrani Dey, University of Wisconsin–Madison, idey2@wisc.edu ChanMin Kim, Pennsylvania State University, cmk604@psu.edu Sadhana Puntambekar, University of Wisconsin–Madison, puntambekar@education.wisc.edu Abstract: Automated assessment can provide timely  feedback on students’ writing, but students often struggle to interpret and apply feedback effectively. This study examined the extent to which collaborative reflection activities can support students to interpret, evaluate, and act on automated feedback to improve their scientific explanations. AI-based assessment of student writing can provide timely feedback (Celik et al., 2022), but research shows that relying on such feedback alone may limit students’ metacognitive engagement (Besigomwe, 2025), selfregulation (Fan et al., 2025), and ability to interpret and apply feedback effectively (Dey et al., 2024; Puntambekar et al., 2023). Thus, AI feedback by itself is often insufficient to support deep conceptual understanding or meaningful revision without complementary human-centered support(s). Collaborative reflection and discourse offer such support by engaging students in cognitive (Kollar & Fisher, 2010) and regulatory (Panadero et al.,

2015) processes that support their metacognitive development (Wiedbusch et al., 2022). By critically evaluating

peers’ work, students can identify gaps in understanding, recognize strengths, and consider alternative approaches (Gerard & Linn, 2022). I ntegrating AI-generated feedback with collaborative reflection can create a synergistic human-AI partnership, in which AI provides diagnostic input and collaborative reflection scaffolds interpretation, evaluation, and application of feedback, thus forming an ecosystem of support (Puntambekar et al., 2024). While prior studies have examined AI-based feedback and collaborative reflection, less is known about the extent to which collaborative reflection serves as a metacognitive scaffold that supports students’ use of AI-generated feedback for revision. Our study explores the extent to which collaborative reflection activities support students in meaningfully revising their scientific explanations. Our research questions were:

1. What issues do students identify and discuss in sample essays during collaborative reflection?

2. To what extent do students’ reflection of AI-generated feedback translate into revisions in their own essays?

Students (n=31) from two classes taught by the same teacher at a Midwestern US school participated in this study. Students engaged in a design-based physics unit on motion, forces, and energy, conducting virtual experiments and writing essays in a digital notebook. Once students submitted their essays, PyrEval (Gao et al., 201 9; Passonneau et al., 2018), an NLP tool, evaluated them for the presence of six science concepts. PyrEval uses a wise-crowd model, comparing student writing to expert-generated refere nce essays by constructing weighted vector representations of concept s and their similarity. The automated f eedback indicated whether each concept was detected or not  in each essay. To support automated feedback interpretation, we implemented participatory structures (Tabak & Baumgartner, 2004), including collaborative group reflection (Kollar & Fischer, 2010) and teacher guidance. Students collaboratively reviewed three anonymous sample essays with PyrEval feedback and noted, in individual reflection worksheets, whether ideas were correctly identified, why they were detected or missed, and how the writing could be improved. Students then revised their own essays using automated feedback. Data Sources and Analysis Data came from three sources: (ii) students’ reflection worksheets, (ii) approximately 125 minutes of video across six groups during their collaborative reflection activity, and (iii) students’ initial and revised essays. We inductively and deductively identified four themes from students’ discussion and annotations of sample essays in their reflection worksheets: (i) missing ideas, (ii) evidence to support claims, (iii) refinement of explanations, and (iv) writing quality. Each theme was coded as 1 (identified) or 0 (not identified). We then applied the same four themes to examine whether students addressed these same issues in their own essays during revision. Revisions were coded as 1 (addressed, i.e, one or more ideas needed to be addressed 653 and at least one idea was addressed), –1 (did not address, i.e., one or more ideas needed to be addressed but none were addressed), or 0 (did not need to be addressed).

Issues identified by students during collaborative reflection We identified four  recurring issues from student reflection worksheets and group discussions. The reflection worksheets showed that all students identified missing ideas  (100%), and most noted the need to add evidence (87.1%), improve explanations (96.8%), and overall writing quality (87.1%) (see Figure 1a). Issues addressed by students in their own essays We examined the proportion of students who identified issues in sample essays (Figure 1a) to those who addressed them in their revisions  (Figure 1b). Students were more successful at identifying issues in others’ writing than addressing them during their own revisions. The most common revisions involved refining explanations (0.70) and adding missing ideas (0.55), suggesting students were more comfortable elaborating on  existing ideas or incorporating identified missing ideas. Although most students recognized the need for supporting evidence in sample essays (0.87), many did not add evidence to their own writing when needed (0.78). Similarly, while students readily identified writing quality issues in others’ essays, applying these insights to their own writing remained challenging, with about one-third (0.37) failing to revise clarity or repetition issues.
