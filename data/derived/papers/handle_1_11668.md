## Introduction

In 2021, Pinkard, Martin, and Jones argued for “eng aging in research and design at a macro level" (p. 753), articulating a need for methods that could support city-wide efforts to understand and transform access to learning opportunities. Opportunity Landscaping (Pinkard et al., 2025) is derived from this call. It involves making visible the collective array of resources and opportunities  available within a specific geographical area or c ommunity— the opportunity landscape. These landscapes encompass the educational, social, and economic supports available within a local learning ecosystem, as well as the b arriers to accessing those resources.  Opportunity Landscaping demands methodological approaches that can illuminate and enhance these landscapes ov er time. That includes mapping resources to assess accessibility and relev ance; leveraging shared grammar to structure data c ollection; using visualizations to reveal patterns and inform decision-making; and dismantling silos that isolate people a nd programs. Central to this approach is engaging comm unity stakeholders —educators, families, policymakers, youth—in co-designing representations and modeling potential futures for local ecosystems. While Pinkard et al.

(2025) outlines the conceptual and infrastructural foundations of Opportunity Landscaping, this poster focuses on

the methodological approaches required to enact it. We describe the rationale for an elastic, participatory research framework and preview three methods developed to su pport it. Each method is previewed here and explored in greater depth in papers presented at  ISLS (Liu et al., 2024; Green, 2025), and a third method described in a forthcoming publication.

## Designing methods for opportunity landscaping

Opportunity Landscaping unfolds within systems that are simultaneously formal and informal, visible and hidden, stable and transient. Learning ecosystems are by na ture in flux —a constellation of institutions, relationships, programs, and opportunities that  shift over time and space.  In order to properly document learning ecosystems, Opportunity Landscaping must be rigid enough to provide structure to the tumult, ye t flexible enough to capture it. We introduce here  methods for Opportunity Landscaping that were developed to respond to a) communitycentered use and research analysis, b) engagement with youth movement, infrastructure, and relational networks—often simultaneously, c) scaffolding of shared data interpretation across highly diverse st akeholders, and d) alignment with a grammar of opportunity land scaping (Pinkard et al., 2025) that links data categories to meaningful questions and use cases.  Our framework foregrounds participatory methods and  recognizes that Opportunity Landscaping hinges on the connective tissue of relationships and support systems that help youth and adults design, navigate, and reshape their local OST learning environments (Chew et al., 2023). Communityled data engagement offers insights often missed by formal systems and increases local buy-in for change. Rather than capturing a landscape solely for external anal ysis, these methods are structured to make data usable and meaningful for the communities that generate it.

## Previewing an elastic suite of methods

Our current research includes many approaches, and here we highlight three methods, each designed to illuminate different dimensions of a community’s Opportunity Landscape. We preview them here to show how they work in concert —shaped by shared grammar, participatory commitments, and attention t o system-level visibility. Across these methods, data categories —such as “location,” “barrier,” or “collaboration” —are co-defined with partners and embedded in our Opportunity Landscape grammar (Pinkard et al., 2025).  Each method not only gathers data but enables stakeholders to see and re shape their learning ecosystem, ensuring that analy sis remains actionable, situated, and co-owned. Our poster will provide visualizations and in-app screenshots of each of these methods.

The first method (Green, 2025) centers youth movement and perception. Drawing on participatory geocapture tools and reflective tagging protocols, our socio-spatial mapping approach invites youth to document their out-of-school-time learning over time, capturing not only where they go  to learn, but how they move, what spaces feel safe, and what opportunities resonate with their goals and identities. These spatial biographies are then aggregated and visualized alongside infrastructural data, providing new insight into how youth actually experience their local landscapes and where interventions might be most impactful. The second method (Liu et al, 2024) uses social network analysis to foreground ecosystem structure. By mapping collaboration and referral relationships be tween OST providers, we reveal not only who is conn ected, but which organizations serve as linchpins—providing unique services that are not redundantly accessible within their subnetwork. The “linchpin score” highlights infrastructural vulnerabilities and helps guide both funding and coordination efforts.

The third method  (forthcoming) focuses on ethnographic study of interactions between community stakeholders and examines how they make sense of Opportunity Landscape data in real-time decision-making. By analyzing data use within community conversations, this work surfaces the a ffordances and limits of Opportunity Landscaping tools and contributes to th e design of future-facing routines, dashboards, and data scaffolds. It is particularly attentive to equity in who speaks, who decides, and how data comes to matter in shared planning contexts.

## Conclusion

We offer this preview of in-development methods to underscore the intentionalit y with which Opportunity Landscaping can—and must—be conducted. These methods are not standalone tool s, but parts of an iterative, cyclical process: community conversations shape research questions; those questions inform our shared grammar; the grammar structures data collection; and data co llection lays the foundation for analysis. Analyses then return to communities, informing new rounds of dialogue, design, and inquiry. This process is rarely linear. Ethnographic study may follow data collection or precede it; socio-spatial mapping may inform network analysis or vice versa. Once a framework is laid, researchers and communities can move fluidly across stages, guided by both need and relationship. Critically, data cannot drive Opportunity Landscapi ng without participatory and community-informed interpretation. Representations of the landscape must resonate with lived experience, and com munity members must be equipped with the tools and confide nce to use the data themselves. A campaign to expand opportunity is ineffectual if it bypasses those most affected by its outcomes. It is within this context that we write: Opportunity Landscaping is not only a methodologica l contribution, but a call to center community voic e in the pursuit of systems-level change.
