# Theory Experience Network Analysis of Primary Source Projects

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study introduces theory experience network analysis (TENA), a proof-of-concept method for examining how principles from multiple learning theories manifest and interact within student learning experiences. We analyzed reflections from students in a mathematics course. Network analysis revealed four distinct clusters representing social constructivist, transformative learning, constructionist, and cognitive constructivist principles while demonstrating that theoretical principles operate as interconnect ed rather than isolated mechanisms. TENA offers an approach for understanding theory-practice relationships in complex learning environments.

The learning sciences recognizes that effective instructional design must be grounded in explicit theoretical principles (Barab & Squire, 2004; Sandoval, 2014). The complexity of learning is such that no single theory is sufficient, and multiple theories a re necessary for designing rich and effective learning experiences and environments (Danish & Gresalfi, 2018). The learning sciences literature investigates theories and how principles from those theories play out in practice (Fischer et al., 2018; Nasir et al., 2020; Sawyer, 2022). Decades of work on project-based learning, for example, have shown how principles from situated cognition (Brown et al., 1989), social constructivism (Vygotsky, 1978), constructionism (Papert & Harel, 1991), and cognitive constructivism (Piaget, 1952) interact in practice (Krajcik & Shin, 2022). While learning designs embed multiple theoretical commitments, how those principles actually manifest and interact within students’ lived experiences remains underexplored. Existing analytical approaches tend to evaluate learning environments through a single theoretical lens. This study addresses that gap by introducing theory experience network analysis (TENA), a proof-of-concept method for mapping how principles from multiple learning theories appear in and relate to one another within student learning experiences. TENA treats theories as complementary, multidimensional lenses for understanding the complexity of learning. Our research question asks how principles from multiple learning theories manifest and interact within students’ reflections on their le arning experiences. The study context is the TRansforming Instruction in Undergraduate Mathematics via Primary Historical Sources  (TRIUMPHS) project (National Science Foundation, 2015), which produced over 100 Primary Source Projects (PSPs) for undergraduate mathematics (Barnett et al., 2014). PSPs engage students with excerpts from original historical texts and ask them to work through mathematical problems as they were originally encountered. Students interpret unfamiliar notation, reason within developing conceptual frameworks, and experience mathematics as an evolving, culturally situated discipline (Jankvist, 2014; Massa-Esteve, 2025). Clark and colleagues (2022) have documented how students working with historical sources develop approaches to mathematical reasoning that extend beyond initial theoretical predictions.  Our analytical framework draws on theories recognized within the learning sciences. These include cognitive constructivism (Piaget, 1952), which describes how individuals build knowledge through active engagement. Social constructivism (Vygotsky, 1978) def ines learning as a collaborative process where knowledge is co-constructed through social interaction and scaffolding. Constructionism (Papert & Harel, 1991) holds that deep learning occurs when people are actively making tangible artifacts that hold personal meaning, a process that often involves productive failure (Kapur, 2014). Transformative learning theory (Mezirow, 2000) frames learning as a fundamental change in beliefs and ways of knowing, often prompted by cognitive dissonance. The dynamic systems model of role identity (Kaplan & Garner, 2017) treats identity as an evolving system shaped by the learning process (Donaldson & Allen-Handy, 2023; Wenger, 2009). Metacognitive theory (Winne & Azevedo, 2022) explains how learners reflect on and regulate their own thinking. These theories were selected because they each offer distinct but potentially overlapping lenses on the learning process, and principles derived from them were operationalized as qualitative codes for the analysis. This study is part of a multi-part design-based research project (Barab & Squire, 2004) investigating how students learn complex mathematical concepts using historical sources. We developed TENA as a method derived from learning experience network analysis, or LENA (Donaldson et al., 2024). TENA combines qualitative coding and network analysis with a specific focus on how learners experience principles derived from various theories and 2830 how those principles relate to one another in practice. Data were collected at the end of Fall 2024 in a History of Mathematics course at a research-intensive university in the southeastern United States. Eighteen students participated, representing diverse disciplinary backgrounds with seven mathematics majors, six computer science majors, and five engineering majors. The course used nine PSPs as its sole teaching materials rather than a traditional textbook. Students worked through seven complete PSPs and engaged with two others in abbreviated form. Student reflections collected at multiple points throughout the course served as the unit of analysis. Two researchers independently coded each reflection to identify instances of learning theory principles, then discussed and resolved disagreements through consensus. Codes were operationalized at the principle level, with examples including zone of proximal development, productive failure, and cognitive dissonance. After coding, a correlation matrix was created  from co-occurrences of each theory principle code across all reflections. These correlations were visualized as a network map, with nodes resized based on betweenness centrality values. Subgroups were identified using Girvan-Newman cluster analysis (Girva n & Newman, 2002), allowing us to identify patterns in how theoretical principles were experienced concurrently. TENA analysis of correlations at p  <.001 produced a map with four clusters and a modularity of Q  =.435, indicating meaningful community structure in the network. The first cluster centers on the social dimensions of learning. Students described how scaffolding from instructors and peers helped them stretch beyond what they could accomplish alone. They emphasized co-creating shared meaning with classmates and discovering the real-world relevance of tasks, painting a picture of learning as collaborative, tool-mediated activity in the zone of proximal development. This cluster reflects social constructi vist learning theory, with support from a constructionist principle (relevance exploration). The second cluster captures moments of transformative learning. Students recounted experiences of cognitive dissonance that prompted deep questioning of prior assumptions, active engagement with others’ perspectives, and the gradual development of new ways of thinking and interacting with the world. Learners described consolidating what they had discovered while remaining open to further revision, an identity-focused, iterative process. This cluster illustrates transformative learning theory and the dynamic systems model of role identity (DSMRI), supported by a cognitive constructivist principle (knowledge consolidation) and a social constructivist principle (engaging with other perspectives). The third cluster focused on the constructive and reflective dimensions of learning. Students described engaging with ideas through multiple pathways, experiencing productive failure, and creating artifacts that embodied their understanding and served as focal points for feedback and further refinement. This creates a feedback loop in which constructing artifacts fuels the construction of understanding. The theoretical core of this cluster is constructionism, supported by cognitive constructivist and transformative learning principles. The fourth cluster highlighted individual sense-making and self-regulation. Students activated prior knowledge, integrated new information, applied insights in new contexts, and engaged in deliberate reflection on their own thinking as explained by cognitive constructivist learning theory. The transformative learning cluster functions as a hub, with each surrounding cluster connecting to it through shared principles. Betweenness centrality analysis revealed that engaging with others’ perspectives scored highest (121), followed by collaborative knowledge construction (100), productive failure (92), and zone of proximal development through peer learning (89). These high-centrality nodes bridge the social and reflective dimensions of the network, suggesting that the learning ecology depends on these connecting practices. This proof-of-concept study demonstrates that TENA can reveal meaningful patterns in how theoretical principles co-occur and interact within student learning experiences. The four clusters correspond to recognized theoretical traditions, yet the connection s between clusters show that principles from different theories do not operate in isolation. High-centrality nodes, particularly engaging with others’ perspectives and collaborative knowledge construction, bridge multiple clusters, suggesting these practic es serve as connective tissue across theoretical boundaries. These findings support the viability of using network analysis to examine theory-practice relationships in multi-theoretical learning environments. The results demonstrate that students’ learning experiences involve complex interactions among theoretical principles in ways that can be systematically mapped and that learning theories can be treated as complementary systems of ideas rather than competing explanatory frameworks. The identification of high-centrality practices also points toward aspects of the learning ecology that may be especially important to support in the design of learning experiences and environments. As a proof of concept, this study has limitations. The coding procedures requi re further documentation and validation. Future work should provide more detailed coding documentation, illustrative examples from student data, interrater reliability measures, and applications across diverse learning contexts to strengthen the methodology and its transferability.
