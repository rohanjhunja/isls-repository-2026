# Using Synthetic Knowledge and Interaction Analysis to Investigate Learning in a Collaborative Digital Climate Learning Experience

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents a theoretical argument for investigating moment-to-moment learning processes around socio-scientific issues (SSIs), or complex, often ambiguous issues that intertwine science and society. It argues that prior theories of learning are not sufficient to explain learning that takes place through engagement with SSIs because of the characteristically modern nature of such issues. We argue that applying synthetic knowledge and interaction analysis can advance theory in this area. In particular, we pay close attention to the science learning that unfolds around SSIs, and we argue that designing effective science learning experiences around such issues first merits thorough investigation of moment-to-moment learning processes. We present empirical evidence from a study of a museum-based collaborative learning experience via a digital simulation about climate change to show the complexities of learning about SSIs and, in doing so, take initial steps towards advancing learning theory around SSIs.

## Introduction

In a time of rapid climatic change, global pandemic, and social unrest catalyzed by these far-reaching  socioscientific issues (SSIs), the need for well-designe d learning experiences about such issues is great. Places of science education, in particular, from K-12 classro oms to informal learning centers, are seeking to im plement learning experiences about climate change, COVID-19, and other modern SSIs (e.g. Owens et al., 2017). However, while research in science education has examined in structional approaches for SSIs, few have investiga ted the moment-to-moment learning processes that take place  as learners participate in such instructional expe riences (Christodoulou et al., 2021). Yet, we argue that attention to these processes is critical when considering the design of new experiences. Furthermore, we argue that give n the nature of characteristically modern SSIs like  climate change, existing theories of learning fall short in explaining learning around them, particularly when considering science learning, because such issues create a novel intersection of science knowledge, identity, values, and risk assessment not previously accounted for with learning theory. Thus, this paper draws together multiple theories to argue for new research in this field. We then ta ke a step towards advancing such theory through an empirical investigation of an experience in an informal science education center in which participants collaboratively engage in a digital climate simulation to learn about climate change.

## Background: Learning through socio-scientific issues

Science education scholars have argued for instruction centered on socio-scientific issues (SSIs)—complex and often ambiguous issues that bridge science and society and demand moral consideration in addition to scientific reasoning (e.g. Sadler, 2009; Zeidler et al., 2005)—such as climate change and genetic engineering. Research has shown that SSI instruction can support science know ledge gains (Klosterman & Sadler, 2010), engagement  in science practices, such as modeling (Ke et al., 2021), in concert with moral development (Fowler et al., 2009). From this work have emerged important pedagogical models for SSIs, as well as cogent claims about the need to adapt science instruction to take up SSIs. Yet, missing is empirically grounded theory to expl ain the learning processes that occur when students engage with SSIs. Studies in the SSI genre tend to measure gains in knowledge and in other indicators of reasoning through pre-and post-instruction assessments (e.g., Romine et al., 2017). While this work has established clear ideas about what can change within learners through SSI instruction, there is little understanding of how that learning process unfolds (Christodoulou et al., 2021). What knowledge and resources students draw on and how various changes in context might influence learning trajectories remain largely unexamined; theory to explain the learning taking place within SSI-oriented learning experiences remain underdeveloped. This dearth of theory is particularly concerning given the nature of SSIs. They are not traditional science topics, around which an array of explanator y learning theories exist s. Rather, SSIs often reflect “unfinished” contemporary science, or science in the making. Such issues are often touchpoints of cont roversy, eliciting strong emotional responses and triggering  aspects of identity (e.g., Druckman & McGrath, 201 9). Furthermore, recent worked has hinted at the comple xity of SSI learning processes, showing that learni ng outcomes are influenced by much more than knowledge gains, and that other factors, such as cultural worldview can interact with knowledge gains in surprising ways (Author et al., 2021). Drawing on this idea, we a rgue that ICLS2022 Proceedings 393 © ISLS existing theory cannot adequately explain the complexity of learning involved when students confront SSIs. Thus, with this study, we offer an investigation into the  moment-to-moment processes of learning involved wh en confronting these issues with the aim of advancing and deepening theory related to learning through SSIs. However, this is not to say that no work progressing towards learning theory has been done. Walsh and Tsurusaki (2018) drew on sociocultural theories of identity to show how students’ negotiation of their dissonant political and classroom identities influenced learning during an instructional unit on climate change over several weeks. Investigating on a much finer timescale, Phi lip and colleagues (2018) drew on theories of ideol ogy and positioning to document how ideology, as made material through social interactions, influenced learning within one session of a university-level engineering class studying drone warfare. Taken together, these studies suggest that learning about SSIs is highly complex and invo lves a large number of likely intertwined variables, such as identity and ideology, more than perhaps can be captured through traditional knowledge assessments.

## Theoretical Framework

To advance theories of learning around SSIs, we approach this study with a multifaceted theoretical framework. This framework aims to capture nuances of why learning of SSIs is likely inexplicable fully by current theories. Additionally, in illuminating the theoretical under pinnings with which we approach this study, the fol lowing framework guides the methodology and analysis presented here. Modernity & risk SSIs are complex and often ambiguous, holding different implications for diverse stakeholders (e.g. Sadler, 2009; Zeidler et al., 2019). Our argument adds that such issues are products of late modernity (Giddens, 1991), which is characterized by abstract knowledge systems typi cally inaccessible to most people. These abstract s ystems allow for the creation of territories of future possibilities, or in Giddens’ words, the “colonization of the future” (p. 111). For example, transforming fossil fuels in to the sorts of energy that power our homes, vehicl es, and buildings relies on a complex network of abstract k nowledge systems, from petroleum geology to electri cal engineering. It is this very network of knowledge s ystems that then mediates our long-term creation of  future possibilities. These systems and associated coloniz ation of the future bring both heightened security and risk. Daily lives are relatively safe, compared to history, yet the risks that do exist are high consequence. For example, with reliable energy sources made possible through knowledge systems related to fossil fuels also come s potentially catastrophic shifts in climate. Such hi gh consequence risks, and individuals’ navigation o f them, is intertwined with identity negotiation in the modern age, Giddens argued. We argue for the development of new theories of learning around SSIs characteristic of late modernity, such as climate change. Climate change embodies the  sort of high consequence risk inherent to colonizi ng the future, precipitated by decades of fossil fuel combustion in attempts to secure and stabilize daily life. Furthermore, like typical issues of late modernity, in Giddens’ view, climate change is characterized by shifting k nowledge claims from experts. Expert knowledge about modern issues is tenuous, as the issues themselves are eme rgent and fluid. Expert claims around such emergent issues and within related knowledge systems are often shifting and unstable. Therefore, connecting non-experts to such fluid and uncertain knowledge is inherently fraught. Recent sociopolitical history of climate change epitomizes  this nature of modern knowledge, with persistent c onflict around climate change within US news media (e.g. Bolin & Hamilton, 2018). Shifting claims from the scientific community (albeit some deliberately manufactured vi a funding from the fossil fuel industry, see Oreske s & Conway, 2011) have made public actions controversial and identity provoking. The fluid nature of expert-produced scientific know ledge around climate change has real implications for the ways in which learners make sense of such a n issue. How people draw on scientific knowledge and practices in conjunction with other resources when learning about climate change is likely not able to be captured by current theories of learning. Predictably, prior  work on climate change education has shown that an  often complex set of factors influence the learning proce ss, including cultural worldview (Author et al., 20 21), social norms (Busch et al., 2019), political identity (Wal sh & Tsurusaki, 2018), in addition to science knowl edge (Stevenson et al., 2014), with research only beginn ing to parse these influences. Investigating learni ng around climate change can provide new insight into how learners draw on science within characteristically modern issues. Synthetic knowledge & interaction analysis To understand learning, this study uses an approach that integrates theoretical underpinnings of both knowledge analysis (KA) and interaction analysis (IA), as cal led for by scholars in these previously disparate t raditions (Brown et al., 2016). Undergirding KA is the theory of knowledge in pieces (KiP; diSessa, 1993), which suggests that individuals’ knowledge systems are composed of  various “pieces”, also referred to as resources (H ammer, 2000), or knowledge elements that learners used to make sense of various situations and compose larger ICLS2022 Proceedings 394 © ISLS knowledge systems. Knowledge pieces are highly dependent on context, activated by some contexts but no t by others. The varied and dynamic nature of these elem ents leads to complex reasoning that can shift quic kly moment-to-moment, as learners draw on a multitude of knowledge resources in sensemaking. In parallel, undergirding IA are theories that emph asize the significance of the social space in the emergence of knowledge (e.g. Hall & Stevens, 2016).  Various contexts created through the constantly sh ifting social space allow for certain ideas or pieces of knowledge to emerge. And, the emergence of various knowledge pieces and consequent responses to them influence t he course of social interaction. Taking up ideas of  scholars seeking to integrate KA and IA (e.g. Azevedo & Lee,  2016), this study recognizes the importance of an individual’s knowledge resources (and, by extension, the influence of context on emergence of different elements) while also recognizing that the bounds of knowledge extend into the social space and the practices enc ountered socially. An individual is not a solitary being functioning on their own; rather, the contexts and social situations surrounding them influence their cognitive work. So, this study adopts the view that learners draw on knowledge elements in context-dependent ways within a learning experience, yet also that the social situation of that learner deeply influences that process.

Applied here, this theoretical view suggests that learning about characteristically modern SSIs will likely involve knowledge pieces traditionally seen as “scientific” about relevant science concepts (e.g. the greenhouse effect), as well as knowledge pieces about societal aspects of the particular issue. Furthermore, within the context of learning in a social space, various social positionings, practices, and resources will influence what knowledge elements emerge, are treated as valid, and consequently get taken up and use by others in a group. Ideology An ideology is a shared system of representation through which humans act, speak, and participate in the social and material world (Althusser, 1971; Hall, 1985). Such a system guides the assumptions that people use to make meaning; the ideology in place influences the meani ng made from an experience. Ideology comes to have “material” existence through human action and speec h; an ideology exists to the extent that it is insc ribed in practices. Recent work in learning sciences has tak en up ideology as a theoretical lens, demonstrating  that the making material of ideology by students in classroo m discourse influences learning that ensues (Philip  et al., 2018). To understand ideology, this work has applied a KiP approach (diSessa, 1993), showing that natu ralized axioms, or “pieces” of ideology emerge in context-dependent ways to influence learning (Philip, 2011). As with an integrated KA-IA views, context is critical to t he emergence of such pieces—certain contexts make s alient certain pieces of ideology, or naturalized axioms, while others do not. Given the focus of this study on modern issues at the interface of science and society, we are particularly interested ideologies about the distribution of goo ds in society related to our colonization of the fu ture, such as energy resources. We argue that these ideologies likely influence the meaning making that surrounds such goods, and, further, that this influence extends to the sc ientific meaning making scaffolded by particular le arning opportunities around such issues. Prior research supports this argument, showing that the discourse students used for scientific sensemaking in a US science classroom was at the same time ideological and served in reproducing ideologies about the distribution of goods in socie ty (Author, 2021; Author, in review). Thus, to this  study, we bring a theoretical lens that integrates a KA-IA approach with notions of ideology to understand learning around characteristic modern, risk-laden issues. We argue that this novel approach can expand theories of lea rning to offer fuller explanatory accounts of the learning that takes place of characteristically modern SSIs.

## Research questions

1. What knowledge, social, and ideological resources do learners draw on while participating in a climate

change digital simulation learning experience?

## Methods

Stemming from the theoretical commitments described above, this study employed methods from KA and IA (e.g. diSessa et al., 2016; Hall & Stevens, 2016) to unde rstand moment-to-moment processes within a collabor ative digital learning experience about climate change.

## Context, participants, & data collection

Data were collected at a science museum affiliated with a large, public university in the US. Data col lection occurred within one of the museum’s galleries, wher e a digital climate simulation is installed. Visito rs interact with this simulation in a partially closed room, with up to five visitors participating at once. Each player sits at a console with an individual touchscreen. One big scr een is projected onto the wall in front of the play ers. The ICLS2022 Proceedings 395 © ISLS projection shows the simulated city and the overall  progress of the simulation, including when and whe re each player chooses to build or demolish something. The simulated city is made to resemble the local landscape of the museum, and its name references the region.

The simulation includes six rounds of 90 seconds ea ch. During each round, each player is given four options for actions and can pick two actions to ena ct. Specific options are unique to each player and are randomized. Potential options include constructing various energy sources (e.g. coal plant, solar farm), agricultural sources (e.g. alfalfa farm, bison farm), or recreational opportunities (e.g. amusement park). Options could also include demolishing structures or installing geoengineering devices, such as artificial trees. At the end of each round, players are directed to look up at t he projection to see how their city has changed. Af ter each player’s decisions are pointed out, the projection shows whether the group achieved a set of goals for the round, including goals for: 1) energy production, 2) food production, and 3) recreation opportunities (“fun produced”). Then, it shows how the group’s actions affected the climate by using a bar indicator that ranges from 0 to 10, with 10 indicating extreme climate change. At the end of some rounds, depending on the degree to which the climate has changed, there are catastrophic natural disasters, such as drought, wildfire, dust storms, or sudden snowpack melt. As climate change increases, so does the likelihood of a disaster. When a disaster occurs, it destroys some structures built by the group, which then impacts energy and food production. After a group finishes playing, the simulated world does not automatically reset. Rathe r, the next group “inherits” the developed world, i ncluding the state of its climate. However, a group can choose to reset the world entirely at any point. Data were collected over five days as visitors interacted with the simulation. Data sources include audio recordings and screencasts of each participant’s screen, and observational notes made by a researcher in the back of the room. For this study, we narrowed down the d ata to recordings and screencasts of 6 groups’ experiences. These groups were selected because they engaged with the simulation for at least 9 minutes, included at least three participants, talked with each other consistently throughout, and represented a diversity of age distributions. Audio recordings for each group were transcribed in entirety. Screenshots of key moments were included in transcripts.

## Data analysis

All transcripts for the selected 6 groups were reviewed and analyzed for knowledge resources, social resources, and ideological resources drawn on by learners at d ifferent moments. This process was guided by princi ples of microgenetic learning analysis (Parnafes & diSessa, 2013), as well as prior work using similar theoretical lenses (e.g. Philip, 2011). Analysis specifically focused on moments of visible decision-making, evidenced th rough verbalizations about uncertainties of what to done xt within the simulation. We chose to focus analysi s on such moments, as decisions represented times in which learners were likely to draw on a variety of cognitiv e, social, and ideological resources (Aikenhead, 1985); analysis of these moments could shape learning theory rel ated to SSIs and decisions around them. Additionally, under standing resources drawn on in decisions could poss ibly provide insight into socio-scientific decisions with which people are confronted in their lives. We bounded moments of decisions in transcripts by i dentifying discourse markers that indicated the initial surfacing of uncertainty or the need to decide a specific action, as well as discourse markers that indicated the decision had been made. If conversation ever returned to that specific decision, for example several minutes later as participants reviewed the actions taken du ring the round, such conversation was included in a nalysis. Guided by extant research in KA and IA (e.g. Brown et al., 2016; Hall & Stevens, 2016; Parnafes & diSe ssa, 2013; Philip, 2011), we analyzed moment-to-moment processes within these instances of decisions, identifying knowledge, social, and ideological resources made salient.

## Findings

RQ1: What knowledge, social, and ideological resources do learners draw on? Across groups, participants drew on a variety of knowledge, social, and ideological resources, and that often these resources were intermixed in moment-to-moment proce sses. However, each group also tended to exhibit th eir own patterns and reliance on certain sets of resources. We will now describe key themes that came from analysis, although given the brevity of this proposal, this is not a comprehensive summary. Knowledge resources related to mechanism relationships among energy, climate, and ecosystems Participants across all 6 groups drew on resources related to mechanistic knowledge of relations among fossil fuel combustion, climate, the built environment, and local ecosystems. In some groups, these knowledge resources of mechanisms, or cause-and-effect relationships, were  drawn on frequently within decision-making. Typica lly, these knowledge resources emerged in moments of dec ision about whether to build a particular energy so urce, such as a coal-fired power plant or solar panels. For example, after one round of a game in which the group did ICLS2022 Proceedings 396 © ISLS not meet their energy goals, a participant said “We  need more energy and more food.” In response, a fe w turns later, another participant posed the question to th e group “Should I do a coal power plant?”. In respo nse, group members shouted “No!”, with one calling such a move  “bad for the climate.” Even without a full mechani stic explanation, such moments indicate participants drawing on various pieces of a knowledge system in which coalfired energy contributes to climate change, likely through exacerbation of the Earth’s natural greenhouse effect. Social positioning resources and moment-to-moment identity work Social resources emerging through interaction were often drawn on in decision-making. Typically, these resources relied on social positioning among group members wi th certain identities—identities that were both rel evant to the simulation and those that were more global. Often, identities local to the simulation were identities related to leadership. In many groups, particularly in family groups with young siblings, one participant would p osition themselves as leader, as indicated by discourse in which that person directed others what to do, or at tempted to shape the group’s direction by using the first-person plural to phrase directives (e.g. “we should…”). Moments in which the group’s progress toward energy, food, fun, and climate goals tended to make salient this position. Another local identity that emerged through interac tion in two groups was an identity of subversion. Such positioning often started early in the game, with a participant audibly positioning themselves as counter to the aims of the whole group. Typically, over the course of the game, they would act in accordance with such an identity, often choosing moves that increased climate change or interfered with others’ goals. When others noticed this interference, they would typically respond with anger and further social positioning of the subve rsive actor into such an identity. ## Discussion & conclusion

While previous work has examined learning outcomes of SSI instruction, little has investigated moment-tomoment learning processes around SSIs. This paper a dvances the field by integrating multiple theoretic al approaches to generate initial ideas about the learning processes unfolding around engagement with an SSI in a digital climate simulation in an informal science center. First, and perhaps obviously, this study shows that  knowledge resources were drawn on by learners. Specifically, we point to the many knowledge resources regarding scientific mechanistic knowledge about climate change drawn on by learners over the course of engagement with the scenario. In particular, specific contexts of the simulation appeared to make salient these resources. Often those contexts were moments of decision-making, and often when that decision-making was encountered  socially, as a group. Given that prior work has demonstrated the significance of mechanistic knowledge in navigating climate change in scientifically accurate ways (Author et al., 2021; Stevenson et al., 2014), we see this finding as an important one to highlight, as it speaks to the promise of leveraging digital simulations for climate science learning. Yet, at the same time, this study also demonstrates the multitude of ways in which scienti fic knowledge resources intersected with both socia l positioning resources and ideological resources to influence the learning process in, at times, surprising ways. Of the complexity of knowledge and interactional re sources involved in SSI learning, we suggest that the intersection of social and ideological resources could be particularly influential, as in this study we observed how moment-to-moment identity work done by participants contributed to the association of various soci al and ideological resources for particular participants, such as Anna, with her subversive, counter-operative efforts to “destroy the environment”. With this finding in mind, we use this study as a call for further research  examining moment-to-moment identity processes and integrating  multiple theoretical approaches to study the learn ing process around characteristically modern SSIs.
