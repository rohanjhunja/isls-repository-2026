# The Complexity of Adopting and Sustaining a Listening Stance

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Prior research has emphasized the importance of centering students’ thinking in STEM classrooms, particularly the need for STEM instructors to notice, interpret, and engage with the substance of student thinking. Our work builds on this, focusing on supporting postsecondary STEM faculty in cultivating their abilities to listen to student thinking in ways that recognize productivity in their sensemaking. We provide an empirical account of faculty listening to student thinking during a professional development session. We argue that the deep disciplinary expertise of college faculty can sometimes constrain their attempts to listen to student thinking in respectful and constructive ways, as they navigate the tension between being responsive to students’ attempts at productive sensemaking and maintaining fidelity to disciplinary meanings. Including faculty from multiple disciplines in the conversation may be helpful, by raising alternative interpretations that may seem sensible to novices but are difficult for disciplinary experts to consider.

## Introduction

Research has documented the need for STEM instructo rs to center student thinking in science classrooms. Research around “student-centered learning”, “responsive teaching” and “acti ve learning” have studied and promoted practices that center the agentic par ticipation of students in their own learning (Land et al., 2012; Lee and Hannafin; 2016, Trinidad, 2019, Sherin, Jacobs,  & Phillip, 2011). These practices emphasize the cu ltivation of student’s intellectual autonomy through construc tivist classroom interactions that support students ’ deep conceptual learning and provide students the opport unity to practice “doing science” like professional  scientists, with all of the uncertainty and confusion that comes with scientific practice. Research on responsive teaching particularly argues  for student thinking to be centered, i.e. for student ideas to be elicited, noticed and engaged respectfu lly, and integrated into the collective class instr uction (Robertson, Scherr & Hammer, 2016). For instructors to be able to do this however, they need to develop abilities and inclinations to notice and attend to the substance of student thinking (Watkins, Jaber, & Dini, 2020; Davidson, Jaber, & Metcalf, 2024). Within K–12 contexts, this has led to teacher preparation and professional development focused on cultivating teacher abilities to inquire into student thinking, as well  as to closely, deeply and respectfully engage with student thinking through interaction with student thinking presented in classroom videos and written work (van Es & Sherin, 2002; Levin et a l. 2012). Such programs aim to promote instruction that is centered around student thinking, emphasizing the teacher's role in listening to understand. There have been some studies around how postsecondary faculty create “active” learning environments focused on eliciting students’ thinking. There has,  however, been relatively little work done around h ow faculty engage with the student thinking they elicit (Gehrt z et al., 2022), how they approach student sensemak ing with a listening stance (Appleby et al., 2024), or professional development for this population of instructors. Ajilore et al. (2024) examined a discussion from The Listening Project, a professional development program which focused on engaging faculty in examin ing student thinking from their classes through wri tten work or video data. In that analysis, we focused on  two faculty members in an interdisciplinary group,  one individual from math and one from chemistry, who were examining an audio recording of students talking in introductory chemistry. We presented evidence of ho w both of those participants framed their work in t he discussion as listening, that is as working to understand (rather than to evaluate) the meaning students were trying to convey from the participants’ different position s within or outside the discipline of chemistry. He re we study another conversation and focus on a participant who  did not seem to focus so squarely on the meaning a student was trying to convey. Our purpose here is to unders tand the nature of his participation: What was the participant doing, in this conversation? And what does this imp ly for supporting faculty members in cu ltivating a listening stance towards student thinking?

We present excerpts from a TLP session in which we saw participants navigating com plexities in adopting and sustaining a listening stance. We inte nd to understand and show in what ways what we see the participant doing is listening and in what ways it is not and to clarify the complexity of the tension the participant was navigating between listening and not listening. Next, we provide some additional context on TLP, the concept of framing, and methods applied in this study. We then present some data excerpts and analysis.

## Background

The listening project TLP ran for six years at Tufts University as professional development for university instructors, including faculty and graduate student teaching assistants. Its design drew heavily on prior work in K–12 teacher education focused on helping teachers to recognize, attend, and engage with the substance of their students' thinking (e.g., van Es & Sherin, 2002; Hammer & van Zee, 2006). That is, it focused on helping instructors listen to students. Listening in TLP meant working to understand the meaning students ar e trying to construct and convey, generally a diffe rent aim from evaluating or guiding. Listening in this sense resembles teacher “noticing” (van Es & Sherin, 2002) with an emphasis on encouraging instructors to interpret student thinking with epistemic empathy (Jaber, Southerland, & Dake, 2018). Listening is a fundamental component of “responsive teaching”  (Robertson, Scherr, & Hammer,

2016) in its focus on understanding and looking for productivity in students’ thinking, though it does not explicitly

include moves to integrate student thinking into instruction. Rather the goal of the PD was to cultivate and practice adopting a listening stance in considering written or otherwise recorded examples of student work. TLP sessions in many ways resembled sessions in noticin g-focused K –12 professional development. Sessions typically involved collaborative examination of student thinking drawn from a participant's course, with facilitators directing attention towards interpreting, rather than evaluating, students’ reasoning (in alignment with Hammer, 2000; Putnam & Borko, 2000). A key differen ce from projects in K –12 contexts was that TLP participants were all STEM professionals who brough t substantial expertise and experience as practitioners and thinkers in their respective STEM fields. K –12 projects do not assume this expertise and experi ence and often include STEM inquiry experiences for their particip ants. There was no corresponding component of TLP. Participants worked in interdisciplinary pairs or larger groups such that at in any session at least some participants were examining student thinking from outside their own field. Ajilore et al., (2024) described benefit s of this arrangement: In that case, a mathematician provided a perspective on student thinking in chemistry that might not occur to chemists.

Framing Frames are widely used theoretical constructs in social sciences, “structures of expectations” shaped by previous experiences (Tannen, 1993) with respect to what is taking place. Research in STEM education has highli ghted framing in individuals and groups forming a sense of what it is they are doing. Jaber et al., (2021) conceptualized framing as “an active, dynamic and multilayered” (p. 227) process that is open to moment-to-moment shifts as individuals and groups continue tonegotiate their understanding of what is happening in a context. In K–12 STEM education, research has argued that teacher s’ framing influences what they observe and value in students’ thinking and further shapes thei r interpretations of this thinking (Levin et al., 2 009; Russ & Luna, 2013). Louie et al. (2021) elaborate on this argument by showing the influence of teachers’ framings of the discipline, students, and interactions between students on their views and interpretation of student’s mathematical activity as either “deficit” or “anti-deficit”. For example, a defic it view of a student's work can result from the teacher's framing of mathematics learning as the absorption of universal truths, which leads to evaluating student work as correct or incorrect and responding with ei ther error correction or praise for accura cy. In this paper, we consider how one participant, “Cole,” framed what w as taking place in a session of TLP, to understand the apparent difficulty for him of listening as well as the possibilities for supporting nascent listening in that session. What do we see Cole doing? The complexity of listening. During the meeting itself, the facilitators experie nced Cole as doing something other than listening— that is something other than attending to the sense Breanna  was trying to convey. But later, after closely ana lyzing the data, it is hard to say simply whether he was listening or not. Multiple things were happening at the same time— some things that seem like listening and some that do not. On the one hand, Cole seems to frame the activity o f the session generally as about understanding Breanna’s thinking. Cole seems to pay close attenti on to the Breanna’s words and sentences, critically  and carefully looking into their meaning, logic and coh erence. And at no point does he wholly dismiss anything Breanna said.

On the other hand, for Cole, in this session, frami ng of the activity as about understanding student thinking appears to involve the discipline as the basal reference of meaning, effectively constraining possibilities of interpretation. That is, Cole may try to understand what Breanna means, but he does this mainly from his own disciplinary (biology) frame of reference. He seems to be evaluating her contribution for correctness based on that meaning and his own sense of logic, flagging what h e perceived to be flaws. Alternative interpretations of the student’s meaning—e.g. RT’s which included the idea that some of Breanna’s words might mean something else to her outside of Cole’s sense of the canonical dis ciplinary meaning—do not seem to have traction with Cole. Though RT proposes that Breanna might have considered random mutation and natural selection to be distinct or even mutually exclusive processes, Cole seems to re ad Breanna's explanation of the random mutation as clearly based on a canonical descriptio n of natural selection in which random mutations ar e a fundamental component. In addition, Cole seems to be critiquing Breanna fo r not acknowledging that, for her theory to be true, humans must have not been in contact with chickens “all the time,” thoug h according to Cole, "the bacteria is always in constant contact with human.” Cole occasionally shifts away from the student's th inking to clarify himself to the group, repeatedly explaining his claim, seeking—himself—to be understood. This presents another layer of complexity to the focal moment. As Cole actively seeks recognition from others of his claim of a contradiction in the student sensemaking, that effort seems to pull his focus off of Breanna. He seems to juggle an effort to be heard himself on the one hand and hearing the student on the other.

There are also moments in which Cole wonders about Breanna’s intentions, and we see these moments as the beginnings of a possible shift into a stance  of listening that centers the student’s perspectiv e. Cole begins to acknowledge that while Breanna’s words imply a contradiction, it is possible that this is not what she intended to communicate (“maybe that was not what she intend ed for us to know, but it ended up this way.”). Thi s seems like a step towards taking Breanna’s perspective: a n acknowledgement tha t there may be a perspective here to take besides his own.

## Discussion and implications

There has been a great deal of attention in postsec ondary pedagogy with respect to students’ “active l earning,” important both for their progress in conceptual und erstanding and for their progress in the practices of the discipline. There has been far less attention, however, to faculty professional development with respect to noticing, understanding, and engaging with the student reason ing that active learning pedagogy elicits. It remai ns crucial for science educators to understand what their stud ents are thinking and the meanings they are intendi ng so they can effectively teach them and support them in their journey of developing their scientific thinking. This goal, in the context of professional developme nt is what  TLP takes up as it aims to promote university instructors learning to attend and respond to their  students’ thinking. It was based on prior work in K–12 teacher education, but we are not aware of precedent in pos tsecondary education. We have set out to understand  the dynamics of how faculty take up the work of listening, expecting similarities as well as differences fro m what researchers have found in K–12 efforts (Hammer, 2000; van Es & Sherin, 2002). In earlier work, we analyzed a session from TLP. We found that the interdisciplinarity of the group supported the participants’ listening with “epistem ic empathy” (Jaber, et al, 2018): A mathematician positioned himself as “with” the student in their reasoning ab out chemistry, and a chemist both heard his ideas a nd formed her own, drawing on her expertise, to consider poss ibilities for what the student may have been thinki ng and feeling. In that session, the focal participants demonstrated their abilities for attending to the thinking and feeling of students in ways that engage the productive subs tance of their thinking (Ajilore et al., 2024). We argued that the different positionings of the participants, all  experts in their own disciplines and relative novi ces in others, provided a rich breadth and depth of resources for listening. The present analysis illustrates complex dynamics —featuring challenges to listening —in a group of individuals from different disciplines. We identifi ed two main tensions in this case: (1) navigating f idelity to the discipline while listening to the student  and (2) between participants listening to the stude nts and being listened to themselves, especially within the interdisciplinary social dynamics of these sessions. We see Cole as having to navigate multiple challeng es, not only of attending to the student Breanna’s thinking as evident in her writing but also to his non-biologist colleagues’ perspectives. Upon reading Breanna’s response to the question, Cole sees a contradiction, and this generates a tension for him resembling t he dilemma Ball (1993) described of being “responsive to stude nts and responsible to content.” Cole then works to  explain that contradiction, but the group does not seem to understand. Other participants then raise questions  and possibilities for how the student’s reasoning might  have had an internal, albeit incorrect, coherence,  but Cole frames their contributions as reflections of their failure to recognize the contradiction he sees as p lainly evident in the data. The other participants interpret Cole’ s response as resistance of their framing of the conversation as listening for the meaning Breanna might have been trying to convey. This interpretation leaves some participants, including LA, a facilitator and also a biologist, with the sense that  Cole was more focused on his own thinking than Breanna’s.

We have seen a similar multi-layered dynamic, including tension between a discip linary expert and someone outside the discipline, at play in other sessions as well. For example, in a session focused on a student’s response to an exam question in a math class, the instructor of the focal class seemed, like Cole, to be constrained by his sense of faithfulness to disciplinary meanings, in this case of mathematical notation: What the student had written was mathematically nonsensical. The facilit ator, a biologist, worked to understand how the student may have been thinking, and perceived the focal participant as resisting that framing of the session. The tension between responsiveness to student and r esponsibility to discipline reflects broader tensio ns in STEM education. For example, Russ and Berland (2019) characterized two kinds of science learning activities: One is “figuring-out”, where the goal o f the learning activity is students’ working to mak e sense of phenomena for themselves, as nascent scientists, developing “ scientific sense-making practices, scientific ideas, and past experiences to develop coherent understandings of complex physical phenomena.” The other is “learning about,” where the goal of the activity is students’ arrival at correct, canonical scientific understanding. There is still work to do to understand and support science educators i n navigating this complexity in ways that promote s tudentcentered learning. Professional development sessions focused on instru ctor listening may be a constructive space for supporting instructors in navigating these tensions. Encouraging a framing of inquiry into students’ t hinking during listening sessions, by activating partici pant’s resources—disciplinary and otherwise —for understanding students, requires that each participant have a cap acity for listening in the first place. Though Cole  may have sometimes framed the session in terms of explaining and defending the logic of the discipline—which is not quite listening—we do not claim that he has no capacity for listening. We in fact recognize, in his participation, potential beginnings of listening. We see Cole’s start at looking past Breanna’s wor ds and wondering at her intended meaning as one such beginning.

We contend that work in the vein of TLP is essential for progress in postsecondary STEM, as it has been in K–12, but it is only just beginning. Research on how faculty engage in these sessions—on the possibilities and challenges—can guide the development and implementation of fut ure efforts in professional development. For now, it remains our conjecture that the best possib ilities for this work to succeed involve interdisci plinary collaborations. As we found in our previous work, faculty who are experts in other fields but relative novices for the discipline at hand may at times be better posit ioned to understand the non-canonical sense of students’ thinking, simply because they are less familiar with or committed to the canon. That was evident here, in physicist RT’s perspective on Breanna’s biological thinking. Still, as this episode seems to show, there will be complicated dynamics to navigate, for participants and facilita tors. One implication we draw —which will inform our work going forward—is of the imp ortance of perceptive listening to participants wit hin sessions on listening: If Cole could have felt heard and understood with respect t o his interpretation as a biologist, it might have helped the group to coordinate the activity of considering the discipline with that of working to understand the student. Ultimately, TLP hopes that through participation in the program, pa rticipants will become predisposed to using their deep disciplinary knowledge and expertise as tools that can help them recognize, notice and engage with the productive work that students do in their sensemaking more than  experiencing them as barriers complicating their attempts to listen equitably to the value and diversity of forms of student’s ways of knowing.
