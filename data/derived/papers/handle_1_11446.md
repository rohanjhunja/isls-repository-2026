# Entangled Gardens, Entangled Lives: Toward an Ethics of Care and Vulnerability in Preschoolers’ Multispecies Encounters

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This praxis-oriented paper draws from a yearlong multispecies ethnography of preschoolers’ encounters in an urban garden through child-led documentation. Thinking-with common worlds pedagogies, I attend to young children’s everyday multispecies encounters with worms, bees, and flowers and describe their embodied, affective, and relational learning-with the more-than-human that was messy, situated, and relational. Important to navigating the environmental precarity of the Anthropocene, I share moments of children’s developing ethics of relational care and environmental vulnerability as hope. Seeking to unsettle disconnected, humancentric, nature-as-resource scientific learning, I mobilized a pedagogy of refiguring more-than-human presences to center human/more-than-human entanglement, interconnectedness, and interdependency. Findings narrate learning encounters and pedagogical moves through vignettes and children’s documentation of photographs, drawings, and writings.

## Introduction

This paper discusses preschoolers’ developing ethics  of relational care (Haraway, 2008) and environmental vulnerability (Hird, 2013) in their entangled, multispecies encounters through a pedagogy of refiguring  morethan-human presences (Nxumalo, 2019). Inspired by critical early childho od multispecies and place-based scholarship of Taylor and Pacini-Ketchabaw (2019) and Nxumalo (2019), this paper adds to the living archive of praxis-oriented research (Lather, 1986) addressing the ethical responsibility of early learning to unsettle humancentric practices implicitly reinforcing human exceptionalism, master y over the more-than-human, and child stewards and/or inheritors of more-than-human “resources” (Tsing, 2012) toward multispecies flourishing as co-inheritors, co-witnessers, and co-agents of the Anthropocene (Steffen, Crutzen, & McNeill, 2007).

## Conceptual framework

My research-praxis draws from common worlds pedagogies (Taylor & Pacini-Ketchabaw, 2019)  that: (a) resituates humans within ecological systems by shifting attention toward the interdependency, co-habitation, and entanglement of all life; (b) recognizes the “micro-effects of these everyday child-animal encounters are part of the macro-politics of mortal ecological entanglements” (p. 5); and (c) engages in an ethics of  “ongoing relational practices involving human and more-than-human actors and situated within the ordinary inter actions and exchanges of everyday life.” (p. 6).  These ethics include relational care (Haraway, 2008), a situated, embodied, and affective-ethical way of interdependent relating, and environ mental vulnerability (Hird, 2012), the “human and non-human asymmetrical vulnerability to an unknowable future” (p. 105). Mobilizing these concepts through a pedagogy of refiguring more-than-human presences (Nxumalo, 2019), I sought to decent er children as sole-protagonists in multispecies encounters and center entangled, agentic living-learning of children and the more-than-human on a damaged planet. By re-storying “material-discursive stories of place, where both the stories and the ‘storytellers’ are more-than-human” (p. 43), children’s awareness of more-than-human entanglements surfaced as moments of relational care and environmental vulnerability.

## Methodology-pedagogy

Data draws from a yearlong multispecies ethnography (Kirksey & Helmreich, 2010; Taylor & Pacini-Ketchabaw,

2019) in my  class of eighteen 4-and 5-year-olds located on a neighborhood Church’s urban garde n in a

northeastern metropolis. Transversing the garden da ily in their comings and goings, children paid litt le attention to more-than-human presences until heavy autumn rainfall brought children into contact with worms. This initial, emergent child-worm encounter sparked our multispecies ethnography until late spring, finishing with refiguring more-than-human presences in child-bee-flower encounters. Using the Mosaic Approach (Clark, 2011), children documented their multispecies encounters with a GoPro camera, bringing “place, materials, and more-than-human worlds into sharp focus”  (Templeton & Vellanki, 2022, p. 230). Thinking-and acting-with Nxumalo’s (2019) methodology-pedagogy of refiguring more-than-human presences, I worked toward decolonizing place  to bring attention “to possibilities for new ethical account abilities in multispecies relations… ground ed in children’s everyday uncertain, embodied, affective, and thoughtful responses” (p. 104).

## Findings

Children’s developing ethics of relational care and vulnerability in their multispecies encounters began with touch. Thinking-with Haraway (2008), touch is the affective, embodied, relational genesis that “peppers its partners with attachment sites for worldmaking” (p. 36). Through touch, children engaged in worldmaking with the more-thanhuman; unpredictable worlds stirred children’s ethics of relatio nal care and vulnerability in entangled learning-living. Touch was the opening for refiguring more-than-human presences, “a form of decolonial interruption,” (Nxumalo, 2019, p. 43),  to bee-garden-child relations bringing “attention to both the limiting effects and ethical potentialities of everyday pedagogical encounters… ground in children’s everyday uncertain, embodied, affective, and thoughtful responses” (p. 104) “They touch; therefore, they are” Preschoolers’ earliest moments of ethical care and vulnerability emerged on the urban garden ’s watery, muddy grounds where children noticed worm movements amidst squelching dirt and dark puddles (Figure 1). Captivated by worm movements, children gathered to get a closer look – “It’s dancing!” a child cheers. Giddy laughter-shrieks are cut short – “they’re drowning!” shouts a child. Children hesitate, pondering. Was the worm dancing or suffering? The message is heard, and children be gin scooping up worms. Although hesitant to touch w orms, preferring to use a plastic spoon and container (Fi gure 1), children begin gingerly pinching their fingers t o lift worms. Child skin meets worm skin, giggles erupt, a  relation is formed  – “ they touch; therefore, they are” (Haraway, 2008, p. 263). Cradling worms (Figure 1), children slow their bodies and bring their faces  closer. Greetings ensue and worms are gently placed in containers. Determined to save as many as they can, children splash along and shout “worm alert!” signaling a worm has been found (Figure 2). Children gather and rescue worms until outside time ended. Someday later, children quickly move amongst  the garden, “worm alert!” fills the air, and worm rescuing unfolds. Children take time to prepare for worm encounters: some hold worms on leaves (Figure 2) while others construct worm “homes.” Containers filled with wet soil, fallen leaves, and dried berries fashion beds for rescued worms (Figure 2). Children adopt high-pitched, sing-song voices to greet worms, give names, and offer farewells. Overtime, children  contemplate where to put worms after housing them in their containers. Whereas some children transferred their  premade homes back to the Earth, other s placed worms on drier ground, under bushes, or in planting pots.

Through touch children opened themselves to caring,  being affected, and accountability. “Touch ramifie s and shapes accountability. Accountability, caring  for, being affected, and entering into responsibili ty are not ethical abstraction” (Haraway, 2008, p. 36). Ethical care a nd vulnerability materialized in children’s embodie d morethan-human caring practices and response-ability. The act of responding follows being open a nd called into connection (Rose, 2015). From gingerly handling wor ms, slowing down, and adopting nurturing voices to building beds, homes, and locating safe places for worms, children negotiated worms’ uneven vulnerabil ities to intense rainfall. Touching worms elicited response-ability, affective-understanding, theory building, and meaningmaking – a thinking through the skin that  reflects “not on the body as the lost object of thought, but on interembodiment, on the mode of being-with and being-for, where one touches and is touched by others. ” (Ahmed & Stacey, 2001, p. 1). Rather than asking children what they know about bees, which solicits factual knowledge, asking how children know it’s a bee elicits more personal, affective knowledge grounded in embo died experiences. Here, scientific information like color, shape, size, features, and behaviors, are mobilized  as relational knowledge to better get to “know” be es and living-with bees in our common world. The topic turns to pollination and children discuss bees, flowers, pol len, and honey. Working toward unsettling human control over more-than-human (Tsing, 2012) and re-storying beehuman-plant relations as commodified pollination networks, children remembered the crabapple tree’s entangled interdependencies (see Figure 4 and following section for more). Pollen wonderings lead children to flowers. Robust in the garden, children pretend to be bees a nd “fly” around looking for pollen and nectar (Figure 3). Lightly bringing their noses against flowers, like a proboscis, children inhale deeply, smelling sweet aromas and releasing blissful sighs. Touching flowers, children encounter unexpected flow er-agencies: pollen. Transferring golden yellow pollen from stamen to stigma, children apply too much pressure and the stamin or stigma br eaks. As Haraway (2008) describes, “touch and regard have consequences” (p. 36). Children gasp and freeze, noticing and feeling their touch’s consequences. Children try to move fast and pollen flies into the air, scattering in their face causing itchy eyes, sneezing, and coughing, particularly fo r those allergic. Responding to flower agencies, children and slow their movements and proceed thoug htfully to transferring pollen again, adjusting their movements toneither hurt flowers nor themselves.

## Seeing and Smelling Flowers From Bee Perspectives

Building upon similar embodied, affective, and relational meaning-making, children’s bee-flower encounters emerged as ethics of care and vulnerability, grappling with  human mastery over the “passive” more-than-human (Tsing, 2012). To learn-with bees, children and teachers consulted science as an advisor for understanding interconnected bee lifeworlds, particularly bee-flower entanglements. “Being” bees, children metamorphosized, “a visible infolding that melts the boundaries that have been constructed between human and animal... a moment of intersubjectivity to be celebrated ” (Bone, 2010, p. 411). Becoming bees attuned child ren to flowers in  “the dance of world-making encounters” (Haraway, 2009, p. 249), where both human and more-than-human agencies co-construct learning. Touching and encountering flowe r agencies, children’s embodied sensemaking of pollination intertwined with flower bodies. However, touch has consequences, and unexpected vulnerabilities emerged. Children were perhaps surprised by flower-pollen agencies, drawing “attention to the extended others – human and non-human – affected by our actions” (Hird, 2012, p. 115). Maki ng slow, precise movements to mitigate both hurting the flower and themselves wer e children’s affective, embodied responses to mutua l vulnerabilities. Becoming bees, touching flowers, m oving pollen, refigured bee-flower presences, unsettled humancentric learning about bees toward situated, relational interspecies learning-with. (Re)-storying entangled gardens One evening a local tour guide introduced me to a crabapple tree who was baring less fruit because of an absence of pollinators. To re-story the garden ’s entanglement, I refigured this crabapple tree’s presence, creating  “an opening for grappling with the ethical potentialities of plural more-than-human worldings where both the human and more-than-human ‘shape and are shaped by political, economic, and cultural forces’ (Kirksey & Helmreich, 2010, p. 545, cited in Nxumalo,  2019, p. 43). With my storytelling, I  visually illustrated the crabapple tree’s interconnections – apple blossoms, bees, apples, more-than-humans, human  – emphasizing interdependency while leaving space for children’s theory making. Visiting the garden and excited to meet the crabapple tree, children grew aware of the lack of bees. Fleeting bee comings-and-goings were quick. Pausing near flowers, children listened to locate bees and get closer. Despite  voicing their knowledge of bee stings, primarily the pain but also how bees die after stinging others (Figure 5), children continued to seek closeness to bees. Whereas before children would yell, swat their hands, or sh rink away when bees darted about, children began practicing slowness and stillness. This attention relationality and interdependence matters in the context of extinctions, because “the death, and subsequent absence of a whole species, unmakes these relationships on which life depends” (vandoo ren, 2010, p. 273). I wonder how re-storying affected children’s thinking-learning-with bees-flowers-trees, surfacing an ethics of asymmetrical, uneven vulnerabilities. Salient occurrences were in children’s embodied att unements

Children’s vocalization and drawings of garden interdependency (Figures 4 and 5), where both humans and more-than-humans were impacted a by bee death, point toward a  deeper ethical negotiation of environmental vulnerability. Perhaps the se situated child-bee-flower-tree encounters within an ecologically damaged environment brought awareness to human and more-than-human precarity and interconnectedness.

## Scholarly significance of the study

The Anthropocene call s for multispecies flourishing (Haraway, 2008), requi ring “robust nonanthropomorphic sensibility that is accountable to irreducible diff erences” (p. 90). The pedagogical and theoretical significance of this practice paper upholds Nxumalo’ (2019) call to action: “these modes of caring are the kinds of attunements needed in the current time of the Anthropocene, gro unded as they are in responding to the situated rea l-life messiness and uneven inheritances of the places children co-inhabit… rather than in universalized or precalculated understandings of what counts as care or of who or what is deserving of care” (p. 112). Exploring common worlds pedagogies and refiguring presences  in my classroom, children’s ethical negotiations of relational care and vulnerability surfaced in their multispecies encounters  and documentation. Through touch, affect, embodiment, response-ability, metamorphizing, and re-storying entangled child-garden interconnectedness, children’s ethics emerged within everyday multispecies encounters, sh owing how seemingly mundane and banal practices can be sites for ethical relations and unsettling colonial legac ies of erasures in early childhood  pedagogies (Nxumalo, 2019).
