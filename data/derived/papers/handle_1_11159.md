# Toward an Understanding of “Math Parenting Identity”: Parent Perceptions of the Home Math Environments of Young Children

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Unveiling a pioneering concept, this research introduces “Math Parenting Identity” (MPI) as an important determinant in the foundational math experiences that take place in the home math environments of young learners prior to the onset of formal schooling in kindergarten. Engaging a national sample of 847 parents of 4 to 5-year-olds, the study employs the RESET framework to explore the existence and complexity of MPI. It articulates important implications for parent decision-making and motivations in fostering the early math learning of their children and provides a new survey instrument to deepen our understanding of how parental beliefs and behaviors not only shape parents’ own MPI, but also children's earliest encounters with mathematics.

## Introduction

The learning sciences have long recognized the infl uence of social and cultural contexts in shaping ed ucational outcomes, yet within this rich tapestry, the nuanced cognitions, perceptions, and motivations of parents regarding mathematical learning in the home environment during early childhood remain a relatively uncharted domain. While the Home Math Environment (HME) has  received more attention over the past decade, there is a distinct gap in our understanding of how parents construct t heir own 'math parenting identity' —the combination of a parent’s self-perceptions, cultural beliefs, and personal motivat ions surrounding their involvement in and influence on their young child's development of mathematical knowledge and understanding. The present study pivots the lens towards this nuanced and unexplored  aspect of the HME, employing the RESET (Role, Expectations, Skills, Efficacy, and Time) Framework to examine the factors that may most influence mathematical parenting (i.e., behavior and actions supportive of math lear ning), and the development of an individual  parent’s Math Parenting Identity (MPI). By doing so, we seek to illuminate how parents' internal narratives and external influences converge to shape the way math parenting behaviors may mold the earliest math experiences of children in the home, and to contribute critical scholarship to this area of the learning sciences.

## Background

Mathematical proficiency is not only crucial for academic achievement but also for success beyond the classroom (Ritchie & Bates, 2013). Despite this, a significant number of children do not show grade-level math proficiency (deBrey et al., 2019), and the origin of these challenges often begins before formal schooling starts (Betts & Son, 2022). Early exposure to math concepts is essential for  building the strong numeracy foundation necessary for later learning (Duncan et al., 2007; Nguyen et al., 2016), and a ddressing learner variability early is vital for consistent math engagement from kindergarten onward.

Instrumental at this early stage is the Home Math Environment (HME), which includes parents’ academic expectations, beliefs, and the resources and practi ces that contribute to developing math knowledge and skills in children (Cankaya & LeFevre, 2016; Daucort et al., 2023; Napoli & Purpura, 2018). Research shows that a rich HME, filled with warm and enriching math conversations as well as both formal and informal parent-child shared math activities, significantly boosts early math un derstanding and achieveme nt (Daucourt et al., 2021; Dunst et al., 2017).

Parents often act as the first and most influential  teachers for their children —especially in the early years—guiding cognitive development in various areas, inc luding math  (Vygotsky,1986). Furthermore, data shows that as many as 60% of pre-primary age children (ages 3-to 5-years-old) may be learning in home environments as opposed to school environments such as preschools or prekinder garten (NCES, 2022), making parents and caregivers the primary facilitator s of children’s early math experiences. However, the processes by which parents learn to engage in “mathematical parenting”—the actions and decisions that shape their children’s math attitudes, learning, and ultimately the foundations for the child’s ow n math learner identity (Betts & Son, 2022)—are less understood. This gap highlights the need to explore the diverse ways parents perceive their mathrelated parenting roles, set learning expectations, leverage their own math skills, and manage their time and energy to support their children's math development (Betts, 2021; Betts & Son, 2022). Understanding these factors is crucial for developing strategies to support  families in providing the kinds of enriching early math experiences that lead to readiness to learn in kindergarten, as well as ongoing math competency.

## Theoretical framework

Parent identity is recognized as a multifaceted con struct that reflects how individuals perceive thems elves in the role of a parent, influenced by personal experience s, societal expectations, and cultural norms (Bronfenbrenner, 1979; Mowder, 2005; Tsushima & Burke, 1999). It encompasses the responsibilities, values, beliefs, and practices associated with parenting, which directly influence the ways in which parent s engage with their children, providing support, guidance, and resources across various life domains (Tsushima & Burke, 1999). Similarly, math learner identity is concerned with an individual's self-concept as it relates to their ability to understand, use, and appreciate mathematics (Boa ler, 2002). It includes one's beliefs about their o wn mathematical capabilities, attitudes tow ards mathematics, and their perceived place within the mathematical community (Cribbs et al., 2015). This identity can profoundly affect an individua l's approach to learning and teaching mathematics, their resilience in the face of mathematical challenges,  and their willingness to persist in math-related endeavors (Dweck, 2006).  Math Parenting Identity  then emerges at the intersection of these two identities, encapsulating how a parent’s view of themselves as a learner of mathematics and their broader parenting role coalesce to shape their involvement in  and support of  their child’s math education. For example, a  parent with a strong set of math skills and positive associations around engaging mathematically with the everyday world may feel confident in supporting mathematical explo ration and growth in their children  (strong MPI), whereas a parent who harbors anxieties or negative experience s with math may approach their child’s math education with trepidation (low MPI), potentially influencing the range and nature of m ath-related activities they endorse or engage in at home (Gunderson et al., 2012; Eccles & Harold, 1996). The development of Math Parenting Identity is thus a dynamic process, where a parent's experiences as a learner of mathematics and their beliefs and prac tices as a caregiver  and supporter of mathematics learning in their child interact to influence their perceptions and behaviors. Here we propose that a parent’s MPI affects how they perceive their role in fostering a nurturing and ma thematically rich environment, their expectations o f their children's math abilities, and the decisions they make regarding the mathematical activities they facilitate. Math Parenting Identity as a specific construct is an emergent one in the field of educational psychology and mathematics education, representing a nuanced amalgamation of parents' roles in leading the development of their children (Vygotsky, 1986) and their self-concept in mathematics (Cribbs et al., 2015). While direct research on this specific identity is nascent, existing literature provides indirect evidence suggesting its presence (e.g., see Berkowitz et al., 2015; Civil & Bernier, 2006; Cross, Woods, & Schweingruber, 2009; Gunderson et al., 2012; Sheldon & Epstein, 2005). These studies, while not explicitly aimed at invest igating MPI per se, underline the importance of parental perceptions and behaviors th at are likely to shape such an identity, paving the  way for further research that explicitly explores this construct. A review of the research corpus suggests that by understanding and supporting the positive development of a parent's MPI, we can create stronger partnerships between home and school environments that are conducive to the cultivation of children’s mathematical under standing and appreciation. This holistic approach r equires acknowledging the emotional, cognitive, and social components of a parent's identity and provides a lens through which to view and support their involvement in their child's mathematical journey. Underpinning theoretical foundations The RESET framework serves as a framework for examining the multifaceted nature of Math Parenting Identity. This framework is predicated on the integration of established theories surrounding parent identity and math learner identity, offering a comprehensive lens thr ough which to view parents' influence on their chil dren's mathematical development. The RESET framework is informed by the understandin g that a parent's identity encompasses both their self-conception as educators at home and their personal experiences as learners. Vygotsky's (1986) social constructivist theory suggests that learning is deeply social and cultural, pointing to the significance of parents as facilitators of their ch ildren’s educational experiences, particularly in m athematics. Mowder's (2005) Parent Development Theory emphasizes the importance  of experiential learning in parenting, where parents' own educational experiences shape their involvement in their children's learning. Bronfenbrenner's

(1979) Ecological Systems Theory adds depth by illustrating how a parent's role and expectations are influenc ed

by interactions across different environmental systems, from the immediate family to broader societal structure. Development of RESET domains Within the RESET framework, the domains of Role, Expectations, Skills, Efficacy, and Time are conceptualized as follows: Role: This domain acknowledges that a parent's role in th eir child’s math education is informed by their pas t experiences and current social context, reflecting the principles laid out by both Mowder and Bronfenb renner. A parent’s role evolves through continuou s interaction with their child and the education sy stem, often shaped by their own educational trajectory.

Expectations: Parents' expectations for their children's math ach ievement are seen as a projection of the value they place on mathematics within the family and soc iety. These expectations are molded by cultural val ues and the perceived importance of math for future success, aligning with the theories of parental and learner identities. Skills: The perceived math skills domain mirrors the parent's math learner identity, influencing how they support their child's math learning. This reflects the conf idence and competence parents feel based on their o wn educational experiences.

Efficacy: A parent's belief in their ability to support their child's math development is intrinsically linked to their self-efficacy, a key component of math learner identity.  This belief directly impacts the strategies they c hoose to foster math skills in their children.

Time: The investment of time and energy in math-related activities with children is influenced by t he parents’ perceived capabilities and the practical realities of their daily lives. This resonates with Bronfenbr enner's ecological perspective, where the availa bility of time and energy can be affected by multip le layers of a parent's environment.

The RESET framework, by systematically examining th ese domains, provides a nuanced understanding of ho w parents' identities as learners and facilitators of learning in the home  intertwine to influence their engagement in the mathematical upbringing of their children. It is through this lens that the authors of this study propose that the MPI can be observed, understood, and supported.  The purpose of the present study is to use the RESE T framework as a tool for better understanding what MPI consists of, how parent perceive themselves in this regard, what relationships might exist between different parent characteristics and their influence on MPI, as well as any relationships might exist between MPI and parent kn owledge and behaviors. The primary question guiding this research is: How does Math Parenting Identity (MPI), as evidence d through the RESET framework, reflect parents' self-perceptions of their role and influence in the home  math environment, particularly as the 'more knowledgeable other' in their young children's early mathematical experiences?

## Methodology

This quantitative study analyzed survey data collected from 847 parents of 4-to-5-year-old children who had not yet begun  kindergarten.  It focused on parent perceptions across the RESET domains, knowledge of early childhood mathematics, and parent self-reports of the frequency of shared math activities at home. Recruitment, which was conducted in Spring 2022 via a commercial panel  provider, aimed for a diverse sample in terms of ethnicity, income, and education.

Data sources The survey included 30 Likert-scale items, six for each RESET domain, rated on a seven-point scale. These items were field tested in a previous study (Betts & Son, 2022) and found to be reliable (all Cronbach Alphas between.7 and.9; see Table 1). The survey also asked parents to evaluate 10 common pre-kindergarten math competencies and report the frequency of shared math activities at home. This information was used to create mean scores and composite variables reflecting parents' perceptions  along the RESET domains, knowledge of early childh ood mathematics, and engagement in shared math activiti es. Demographic information was also collected to p rovide context to the findings, and to generate insights about the nature of Math Parenting Identity. Characteristics of sample Sample demographics included a diverse set of ethnic (Caucasian 70%, African American 10%, Multiracial, 9%, Latinx 7%, Asian 3%, Indigenous 1%), income (over 1 00K annually 19%, from 50K-100K annually 46%, less than 50K annually 35%), and educational backgrounds (Undergrad/Graduate degree 34%, some college 29%, HS grad/trade/tech school 37%). An important consideration in this study, parent re sponses to the gender question were categorized into Female (n=626), Male (n=210),  and Non-cisgender (n=11) groups. Despite the small size of the non-cisgender group, ethical considerations led to their inclusion in the analysis to avoid erasure. Given the statistical challenges with the non-cisgender group's size, various strategies were emp loyed to determine their placement within the RESET analysis. These strategi es included reviewing RESET scores, consulting with  noncisgendered colleagues, and considering the margina lized status of non-cisgender individuals, leading to th e placement of eight non-cisgender parents in the Female group and three who  identified as Male (AFAB) in the Male group. The categorization acknowledged limitat ions and aimed to respect individuals' stated gende r identities which also may impact their parenting identities. Future research may delve deeper  into non-cisgender parents' perspectives through a case-study approach, although this was outside the current study's scope.

## Data analysis

The authors employed a systematic approach to data analysis that included descriptive statistics, correlational analysis, regression analysis, and other analyses through SPSS for statistical examination of the survey data. The analyses focused on quantifying parents' perceptions withi n the RESET domains, their knowledge of early childhood math competencies, and their reported engagement in math activities, aiming to contextualize findings within the broader scope of Math Parenting Identity. Constructs of MP I were evaluated against theoretical predictions to validate the findings.

Parents demonstrated strong perceptions of their Role and Expectations, foundational elements of MPI, signaling a proactive stance towar ds their child’s math education. Nonetheless, a dis parity in Skills and Efficacy points to areas where MPI could  be fortified, especially consideri ng the wide range of perceived math skills (Table 1).

Gender In examining the MPI against demographic backgrounds, gender differences were significant (Table 2). Mothers (n = 634) reported significantly higher perceptions thandid fathers (n = 213) of their math parenting Role. Conversely, fathers reported significantly higher Skills and Efficacy, reflecting both traditional gender norms and mathematics education research that shows males wit h higher math confidence levels than females (PerezFelkner, Nix, & Thomas, 2017).  However, these resu lts suggest potential areas for parental support and development.

Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to ascertain the effects of Gender  when controlling for Education, and Income on the perceptions of math-parenting Role, Skills, and Efficacy. For the Role domain, Gender remained a significant predictor (β = −.206, t(845)= −3.015, p <.01), indicating that Fathers (coded as the second group), reported significantly lower perceptions of their math-parenting role compared to Mothers (coded as the first group), even after controlling for Educat ion and Income. Regression analyses revealed that while Education had a positive but non-significant relationship with the Role domain (β =.061, t(845) = 1.528, p >.05), Income was significantly and negatively associated (β = −.095, t(845) = −2.081, p <.05). This suggests that higher Income levels were correlated with lower perception s of math-parenting Role, perhaps due to greater access to outside resources (e.g., preschool or enrichment services) that reduced parents’ perceptions of their personal role. Despite the non-significant effect of Education, the significance o f Gender and Income remained robust, highlighting the persistent influence of these fact ors on the perceived Role. The regression analysis underscores the importance of considering socioeconomic factors  when examining parental perceptions of math-related responsibilities, with the influence of Income meriting further examination. Ethnicity Interesting findings emerged during an examination of Ethnicity related to RESET. Kruskal-Wallis tests revealed that African American and Multiracial parents hold higher perceptions across all domains, challenging deficitbased narratives historically associated with these  communities. Notably, in the Role domain, these pa rents reported significantly higher perceptions (African American:  M=5.84, SD=0.92; Multiracial: M=5.94, SD=0.76) than their White, AAPI/Indigenous, and Hispanic/Latino counterparts, and they also reported higher Expectations (African American: M=5.96, SD=0.82; Multiracial: M=5.93, SD=0.77). The significant statistical differences indicate a rich MPI in these communities, suggestin g a dynamic interplay of cultural identity and enga gement in math parenting that supports children's mathematica l development —yet may not be recognized by traditional notions of kindergarten readiness at school entry. It is important to note that given the research-supported connection between rich home math environments and readiness to learn math at kindergarten entry, we w ould expect to see higher levels of kindergarten readiness in these communities—a trend that is not presently supported by the research literature  (deBrey et al., 2019).  This is an area that requir es further examination to uncover whether the lack is in schools’ limited ability to assess the different “ways of knowing” that children may bring with them to the onset of school, or whether African American and Multiracial families would benefit from greater support on knowing which skills and knowledge to focus on in the home math environment (Betts, Son, & Bang, 2024).  Data from the present study suggests that by elevating and acknowledging the math parenting practices of African American and Multiracial families, we may move towards a more inclusive understanding of how diverse experiences contribute to the fabric of MPI, providing invaluable insights for educational theory and practice. Parent age A one-way ANOVA test was used to examine the relationship between parent age and perceptions along RESET, revealing that older parents have significantly higher Expectations for their children’s math learning and perceive more available time available for engagement. Specifically, parents aged "45 and Older" demonstrate d higher Expectations and a greater perception of available time compared to younger parents, with statistical significance (Expectations: F(3,843)=5.229, p <.001; Time: F(3,843)=5.769, p <.001). These findings, corroborated by non-parametric testing due to non-normal distributions in Expectations, suggest that older parents may possess a more established MPI, potentially influenced by a greate r accumulation of experiences and resources  (cognitive, emotional, physical, etc.). This aligns with research that indicates developm ental and life stage factors can significantly shape parental involvement in their c hildren's educational processes (An & Cooney, 2006; HooverDempsey & Sandler, 1997). Income The influence of income on MPI is evident through its significant impact on the RESET domains—Expectations, Skills, and Efficacy. Specifically, one-way ANOVA tests showed that higher-income parents exhibit stronger Expectations (F(2, 844) = 9.535, p <.001), more positive self-perceptions of Skills (F(2, 844) = 18.661, p <.001), and greater Efficacy (F(2, 844) = 7.393, p <.001) than their lower-income counterparts. These diffe rences, substantiated by post hoc analyses, align with the broader discourse on MPI, underscoring the role of socioeconomic factors in shaping the beliefs and be haviors integral to math-related parenting. Such findings affirm that MPI is not only a reflection of parents’ educational and emotional engagement with their child's math learning but also a manifestation of the resources available to them, which are often influenced by income. Educational attainment ANOVA analyses showed that educational attainment is also influential in shaping MPI, as evidenced by its strong correlation with enhanced Expectations, Skills, and  Efficacy within the RESET framework. The findings underscore that higher educational levels, specifically holding undergraduate or graduate degrees, are significantly associated with more robust expectations for a chil d's math learning, a more positive self-assessment of math skills, and heightened math-parenting efficacy (Expectations: F(2,844)=14.494, p<.001; Skills: F(2,844)=19.282, p<.001; Efficacy: F(2,844)=9.819, p<.001). These statistically significant effects sol idify the notion that educational experiences not only bolster parental confidence but also actively engage parents in the mathematical nurturing of their children. Drawing from the learning sciences, this relationship may be attributed to the cognitive and emotional resources that higher education provi des, equipping parents with the tools to foster a mathematically enriched learn ing environment. When paralleled with income trends, the data suggest that both educational and socio-economic factors are integral to cultivating a strong MPI. Child learning environment (i.e., formal school environment vs. learning in the home) The connection between a child's early learning environment and parental perceptions of math-parenting Role, as examined through One-Way ANOVA tests, provides insights into Math Parent ing Identity (MPI) as well. Significant differences were found in the Role domain (F(2, 844) = 4.667, p <.01) indicating that parents with children in a home learning environment perceive a more active math-parenting Role compared to those whose children attend Preschool/Pre-K. This finding  suggests that the direct involvement and unique int eractions provided at home may enhance parents' engagement and sense of responsibility in their child's early ma th development. The higher Role perceptions among these parents reflect a more pronounced MPI, illustrating how varied educational settings can influence parental beliefs and behaviors in the context of their child 's learning trajectory.

Parent self-reports of shared math activity Analyses highlighted the significant relationships between parents' math ematical parenting beliefs and perceptions within RESET and their reported frequencies of shared math activity in the home. Pearson’s Bivariate Correlation analysis revealed that all domains of R ESET were significantly correlated with the frequen cy of mathematical activities. Notably, the Expectations domain exhibited the strongest correlation with overall activity frequency (r =.451, p <.001, 95% CI [.395,.503]), suggesting that parents with higher expectations are likely to engage more frequently in mathematical activities with their children. A similar trend was observed for the Time domain, indicating that parents who perceived more time available for math parenting also reported higher engagement frequencies (r =.428, p <.001, 95% CI [.372,.482]). The Role domain showe d a moderate positive correlation with both informal (r =.391, p <.001, 95% CI [.332,.447]) and advanced skills activities (r =.356, p <.001, 95% CI [.296,.414]). The Skills domain cor relations were relatively lower, suggesting a weake r, yet statistically significant, relationship (r =.232, p <.001, 95% CI [.168,.295] for overall activity fr equency). Efficacy was consistently correlated across all typ es of activities, highlighting the importance of pa rental confidence in facilitating mathematical interactions (r =.353, p <.001, 95% CI [.293,.411] for overall frequency). These statistically significant correlations, all w ith p-values less than.001, indicate that there is a robust connection between the RESET factors and the parent-reported frequency of shared math engagement in the home environment—suggesting a relationship between stronger Math Parenting Identities and shared parent-child math engagement.

Knowledge of early childhood math skills In examining parent knowledge of preprimary early c hildhood education (ECE) math skills, analysis revealed a substantial dichotomy between perceptions of simple  and advanced number skills (Table 3). A paired samples ttest indicated a significant difference in parent p erceptions, with more accurate knowledge for simple  number skills (Mean = 2.45, SD = 0.677) compared to advanc ed skills [(Mean = 1.93, SD = 0.793); t(846) = 21.941, p <.001], indicating that parents are far less aware of the early number skills that are more associated with later math achievement (i.e., Advanced Number Skills; Table 3). The large effect size, Cohen's d = 0.754, and a standardized meandifference of 1.09 (SE = 0.126), underscore th e magnitude of the discrepancy. Further analysis th rough a series of ANOVAs linked parent knowledge to the RES ET domains. For simple skills, s ignificant relationships emerged across all domains: Role [F(2, 844) = 83.193, p <.001, ω² =.163], Expectations [F(2, 844) = 28.022, p <.001, ω² =.060], Skills [F(2, 844) = 3.003, p =.050, ω² =.005], Eff icacy [F(2, 844) = 19.922, p <.001, ω² =.043], and Time [F(2, 844) = 28.484, p <.001, ω² =.061]. Conversel y, for advanced skills, although the significance persisted, the effect sizes were relat ively smal ler, suggesting a less robust but still meaningful relationship between knowledge and RESET domains. The findings convey a critical message: parent knowledge of ECE math skills is intricately linked to their e ngagement and efficacy in their child's early educa tion, with a clear need for enhancing understanding of advanced ECE math concepts. ## Conclusion

The concept of Math Parenting Identity (MPI), as evidenced by our research, is a complex and dynamic construct, deeply embedded in the sociocultural fabric of the home math environment. Through the RESET framework, this study strongly suggests the existence of MPI, revealing its diverse manifestations across gender, ethnicity, income, and education. Significant correlations between RES ET domains and parental engagement in mathematical activities underscore the validity of MPI. The data compellingly argue for the acknowledgment of MPI within the learning sciences, advocating for strategies that recognize and nurture the math parenting strengths in all families. In fostering a positive MPI, we not only respond to  the call by Berkowitz et al. (2015) to understand parental involvement in math education, but also lay a foundation for children's lifelong mathematical journey, a testament to the transformative power of informed and supportive math parenting. Future research might consider the nuanced aspects of Math Parenting Identity (MPI) across a broader demographic spectrum, examining how cultural, societal, economic, educational, and generational  forces may impact the formation of MPI as well as math parenting practices. For example, qualitative research might capture the narratives of families of color, providing a richer context to the quantitative data.  Mixed-methods studies might examine the relationship between MPI and the child’ s formation of Math Learner Identity or explore the impact of early childhood parenting roles (e.g., moth ers—who perceive themselves to be less skilled and less confident--are typically those parents most responsible for early childhood nurturing) on both MPI and children’s early math learning.  By building a body of research that helps promote unde rstanding of MPI, we can enhance the efficacy of educational interventions, promote richer h ome math environments, and ultimately, support the development of children’s mathematical understandin g—a goal that is central to the learning sciences and  vital for the success of generations to come.
