# Play Across Disciplines, Across Ages: What is Lost Without Play?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This symposium asks one central question: what is missing when we don’t make room for play? The papers in this symposium foreground play and examine its role across diverse disciplinary contexts (math, literacy, and science), and across the lifespan (from five-year-olds to practicing teachers). Together, we consider the potential for play in supporting disciplinary learning as well as student autonomy and agency and make a case for why the disappearance of play is about more than a loss of simple fun.

## Introduction

Research has demonstrated that play offers a genera tive context for rich, conceptual learning (Wager &  Parks,

2014) and can offer one way of addressing widesprea d concerns about children's mental health (Allee-Herndon,

et al., 2019). Scholars have argued that play allows one to think beyond oneself, to test and explore the li mits of ideas, to create and invent, and to enter the socia l worlds of others (Brown & Vaughn, 2009; Gadamer, 1989; Vygotsky, 1978). Empirical research ha s demonstrated that play suppor ts creativity, content learning, and social engagement (e.g., Diamond, 2014; Hirsch-Pasek et al., 2015; Tsai, 2012). Most of what we know about play comes from research  with young children, primarily in educational spaces. Far less is known about play in any other c ontext, such as the potential of play as a useful s pace for disciplinary learning in later years, the ways that play might serve to perturb existing education al spaces, or how play changes and develops over the lifespan. This seems especially dangerous in a context when opportunities to play are disappearing from schools and other formal  spaces (Chang & Coward, 2015)., though the reduction and removal of recess and by mandating more prescriptive academic curricula. As learning gains stagnate and teachers leave the profession as curricula become more presc riptive, less open, and frame play as a deficit to learning, in this symposium, we ask: what are we missing when we don’t make room for play? Structure of the symposium These sessions weave together stories from projects  that foreground play in different ways, focusing o n exploration, improvisation, personalization, and di fferentiation as aspects of what play might look li ke in disciplinary learning spaces. Play often seems to fall into the category of “you know it if you see it,” and various definitions exist across the literature. However, c ommonalities in definitions of play include the ide a that play is “spontaneous, naturally occurring [activity] with o bjects that eng age attention and interest” (Lifter & Bloom, 1998, p. 164). In this work, we draw on an especial ly open definition proposed by Zosh and colleagues (2018), who define play as active, engaged, meaningful, socially interactive, and joyful. All sessions in this symposium ask questions about the appearance and impact of play, looking across disciplines and ages. Brownell focuses on how three cohorts of children across the lifespan managed to find spaces for play even amongst top-down constrain ts. Ryan & Danish begin with an entirely different starting point, looking at when students are engaged in free play i n ways that mediated moments of scientific inquiry within a co-designed playful learning context. Gresalfi et al. explore what mathematica l play looks like and how opportunities for mathematical thinking and persona l positioning are influenced by mathematical play. And finally, Maltese et al. consider the role of play f or adults and discuss how playful exploration can b e useful for teachers to get them engaged and lessen their fears of failing during professional development. In examining these questions, the symposium will contribute to our understanding of play across ages and disciplines, demonstrating what stands to be gained when we reform educational spaces to make room for play. ## Introduction and framing

The definition of play is  seemingly ambiguous for researchers, teachers, and caretakers alike (Sutton-Smith, 1997). Yet, despite this persistent uncertainty, a growing number of adults appear to agree that more research that critically considers and nuances play as representa tive of sense-making – primarily as related to children’s early literacies learning, their identities, and their co nstruction of social worlds – remains necessary (Wohlwend, 2023). Much like play, distinctions about what does or does not “count” as literacies remain somewhat muddled. Whereas some view “literacy” as a singular, standardized, and autonomous set of skills that are readily measurable and cultivated across a chronological scale of humandevelopment, others understand literacies as social practices that are always already cultural and ideological (Str eet, 1984). My understandings align with the latter conceptualization: I conceive of literacies as situated and relational (Compton-Lilly, 2003; Heath, 1982). Within early literacies scholarship, many researchers have – for decades – helped to amplify the playful, often critical, nature of children’s words and worlds (Dyson, 1997; Ghiso, 2014; Roskos, 1990). Likewise, many early educators have documented their noticings of play and learning, including how they attended to c hildren using play as a personally meaningful tool for sens e-making (Edstrom, 2003; Paley, 1990). Interestingly, alongside scholarly and practitioner writings – even children’s parents have extended understanding s of the possibilities of play as a generative, creative, and constructive cultural practice and for literacies  (Long et al., 2007; Wood et al. 2022; Yoon et al., 2016). Togethe r, these diverse perspectives and dynamic contribut ions foment play’s enmeshment with everyday practices of reading, writing, speaking, viewing, and listening in school and society.

In this study, I specifically built from the work o f Karen Wohlwend (2011), a notable scholar whose research projects stem from her 20+ years as a Kind ergarten teacher and whose scholarly contributions have enriched academic understandings about the inters ections of literacies, learning, and play. For exam ple, Wohlwend (2011) demonstrated how children used thei r play as literacies to "draw upon their imaginations and their lived experiences to tap into their passions and expertise" (p. 3). Moreover, by closely examini ng Kindergarten children's unsanctioned play during th e daily writing block, Wohlwend (2013) showcased th e persistence of play’s existence, even amidst top-down restrictions. In this paper, I adapted Wohlwen d’s (2011,

2013) conceptualization of play as literacies as I critically analyzed how three cohorts of children learned to play

“under the radar.” As outlined in the findings, chi ldren learned to create opportunities for play even  amidst topdown constraints adults sought to impose in grades 3, 8, and 11. Theoretical and methodological approaches pursued In addition to the work of Wohlwend (2011), I also built from other literacies scholars (Gutiérrez & Larson, 1994) and learning scientists (Vossoughi & Gutiérrez, 201 7) as I invoked a critical sociocultural approach i n my analysis. In doing so, I closely attended to how po wer and identity o perated (Lewis et al., 2007) and aimed to highlight and transform the unjust systems and structures at play (Lee, 2017). The focal data was generated as part of a multi-year, cross-comparative case study (Bartlett & Vavrus, 2016). The study site was one school district in the Midwestern United States, but focal children were enrolled in either a district elementary, middle, or high school class. The broader longitudinal stud y examined children’s evolving rhetorical practices from elementary schoo l onward in each grade-level cohort. However, I generated the focal data during the same academic term for al l three cohorts of chil dren (i.e., grades 3, 8, and 11) through daily site visits that typically lasted one to two hours. Data was generated using ethnographic methods (Dyson & Genishi, 2005), including participant observation, field notes and jottings, interviews, photographs, and audio/video-recordings. Because data generation was an iterative process, I wrote weekly conceptual memos (Heath & Street, 2008); here, I recognized multiple instance s when children in all three cohorts (i.e., grades 3, 8, and 11) appeared to engage i n seemingly (un)sanctioned play. Upon reviewing the  conceptual memos I created and the various artifacts generated in the field, I identified instances wherein children appeared to play “under the radar” in each grade. When possible, I traced the evolution of this play across the term. As I analyzed, I also considered how children’s (un)sanctioned play was organized in  response to certain (top-down) discourses and their identities.

## Major findings

Across all three grades, children demonstrated adaptability and creativity as they leveraged opportunities to move play from the margins to the center of classroom ac tivity, even in contexts constrained by adult-imposed restrictions. In all three grades, children leveraged relationships with their teachers or peers to create informal yet significant learning opportunities. However, the de gree to which children’s play was “visible” to auth oritative others varied. For instance, the grade 8 children a ppeared to divert their teachers’ attention from their gamep lay on school-issued computers so as not to have the prohibited p lay exposed and, ultimately, shut down their individual play.

Comparatively, a small contingent of children in th e grade 11 cohort collectively negotiated with thei r teacher using a restricted tool – their cell phones – by inviting the teacher into their digital play. Mo reover, the grade 11 children argued that their interest in the daily puzzles published by the New York Times was explicitly educational, and their desire to play with their teacher furthered their argument insofar as i t allowed them to practice positive social interactions. In this way, play served as a vehicle for self-expression and identity formation and provided a rich context for negotiating power dynamics within the school environment. Grade 3 similarly navigated top-down surveillance measures as they learned to engage in (un)sanctioned play, individually and collectively. Early in the t erm, children cautiously tested the boundaries of a n open-ended learning block. As the term progressed, children de monstrated more confidence as they outwardly crosse d the boundaries of approved activity during the daily bl ock and leveraged opportunities for cultivating per sonally meaningful peer play. ## Conclusions and implications

The findings illustrated that children are not pass ive recipients of imposed educational structures. I nstead, they actively navigated and manipulated their environmen ts and relationships to engage in play, even when i t was deemed (un)sanctioned. Furthermor e, the findings underscored the importance of recog nizing, valuing, and cultivating children's agency across their schooling. As evidenced here, reframing play as literacies can help adults better understand play as an integral part of learning rather than a distraction from it. Paper 2: Free play as emergent inquiry into the natural world within an informal playful place-based learning environment Zach Ryan, Joshua Danish

## Introduction and framing

Recent work in the learning sciences has been concerned with how the practice of play can be taken up to support and shape students’ inquiry-based learning (DeLiema et al., 2019; Guttierez et al., 2019). While work has and continues to support the use of guided play (Zosh et al., 2018) to design playful forms of inst ruction in learning environments, less is known about how students carr y out processes of inquiry in moments of unstructur ed free play amongst each other. In fact, free play itself is often viewed from a deficit perspective and seen as unproductive to learning by many (Souto-Manning, 2017). In this study, we analyzed qualitative video data to examine how students’ free play in place-based learning environments mediated moments of sci entific inquiry and their collective negotiations of epistemic and axiological perspectives about the natural world.

## Theoretical framework and design

Play as a cultural practice is an intimate window into the lived experiences and cultural ways of knowing of young children (Duncan & Tarulli, 2003) and is a naturall y interactive and negotiated process that lends its elf to processes of inquiry of the natural world (Goncu & Vadeboncoeur, 2017). During moments of unstructured  free play, young students have a chance to freely negoti ate among themselves, and put forth their epistemol ogical perspectives on the natural world as they attempt to make sense of phenomena they observe. The structure of the nature club was inspired by Zo sh et al.’s (2018) spectrum of playful learning experiences, primarily free play, guided play, and games. Naturalists and workers at the community cen ter had previously struggled having campers connect wit h place-based learning activities which resembled more structured lessons similar to a classroom learning environment. In response, we sought to integrate more play and exploration into the nature club, while also keepin g the plans flexible to allow campers to shape the agenda throughout the summer. We implemented 30-minute blocks, beginning with free play and moving along the spectrum into either guided play or games centered on the outdoors.

## Methodology

The current study was situated in a participatory design research (PDR; Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) context, which centered the spectrum of playful learning experienc es (Zosh et al., 2018) in the design of curriculum for naturebased and place-based learning environments. It involved youth and workers at a local community c enter’s summer camp which primarily serves marginalized com munity members in a predominantly White city in the midwest US. Author 1 previously worked with the com munity center as a Naturalist the past three years,  and reached out to form a research-practice partnership (RPP; Penuel et al., 2015). Data for this analysis primarily comes from youth p articipants’ use of action video cameras during moments of free play. As they played, participants recorded their outdoor exploration and interactions with peers in different place-based learning environments (e.g., local park, nature preserve, or communit y center). We then utilized interaction analysis (IA; Jordan & Henders on, 1995) to observe interactions of youth particip ants during moments of free play where they discussed phenomena  they observed in the natural world (e.g., a bug on a tree, a crayfish in a stream, a bird flying overhead). In  these moments, we aimed to understand how their pl ayfulness mediated or was mediated by their negotiation of their epistemic perspectives of the natural phenomena they had just observed.

## Major findings

Our analysis found that when situated in a larger p layful place-based learning context, free play in place-based learning environments provided youth a chance to pursue forms of scientific inquiry in their everyday interactions among peers without any sort  of prior prompting from adults. While the larger co ntext of the study focused primarily on guided play activities which centered selected phenomena and activities for children to p layfully engage with such as beavers and wetlands.

Remarkably, as time went on in the nature club camp ers across the elementary age range (5-12) began to notice and reason about natural phenomena they observed during their free play. For instance, one camper used a selfie stick on the camera to get it as h igh as it could go into a tree and asked an imagina ry audience “what do you all see up there?”. Another pair of campers not iced a white bug on a tree and determined that it m ust have hopped here all the way from Antarctica, and debate d on how long it would have taken it to get here, which they negotiated to be about two weeks. They also began u sing naturalist tools available to them during thei r free play such as binoculars, insect viewers, sun printing kits, and magnifying glasses.

## Conclusion and implications

The results of this study revealed that during moments of unstructured free play situated in a place-based learning environment youth participants engaged in rich scie ntific inquiry around observed natural phenomena. F urther study of the data is needed, bu t this likely was mediated over time by the other a ctivities campers were involved in during the nature club such as guided nature wal ks through local ecologies, and guided play activit ies and games with specific learning goals. The implication s of this study suggest that free play situated wit hin playful learning environments can offer valuable moments of independent learning alongside more structured guided play to facilitate self-guided and self-initiated inquiry of the natural world. Paper 3: What is different about mathematical play?

Melissa Gresalfi, Amy Parks, Madison Knowe

## Introduction and framing

It might seem that the worlds of “mathematics” and “play” anchor two opposite extremes (Clements & Sam ara, 2017). But for mathematicians, play is an acknowled ged element of engaging the discipline, in part bec ause mathematicians are offered a different ver sion of mathematics with which to engage. Rather th an taking as their task the acquisition of other people’s knowledge, m athematicians are afforded the opportunity to think  with and about mathematics, to inquire into its structure, its limits, and its possibilities (Bergen, 2009; Su, 2020). Research on children’s play argues that play allows one to think beyond oneself, to te st and explore the limits of ideas (Gadamer, 1989; Vygotsky, 1978), and to facilitate more humanizing experiences that engage children’s whole selves (hooks, 1994; Ladson-Billings, 1994). Yet, while the role of play in sup porting student learning is well-understood and often advocated for very young students, less research has focused on mathematical play for older children. Indeed, once children enter formal schooling, discussions of lea rning almost never reference play, and work investigating the design of mathematical learn ing environments has seldom explored whether or how  play might be involved. This presentation speaks into this void, by considering the features of mathematical play, and how those features are different from everyday school mathematical engagement. It is already well-established that the design of learning environment s matter in terms of what students come to know and how they come to think about thems elves. Studies of learning environments that create opportunities for students to explore, make their o wn decisions, and feel comfortable making mistakes have demonstrated their effectiveness at supporting stud ents’ development of identities  as learners and doers of mathematics (Gutiérrez, 2012). Agency, the opportunity to take action with regard to one’s own learning (Gresalfi, Martin, Hand, & Greeno, 2009), enables students to participate in mathematics in ways that are meaning ful and sensible to them (Gresalfi & Cobb, 2006; Nasir & Ha nd, 2008). Classroom environments that provide opportunities to exercise agency can “transform how students see themselves as mathematical thinkers, how they see the discipline, and ultimately, the mathematics they learn” (Turner, Dominguez, Maldonado, & Empson, 2013, p. 229).  This paper focuses on student mathematical enga gement, and specifically explores the nature of mathematical thinking, peer collaboration, and problem solving in play versus in standard mathematics activities. Specifically, we pose the following questions: 1) what is the nature of diversity of mathematical thinking that we see during baseline and playful learning; 2) what is the nature and diversity of students’ mathematical positioning during baseline and playful learning?

Theoretical and methodological approaches This analysis draws from year two of a three-year study of early elementary mathematics play. Within this larger project, participating kindergarten through second grade teachers engaged in five professional develop ment and co-design sessions per year with  the goal of supporting their use of play during mat hematics instruction. Each year of the study, participating teachers enacted s everal play-inspired units with varying mathematical content-related goals depending on the given time of year.  During the 2023-2024 academic year, we collected data from eight weeks of instruction in six kindergarten and first grade classrooms, captured in two-week increments at four times throughout the year. For each two-week increment, the first week focused on whole-class and small group interaction during “business as usual” mathematics instruction, using the school’s regular mathematics curriculum. Th e second week focused on whole-class and small group interaction while the teacher s were implementing the playful mathematics lessons they had co-designed with us. These lessons were co-designed by the teachers and the research team, and loosely fol lowed a “launch-explore-discuss” structure, with the explore phase generally including 1-4 different mathematical play centers. This  analysis focuses on one first-grade classroom taught by a teacher with five years of te aching experience.The data for this analysis comes from 360 cameras that were placed at small-group tables, capturing all students at the table. We focus on six weeks of data, selected based on having sufficient “baseline” and “play data.” Videos were analyzed using an emergent coding schem e that focuses on the mathematical ideas being discussed or explored (with a focus on both verbal and non-verbal interactions). To address RQ 1, we developed a set of mathematical codes driven by the common core content and practice standards, which were then modified and defined by a team of researchers for each conte nt area. We applied these codes first by looking at  the forms of mathematical thinking (content and practice) that were present at any time in the groups, and second, how many students in the group were thinking mathematically at any given time. We then identified five students  to follow more closely as purposely selected cases, so as to look closely at contrasts between student positioning across the standard and playful units (RQ2). These close level  analyses contrasted the individual level coding fo r mathematical thinking but also expanded to consider  the ways the student was seen and treated during t he group work by the teacher and by other students. This lat ter analysis using methods of Interaction Analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995), and paid particular attention to talk and gestures for evidence of the ways stud ents were positioned (Harre & VanLangenhove, 1999).

## Major findings

During baseline lessons, all groups demonstrated mathematical engagement, typically with a single mathematical idea. Looking closely at the students within each group, we found that students were generally engaging with the mathematical idea that was the target of the lesson or doing something not visibly mathematical (being off  task, waiting for help). At the individual level, student s either appeared to understand the assignment and to proceed towards its completion, or not understand it or not  be interested in it. During mathematical play, there was more variation in activity. While almost all groups again demonstrated engagement with mathematics during play time, there was significantly more variation in the mathematical ideas engaged. There was also time when students were playing with the materials but not in ways that were obviously mathematical. However, there was very little “off task” behavior that required teacher intervention a nd little time spent waiting for teacher assistance. At the individual level, most students engaged with mathematics at le ast some of the time, although all students spent time exploring materials, telling stories with mate rials, or otherwise engaging in playful activities in ways that were less mathematical.

Our five case studies, which will be explored in greater detail in the session, showed that play can create opportunities for students to be re-positioned with respect to mathematics. While durin g baseline mathematics students often gave up quickly if they  didn’t know what to do or didn’t understand the mat hematics, it was clear that the mathematical toys and tools created multiple entry points for the students. Even if students didn’t directly and immediately engage the playful mathematical mat erials in the ways the teacher had outlined, their play and exploration of the materials often led to mathemati cal engagement, albeit not always of the specific i deas that were targeted in that day’s lesson.

## Conclusions and implications

This analysis reveals the vast potential of play fo r supporting mathematical thinking, but it is not a  magic bullet that alleviates all problems. It is clear that incl uding mathematical play as part of the sanctioned a ctivity in mathematics class results in less certainty about which mathematical ideas students will engage. However, there are many affordances of mathematical play that, we argue, makes the uncertainty worthwhile. First, mathematical play creates more entry points for engagement—from students who follow directions to those who explore before engaging, it is clear that there is more than one w ay to play appropriately with mathematics. Second, math play offers opportunities for natural differentiation  amongst students, as they can pose and explore the questions that are interesting to them and relevant for what they already know and understand about mathematics. Finally, math play seems to create a space for more overall enjoyment of mathematics, an experience that is likely to have longlasting benefits. Paper 4. Playful exploration as a design feature in professional development Adam Maltese, Amber Simpson, Alice Anderson, Kelli Paul, Lauren Penney

## Introduction and theoretical framing

In this paper, we share our reflection on the learn ings from many instances where we have been working  with educators to support them in learning new approache s to instruction, develop skills or explore difficu lt topics. In these situations, and others, we  have found that having educators engage in active t asks that have playful components can ease tension, reduce concern about failure or not doing it right and generally seems to set the tone for richer exploration of the topic (Tsai, 2015). T here is a pretty extensive literature based on play  in education (e.g., Skene et al., 2022), but there are fewer pap ers on how to get teachers to facilitate play (e.g., play pedagogy (Andrada-Narcisa & Simona, 2024) or game-based learning (Meredith, 2016), yet almost no  papers on how to have teachers play as a part of their learning.

We know play and playful are interpreted in many different ways (e.g., Smith & Vollstedt, 1985). When using “playful exploration” we mean that we set up activities that allow for educators to engage in the substantive parts of a process while embracing the notion that there’s no right answer and have fun while doing it. From the outside it might look like we’re simply having adul ts engage in tasks that we would have kids do – and that’s usually what we’re doing. However, this allows the adults to have fun engaging in the experience which we hope reduces any risk aversion or concerns about failing (i.e., not meeting their goal)(Tsai, 2015). Experiences In this section we will briefly describe two differ ent activities we ran with educators that we think fit with our framing of playful exploration. One activity that we have used a number of times is a modification of the “spaghetti tower” activity where we provide groups with 20 pipe cleaners and instruct t hem to make the tallest tower in the allotted time. In a number of cases we used this ac tivity as an example maker activity where part of t he group is building and part of the group is observing the mak ing to test out various assessment tools. While the i nitial prompt of the activity may lead to playful exploration, we stir things up a bit by adding in the wrink le (i.e., constraint) of not allowing them to speak to one an other or being able to only use one  hand (assuming they have use of both) for part of the time. In the many time s we’ve run this activity it has worked to be engag ing, lead to laughs and smiles, and rich conversation afterwards.

Our second example comes from a workshop we were ru nning to get educators to engage with the idea of failure and the role and choices educators play in the experience. The goal of the activity was to make a kite out of simple materials, including string, d owels, chopsticks, newspaper, plastic bags, tape, c ardboard, etc. We chose this because, like some games or puzzles, it seems quite simple but is actually quite a challeng ing task (Greger, 2013). This makes the task at the desirabl e difficulty (de Bruin et  al., 2023) we sought-a task within reach but that would likely involve various failure s along the way. An additional complication is the wind-strength, direction, consistency-all have a huge effect on the outcome. The combination of the inherent challenge of building a kite, the unpredictability of the wind and evaluation of a goal that is clear (it flies or it doesn’t) could lead this to be a stressful situation. However, wit h an outwardly positive countenance, encouraging le arners to rapidly prototype and learn from each test, vigorously celebrating any small successes, and generally finding ways to make the atmosphere more playful, it is possible  to get a large group of educators who “should know  how to do this” to feel ok when their designs don’t work out. Despite only having one of five groups attain flying success, everyone agreed that this was a great learning experience and that they had fun despite being quite unsuccessful.

## Conclusion and implications

We present our experiences to demonstrate that playful exploration is an approach that we find to be helpful in educational situations, particularly when working w ith those who are in a position of regularly demons trating knowledge (i.e., educators). One challenge for educ ators is that it is difficult for them to shift o ut of the role of leading instruction and be open to gaining knowledg e (Musanti & Pence, 2010). Creating joyful and play ful experiences as part of the professional development  seems to be a quick way to get people to meet one another, break down social barriers to communicating and wor k with peers. Additionally, evidence from gaming demonstrates that individuals are more receptive to  failing when engaging in playful experiences like making, design challenges/games (Frommel et al., 2021). These playful explorations often lead to a higher energy level in the group and that typically leads to much richer e ngagement in follow up discussion and helps educato rs to see how they can carry over this approach when working with their own learners. This approach does not work for everyone. There are  educators who resist situations where there isn’t a clear set of directions. If the playful elements le ad to winning or have the potential for someone to feel they are being made fun of (e.g., because the ir team couldn’t complete the silly task you gave t hem), there’s the risk of fun/joy being lost. Ironically, some take the playf ul component too far and can annoy their peers. For  example, we have set up some simple challenges and competiti ons, and sometimes an individual or a group has tak en the challenge too seriously (e.g., wanting to measure t he height of their tower), which caused other membe rs to be frustrated. Depending on your audience, a playful a pproach may lead some participants to think that yo u’re not being serious enough. Although it is not the focus of our work, it is worth considering a potential si de effect of this approach is that through engaging them in play ful exploration, it is possible to improve their we llbeing (Dierenfeld, 2024).
