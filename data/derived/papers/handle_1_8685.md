# Processing Team Feedback: Exploring Emotional and Cognitive Responses to Team-Level Feedback

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research on feedback in teams acknowledges the importance of feedback mechanisms to team success. However, although processing performance feedback in teams is seen as an important prerequisite to its effectiveness, the processes emerging after feedback reception have been overlooked in past research. This paper provides insights into the emotional and cognitive reflective responses of graduate students (N=111) to team-level feedback. The results of our explorative study reveal that (a) students from high-performing teams generally report positive activating emotions and generally reflect on feedback when prompted to do so, (b) many students from low performing teams were able to show adaptive emotional responses to team-level feedback, and (c) there is a complex interplay between feedback valence and affective and cognitive reactions to team feedback as evidenced and visualized by Epistemic Network Analysis model.

## Introduction

The purpose of the present paper is to explore emotional and cognitive responses following team-level feedback of different valences. Specifically, we explore the  extent to which members of high vs. low performing  teams express (a) (de)activating positive or negative emo tions and (b) feedback processing and feedback hand ling intentions (i.e., reflective statements and formulation of a general vs. specific focus and/or a general vs. specific strategy) when prompted to react to team-level feedback. We are primarily interested in examining how students made judgments about their emotions and states rela tive to their team performance (self-evaluation), a nd what they perceived that they needed to do to improve fu ture team performance (adaptive inferences) using microanalytic protocols (Cleary et al., 2012). This paper contributes to understanding in three related areas: nature of emotions following team-level feedback delivery; relationships between feedback valence, types of emotions and feedback cognitive processing; and team-level considerations of feedback processing.

## Methods

Subject and setting The participants (N = 111) were last-year business students attending a Performance Management course at a French university. This simulation-based course aimed to provide students with opportunities for practice of and reflection on instruments and techniques to track, monitor and communicate about the employee performa nce. Students were assigned to teams of five to complete team tasks. Over 3 days, they functioned as units of a fictitious company that produced folded paper flowers colored with felt-tip pens. Intervention We used microanalytic protocols, a type of structur ed interview, to access students' emotional and cog nitive responses to feedback. They target students’ beliefs and processes after engaging in well-defined tasks. We used two questions: (a) a self-evaluating question (i.e., You have just received customers’ feedback on the product that your team has been crafting for the last two days. What was your immediate reaction to this feedback?)  and a strategic planning question (i.e., Moving forward, how will you use this feedback to improve your team performance? Please be as specific as possible). We received 111 responses for Q1 and 82 responses for Q2.

## Measures

To understand how students perceived their feedback, meaningful units of analysis (i.e., phrases, sent ences or series of sentences), were hand-coded using two cod ing schemes to affective and cognitive dimensions o f feedback perception: team reflexivity coding scheme, and the taxonomy of academic emotions. Authors collectively coded all responses using a joint codebook and reconciled disagreements via social moderation. The team reflection coding scheme was adapted from Gabelica et al. (2014) to capture the degree to which team members engaged in three cognitive (reflective) behaviors. Each student response to Q1 was coded as 0 (absence of reflective behavior), 1 (evaluating or reviewing team performance and strategies), 2 (looking for alternatives), 3 (Making decisions). ## Results

The results of our team-level qualitative analyses show that members of high-performing teams predomin antly express activating positive emotions when asked for  their immediate reactions to feedback. Additionall y, they provide little evidence of initial team reflection compared to members of low-performing teams that express more team reflexivity in the first place (Q1). However, it seems that when members of high-performing teams are asked to think about how to use their team feedback for e nhancement purposes, they start processing it cogni tively. When prompted to strategic planning, they predominantly state (general) strategies to act on feedback. This trend aligns with and extend results from individual-level research on feedback and emotions (e.g., Winstone & Carless, 2019). Though positive feedback can elicit positive  emotions, it could concurrently reduce their motiv ation to invest future effort, more specifically when not prompted to cognitively process feedback. Low-performing teams seem to show more complex patterns of feedback processing. Half of those teams express positive, activating emotions. There are no  teams that express team reflexivity statements and  negative emotions. Further, team reflexivity rather occurs in teams that have most members expressing positive emotions. Half of those teams already engage in cognitive thinking/processing of feedback at the first stage (Q1). However, after the second question that prompts team strateg ic planning, even teams expressing deactivating neg ative emotions state a focus and a strategy. Half of those teams even express a specific focus for their next team task. It appears that members of those teams resolved their negative emotions and moved on to cognitively assessing and integrating data from their feedback. Further, in contrast to high-and medium-performing teams, t he individual network for low-performing teams show that (de-)activated negative emotions are linked to s trategic planning comments. This indicates that generally, low-performing teams can harness the emotional impact of their negative team feedback and plan productive actions accordingly. We find more diverse trajectories for those teams. Some teams seem to show ability to separate emotional and rational responses to feedback and show striving for improvement; others jumped immediately to rational responses, while a few remained stuck at the emotional level of feedback reception. We could speculate that team  members of low-performing teams that did not proce ss feedback cognitively had difficulty determining what actions they should take based on feedback given by external customers (Winstone & Careless, 2019).

## Conclusion

Our results align with and extends the broader lite rature on emotion and learning (Winstone & Carless,  2019) which has shown that the premises that negative emotions lead to detrimental outcomes while positive emotions fuel positive outcomes, are too simplistic to expla in the effects of emotions on cognitive reactions i n team feedback situations. We have rather shown that inte rplay between affective and cognitive reactions to different types of feedback can lead to differential behavioral responses to team feedback.
