# Becoming a Critical Action Educator: A Comparative Analysis of Formative Interventions in Canada, China, and India

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We present a comparative analysis of professional learning communities (PLC) of teachers in Canada, China, and India, in the context of a project called the Critical Action Learning Exchange (CALE). CALE is an international community of educators dedicated to designing, enacting and sharing critical action curricula that empower students in responding to complex socio-environmental issues such as climate change, social justice, pandemics, and more (Carvalho et al., 2021). We analyze the three PLCs through the lens of activity-theoretical formative intervention research (Engeström et al., 2014). Comparison of the three cases identifies a series of key factors for the successful professional development of critical action educators and informs the design of future CALE elements.

## Introduction

This paper presents a comparative analysis of three formative interventions for the professional development of critical action educators. Activity-theoretical for mative intervention research has its roots in cultu ral-historical activity theory (CHAT), building on the works of Vygotsky, Ilyenkov, Leont’ev, and Davydov, with the goal of understanding transformations in activities that ar e undergoing some sort of risk, crisis, or pressure  for change (Engeström et al., 2014). Our study investigates fo rmative interventions in professional learning comm unities (PLCs) of educators in Canada, China, and India, in  the context of a project called the Critical Actio n Learning Exchange (CALE). CALE is an international community  of educators dedicated to designing, enacting and sharing critical action curricula that empower stud ents in responding to pressing, complex socio-envir onmental issues such as climate change, social justice, pandemics, economic inequality, and more (Carvalho et al., 2021). Educators that join CALE are presented with a pedagogical framework for critical action education, and a platform for peer support in the form of an online community  for collaborative design, implementation, exchange, and advancement of curriculum that empowers students as agents for positive social change. Critical Pedagogy (Freire, 1970; Kincheloe, 2004) is applied within the context of empowering teachers to help students overcome the sense of powerlessness regarding overwhelming socio-environmental issues, and feel empowered as agents capable of contributing to solving those problems. Our hope is that a comparative analysis of the formative interventions in these PLCs helps us to identify key components, features, needs and obstacles of these communities that inform our own understandings of how teachers candevelop new pedagogies and practices, and guide future implementations.

## Theoretical framework

The research literature describes professional lear ning communities (PLCs) as groups of professionals collaborating in an ongoing, learning-oriented, cri tical interrogation of their practice (Toole & Loui s, 2002). Supovitz and Christman (2003) stress the need for t he community to be grounded in supportive structure s and strategies. To design PLCs that help teachers adopt new approaches that reinforce students' sense of agency and collective responsibility, CALE draws upon the theo retical perspectives of Critical Pedagogy (CP) and Knowledge Building (KB). CP is based on a philosoph y of praxis that stresses the importance of an educ ative process that interweaves theory, action, and reflection as a means to advance the broader society towa rds social change and justice (Freire, 1970). This perspective  is grounded on the concept of conscientização, which Prilleltensky (1989, p. 800) describes as “The process whereby people achieve an illuminating awareness of the socioeconomic and cultural circumstances that shape  their lives and their capacity to transform that r eality”. Conscientização, therefore, aims beyond critical thinking to include a sense of critical consciousness that enables students to make judgements on their current and ideal realities and empowers them as agents for positive change. KB, in turn, offers an important theoretical founda tion for CALE's communities of practice, including fundamental principles of collective responsibility and epistemic agency, and constructivist practices to promote continuous improvement of ideas through dialogue and collective investigation (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2010). The emphasis on fostering and leveraging the agency of participants in designing their own inquiry process makes this methodology particularly adequate to our research of a community of critical action educators who are dedicated to investigating their own practice. The formative intervention methodology relies on two epistemological principles, double stimulation (Vygotsky, 1978) and the principle of ascending from the abstract to the concrete  (Ilyenkov, 1982). Through collaborative inquiry, participants generate future-oriented concepts and designs that open possibilities for changes in thei r own practices and processes, while allowing researchers  to investigate the generation of actionable knowle dge. Formative interventions use double stimulation to illustrate conflicts of motives. The first stimulus is typically a conceptual mirror that "reflects" the problematic situation being experienced. In trying to tackle the problem, the community may resort to external artifacts, or visions, which function as a second stimulus that supports  or sustains the transformation of practices (Engeström et al., 2014). These stimuli help participants to visualize their collective zone of proximal development (ZPD) which is “the distance between the present e veryday actions of the individuals and the historically new form of the societal activity that can be collectively generated as a solution to the double bind potentially embedded in the everyday actions” (Engeström, 2014, p. 138).

## Methods

Members of CALE PLCs participate in professional de velopment activities to advance their understanding  of critical action education and engage in designing n ew curricula (Carvalho et al., 2021). Participants can find technology support within the CALE online environment, where they can share their experiences designing and enacting new critical action lessons and engage in further authoring, discussion, and resource sharing. In the CALE PLCs, our goal has been to understand how educ ators' critical interrogation of their practice lea ds to transformative action. This question has guided us to model distinct interventions that accounted for each PLC's socio-cultural context. The underlying questions we have asked in developing our modelling workshops were: (a) What interventions are productive in a particular c ontext? (b) How can those interventions be conducte d to generate actionable knowledge that enables transformation of participants' teaching practices? (c) How  can our workshop model help teachers become critical action educators? Three teacher cohorts were organized, e ach of which can be considered an autonomous PLC in its own right: (1) 45 teachers in Canada who focused on climate change education; (2) 68 teachers in southern China who are also engaged in designing climate action curriculum; and (3) 60 teachers in Bangalore, India, coordinated by local partners. The formative intervention in Canada was organized in four sessions, carried out in Summer 2021. A pre-survey and critical questions functioned as mirrors; lectures and resources on critical action pedagogy and the CALE pedagogical framework for designing critical a ction curricula (Carvalho et al., 2021) were offere d to generate visions, and Zoom breakout rooms, Google Jamboard, and Google Docs functioned as a discursive space. As members of this PLC were interested in understanding the importance of changing teaching practices relating to climate change education and student climate anxiety, the socio-cultural context was adjusted to provide critical questions to help educators conceptualize and respond to these interests. The formative intervention in China was organized i n six one-hour workshop sessions that were conducted after a week of pre-workshop discussions and introductions. The aim of the pre-workshops was  to function as a mirror, aiming to engage teachers with new ideas about 21st-century competencies. In the following five sessions, a pattern was implemented with detailed and specific expectations of topics and activities. Weekly questions were offered as mirrors, content videos, supplementary resources, and "mic ro-lessons" functioned as visions, and a second mirror of each week were reflexive questions on learning strategies. Zoom meeting rooms and Google Docs were provided as a discursive space.

The formative intervention in India was organized i n four sessions. One distinction between the interventions in Canada and China was the inclusion of paper handouts that aimed to help participants interrogate their current educational approaches and reinforce their sense of identity as a professional learning community. The first two sessions were planned to function as mirrors of participants' current teaching practices and of fer possible visions in the form of lectures, examples of critical acti on curriculum, and discussions with a teacher guest. The final two sessions included a representation of the ideas and understandings of participants as mirrors and reiterated the discussion of visions to reinforce the pathway toward transformations.

## Findings

We describe our findings in terms of core concepts from activity-theoretical formative intervention re search methodology. Contradictions and conflict of motives  are concepts that relate to the initial state of t he system. Contradictions refer to the main conflict identified by the researchers in the activity system. This takes form as a kind of working hypothesis, which is then submitted  to the participants as an object of reflection to help them ICLS2022 Proceedings 1482 © ISLS articulate their own interpretation of the contradi ctions they face—referred to as conflict of motives. Finally, through the process of double stimulation, particip ants envision alternative possibilities and future directions, which constitute the collective ZPD (Engeström, 2014).

The contradictions in formative interventions are typically identifie d and represented through the modelling of an activity system (Figure 1). We identified the following contradictions in activity systems for all three communities, providing a set of working hypot heses: (1) the curricular expectations, which demand that teachers “cover” a broad range of topics, discourag e the time-consuming deep exploration of topics nee ded for critical inquiry; (2) there are conflicting pressur es on teachers, between the demand that students ar e equipped with “21st-century competencies” (e.g., critical th inking and collaboration) and the heavy content sta ndards measured by student success on standardized tests or competitive selective processes for higher education. These contradictions were presented to participants as introductory objects for critical reflection and discussion. Participants' critical reflection on artifacts that functioned as mirrors of their contradictory states was found to be consistent with the working hypothesis. Participant s reported some degree of anxiety or confusion, hav ing identified a need for incorporating 21st-century competencies and critical action into their curriculum while also confronting barriers to doing so (e.g., heavy conte nt expectations). After having identified and articulated these conflicts of motives with the help of activities that functioned as a first stimulus (mirrors), the three PLCs engaged in activities that operated as a second stimulus (visions). Through this process of double stimulation, participants were able to articulate their collective ZPD, which represents possible future directions for t heir activity. The collective ZPD of the three PLCs varied significantly, distinguis hing the communities and pointing them in particular future directions. Participants of the C anadian PLC focused on ideas for increasing collabo ration, exchange of ideas and resources, and sense of community. The primary focus was on climate anxiety and climate action, with the recognition that it is much easier to develop supplemental activities than ones that are central to the curriculum (i.e., that cover content through engagement in critical action and inquiry). Teachers in the Chinese PLC identified the enhancement of students' communication and collaboration skills as a fundamental need that underlies critical action inquiry. The assumption, manifested frequently, was that students are currently lacking those skills, which creates obstacles for critical inquiry. This was clearly a “slide back” from our initial discussions about pedagogy, to address an even more basic chara cteristic of Chinese classrooms (particularly at th e high school level)—that they do not present a conducive climate for collaboration and inquiry. Students and teachers alike have strong epistemic commitments to a didactic approach to lectures and exams, and this climate must be addressed before any form of inquiry can be attempted. Finally, in the Indian PLC, after some reluctance to begin designing activities, there finally emerged a parti cular interest in art-making as a strategy to engag e students in critical action. Accordingly, the initial activity designed and enacted by teachers used the rangoli traditional Indian art form as a medium for students to investigate and address diverse socio-environmental problems, such as the Indian farmers' crisis, population explosion, gender inequality, and more. This activity was a success, providing an illustration and grounding for other teams of teachers to begin their own process of designing for critical action.

## Discussion

The use of a formative intervention approach has he lped us to articulate important components of the d esign of effective PLCs in general, and of communities of critical action educators in particular. The study also highlights ICLS2022 Proceedings 1483 © ISLS some obstacles and eventualities that should be con sidered when designing such communities. One key de sign principle identified by this study is related to th e importance of cultural responsiveness. The invest igation of communities embedded within distinct cultural conte xts (i.e., Canada, China, and India) reinforces our understanding that critical action education does not and cannot mean the same thing for different contexts. The principle of cultural responsiveness is especially relevant in the case of international collaboration s, where teachers operate within very different cultures and educational systems, with strong norms of practice and diverse pedagogical understandings and epistemic commitments. Although the core principles of critical pedagogy have a universal character, their embodiment can take di stinct forms that must be co-constructed with commu nity stakeholders. In the formative intervention methodo logy, one important objective is to understand how an organization changes its existing state—deemed as problematic by the community itself—to a targeted improved state. It thus becomes crucial to recognize that ea ch cultural context may contribute to producing ver y distinct starting and/or final states. This study also found that the target itself has to be adjusted to present a viable pathway, in accordance with the communities’ respective starting places. Finally, we must recognize that the forms of discourse and interaction employed in the intervention must also be culturally responsive. To engage participants in acknowledging and responding to the double stimulation and progressing through reflective activities, it is crucial to design pathways and employ forms of discourse that find resonance within the cultural context. In each  CALE PLC, we recognized distinct forms of interact ion and discourse. Canadian teachers preferred to be left to their own devices and did not stand for many lect ures. They were good at self-organizing. In China and India, there was a more formal characteristic, where stronger central leadership was valued, and small groups still requi red close support. Chinese teachers valued the fina l presentations with feedback from visiting experts. Indian teachers felt the need for a strong "CALE id entity", including certificates of membership. While such el ements do not solve the problem of advancing critic al pedagogy, it would certainly not be possible to solve it without them.

## Conclusion

This study is significant in its application of a methodology from cultural-historical activity theory to the context of teacher professional development in critical pedagogy. It brings several distinct theoretical perspectives (i.e., critical pedagogy, activity theory, arts-based pedagogy, learning communities) to bear on the formation of three distinct professional communities, and studies the discourse and progression of those three through a lens of cultural responsiveness. The study helps respond to the persistent challenges of supporting teachers in developing new pedagogical understandings and practices. It will hopefully inspire other studies and serve as a reference for cross-cultural research within the learning sciences.
