# The Enactment of an Instant: A Study of Gesture in Introductory Physics

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Gesture has been shown to play an important role in how learners conceptualize phenomena in physics. However, we know little about how gesture is used to conceptualize instantaneity. Drawing on multimodal microanalysis of interaction, we examine how undergraduate physics students use representational gesture to make sense of instants while modeling energy dynamics. Our analysis describes four different forms of representational gesture used to capture instantaneity: These include (1) Replay loop of scenario, (2) Subinterval on timeline, (3) Freeze frame of scenario, and (4) Indexical location on timeline.

## Introduction

Gesture is a key resource for sense making in scien ce and mathematics educational contexts (for a revi ew see Singer et al., 2017). Students use gesture to conceptualize phenomena in physics (Scherr, 2008). However, with the exception of a recent study on spacetime (Steier & Kersting, 2019), we know little about how gesture is used to conceptualize time in physics. When modeling energy, what is included in a model is determined by an interval of time down to an instant (Harrer & Paul, 2019). Since a large body of researc h has established that people use gesture to make sense of time (e.g., Cienki & Müller, 2014, 2018; Tversky & Jamalian, 2021), we set out to better understand the role of students’ gesture in determining instants of time in modeling energy in physics. In this study, we use a multimodal microanalysis of  interaction (Goodwin, 2018) to examine undergraduate physics students’ use of representational gesture to make sense of instantaneity. We contribute a scheme made up of four types of gesture to enact instants. These types include (1) looping an interval of action in a physical scenario, (2) creating subintervals on imaginary timelines, (3) abruptly stopping a performance of action in a physical scenario, and (4) using a chopping motion to mark a location in time. Theoretical approach From research in linguistics, anthropology, and psychology, we kn ow the body  and spatial metaphors derived from embodied experiences are important tools for how people think about and communicate about time (Cienki & Müller, 2014; Tversky & Jamalian, 202 1). Human conceptualization s of time are embodied (Lakoff, 1992; Núñez, 2008), and cultures all over the world draw on gesture and  other spatial resources to communicate about and conceive of time (Cienki & Müller, 2014; Tversky & Jamalian, 202 1). However, no studies we are aware of have looked at how people in physics contexts use gesture to conceive of and convey ideas about instants of time. To appreciate the role gesture play s in how students capture instants in time, we adopt an approach to gesture that draws on Streeck’s conception of “ceiv ing” (2009). Ceiving is defined as using representa tional gestures to evoke schematized, selective interpreta tions of an image, event, or experience.  We also draw inspiration from Woodin & Winters’ (2020) investigation of representational gesture evoking “tiny” quantities to examine how undergraduate physics students use representational gesture to evoke the idea of an instant in time.

## Methods

Five groups of three to four  students were observed completing nine problems from the Collaborative Learning through Active Sense-Making in Physics curriculum (Potter et al., 2014) in an undergraduate physics discussion/laboratory class at a large university in the United States. The student-centered course offers opportunities for discussion, and gesture is  often utilized as a communicative resource (Scherr, 2008). When modeling energy dynamics of physical scenarios, students ide ntify relevant entities involved in a particular process and define a time interval of interest by determining suitable start and end points along a timeline. We created a collection of  55 instances of  representational gestures with accompanying  speech referencing an instant in time. Verbal references included the use of words like “second,” “instant,” “moment” “when,” “right before,” and “right after,” or direct mentions of interval boundaries such as “the initial” or “the final.” Through multimodal microanalysis of these interactions  (Goodwin, 2018)  four reoccurring yet distinct gesture types emerged and were developed into a  formal scheme. A second coder independently categorized 25 additional randomly selected instances, reaching 92% agreement (Miles & Huberman, 1994).

## Findings

We characterized four distinct forms of gesture rel ating to instantaneity (Figure 1): (1) replay loop of scenario, 24.5%; (2) subinterval on timeline, 15.1%; (3) freeze frame of scenario, 28.3%; and (4) indexical location on timeline, 32.1%. We chose representative examples of each type of instantaneity gesture to present in the findings. Figure 1 Illustrations and Brief Overview of Instantaneity Gesture Forms Replay loop of scenario  gestures describe situations where students repeate dly enact a process that includes an instant of interest. These representati onal gestures depict a physical process. Replay loops  allow students to “slow” processes occurring in a tiny time span and repeat them for further consideration. In addition, students are able to enlarge the representation of physical objects to zoom in on relevant details of the scenario. Figure 2 Example of Replay Loop of Scenario Gesture In Figure 2, we present an example of Kevin using a replay loop of scenario gesture. The group is trying to explain why a Tic Tac, when dropped on a table, can bounce higher on the second bounce. They are trying to break the event into small subintervals. One member suggests the initial time of their second subinterval should be right before the first bounce. Kevin responds by using his hands to enact a Tic Tac fall ing to the table (Figure 2a) and then brings his hand back up (Figure 2b) to repeat the action – dropping his hand to show the instant of contact again (Figure 2c, 2d). Verbally, Kevin suggests the initial time of the second subinterval to be the instant the Tic Tac hits the table. His gesture slows down and repeats this rapid sequence, elongating the amount of time the group has access to relevant imagery  to pinpoint the instant. This replay loop allows the group to “zoom in” and focus on the action that occurs too quickly to be analyzed as it occurs in the experiment. Subinterval on timeline  gestures describe situations where students bound a tiny dis tance in space, representing an instant in time as a very tiny subinterval. Subinterval gestures spatialize an instant, making it more visible and tangible (Woodin et al., 2020). They can also expand the representation of an insta nt in time, similar to how physicists use a tiny distance and interval in time to find instantaneous velocity Figure 3 Example of Subinterval on Timeline Gesture In Figure 3, we present an example of Jani using a subinterval on timeline gesture. Jani’s group is creating a model of a person jumping on a trampoline.  The group decided to start their interval at the maximum height of the jump where the person’s speed is zero, but they are unsure if the beginning of the interval should be the instant the person reaches max height or the instant just before the person falls. Jani questions if the speed would be the same for the “whole second” while making a subinterval on timeline gesture (Figure 3b). This mapping of time to space shows the tiny interval the person is at their maximum height. While the events Jani considers occur at the same instant, it is a productive practice to spread out the options tonegotiate the precise instant in time. Freeze frame of scenario  gestures describe the enactment of a process abruptly stopping or freezing at the moment in time being represented. As depictions of physical processes, freeze frame gestures allow students to depict the series of events they consider releva nt and to break down  processes, frame by frame, to show distinctions between parts of events. Like the replay loop scenario gesture, the freeze frame gesture is also like playing a short clip of video, except the video can be “paused” at a specific frame for consideration and emphasis. Figure 4 Example of Freeze Frame of Scenario Gesture In Figure 4, we present an example of Tom using a freeze frame of scenario  gesture. The group  is deciding the initial time for their interval when modeling a bal l being thrown. Tom explains that the instant the ball leaves the hand should start the interval so they will not have to include the hand in the model. He draws his arm back (Figure 4a) curling his fingers into a fist (Figure 4b) and then brings his hand forward  (Figure 4c), but abruptly stops his “throw” (Figure 4d) at the point where the imaginary ball leaves his hand.  The stopped short action of Tom’s freeze frame highlights this moment in time, making it available to explore and reflect on. Indexical location on timeline  gestures are chopping motions that index a precise spatial location as an instant in time. These representational gestures can be viewed  as stopping or starting lines /points, on a timeline or a long a path of motion. The indexical location gesture highlights the precision of a point in time and can increase specificity of language through synchronization with verbal descriptions of the physical scenario. Figure 5 Example of Indexical Location on Timeline Gesture In Figure 5, we present an example of Tatiana using an indexical location gesture. In this example, the group is creating a comparative  model for toy cars with different masses being launched by a spring. While discussing possible initial time s for their models, T atiana suggests the group should choose the point “right” (Figure 5b) “after” (Figure 5c) the spring returns to equilibrium, as that is the i nstant the car is released. Her use of the indexical location gesture makes salient the precise moment being refe renced; her timing of this motion with her speech increases the specificity of her language. The vertical chopping movement of her hand also serves as a starting line for the horizontal path of the car, as if operating on a timeline super-imposed on the scenario.

## Discussion and concluding remarks

This study examined undergraduate physics students’  gestural representations of instantaneity. We discovered four different types of instantaneity gestures  used by students. (1) Replay loop of scenario, (2) subinterval of timeline, (3) freeze frame of scenario, and (4) indexical location on timeline gestures support students’ negotiation of bounds as they develop timelines with appropriate intervals for modeling energy dynamics. This investigation adds to previous studies examining the ways the body is used to conceptualize time (e.g., Lakoff, 1992; Núñez & Sweeser, 2006; Núñez, 2008). We also contribute to a n understanding of how STEM students engage with and represent abstract topics like time using gesture (e.g., Steier & Kersting, 2019).

When students model the energy dynamics of physical scenarios, their gestures create important imagery to evoke schematized, selective interpretations of what counts as an important instant for their timelines (Streeck, 2009). Replay loops of scenario gestures and freeze frame of scenario  gestures both involve depicting physical processes and make it possible for students to imagine where they  should place the boundaries of the interval of interest. These gestures can be used to slow down, repeat, and pause the phenomena.  Subinterval on timeline gestures are more abstract representations of time, spatializing the instant being considered. Indexical location on timeline gestures are also more abstract and may be used to reference a point along the motion path of an object or seen as a point on a timeline by demarcating a gestured line. While freeze frame and indexical location gestures identify the precise moment of an instant, the replay loop of scenario and subinterval on timeline gestures can be seen as less precise and used to conceptualize an instant as existing somewhere within a sequence or space. Since many studies have found cultural and linguistic differences in how people gesture about time (e.g., Tversky et al., 1991), we cannot be sure if the types of instantaneity gestures we found are exhaustive of how undergraduate students gesture about instants. This study is limited to 19 introductory physics students’ use of these gestures in a specific regional context. Future studies might explore alternative types of instantaneity gestures used in different areas of the world as well as in different STEM disciplines. Our study also offers some implications for teaching: Instructors may wish to be intentional about how they use gesture (Alibali et. al., 2011) to model instants of time in order to support stude nts in visualizing and determining appropriate time intervals. Additionally, by attending to students’ time modeling gestures, instructors may gain valuable insight into students’ understanding of time in STEM learning contexts (Flood & Harrer, 2023; Kelly et. al., 2002).
