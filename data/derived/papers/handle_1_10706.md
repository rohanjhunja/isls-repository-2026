# Students’ Representational and Relational Caring in STEM

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Studies of both professional science practice and children’s science learning show that emotions, including care, are not simply ancillary to disciplinary work but a generative part of science practice. In science education research, however, students’ care is often overlooked. In this paper, we describe the nature and expression of care across two classrooms studying biology and ecology and participating in a pen pal exchange. We analyze artifacts from the pen pal exchange, as well as students’ retrospective interviews and written reflections. Two dimensions of care surfaced in students’ letters: representational care and relational care. We discuss practical implications for learning environment design and methodological implications for recognizing and analyzing how students’ caring manifests in the classroom.

## Objectives and significance

Studies of both professional science practice and c hildren’s science learning show that emotions, incl uding care, are not simply ancillary to disciplinary work but a  generative part of learning (e.g., Pierson et al.,  2023b; Fox Keller 1985; Haraway, 2016; Jaber & Hammer, 2016). Yet, as critical scholars have noted, care has been invisibilized in science (Fletcher, 2001; Kimmerer,  2013). In science education research, students’ ca re is often overlooked (exceptions are Krist & Suárez, 2018 and  McGowan & Bell, 2022); instead, this literature primarily focuses on teachers’ caring toward students (e.g., Velasquez et al., 2013) or pedagogies that promote care as part of socio-emotional learning (e.g., Garner et al., 2018). Moreover, students’ exp ressions of care may not be recognized as such by researchers or educators; car e is interpreted and enacted differently across com munities, and individuals’ perceptions and experiences of car e are shaped b y identities such as culture, race/ethnicity, gender, and religion (Antrop-González & De Jesús, 2006; Rogers & Weller, 2012).  In light of these issues, w e propose that it is important to foreground students’ expressions of care in science classrooms in order to understand the relational, emotional, and ethical w ork needed to sustain generative investigations.  In this paper, we describe the nature and expression of care acros s two classrooms studying biology and ecology and participating in a pen pal exchange.

## Theoretical framework: Theories of caring

In Toward a Feminist Theory of Caring, Tronto and Fischer (1990) proposed a definition o f care situated in political science that resonates across fields, wri ting that care “includes everything that we do to m aintain, continue, and repair ‘our world’ so that we can live in it as well as possible” (p. 40). Tronto (2013) elaborated this definition with five dimensions of care: “ caring about, or recognizing caring needs in the first place; caring for, or assuming responsibility for caring; care-giving, or the hands-on work of caring; care-receiving, that is being responsive to the ways in which the caring processes either have or have not met the initial needs… [and] caring with, or thinking about the effects of multiple care pr ocesses on trust and respect”  (p. 148).  María Puig de la Bellacasa (2017) connected Tronto and Fischer’s the ory of care to affective and ethico-political dimensions of scientific work, offering an image of care as hands-on agency, ethical obligation, and affective concern. Within scientific work, she cautioned against idealizing care, des cribing care as essential, collective, and disrupti ve. For instance, care includes mundane everyday activities  that are necessary to maintain ‘our world’ and tha t can also be harmful, such as “weeding one’s garden to make possible more fertile growth” (p. 164)—in this case, care can be tedious, and it is also inseparable from killing. Puig de la Bellacasa noted the power of such exam ples for questioning what is valued, prioritized, and obligatory. Still, Antrop-González and De Jesús (2006) cautioned that the White feminist conceptions of caring prevalent in education literature are susceptible to cultural ly, racially, and gender biased lenses. As McKamey (2004) argues,  “in the education literature, it seems that caring  is not a common reference point—it does not hold the same meaning across texts, and often does not even hold the same meaning within one article or essay. Caring is a symbolic concept  charged with multiple political, social, and cultural meanings” (2004, p. 5). In light of this critique, rath er than applying feminist theories of caring to our data as a priori categories, we take students’ expressions of caring as a starting point in our analysis.

## Literature review: Caring in science

Scientists and critics, drawing on feminist perspectives (among other criti cal stances, e.g., post-humanism, ecofeminism, and materialism), have critiqued notions of science that: (a)  devalue care as part of a project of objectification and asymmetric power relations (Fox  Keller, 1985); (b) undermine relations of kinship with the more-than-human (Haraway, 2016); and (c) neglect the entanglement of ethical, epistemological, and ontological dimensions of science (Barad, 2007).  A common theme across these cri tiques is the integration of the scientist with the world. A key marker of integration is the incorporation of care in disciplinary practices.  For example, Fox Keller (1985) describe d McClintock’s sensitivity to the uniqueness of each organism: “No two plants are exactly alike. They're all different, and as a cons equence, you have to know that difference … I start with the seedling, and I don't want to leave it. I don't feel I really know the story if I don't watch the plant all the way along. So I know every plant in the field. I know them int imately, and I find it a great pleasure to know the m” (p. 164). From years of patient observation comes what looks like privileged insight: “When I see things, I can interpret them right away” (p. 164). Fox Keller explain ed how McClintock was able to use the language of identification, affection, empathy, and love without jeopardizing h er scientific stance: It is “because, to her, science is not premised on…division. Indeed, the intimacy she expe riences with the objects she studies —intimacy born of a lifetime of cultivated attentiveness—is a wellspring of her powers as a scientist” (Fox Keller, 1985, p. 164). Care is not limited to individual scientists; it can also be fundamenta l to the culture of a lab. Analyzing interview data from a 5-year ethnographic study of the practices of biomedi cal engineering labs, Osbeck and Nersessian (2011, p. 57) argued that caring for organisms of study is “integral” to these labs’ broad research goals and to their local problem-solving. For example, they describe d how scientists in a neural engineering lab “anthropomorphized” neurons. Scientists from across the lab spoke about the importance for their research of the cells being “happy.” They described how laboratory protocols served the purpose of keeping cells in a “happy” state and how this sustained effort was rewarded in results. Overall, Osbeck and Nersessian (2011) described the feelings of care, concern, and reciprocity that thi s lab community has with their neurons as an exampl e of “cognitive partnering” (a system of distributed cognition) in laboratory practice (Nersessian et al., 2003). One field in which care has been extensively studied is laboratory animal science. Drawing on Tronto’s multidimensional care and Haraway’s multi-relational approach, Williams (202 3) analyzed human-animal and human-human relations that shape institutional cultures o f care. For instance, she describe d how researchers in these labs “held” responsibility for invasive and c hallenging tasks—either taking them on themselves to shield technical staff, or delegating to technologists (providing technologists with opportunities to build  skills that supported their career development). Williams explained: “a culture of care demands more than attentivenes s to the needs of the animals; it requires attentiveness  to the needs of others working within the organisa tion” (2023, p. 41). Despite care being essential in sc ientific work, in a study of female engineers in a high-technology firm, Fletcher (2001) described how care-giving work critical for sustaining design teams was performed by women and construed as “feminine” (e.g., nurturing, community  building). This work was devalued and “disappeared” institutionally. Sengupta et al.  (2021) noted that the devaluing and disappearance of such forms of care work, especially among marginalized learners, is also common in STEM education. In science education, research in formal K-12 contexts has primarily focused on the role of th e teacher as the “one-caring” (Noddings, 2013) or on pedagogical approach es for social-emotional learning (Garner et al.,

2018) or social and ethical education (Zeidler & Sa dler, 2008). Instead, building on a robust body of work on

caring in informal science learning settings (e.g.,  Parekh et al., 2023; Pugh et al., 2019) recent research in K-12 settings has begun to focus on students’ caring tha t emerges as part o f their participation in classroom investigations. McGowan and Bell (2022) focused on what we call representational caring —caring about the object of study, which is a representation of a lar ger phenomenon. Their design based-research project centered multispecies caring as part of ecological and climate science learning, and they found that meaningful engagement in problem-solving around sea star wasting disease fostered car e for species and their environments, as well as hope and agency. Krist and Suárez (2018) focused on what we call relational caring—caring about interpersonal relationships with peers. Using empirical examples, they illustrated how students attended to social and epistemic goals simultaneously, critiquing perspectives that position social interactions in service of epistemic goals. Drawing inspiration from the work reviewed above, t he current study considers the nature of care and expression of care in the context of a specific classroom activity: exchanging pen pal letters. Across content areas and contexts, a wealth of research demonstrates tha t pen pal letters can help students develop empathy  and humanistic values (Kneese et al., 2020; Tsang et al., 2021) and help students acquire cultural knowledge, identify similarities across cultures, and challenge stereotypes (Martin,  2019; Riley et al, 2022; Shandomo, 2009). In the context of a literacy class, Wiener and Matsumoto (2014) demonstrated how pen pals might also support science learning. They analyzed a year-long literacy project in which 4th and 5th grade students exchanged letters with pen pals across the Pacific about local marine ecos ystems. From their part icipation in this project, students reported that they valued their new relationships, learned about new cultures and marine species, and learned how to take care of the environment. The research reviewed above suggests that pen pal letters could be a fruitful context for analyzing the nature of students’ care and their expression of care. Writing letters can s hift students out of typical school science activities (e.g., lab  reports, presentations) in which  emotions, including care, may have been characterized as  inappropriate or unscientific. Moreover, in compari son to public classroom discussions, letters provide students with a less s urveilled space to express emotions in the context of their investigations.  To build on and extend the literature reviewed above, we analyze pen pal letters exchanged between 6th and 9th grade students. We ask: What is the nature of care in students’ letters, and how is it expressed?

## Method

We analyze data that were collected during a design  study (Cobb et al., 2003) that iteratively refined  a modeling curriculum intended to support students’ sensemakin g about the flow of energy and matter in an ecosyst em. In this paper, we focus on data collected from one implementation of the 9-week curriculum. The unit was designed by the research team  to enable students to engage with topics from the s tate and NGSS standards. The unit was framed by two design challenges: students designed a physical model (a “biosphere” —closed system physical model that included plants, snails, and guppies), a nd they programmed a computational model that repre sents a larger ecosystem of plants, snails, guppies, and guppies’ competitors and predators. Throughout the unit, students gathered information and re-represented the guppies’ environment with increasing complexity. Lessons took place three times a week during the students’ 56-min STEM class (22 class sessions per 9-week cycle). This study was conducted in a public middle school in a small suburban school district in the southeastern United States in the fourth author, Ms. S’s, classroom. Ms. S’s January-March 2023 STEM related arts class participated in this iteration of the unit (18 stud ents). During this quarter, the 6th grade students also interacted with 26 9th grade students participating in their first year of a four-year joint venture between a university and an urban school district, which offered science and math enrichment one full day per week on the university campus during school hours. Admission to this program was based on a competitive application process that tak es race, SES, and zoned school into account in an effort to make opportunities to participate equitable to stud ents in the district and to create classes that are representat ive of the large metropolitan school district that it serves. In the 9th grade classroom, students were tasked with building terrariums that represented a specific biome using plants and abiotic components of the ecosystem. In our study, each 6th grader was matched with one or two 9th grade pen pal(s). The second author and Ms. S collaborated with the 9th grade instructors to introduce and facilitate the pen pal project. Throughout the quarter, pen pals exchanged 3 sets of letters and 1 set of videos. We analyze pen pal artifacts, including scans of 102 letters (51 written by 6th graders, 51 written by 9th graders), 16 videos produced by 6th graders about their models, and 11 videos produced by the 9th graders about their terrarium projects. To triangulate our interpretation of these artifacts with students’ experiences, we analyzed 6th grade interviews and 9th grade written reflections.  Our retrospective analysis uses constant-comparative methods and inductive coding (Charmaz, 2006; Straus s & Corbin, 1990). We primarily drew upon pen pal d ata to identify and describe the nature of care in students’ letters and the ways that they expressed care. We considered care as a commitment (Pierson et al., 2023a): a resource (Hammer et al. 2005) that shapes how students use, value, and learn from models. From a resource perspective,  commitments are activated and expressed by individuals in response to conceptual and social contexts (Elby et al., 2016; Philip, 2011; Richards et al., 2020; Sandoval, 2005). In this study, we focused on commitments of care that surfaced in pe n pal letters. To identify instances of care, we analyzed students’ letters using qualitative data analysis software. We created initial coding categories in part based on students’ language (e.g., “fish needs,” “p rotect”). We grouped these categories of c are by their object (representational, e.g., fish, biosphere, or  relational, a person). We developed subcategories that more closely describe the nature and forms of care represented in the letters, which we elaborate below.

## Findings

Of the 44 6th and 9th graders, all students expressed at least one form of care in at least one of their letters. Across our data, we found two dimensions of care that surfaced in students’ letters, which we organized by the object of care: (a) representational care: caring ab out the guppies and investigations, and (b) relatio nal care: caring about peers and pen pals. For representational and relati onal care, we provide a list of all sub-categories in Table 1. As an example of our analysis, we present data for one sub-category from representational care and one sub-category from relational care. Then, we summarize sub-categories and make connections to studies of professional science. Rather than taking an objective or antiseptic stance toward the guppies in their jars, both 6th and 9th grade students demonstrated representational care (56/102 letters; 33/51 6th grade letters; 23/51 9th grade letters). Across the participants, almost all  6th and 9th grade students included an expression of representational care in at least one of their letters (43/44 students). Like Ge orge, Flora took responsibility for the fish (caring for) and did research to determine what she believed the fish could eat in the jar (care-giving). In such cases, care for the fish was generative not only for the 6th graders, but also for the 9th graders, who asked and explored their own questions about the biological and ecological needs of guppies. In addition to practical concerns, students also di scussed ethical implications of their care work. Fo r instance, CJ’s question (6th grade, letter 1) —“my teacher says that failure isn’t bad, but what if a life is on the line?”—is an example of a student considering both the moral and ethical implications of the project. CJ’s question suggested that he was not comfortable with the poss ibility that the fish could die, asking the 9th graders whether failure could be considered “bad” if a fish’s life was at sta ke. At the same time, he questioned a narrative that children often hear in STEM classrooms—“failure isn’t bad.” In such cases, the 9th graders offered the 6th graders an additional  safe space  to question the ethical assumptions of this image of science, extending similar conversations that occurred in the 6 th grade classroom. In addition, the letters offered the 6th graders a space to explore their own personal feelings of moral obligation in the context of their investigations. Almost all students demonstrated at least one form of representational care in their letters by: supporting fish survival (described above), protecting fish, attending to fish feelings, taking care of fish, or naming their fish (Table 1). In doing so, the students positioned their guppie s not only as objects of study but also objects of care. Just as scientists are attentive to the needs of an imals in their labs (Williams, 202 3), the students in our study demonstrated attentiveness to the survival needs of their guppies (caring for and care-giving). Some students also attended to the feelings of the fish (happiness, st ress). This form of caring about and care-receiving resonates with the labs described by Osbeck and Nersessian (2 011) that worked to keep their cells “happy.” A few  other letters hinted at the possibility of reciprocal car ing relationships with the guppies (caring with)—being excited about working with the guppies and naming the guppies. Studies of professional science (Barad, 2007; Fox Keller, 1985; Haraway, 2016) suggest that care toward objec ts of study (in this case, guppies) could be genera tive for students and could facilitate positive emotional experiences as part of their investigations. Relational care The pen pal letters were designed to offer students  additional space for emotions, such as care, as part of their investigations. We anticipated that students would demonstrate care for their guppies in their letters, but we also found that students demonstrated relational care in  their letters —they cultivated caring relationships with their pen pals (64/102 letters; 26/51 6th grade letters; 38/51 9th grade letters). Almost all students, including all 9th graders, demonstrated a form of relational care in at least one of their letters (40/44 students). Both 6th and 9th graders saw their relationship as a form of mentori ng. In their interviews, eight of the nine 6th grade students that we interviewed  described the pen pal exchange as a good experience  because their pen pal provided them with information and helped t hem make decisions. In their written reflections, all 23 9th graders positioned themselves as helping the 6th graders by sharing information, providing feedback,  or helping them understand science. Beyond sharing information, mentoring required significant care work on the part of the 9th graders that involved taking responsibility for the 6th graders’ learning and also attending to and respond ing to their specific needs (caring about, caring for, care-giving, care-receiving; Tronto, 2013). This work is handson, and it can also be ethical and affective (Puig de la Bellacasa, 2017). In written reflections, 9th graders described writing letters as “fun” but also as “stressful” (affective). Moreover, as they completed their written reflections, two 9th graders (Holmes and Jester) expressed concern to the second author that they were culpable for what they perceived as unethical choices made by their 6th gr ade pen pal. They were worried that they had responded too flippantly to CJ’s concern about guppies’ lives, se tting him on a “dark road” leading to his plan to set things up so that his guppies could eat their potential babies (ethical). We identified care enacted as mentoring in more tha n half of the 9th grade letters (38/51 9th grade letters). Some 9th graders worked in their letters to frame students’ investigations in a positive way. Some aimed to understand 6th graders’ concerns and be responsive to their emotional needs (in terms of feelings, sensemaking, and practices). In addition, some 9th graders demonstrated care for the 6th graders as emerging scientists, working to support their developing identities and competen cies as scientists by sharing information about sci ence as a discipline, assuaging their concerns, and affirming their ideas. Most often, 9th graders aimed to support 6th graders’ learning in their projects by asking pedag ogical questions to guide the 6th graders’ investigations (18/51 letters). For exampl e, when Ben (6th grade, letter 1) asked about diseases, Buzz and Rae (9th grade, letter 1) responded, “If the water gets contaminated by something, this can lead to the fish getting sick, which is no t good. In order to prevent this from happening, yo u clean your jar… What are some things you cando to make sure your fish doesn’t get sick? Are there other things that might cause the fish to get a disease?” While it is possi ble that Buzz and Rae were genuinely concerned abou t the fish, it also seemed that 9th graders were mimicking teachers’ pedagogical questi ons in this case, encouraging Ben to consider and research other possibilities. Questions like these indicate that the 9th graders cared not only about outcomes for the fish but also about supporting 6th graders’ competence as emerging scientists. Another way that 9th graders worked to support 6th graders as emerging scientists is by explicitly describing what science involves (8/51 letters). Fo r instance, when CJ (6th grade, letter 1) raised concerns about “failure,” Jester and Holmes (9th grade, letter 1) responded, “Failure is completely okay. It is actual a big part of science and learning in general.” Responses like th ese aimed to convey to 6th graders what science entails but also to comfort them in the event of a potentially disappointing outcome (the death of a fish). In addition to letters reassuring 6th graders by explaining that “failure” was a part of science, 9th graders also worked to assuage 6th graders’ concerns in other ways (15/51 letters). For instance, when Kendall (6th grade, letter 1) said that she did not “want the fish to b ecome stressed,” Slash and Mars (9th grade, letter 1) worked to reassure her by considering probabilities and by suggesting actions she could take to monitor and care for her fish. They wrote: “Don’t worry too much about your fish. There is a sl im chance your guppy will pass out because there isn’t anything in the jar that will stress th em. If you would like to study their stress levels,  you could spend time observing their swimming patterns and if they have any erratic movements. If you provide a nice environment with clean water and healthy plants, your guppy should do fine.” In this letter, Slash and Mars worked to support Kendall emotionally by considering her feelings (worry), appealing to reasoning (probability, “slim chance”), and suggesting specific practices (observation) that she could use to move forward with her investigation. The 9th graders also worked to build 6th graders’ confidence as scientists by affirming thei r ideas. For instance, Chobi and Nowhere (9th grade, letter 2) ended their letter with “you had g reat observations!” This approach was also used to preface suggestions; for instance, Pineapple (9th grade, letter 2) wrote, “I think your hypothesis is definitely heading in the right direc tion, but many of the variables which you can’t see, such as natural sprouting temperature, humidity, and soil type will affect which seed will sprout.” In this case, Pineapple worked to protect his pen pal’s feelings by offering critical feedback along with affirmation. Across the 6 th and 9 th graders, almost all students demonstrated at least one form o f relational care in their letters by: mentoring (described above), building personal relationships, or working with colleagues (Table 1). In doing so, the students positioned their pen pa ls not only as resources for learning, but also obj ects of care. While 6th graders primarily focused on building personal rela tionships with their pen pals (caring about, caring with), 9th graders also took on substantial care work in the f orm of mentoring (caring for, care-giving, care-receiving; Tronto, 2013). Studies of professional science un derscore the importance of caring not only for the object of study but also for other humans in a lab setting (Williams, 202 3). Yet, they also emphasize  that interpersonal care work is often taken-for-granted and invisibilized (Fletcher, 2001). Indeed,  in this study, we planned to analyze 6th graders’ care for  guppies (overlooking interpersonal care work), but we found that 9th graders took on significant work by mentoring their  6th grade pen pals. Reflecting on our project in the co ntext of Fletcher’s findings raises questions about how care work is distributed in schools and classrooms.

## Discussion and implications

In the analysis above, we illustrate the nature and  expression of care in pen pal letters. Taking stud ents’ expressions and interpretations of care as a starting point, we identified two dimensions of care: representational care and relational care. However, we are cautious about presenting these f orms of care as distinct categories.  Krist and Suárez (2018) note that science education  has often prioritized epistemic ends (representational) over social ends (relational). S eparating dimensions of care in this way thus risks  perpetuating a narrative that relational care is ancillary to disciplinary work rather than integral to disciplinary learning. While we separate representational and relational care in our analysis in an effort to describe the nature of students’ care, we also recognize that, in reality, the lines between these categories are blurred. In fact, our own data demonstrate the overlap between representational and relational  care. For instance, Tripp (6th grade, letter 3) br idged representational and relational care in his letter by describing cho osing names for his fish: a way of connecting with his “objects of study” (representational care) as individuals (relational care). Though names for the fish only surfaced in the letters once, it was common practice in this classroom and in previous implementations of the unit for students to name their guppies, and many students in this classroom shared the names of  their guppies in the end-of-quarter videos that they created for their pen pals. In addition, during the 4th quarter of the school year, when Larry (a fish with a bent tail from the classroom tank) died, students from the 3rd quarter class came back to the classroom to honor Larry with a sign and a “gra ve.” Getting to know fish as individuals was meaningful to students personally, but it also enabled students to attend to potential signals about guppies’ survival and wellbeing and, in turn, the health of their jars as model eco systems (just as getting to know plants as individu als supported McClintock’s research; Fox Keller, 1985). Examples like  these demonstrate the fluid nature of  the categories of representational and relational care, and they unde rscore the way that caring is intertwined with, rather thandistinct from, science sensemaking and practice.

These findings offer methodological and practical implications. In  our analysis, we worked to compare our interpretations of care in students’ letters wi th students’ accounts of their experiences using th eir interviews and written reflections. While we initially conside red this practice  as triangulation (using multiple sources to confirm the presence of a phenomenon), we later recognized this practice as a form of sensitizing to students’ caring. We see the practice of sensitizing as responding to two concerns from the literature: (1) care work is devalued because it is disproportionately performed  by women (Fletcher, 2001), and (2) conceptions of caring prevalent in education literature are susceptible to biased lenses (Antrop-González & De Jesús, 2006). Rather than focusing solely on students’ letters, we also sought students’ interpretations by casting a wider net in terms of our data sources (interviews, reflections)  to sensitize ourselves to students’ caring. These data sources enabled us to see forms of care that we would have otherwise miss ed. For instance, we initially aimed to analyze stu dents’ caring relationships toward their guppies, and we s aw the 9th graders as offering a context for 6th gr aders to express and explore care related to t heir investigations. From the 9th graders’ written reflections and discussion of their reflections, we realized we had been overl ooking the significant mentoring work the 9th grade rs were taking on, because we initially saw this work as di stinct from the e cosystem investigations (similar to how relational care is overlooked and devalued in disci plinary contexts; Fletcher, 2001). The se reflections and conversations helped us to recognize the sustaining and generative role the 9th graders took on as mentors, as well as the emotional and ethical labor involved.

In addition to recognizing the care work of 9th gra ders, this sensitizing approach to data enabled us to recognize and interpret forms of care from students ’ perspectives that we initially interpreted as a lack of care. For instance, we initially interpreted Jester and H olmes writing “failure is okay” as a form of callou sness and distance from the guppies; our later interactions, however, helped us understand that “failure is okay” was an enactment of care for their 6th grade pen pal, whom they were trying to protect from negative feelings in the event of a perceived failure. Similarly, we initially int erpreted CJ’s plan to have adult guppies eat baby g uppies as a lack of care; however, interacting with CJ in class and in the interview helped us realize that CJ’s plan was a form of caring for the adult guppies in the jar. In his interview, he explained: “I based cannibalism as the backup source of food in the tank…if the plant had died, then I w as hoping that you know, if they had children that they could eat the children instead of dying themselves…so my goal is to keep the main guppies alive, but to see like, if they would refrain from eating the babies as long as they could.” In moments like these, we realized that we, as adults, were often overlooking or misinterpreting students’  conceptions and enactments of caring (echoing Antr opGonzález & De Jesús’s [2006] warning about research ers and educators misinterpreting caring in educational literature). Thus, w e see the use of multiple data sources in these ca ses as not only a form of triangulation (establishing the trustworthiness or our data) but also as a form of sensitizing to students’ interpre tations and enactments of care. This approach has methodological implications for researchers and practical implications for educators. As we increasingly acknowledge the importance of care in science, both researchers and educators will need to be able to recognize the dimensions of care  that drive and sustain classroom investigations. A nd while sensitizing is a step  toward recognizing students’ care, researchers and educators will always overlook and misinterpret some aspects of students’ care. In light of this challenge, a path forward might also include helping students recognize and articulate their own understandings of care and implications for their investigations so that these interpretations of care can become an explici t and recognized component of classroom science learning. From a pedagogical perspective, this would require an understanding of how teachers can facilitate such discourse by creating a non-judgmental space for students to share understandings of care and their implications. The task of sensitizing ourselves as researchers and educators will be an important and continued feature of this work, along with exploring design conjectur es about learning activities that cultivate care. A lthough both of the classrooms involved in this study were familiar to the autho rs, we still found that our analysis questioned and problematized our initial understandings of how  care infused students’ actions —both in composing their letters and in their broader work. The failure of o ur habitual interpretations of students’ work measu res both the challenges involved in analyzing care and the opportunities for new insights in this line of research.
