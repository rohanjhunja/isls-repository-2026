## Process in the Context of Textbook Analysis

Boram Lee, Utah State University, boram.lee@usu.edu Seyedehkhadijeh Azimi Asmaroud, Virginia State University, sazimi@vsu.edu Abstract: Qualitative research often employs coding to analyze complex data, yet even with codebooks, the operational criteria guiding coders’ decisions frequently remain implicit. This gap limits transparency and comparability across  studies. We explore how an AI agent (ChatGPT-5) can function as a refinement tool to surface and formalize tacit reasoning in coding. Using textbook analysis as a case, we applied the Cognitive Demand Level (CDL) framework to 47 fraction-related problems from Grade 3 mathematics textbooks in Iran and Korea. Through iterative engagement, ChatGPT-5’s discrepant coding prompted us to articulate, refine, and document operational criteria, evolving from an intuitive flowchart to a detailed criteria table. This process transformed implicit reasoning into explicit decision rules that can be scrutinized and tested by others. We highlight AI’s affordance as a collaborative refinement tool: not replacing human coders, but scaffolding transparency, comparability, and methodological rigor in qualitative research.

Qualitative research has applied coding as a major method, yet even when studies provide codebooks with definitions and examples, the operational reasoning guiding coders’ decisions frequently remains implicit (Elliott, 2018; Roberts et al., 2019). This gap between conceptual definitions and applied criteria limits transparency and comparability across studies. In this proposal, we explore how an AI agent (ChatGPT-5 (OpenAI, 2025)) can act as a refinement tool in the coding method, prompting researchers to surface tacit reasoning, iteratively articulate decision rules, and make the coding process more explicit and communicable. Literature reviews Coding in math textbook analysis Qualitative research frequently employs coding as a core analytic method (e.g., Oliveira, 2022). To enhance rigor, many studies publish codebooks or tables that include each code’s definition and illustrative examples (Tondeur et al., 2025; McNeill & Fitch, 2022). These resources support transparency by clarifying how codes are intended to be applied. However, the presence of a codebook does not fully resolve a deeper methodological issue: the distinction between defining a code conceptually and applying it operationally to empirical data. We will address this issue more specifically in the context of math textbook analysis in the following section. Textbook analysis is a widely used method for investigating curricular content and structure (Chandra et al., 2025; Hadar & Tirosh, 2019; Vanden Ham & Heinze, 2018), that helps researchers identify what concepts are addressed, in what sequence, and at what depth. The Cognitive Demand Level (CDL) framework (Smith & Stein, 1998) is one framework, widely used to classify tasks of textbooks into four categories (Memorization, Procedures without connections, Procedures with connections, Do mathematics) by the complexity of thinking required (Haavold et al., 2025; Johar et al., 2018; Namh & Ozcakur, 2024). However, as in other qualitative contexts, coding processes using this framework face the same difficulty: how to consistently and transparently operationalize theoretical frameworks. Although the CDL provides conceptual definitions, these definitions do not function as ready-made operational criteria. Each study must therefore translate the CDL categories into specific rules for identifying and classifying textbook tasks. For example, Namh and Ozcakur (2024) presented problems such as 2a + 5 = 13 or 3k – 2 = +7 (p. 485, Figure 3) as a coding example of Procedures without connection category. However, the authors did not explicitly clarify what constituted the “no connection to the underlying concepts (p.484)” in these specific problems in their coding rationale. Other researchers might reasonably argue that such problems are, in fact, connected to important mathematical concepts —such as the meaning of the equal sign, the concept of a variable, or the structur e of equations. If so, there is no transparent way to resolve discrepancies between Namh and Ozcakur’s interpretation and alternative interpretations by other researchers. Likewise, the operationalized reasoning process through which researchers arrived at a certain coding result is often hidden. 1347 Beyond objectivity: Why operationalization still matters We acknowledge that the purpose of qualitative research using coding is not to secure objectivity or identify a single “correct” way of applying a framework to data (Dunivin, 2025; Maher, 2025; Saldaña, 2021). Researchers are free to develop contextually meaningful interpretations of their data, and such interpretive diversity is a strength of qualitative inquiry. Nevertheless, we argue that the specific and context-dependent operationalized criteria guiding researchers’ coding processes should be made explicit and shareable. This is because hidden, implicit criteria can limit transparency, making it difficult for others to trace or evaluate the coding process. AI in qualitative research Recent scholarship has begun to explore how AI can be integrated into qualitative research processes. Studies highlight several affordances: AI can assist with managing large volumes of data, support codebook development and refinement, generate alternative interpretations that prompt researchers to clarify their criteria, and document iterative changes to coding schemes (Christou, 2023). Researchers have also examined the comparability of AI-assisted analysis with human work, noting that AI can increase efficiency and consistency while making aspects of reasoning more visible (Feuston & Brubaker, 2021; Jiang et al., 2021). At the same time, scholars emphasize important constraints: AI cannot replace the interpretive judgment that lies at the heart of qualitative inquiry, and its use raises concerns around epistemological fit, transparency of algorithmic processes, and the risk of reinforcing rather than challenging coders’ assumptions (Roberts et al., 2024; Brailas, 2025; Franken & Vepřek, 2025). Taken together, this growing body of work suggests that AI is best positioned not as an autonomous coder, but as a collaborative partner that can scaffold and refine humandecision-making.

## Research question

Building on the insights from the previous studies, we position AI not as a substitute for human interpretation but as a refinement tool that helps researchers make their implicit reasoning explicit and communicable. Specifically, we explore this question with ChapGPT-5 in the textbook analysis context where CDL is applied as a coding framework. Accordingly, our study addresses the following research question:  How can researchers use an AI agent, ChatGPT-5, as a refinement tool to support the operationalization of coding process when analyzing cognitive demand levels in mathematics textbook tasks?

Materials This study is part of a larger cross-country textbook comparison project, and in this proposal, we will focus specifically on fraction-related chapters to address the current research question. We examined two Grade 3 mathematics textbooks: the nationally published Iranian textbook (Ministry of Education) and a widely adopted Korean textbook (Chunjae, 2018), used in over 80% of Korean public elementary schools (Son, 2025). From these, we analyzed 21 problems from the Iranian text and 26 from the Korean text, totaling 47 problems. All 47 problems were translated into English by the two authors to ensure a consistent linguistic baseline before any coding or AI interaction. Although textbook analysis is often used to derive curricular insights, in this study, our aim was not to present findings about the textbooks themselves. Instead, we used this dataset to illustrate how an AI agent (ChatGPT-5) can function as a refinement tool for operationalizing coding process. Iteration 3. In a newly initiated conversation, we asked ChatGPT-5 to code all 47 problems using the Version 7 Criteria. Agreement decreased to 33/47 (about 70%). Of the 14 disagreements, the largest share (7/14) involved problems that should have been coded as Doing Mathematics. We therefore focused on those cases first. As before, we provided our rationales and asked ChatGPT-5 to reapply the Version 7 Criteria to all 47 problems. We repeated this refinement eight times. By the ninth attempt, the agreement rate reached 89% (42/47), with five problems still remaining misclassified: two coded by the model as PWOC  that we had coded as PWC; two coded as PWC that we had coded as PWOC; and one coded as Doing Mathematics that we had coded as PWC. Interestingly, at this stage, the remaining discrepancies did not stem from omissions in the criteria but from ChatGPT-5’s difficulty applying the criteria as intended. Even though the Version 7 Criteria explicitly included the relevant decision rules, the model failed to implement them consistently. Rather than updating the table further, we instructed the model to adhere more strictly to the existing criteria and concluded the iteration process, judging that the criteria table sufficiently captured our coding logic while the residual disagreements reflected the inherent limitations of an AI agent in applying the criteria to borderline problems. The purpose of this study was to examine how engaging with an AI agent can help researchers make their reasoning more explicit throughout the coding process. Across the iterations, our initial flowchart —representing a relatively intuitive and implicit understanding of how we applied the CDL framework— gradually evolved into a more detailed and transparent criteria table. By engaging in this refinement process, we externalized the implicit reasoning that guided our code assignments. Such explicit documentation does not resolve the interpretive flexibility of qualitative research, nor should it, but it creates opportunities for dialogue about how researchers operationalize frameworks like CDL in specific contexts. With criteria articulated in detail, others can trace our thought process, scrutinize decisions, and apply the criteria to their own data, shifting coding practices from tacit and personal to open and communicable.

At the same time, limitations must be acknowledged. Given that the dataset was relatively small, further exploration with larger datasets should be conducted. Outcomes may also vary with different prompting strategies or researcher expertise, leaving quest ions about reproducibility for future studies. Nonetheless, the findings highlight the affordance of AI agents as refinement tools: not as replacements for human interpretation, but as mirrors that reveal ambiguities and prompt explicit articulation of cod ing choices.
