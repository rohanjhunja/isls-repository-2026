# Learning to Look Through the Lens: How Youth Practice Perspective-Shifting Through Filmmaking

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Perspective-taking is essential to critical thinking and empathy development. Perspective-taking involves looking at a situation from a viewpoint that differs from one’s usual viewpoint, and understanding another person’s thoughts, feelings, and how they experience the world. Filmmaking is a medium that affords many opportunities for perspective-taking; yet little is known about how youth practice perspective-taking in filmmaking programs. In this paper, we use qualitative data analysis to examine interview s with three young artists in a summer youth filmmaking program. Our analysis reveals specific instances of perspective-shifting, such as considering the audience’s point of view, which we use to create a codebook highlighting occurrences of perspective-shifting in the filmmaking process. We then examine these sections of dialogue and identify linguistic markers of perspective-shifting in the youth filmmakers’ language. The aim of this research is to inform learning design that supports a young person’s capacity to shift perspectives through creative media production.

Perspective-taking, in which one can explore a situation from a point of view different from one's usual viewpoint (American Psychological Association, n.d.), is an important skill to cultivate. It has implications for how we can: navigate interpersonal relationships (Epley et al., 2004), reduce implicit biases (Todd & Burgmer, 2013), encourage helping behaviors (Batson et al., 2002), and practice empathy (Batson, 2011; Oswald, 1996). In this paper, we examine how youth engage in perspective-taking within a  media arts program as they consider the perspectives of the audience, collaborators, mentors, and characters in a film. Specifically, we examine their “perspective-shifting” practices —a term intended to replace “perspective-taking” to emphasize the multidirectionality, fluidity, and flexibility of shifting one’s thinking to consider different points of view. As a lens-based, frame-based, and other-oriented medium, filmmaking affords opportunities to practice perspective-shifting throughout the creative process. Filmmakers look through a camera's lens and make artistic choices about angles, framing, composition, and lighting to construct meaning and facilitate emotional experiences for the audience. For instance, a close-up can provide the audience with an intimate view of a character’s emotional state, and pointing the camera upward (i.e., a low-angle shot) can make a character appear superior or powerful (Bordwell et al., 2010). Throughout the production process, filmmakers make artistic choices by shifting their perspective from their own to that of the character(s) and/or imagined audience. For example, when editing their work, youth filmmakers play with diverse multimodal resources (e.g., images, text, music, style) and then manipulate and synthesize them to construct meaning for the audience (Burn & Parker, 2003; Halverson, 2010). Additionally, during collaborative film production, filmmakers shift perspectives as they make decisions within their teams and listen to feedback on storytelling, planning, and other project elements. To better understand these moments of perspective-shifting during the filmmaking process, we asked the following research questions: RQ1.  When do young filmmakers engage in perspective-shifting during the film production process and learning activities?

RQ2.  How can we identify these moments of perspective-shifting in the young filmmaker's language? By examining the young filmmaker’s language, we can gain insight into their thinking and understand how they consider perspectives beyond their own. This is valuable for research in the arts and other disciplines that engage learners in user-centered desig n (e.g., game design, interaction design) because it highlights the enduring understandings that youth gain beyond the development of technical skills. Our research contributes to the design of learning environments that support open-mindedness, self-reflection, and social awareness through creative production.

## Theoretical background

Perspective-taking Perspective-taking is commonly defined as perceptual or conceptual. Perceptual perspective-taking concerns how others experience the world through their senses, and studies have generally focused on how objects are physically seen from varying vantage points (Flavell et al., 1981; Salatas & Flavell, 1976). Conceptual  perspective-taking (Gehlbach & Mu, 2023; Johnson, 1975; Oswald, 1996) focuses on the social and affective dimensions of how individuals consider others’ thoughts, feelings, motivations, cultural frames, and attitudes. In filmmaking, artists consider how meaning is communicated to the senses (e.g., visually, auditorily, gesturally, linguistically) and how different modes of communication, or their combinations, might affect the audience’s thoughts and feelings. For this paper, our focus will be on perspective-shifting, encompassing both perceptual and conceptual perspective-taking, but from a dynamic perspective that also accounts for the learning occurring within the process. We examine how young filmmakers shift their perspectives on situations, concepts, people, and themselves. Perspective-taking in disciplines related to media arts Perspective-taking in media arts has yet to be widely studied; however, a growing body of literature suggests it is an important area for exploration and for expanding our understanding. In a 2025 study, perspective-taking was one of 12 critical thinking practices identified when researchers examined youth’s artistic decisions and thought processes within a filmmaking program. They observed perspective-taking when filmmakers (1) considered the point of view of a character or imagined audience, (2) changed t heir perspective as a result of interaction with a tool or artifact (e.g., editing their film), and (3) considered feedback from peers (Beale et al., 2025). Other researchers have noted the importance of media arts tools in facilitating learners' exploration of different points of view. In an informal digital game design setting where students engaged in 3D modeling, Sheridan (2011) found that students “needed to learn to envision how the unseen parts of the object should look and use the camera to observe whether they modeled it accurately based on this envisioning” (p. 22). The learners engaged in a form of perceptual perspective-taking, first imagining how something would appear from a different angle, and then using the camera to view their designs from different vantage points. In another study on game design with youth, Dishon & Kafai (2020) found that engagement in perspective-taking depended on how youth designers understood and resolved the gaps between their initial designs and players’ actual behavior. The authors suggest t hat by practicing an iterative feedback process (e.g., playtesting), learners can cultivate habits for perspective-taking. Iterative, in-process feedback is also an integral part of the filmmaking process. For instance, youth filmmakers screen “rough cuts”  for their peers to determine if their intended messages are clear or if their artistic decisions are impactful. In theater education, Thalia Goldstein and colleagues have studied how acting training may increase social perspective-taking, theory of mind, and empathy in children and adolescents (Goldstein, 2011; Goldstein & Winner, 2012; Troxler et al., 2023). The results of these studies suggest that theatre workshops incorporating role-playing may positively affect a young person’s capacity for empathy and social perspective-taking, even without explicit instruction in either area. This is significant because it implies that perspective-taking can be intrinsic to theatre education. In filmmaking, learners engage in activities similar to those in theatre, such as acting/role-playing and producing work for an audience. This is important for learning design because thes e domains can support self-reflection, social awareness, and open-mindedness through experiential and project-based learning.

Linguistic markers of perspective-shifting In this paper, we identify linguistic markers  to better understand moments of perspective-shifting that occur throughout film production. There is no existing framework for linguistic markers of perspective-taking; however, we have drawn on prior research identifying linguistic markers of “agency” in certain pronoun-verb combinations (Konopasky & Sheridan, 2016; K. M. Sheridan et al., 2024; K. Sheridan & Zhang, 2023) to inform our analysis. Linguistic markers can help us highlight utterances in young artist s’ language that indicate a shift in their perspective, even when they do not explicitly articulate it. Often, for young people, reasoning about artistic decisions—such as those intended to affect an audience—is a developing skill. Youth do not necessarily come to interviews with fully formed thoughts about their artistic process. By identifying linguistic markers of perspectiveshifting, we can help researchers and educators notice  these moments in youth language, thereby marking occasions to cultivate greater curiosity and to ask probing questions. 613

## Setting and participants

Using an exploratory case study methodology (Merriam & Tisdell, 2015), we observed a week-long summer filmmaking intensive in Richfield, Utah. The program was run by Spy Hop, an award-winning media arts organization that provides after-school, in-school, summer camp, and youth-in-care programs. They offer handson, project-based programming in Salt Lake City and throughout Utah. Their approach combines youth development, arts education, and career preparation, equipping young people with the skills they need for success in and outside media arts. Founded in 1999 as an alternative after-school tutoring program, Spy Hop started with 12 students in its first year. Since then, the organization has experienced tremendous growth and now serves over 18,000 students annually from more than 120 schools across the state. Over the years, the staff and mentors have explored, refined, and defined what constitutes their unique approach, which includes youth-centered learning design, youth-powered spaces, professional mentorship, high-quality media production, community involvement, and a commitment to enduring values.

In 2018, Spy Hop initiated a unique expansion of its media arts programming with the launch of  “Voices of the West,” a program supported by multi-year funding from the MacArthur Foundation’s Media and Journalism (JAM) program. This program features a seri es of week-long media production intensives held in rural Utah communities. Richfield is a new site for the Voices of the West program. The participant group included 12 students, aged 13 to 18, and three teaching artists. Over five days, youth participants engaged in 30 hours of handson learning. They worked in small groups, each producing three films, including (1) a silent short film created in response to a prompt, (2) a parody of a "how-to" video, and (3) a short documentary spotlighting a person or place in their community. Of the 12 youth participants in the program, we have selected three of the final, semi-structured interviews for this paper. Each of these three participants discussed a different project, thereby providing the researchers with an understanding of perspective-shifting across the three projects mentioned above. The participants chose the pseudonyms Wes, Sophia, and Greta after filmmakers they admire (i.e., Wes Anderson, Sophia Coppola, and Greta Gerwig).

The first author conducted field observations of the program and participated in activities throughout the week. She recorded approximately 30 hours of field notes, audio of informal and semi-structured interviews with youth participants, audio of debriefing sessions with teaching artists, and creative artifacts (e.g., storyboards, final films). Embracing the interview as a “pedagogical encounter” (Vossoughi & Zavala, 2020), she employed reflective interviewing practices  (Nardon et al., 2021; Roulston, 2010) during on-site conversations throughout the production process and in the final interviews. Specifically, she listened to the participants as experts in their own experiences, built trust throughout the week by participating in activities, practiced active listening during the interviews, and facilitated reflection through dialogue and probing, asking follow-up questions such as “What makes you say that?” (Ritchhart et al., 2011). In the final interviews, the participants reflected on their process, shared their inspirations, intentions, and challenges, and discussed their artistic decisions.

## Data analysis

First, using the codebook for Critical Thinking Habits of Mind in Media Arts Learning (Beale et al., 2025), we identified moments of “perspective-taking” in the interview transcripts of the three participants mentioned above. Specifically, we looked for moments when they considered perspectives other than their own (i.e., peers, characters, audience) or when their perspective changed. To answer RQ1, we developed a sub-coding scheme to highlight different occurrences of perspective-shifting in the participan ts’ language. Then, to answer RQ2, researchers examined each example, iterated on what qualified as a “linguistic marker of perspective-shifting,” and developed a consensus through discussion.

Perspective-shifting to consider the point of view of an imagined audience When participants considered the point of view of an imagined audience, we noticed verb phrases such as “make it seem” or “give the viewer” (see Table 2). In Wes’s interview, he discusses his group’s film about a local restaurant and reflects on how his artistic choices will impact the audience’s experience. He states that he wants to “give time for the viewer to just appreciate the music, appreciate the shots, think about what is being said, and yeah, just really take in everything.” This statement follows Wes's discussion of using a slow musical score (“laid back guitar”) and a slower editing pace, specifically through “pillow shots” to create visual pauses in the film. A “pillow shot” is a term popularized by director Yasujirō Ozu to describe a momentary,  tranquil cutaway to an object or setting that offers the audience a moment of reflection. Wes was inspired by Ozu’s films after a teaching artist screened a short documentary. By intentionally using this type of shot, combined with slower editing and music, Wes is trying to create an experience that allows the viewer to appreciate the cinematography and reflect on what is being said in the interview.

In Sophia’s interview, she describes the short film she helped create for the first assignment, known as the “film blitz.” During this activity, each group used a randomizer tool (see Figure 1) to generate three prompts: number of scenes, number of objects/actors, and a feeling (e.g., anger, hope). Sophia’s group was assigned “anxiety,” 5 scenes, two actors, and one object. They produced “The Seed,” a comedic short about a woman who experiences anxiety after seeing an Instagram post about the dangers of eating watermelon seeds (see Figure 2). Sophia imagines how she would react to her film if she were not “a part of it,” stepping outside her own perspective to imagine what the audience might say, think, or feel. We labeled these linguistic markers as “speaki ng as an imagined other” (see Table 2), in which the learner speaks using imagined dialogue, as if they are an audience member, peer, or mentor: Sophia: So, honestly and truly, if I think about how I would watch it if I wasn't a part of it, I'd be like, ‘Oh, this lady's thinking she's going to die because she saw an Instagram post.’ ‘What is going on?’ And then I think the whole thing is just kind of ironic, how the doctor's like, ‘Oh, no, get out.’ And then he goes to the watermelon. I think that's really funny. And that'll give the audience the ‘what the heck’ kind of emotion.

The use of reflective interviewing practices  was essential in facilitating this type of critical reflection. For example, when the interviewer asked Greta why she used a close-up of the popcorn machine in a documentary about the local movie theater, she said, “It looked cool.” Then, the interviewer encouraged deeper reflection by asking, “If you were to shoot the popcorn from far away, as you did in the interview, and then a close-up of the popcorn being made, what is the difference between those two shots?” (interview transcript, June 27, 2025). Greta then explained, “A close-up would make it seem like it's more important compared to everything else.” Greta articulated how a close-up creates emphasis, alerting the audience to the importance of what is in the frame, thereby imagining how the shot would “seem” to an audience and considering their point of view. Perspective-shifting through interaction with a tool or artifact The participants often compare prior knowledge with knowledge gained through interaction with tools such as cameras and editing software, or with artifacts such as professional films. When asked what she learned through the program, Greta originally shared, “stuff about the camera.” When asked probing questions, it becomes clear that she is not simply referring to technical knowledge; she speaks about learning how to “look through the lens” and how using the camera has shifted her view of herself: Interviewer:  Do you trust yourself more now, after this week? Greta:  Definitely a lot more, especially since I feel like using the camera has been a lot easier compared to other art I've done. Not to say that it's not hard, but just compared to drawing, which can take hours and hours, finding a good shot can take you maybe 10 minutes.

Interviewer:  Do you want to say one last thing, just about what you learned this week? Greta:  Probably mostly just stuff about the camera. That's mostly what I learned. I had never ever used a camera in my life. I had never touched a camera. I think the closest to a camera I've gotten was a Polaroid that my cousin had, and she just took three pict ures of me, and then I never saw it again, so I've never been around cameras and stuff.

In this section of the interview, we can see how using the camera has been central to Greta’s newfound confidence as an artist and decision-maker. There was a shift in her self-image, as indicated by temporal signifiers (e.g., “a lot more now”) and hypothetical statements (e.g., “I could…”). She trusts herself more than before and feels more confident in her decisions. The interviewer also mentions that she observed a teaching artist referring to Greta as “the cinematographer.” The teaching artist’s confidence in Greta’s ability and his acknowledgment of her role as a decision-maker also influenced her self-view. Additionally, Greta interacted with other artifacts, viewing numerous examples of shot composition, camera angles, and camera movements, and engaging in handson exercises related to cinematography throughout the week. This combination of tools, artifacts, and mentorship helped shift her perspective of herself.

Perspective-shifting to consider peers’ or mentors’ point of view or feedback The mentors’ perspectives are most often reflected in curricular choices and learning materials. For instance, Wes shares that the teaching artist has “given me Ozu, Wes Anderson, different perspectives on how I should be filming with different depths of field.” While the artifacts (e.g., clips from professional films) and tools (e.g., aperture and zoom lenses) helped Wes see different perspectives and develop a new understanding of filmmaking, the teaching artist also played a highly influential role in this process. The teaching artist worked in conjunction with the tools, artifacts, and the learning environment to help Wes see things in new ways. Each interviewee considered their peers' perspectives throughout the filmmaking process. For instance, Sophia mentions multiple occasions when she needed to pause and listen to her teammates' points of view, shifting 618 her own perspective. She often refers to others’ points of view by using indefinite pronouns such as “people” or a generic “you” (see Table 2): Sophia: Yeah, I kept a really open mind about people's opinions in this, because nobody wants their creative freedom taken away. There were moments when I'm just like, “no…no,” but you have to be nice and be open to what they want to do too, because that's what you do when you're in a team and you don't want contention or anything like that. So even if it's hard, put down your pride and just let them have fun. This is what this is all about. In the context of media arts learning, youth-produced films are “objects-to-think-with” (Papert, 2020). In this study, we engaged youth in reflective dialogue about their films and the artistic process, seeking to make their thinking visible. Our methods allowed us to identify occurrences of perspective-shifting that might not be apparent in observations or surveys alone. Close analysis of participants’ language revealed linguistic markers (e.g., verb phrases like “give the viewer”) that signal when learner s are considering perspectives beyond their own. These markers make visible a form of thinking that is often hidden, offering researchers and educators a way to notice perspective-shifting as it occurs.

Treating the interview as a pedagogical encounter (Vossoughi & Zavala, 2020) further enabled this visibility. We embraced a dialogic interview approach in which meaning is co-constructed as the interviewee reflects on their experience. Reflective interview ing practices supported participants in articulating thoughts and moving beyond initial responses (e.g., “it looked cool”) toward deeper insight into their process. For example, Greta’s reflections revealed not only technical learning but also a shift in self-perception (i.e., greater confidence and trust in her decision-making), suggesting that perspective-shifting is not limited to understanding others but also encompasses how learners view themselves. When asked clarifying questions about her learning experience, Greta shared that she learned to “look through the lens” —a metaphor for seeing the world from a different perspective.

Perspective-shifting in this study was supported by an interplay of teaching artists, tools, artifacts, and collaborative processes. Instructional choices —such as introducing certain film examples or assigning roles like “cinematographer”—worked in tandem with hands-on production to expand learners’ ways of seeing. Importantly, these moments did not occur in isolation; a single creative decision often involved simultaneous consideration of audience, character, and tools. The codebook and linguistic markers developed here are not intended to fragment these experiences, but to help identify when and where perspective-shifting occurs within this complexity. The linguistic markers we identified demonstrate a cognitive dance: a stepping in and out of one’s own perspective to consider others' perspectives (e.g., audience, character, peer), see the world differently, and shift one’s understanding of oneself. Because of this fluid movement between external and internal, we use the term “perspective-shifting” in place of perspective-taking. These findings have broader implications for how we conceptualize critical thinking in creative learning environments. Perspective-shifting is a critical thinking practice in which learners continuously orient themselves in relation to others, imagined audiences, tools, and creative artifacts. In filmmaking, youth are not only expressing ideas, but anticipating interpretation, negotiating meaning, and revising decisions through iterative feedback. This suggests that creative production environments may uniquely support forms of critical thinking that are social and flexible.

Finally, this work has implications beyond media arts, particularly for fields that engage young people in design, communication, or user-centered processes. Perspective-shifting may serve as a foundational learning objective in disciplines such as interac tion design, game design, and even civic or community-based learning, where understanding diverse perspectives is essential. By framing perspective-shifting as a dynamic, nuanced practice—rather than a fixed trait or skill—we can begin to design learning e xperiences that intentionally cultivate it across contexts. Future research might explore how perspective-shifting develops over time, how it transfers across domains, and how it intersects with identity, culture, and lived experience. Such work would furt her position media arts learning not as peripheral, but as central to developing the flexible, empathetic, and reflective thinkers needed in an increasingly complex world.
