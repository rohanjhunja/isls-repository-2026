# The Instructional Legacy of COVID-19: Teacher Adaptation in Response to the Pandemic

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While being adaptive is a key characteristic of effective teaching, teachers can be resistant to change. In Spring of 2020, American teachers transitioned suddenly to distance learning because of the COVID-19 pandemic. This presented an opportunity to investigate teacher adaptations to extreme changes in teaching conditions. A survey study of 71 K-8 teachers conducted in Spring of 2020 found that teachers engaged in three of the core practices of adaptive experts. Moreover, most teachers planned to transfer what they learned to adapt future in-person teaching, and 30% of teachers planned to adopt transformative teaching practices. One positive outcome of the pandemic is that it promoted teacher adaptation and may have even prompted some teachers to develop lasting new practices.

## Introduction

At the height of the COVID-19 pandemic in Spring of 2020, most K-12 schools in the United States closed, and teachers and students were suddenly forced into distance learning. Almost overnight, teachers were expected to shift from teaching in-person to teaching remotely under drastically new conditions. Many K-12 teacher s had never taught virtually prior to this pandemic, and as such were required to adapt to these new conditions with very little prior experience or familiarity with digital  tools and technologies (Herold, 2020; Mupinga, 200 5). While these changes produced several challenges for teachers and students, they also presented a unique opportunity to study teacher adaptation to very novel conditions.

Teacher adaptation When teachers adapt, they change their teaching practices in response tonew student needs, situations, or challenges. For example, to support a new student who is an English language learner, a teacher might adapt his lessons to include visual instructions. To implement a new inquiry-based curriculum, a teacher might a dapt her pedagogical style to include more questioning and c hallenging of student thinking. This kind of adapti vity has been lauded as a key characteristic of effective te achers (Borko & Livingston 1989; Corno, 2008; Darli ngHammond & Bransford, 2005; Fairbanks et al., 2010). Furthermore, research suggests that adaptive teaching can boost student achievement (Collie & Martin, 2017; G ambrell et al., 2011; Hattie, 2009). However, adapt ing teaching practices is difficult. For example, in ob serving 51 elementary literacy teachers over a 9-ye ar period, Hoffman & Duffy (2016) found that teachers seldom a dapted their instruction significantly, often stick ing to preset scripts. Other studies have shown that teach ers struggle to adapt in response tonew curricula (Duffy & Roebler, 1986), technology (Cuban, 2001), education al reforms (Hiebert & Stigler, 2000) and profession al development (Ketelhut et al., 2020; Hamilton, 2018).

Adaptive expertise and adaptive practices Teacher adaptation has been associated with many overlapping constructs such as adaptability (Collie & Martin, 2017), improvisation (Borko & Livingston, 1989), th oughtful teaching (Fairbanks et al., 2010), teachin g adaptively (Corno, 2008), and teachers’ adaptive ex pertise (De Arment et al., 2013; Lin, Schwartz, & H atano, 2005; Schwartz, Bransford, & Sears, 2005). In this paper, we view teachers’ adaptations through the framework of adaptive expertise, which was proposed by Hatano and Inagaki (1986) to describe expertise in many domains. Adaptive expertise is often defined in contrast to routine expertise. Routine experts are highly efficient and accurate when performing under conditions that do not change. In contrast, adaptive experts are able to adapt to the demands of a novel situation. They can invent new procedures when situational demands call for it, let go of old ways that are suboptimal, and seek to contin ually learn and grow. For example, a routine expert  teacher might deliver lessons that are highly effective und er typical conditions. However, when conditions cha nge-a new curriculum, new standards, new assessments, new kinds of students-this teacher would falter. On the other hand, a more adaptive teacher would nimbly adjust and modify lessons appropriately for each new context. ICLS2022 Proceedings 655 © ISLS In this paper, we do not study expert teachers per se, but we use the framework of adaptive expertise to identify adaptive practices that teachers might adopt when thrown into the novel conditions of distance learning. Adaptive experts engage in three core adaptive prac tices. In this study, we looked for self-reported e vidence of each of these three adaptive practices in K-8 teachers. First, adaptive experts can perceive new needs or challenges that call for adaptation (Lin et al., 2005). Without perception of a new need or challenge, there is  no motivation to change. To assess this, we asked teachers to describe the key challenges they faced in designing instruction for distance learning. Second, adaptive experts modify or adjust current practices in response tonew need s and challenges (Martin & Schwartz, 2009). We call these  reactive adaptations because they occur in response to a perceived challenge. To assess this practice, we as ked teachers to explain how they had changed their teaching during distance learning. Third, adaptive experts e ngage in prospective adaptations which are motivated by a desire to continually learn and improve their performance in the long-run, rather than in response to an immediate need (Martin & Schwartz, 2009). To assess prospecti ve adaptations, we asked teachers what they planned  to change about their teaching upon return to the in-person classroom. How did teachers adapt to distance learning during COVID-related school closures? A small body of emerging literature explores how teachers adapted their instruction during COVID-related school closures. We focus our review of this literature on evidence related to the three core practices of adaptive experts discussed above.

There is research suggesting that teachers faced several challenges during the transition to distance learning. Chief amongst them is lack of access to technology and/or internet connections (Francom, Lee & Pinkney, 2021; Simmons, 2020) and low familiarity with online learning tools and technology for both students and teachers (Anderson & Hira, 2020; Blagg & Luetmer, 2020; Doiron & Marsigliano, 2020; Herold, 2020; Mupinga, 2005; Saad & Sankaran, 2020). Studies also report high rates of absenteeism among students during the pandemic, particularly in less advantaged rural and urban populations (Anderson, 2020; Bintliff, 2020; Leech et al., 2020). One survey study suggested that teachers met with instructional challenges, such that 55% of teachers said that their lessons hadn’t translated well to distance learning (Leech et al., 2020). Most of the research documenting reactive teacher adaptations in response to COVID-related distance learning focuses on the adoption of new technological tools and their use. It is not surprising that teachers began using new technological tools, such as video conferencing platforms like Zoom and learning management systems such as Google Classroom to enable online mediums for previously in-person activities and functions. Teachers also adopted technologies for communication with parents, such as ClassDojo (Francom, Lee & Pinkney, 2021; Lieberman, 2020; Leech et al. 2020). There is also evidence that teachers engaged in new practices, such as prerecording their lectures for students to watch on their own time (Pilkington & Hanif, 2021) or organizing virtual social gatherings to provide social and emotional support to students (Chamberlain et al., 2020). Little research examines prospective teacher adaptations or planned future changes to teaching after the pandemic. One study found that teachers planned to continue using many of the technological tools discussed above to record their lectures, communicate with students more frequently, or even take students on virtual field trips, which suggests a positive outcome of COVID-19 (Francom et al., 2021). However, this study focused on technology adoption, not teacher practices.

A few patterns emerged from this literature review. The majority of the research on teacher adaptation during the pandemic was conducted in the context of  higher education, used case study methods, or focu sed on technology. In contrast, our work examines K-8 teac hers, uses survey methods, and focuses more broadly  on teacher practices. Moreover, most research describe s what teaching looked like during the pandemic, bu t very little work has explored how the pandemic could spur long-term changes to teacher practice upon a return to inperson instruction. Finally, we have not come acros s other papers that use the adaptive expertise fram ework to examine pandemic-related teacher change. We aim to fill these gaps with the current study. The Current Study In this study, we investigate teacher adaptation during the pandemic by surveying K-8 teachers about use of three adaptive teacher practices: (1) perception of challenges associated with distance learning, (2) reactive adaptations during distance learning (or adaptations made in response tonew needs or challenges), (3) planned prospective adaptations for future in-person learning (or changes intended to be long-term improvements). This led us to examine the following research questions:

1. To what extent did teachers perceive challenges of distance learning, engage in reactive adaptations in

response to the new conditions of distance learning, and plan to make prospective adaptations upon return to in-person learning?

During the sudden shift to distance learning, we ex pected that teachers would perceive many new challenges to teaching and respond by reactively adapting their teaching practices to meet at least some of these new challenges. Given the extremely novel conditions posed by distance learning, one could argue that teachers were essentially required to adapt their teaching to some extent, and we found some evidence of teacher adaptation during COVID19 from our literature review. However, a more open  question for us was whether the pandemic would spu r teachers to plan prospective adaptations when they returned to in-person learning. While we found evidence that teachers may use newly learned technology after the pandemic (Francom et al., 2021), we were interested in better understanding the extent and types of planned chang es to future teaching practices. In other words, wo uld this experience of distance learning cause lasting changes to teachers’ practices? Or would teachers simply go back to their old, routine ways of teaching once the pandemic ended?

## Methods

Sample In May and June of 2020, 71 K-8 school teachers completed the survey study. Teachers hailed from 8 public and 4 charter schools throughout New York and Connecticut. Overall, the sample contained teachers associated with a variety of different grades, subjects, experience levels, school types, and student SES levels. Our team contacted 103 principals across two states for whom contact information was publically available, inviting their school to participate in the study. About 23% of teachers who received the survey completed it. Survey While the survey included several demographic questions, this paper focuses on three open-ended survey questions about teaching both during and after the pandemic:

1. What key challenges have you faced in designing instruction for distance learning? Give one or two

examples.

2. In which ways have you had to change your teaching practices to meet your students' needs during

distance learning?

3. Is there anything you have learned from your experience with distance learning that will change how

you teach once you are back in the classroom? If so, please describe.

## Measures and Coding

Responses to questions 1-3 were coded for types of challenges, reactive adaptations, and prospective adaptations, respectively. Additionally, responses to the third question were coded for prospective adaptations that were transformative. We conducted primarily bottom-up, emergent coding of the types of challenges and adaptations, with some top-down coding based on our definition of adaptation (Chi, 1997). Each response was coded by two coders and acceptable interrater reliability was achieved (κ>.7). Types of challenges & adaptations (reactive and prospective) Nine types of challenges and adaptations emerged from the data. We coded for these in all three questions. Coding categories were not mutually exclusive. These categories were: (1) lessons and instructional design (how teaching happens), (2) curriculum, goals and expectations (what is taught), (3) engagement and motivation, (4) technology access and learning (as it relates both to teachers and students), (5) differentiation, (6) noninstructional teacher responsibilities (such as providing technology support to families), (7) assessment and feedback, (8) student autonomy, and (9) communication (with students and families). Identifying prospective adaptations that transform the classroom We looked more deeply into the prospective adaptations category to identify prospective adaptations that had the potential to transform a teacher’s practice. We noticed that some prospective adaptations were improvements or variations on existing practices. Yet other prospective adaptations were more significant-they represented a new teaching practice or a big change relative to the teacher’s practice before the pandemic. ICLS2022 Proceedings 657 © ISLS We call these transformative adaptations.  For example, using a virtual platform to provide engaging visuals during a lesson is an improvement to an existing practice, as the tool enhances the lesson, but it does not create a significant change in the classroom. However, a new type of instructional design, such as a flipped classroom model, constituted a significantly new practice, potentially transforming the way students learn.

## Results

Extent of reported challenges and adaptations All teachers, save one, perceived challenges during distance learning (Table 1). Similarly, the vast majority of teachers (96%) reported reactively adapting their practices in some way during the pandemic to meet their students’ needs. This was not surprising, given that distance learning created drastically new conditions requiring some level of adaptation. More surprisingly, 89% of teachers planned prospective adaptations to inperson teaching practices. The surprisingly high percent of teachers planning to change future practices suggests that post-pandemic classrooms may look different. These outcomes did not vary by teachers’ characteristics, such as years of experience, subjects taught and grade. Prospective adaptations to future in-person instruction 65% of teachers planned to adapt in-person lessons and instructional design based on what they learned during the pandemic (green bars Figure 1). This includes providing clearer and more concise directions, creating more effective visual aids, reducing speaking time by focusing on key ideas, and allowing more time for student practice. 24% of teachers reported they would make changes involving technology access and learning, by incorporating newly learned technology into the classroom. 20% of teachers planned to improve student engagement in the future, by utilizing culturally relevant resources, allowing students to respond to teacher feedback using digital tools, and promoting online student-to-student discourse. In comparison to adaptations during distance learning, fewer teachers reported future changes to curriculum, goals and expectations, suggesting these types of changes may have been short-term responses to reduced instructional time during the pandemic.

Prospective adaptations that transform the classroom 30% of teachers reported planning to implement a practice that would transform their classroom after the pandemic. Four themes emerged in the types of significantly new practices teachers reported: expanding the learning environment, new instructional approaches, expanding the learning community, and a new focus on student needs. ## Discussion

During the COVID-19 pandemic, teachers were suddenly forced to teach remotely. This may have had the hidden benefit of provoking teacher adaptation. In fact, we found evidence that teachers engaged in three of the core practices of adaptive experts during the pandemic. First, all of the teachers we surveyed (save one) perceived key challenges that motivate the need for adaptation. Teachers faced challenges in adapting their inperson lessons to an online format, motivating and engaging their students, and supporting technology learning and access. Second, almost all teachers reactively adapted their practices in response to the new challenges. They modified instruction for an online format, re-prioritized content due to limited instructional time, and simplified or shortened tasks. While challenges were broadly distributed across categories, teachers most often reported adapting lessons and instructional design in response to those challenges. One possible explanation is that teachers have significant control over lessons and instructional design, and are most comfortable enacting changes in that area. Third, most teachers in our study planned to prospectively change future classroom practices by applying what they learned during the pandemic to future in-person teaching. Overall, our evidence suggests that the pandemic acted as a catalyst for teacher adaptation. Even more promising is the finding that 30% of teachers planned to adapt their future practices in ways that could transform their classrooms. Teachers learned a great deal from the experience of remote teaching which may have motivated them to adopt entirely new practices. Given the many examples of failed uptake of reforms by teachers (Cuban, 2009; Duffy & Roebler, 1986; Hiebert & Stigler, 2000), we find this to be a particularly encouraging result. The instructional legacy of the pandemic may be that it provoked some teachers to make major changes to their practice.

Why was the experience of remote teaching during the pandemic so successful at promoting teacher adaptation? We did not collect data on this directly, so we can only speculate. However, we see a parallel between the conditions of remote teaching during the pandemic and learning activities that promote the development of adaptive expertise. One way to develop adaptive expertise is to provide learners with exploratory, inventive learning experiences (Kapur, 2008; Schwartz & Martin, 2004). The pandemic may have provided a similar sort of learning experience for teachers, where they freely experimented with new teaching methods under novel constraints. This is different from typical models for affecting teacher change, such as traditional professional development. Therefore, our work may carry implications for the design of teacher education. Future research should investigate whether professional development which allows teachers to explore the design of instruction under novel conditions might spur teacher adaptation and the development of teachers’ adaptive expertise.

The emergent themes in teachers’ transformative changes offer a possible silver lining of the pandemic. If the learning environment can be extended, students can access learning materials outside of the classroom and during absences. This may mitigate some of the negative effects of absences on academic performance ICLS2022 Proceedings 660 © ISLS (Gottfried, 2011). Similarly, expanding the teaching community may allow greater family involvement in education which is important for academic performance (Weiss, Bouffard, Bridglall & Gordon, 2009). Specifically, keeping families informed and providing access to educational resources can facilitate out-ofschool learning and support. New instructional approaches might allow for increasingly personalized learning, or other teaching models that offer benefits to students (Pane, Steiner, Baird & Hamilton, 2015). Finally, by focusing on student needs, teachers become more attuned to the challenges their students face, which may prepare teachers to better adapt to the needs of their students in the future. Additionally, the increased focus on social-emotional needs may motivate educators to adopt social-emotional learning interventions in the classroom, which can offer benefits for childrens’ mental health and academic performance (Zins, 2004). One limitation of this study is that the data are obtained from self-reports. Future research should aim to obtain additional evidence of teachers’ adaptive teaching practices during the pandemic. Additionally, while we collected data on planned adaptations, we do not know which of these were subsequently implemented in the classroom. We are currently analyzing data from a follow-up interview study to investigate which types of adaptations are actually implemented and which conditions make those changes possible.
