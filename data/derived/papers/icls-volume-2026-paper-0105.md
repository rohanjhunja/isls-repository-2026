# An Analytical Approach to Examining Learner Feedback Agency in Social Contexts

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Existing literature on Learner Feedback Agency (LFA) highlights its inherent complexity, where it is situational, adaptive to changing contexts; deliberative, shaped by learners’ reasoning rather than just actions; and entangled with structures, shaping ea ch other rather thandetermined by them. Yet, existing analytical approaches often fall short in capturing LFA’s complexity. This paper proposes the Multi-Agent Interplay Analysis (MAIA) to address this limitation by (1) examining LFA with multi-agent and temporal structures, (2) synchronising behaviour and deliberations with time, and (3) utilising visualisations to examine the interplay of LFA and structure. Through an illustrative example, I demonstrate how MAIA revealed insights into how learners exercise their LFA in social contexts.

Feedback is essential for learning, and there is growing advocacy for student-centred feedback practices that foreground learner agency. As learner feedback agency (LFA) becomes increasingly recognised, emerging conceptualisations of LFA highlight ed its inherent complexity (e.g., Nieminen et al., 2022), discussing it as situational, adaptive to changing contexts; deliberative, shaped by learners’ reasoning rather than just actions; and entangled with structures, shaping each other rather thandetermined by them (Lee et al., 2024). However, current analytical approaches have yet to capture this complexity adequately. This study addresses this by proposing the Multi-Agent Interplay Analysis (MAIA) for examining LFA. This study defines LFA as a process of learners’ self-reflective and intentional actions and interactions through which reproduces and transforms the structures that shape the feedback process. Structure is described in this study as recurring patterns of interaction among agents that coalesce into shared practices, expectations, and norms (Liddicoat & Taylor-Leech, 2021). While structure is commonly depicted as broader institutional or cultural norms, this study specifically focuses on immediate structures in the learning space. Further details on structure will be provided in subsequent sections. Lastly, agents refer to individuals participating in the interaction, including students and teachers. This study addresses the question: How could LFA be examined? This paper begins with a brief review of existing analytical approaches, highlighting their limitations in accounting for LFA’s complexity. To address these limitations, I propose the MAIA analysis, which addresses these limitations by (1) examining LFA within multi-agent and temporal structures, (2) synchronising enacted actions and anticipated actions, and (3) employing systemic examination of LFA and structure through Enacticipation Graphs. The paper concludes with an illustrative example demonstrating how MAIA reveals insights into LFA in naturalistic settings. Existing conceptualisations and analytical approaches to LFA Recent conceptualisations of LFA evolved to embrace its inherent complexity. Earlier frameworks discussed LFA centred on individuals (e.g., Winstone et al., 2017), emphasising the learner’s active role in regulating, appraising, and utilising feedback. Gradually, more scholars extended to include broader sociomaterial and ecological influences, recognising LFA’s interplay with contextual factors (Nieminen et al., 2022; Gravett et al., 2022). As interest in LFA continues to grow and its conceptualisation evolves, it is vital to evaluate whether the analytical approaches to studying LFA align with this expanded theoretical framing. A review of the literature identified four predominant analytical approaches: sense-, occurrence-, type-and sequence-of-LFA. Sense-of-LFA approach examines students’ traits and dispositions relating to LFA. For example, the Receptivity to Instructional Feedback scale is an instrument developed to examine students’ traits in receptivity to feedback (Lipnevich & Lopera-Oquendo, 2022). Typicall y grounded in psychological frameworks, this approach examines how personal characteristics, such as tendencies and perceptions, influence students’ feedback involvement (e.g., Dawson et al., 2024). Studies adopting sense-of-LFA often utilise self-report instruments, providing a scalable approach for quantitatively examining LFA across large populations. Occurrence-of-LFA approach examines LFA through the frequency of observable behaviours, such as seeking or acting on feedback (e.g., Cutumisu et al., 2015). For example, Hwang et al. (2002) analysed variations in students’ feedback-seeking behaviours, such as frequencies of  seeking feedback from peers versus professors or inside versus outside of class, to understand the extent of student involvement in feedback. This approach relies 936 on quantitative assessments of observable behaviours as proxies for LFA, where more enactments of certain behaviours correspond to greater exercise of LFA.

Type-of-LFA approach examines LFA through the qualitative differences in how students are involved with feedback. For instance, Ellegaard et al. (2018) categorised students’ responses, ranging from no response to highly reflective responses, to illustrate the diverse ways learners interact with feedback. These approaches typically categorise feedback behaviours into types which correspond to different degrees of LFA. This approach ranges from focusing narrowly on a specific feedback action, such as feedback-seeking (e.g., Li & Han, 2024), to broadly across different types of LFA actions (Ellegaard et al., 2018). Sequence-of-LFA approach adopts a process-oriented perspective, tracing how feedback-related actions unfold over time. Studies often employ interactional or conversational analysis to capture feedback involvement and LFA (e.g., Ajjawi & Boud, 2018). For instance, Waring (2009) used conversation analysis to demonstrate how students exercise their agency by disrupting sequences, while Zhu and To (2022) used interaction analysis to map moment-to-moment changes in involvement. Although less commonly used, this approach offers a more contextualised understanding of LFA, accounting for its evolving nature within feedback interactions.

## Limitations of existing approaches to examining LFA

While existing analytical approaches have made significant strides in advancing our understanding of LFA, they often fall short of capturing its inherent complexity. Most approaches reflect limitations rooted in earlier conceptualisations of LFA, which tend to depict it as static, behaviour-focused, and unidirectional (Lee et al., 2024). Lee et al. (2024) argued that  LFA is Situational, Deliberative, and Entangled (SDE). This perspective acknowledges that LFA is situationally constructed, emerging through interactions with evolving structures; deliberative, encompassing both externalised behaviours and internal cognitive processes; and entangled, where feedback involvement not only responds to but also actively shapes structures. This perspective highlights the limitations of existing approaches that need to be addressed to advance the field. First, existing approaches often portray structure as monolithic and static, downplaying its evolving nature and obscuring the situational nature of LFA  (Lee & Chen, 2025). For instance, type-of-LFA approaches that classify agency as maladaptive, adaptive, or lacking (e.g., Nieminen & Tuohilampi, 2020) offer useful categorisations but give less attention to how agency is enacted and transformed in relation to changing structural conditions. This assumes structure as a singular and unchanging backdrop against which LFA plays out. Understanding the situational nature of LFA requires looking into how learners adapt their involvement in their interactions with existing structures. Consequently, just as LFA is situational, structure needs to be re framed as dynamic and continuously shaped through interactions with agents over time. This shift calls for analytical approaches capable of accounting for the dynamism of structure, like CORDTRA (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2008) and uptake analysis (Suthers et al, 2010), to offer deeper insights into how LFA operates as an emergent phenomenon. Second, the existing analytical approaches rarely examine students’ deliberations in the feedback process, overlooking the deliberative aspect of LFA. Nicol and Kushwah’s (2024) study is among the few studies that accounted for students’ deliberation by analysing self-feedback comments and reflections on their feedback experience. Their approach sheds light on how students generate internal feedback through comparisons with information. However, their analysis does not extend to other areas of deliberations, such as initiating feedback dialogue or co-constructing feedback in multi-agent settings. As such, deliberations remain underexamined within LFA studies.  Accounting for the deliberative aspect of LFA also presents methodological hurdles. In earlier attempts at examining students’ deliberations in feedback (Lee et al., 2025), I encountered two challenges. The first lies in the potential divergence between behaviour and deliberations; a student’s visible actions may not always be congruent with their internal reasoning or considerations. For instance, the behavioural act of sharing work may seem to be irrelevant to feedback, but with insights gathered from the students’ deliberations, the student revealed that she was seeking feedback from her peers. Overcoming this limitation requires an analytical approach that integrates both actions and deliberations for a more accurate depiction of LFA. The second arises from the varied formats and modalities of the data. Actions are typically captured through observations or recordings, providing a precise record of what learners do at specific moments. Conversely, deliberations are often gathered through self-reports or interviews, offering insights into why learners act but often in a temporally diffuse manner. The differing nature of these data types makes it challenging to link specific enactments with the deliberations that inform them. Addressing this challenge require analytical frameworks capable of integrating diverse data sources on learners’ enactments and deliberations to provide a more holistic examination of LFA. Lastly, current analytical approaches often neglect the entangled nature of LFA, falling into one of two camps: an individualistic focus that isolates LFA from its structural context (e.g., sense-of-LFA), or a deterministic view that frames LFA as an outco me of structural conditions (e.g., Gaunt et al., 2017). The first obscures how structures enable or constrain LFA, while the other overlooks how learners can shape the structures 937 that they inhabit. Neither approach adequately captures the entangled interplay of structure and LFA. To account for LFA’s entangled nature, it needs to be examined as both emerging from and contributing to evolving structures. This calls for an analytical approach that takes a macro-micro vantage point to examine how learners’ involvement emerges from and simultaneously contributes to the structures of learning environments. This analytical challenge is not new. The difficulty in examining the entanglement between structure and agency has been a longstanding problem within agency literature (Archer, 2000). While attempts have been made to account for it (e.g., Emirbayer & Mische, 1998), they often remain centered on the individual. The challenge in accounting for entanglement lies in bridging the gap between individual-level (e.g., actions and deliberations) and system-level data (e.g., how classroom practices shift). Without an approach to systematically examine these levels, risk reducing LFA to individual involvement or an outcome of pre-existing conditions. To address the limitations discussed above, the next section introduces the proposed Multi-Agent Interplay Analysis (MAIA). The Multi-Agent Interplay Analysis (MAIA) This section introduces the Multi-Agent Interplay Analysis (MAIA), a qualitative analytical approach to examine LFA in collaborative learning settings. While quantitative methods are effective in identifying trends and frequencies, they often reduce LFA to isolated variables, oversimplifying its complexity. In contrast, a qualitative approach aligns with the SDE perspective, offering a more holistic understanding of LFA and its interplay with structure. MAIA achieves this through three core pillars: dynamic, synchronic, and systemic. These pillars, which underpin the conceptual and methodological foundation of MAIA, are elaborated in the subsequent sections. Dynamic: LFA with multi-agent and temporal structures The dynamic pillar accounts for the situational aspect of LFA by conceptualising structure as evolving across two interrelated dimensions: the multi-agent and the temporal. Multi-agent dimension of structure The multi-agent dimension represents the sociality of structure, depicting it as emergent from the collective interactions of multiple agents. For instance, when a teacher mandates that students write corrections alongside teacher-provided feedback, and students consistently adhere to it, their repeated enactments contribute towards establishing the practices as a class-wide norm. Over time, such recurring patterns of interactions among agents coalesce into shared practices and expectations that constitute the structure of the learning environment (Liddicoat & Taylor-Leech, 2021). Thus, rather than being an external pre-existing entity, structure is emergent from the interactions and contributions of agents within a learning environment. The multi-agent dimension also reveals how structure is subject to reinforcement and transformation through ongoing negotiations among agents. For example, when a learner provides feedback to a group, this enactment alters the immediate conditions other ag ents must navigate. This suggests that agents’ enactments not only respond to existing structures at play but also contribute to the co-construction of new ones, influencing the trajectory of subsequent feedback processes. Through the actions of agents involved, norms and practices are continuously re-negotiated, creating immediate enablements and constraints, forming emergent structures that jointly determine the conditions for subsequent involvement (Soto-Andrade, 2023). As such, the multi-agent dimension aids in capturing the interplay between LFA and structure, revealing how learners exercise LFA within a network of agents, including teachers, peers, and technological tools, whose respective involvement reinterprets established norms, practices, and expectations, thereby shaping the evolving structures that govern future actions. While existing literature typically refers to structure at broad levels, such as national culture or disciplinary norms, MAIA focuses on immediate structures, limiting the multi-agent dimension to agents directly involved within specific feedback episodes to ensure analytical feasibility. Temporal dimension of structure The temporal dimension depicts the continuous evolution of structures over time. As described earlier, norms and practices are renegotiated through the interactions of multiple agents, shaping structures as time progresses. In the short term, agents’ actions generate immediate structures that shape subsequent actions. Over extended timelines, these cumulative recurring interactions could result in shifts in feedback cultures and practices. Moreover, LFA is influenced not only by present circumstances within a feedback episode but also across a longer timeline, such as past experiences and future aspirations (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998). For example, a student with a history of negative feedback experiences, such as receiving harsh criticism from teachers, may develop a heightened wariness to engage with feedback, and thus shaping their subsequent feedback involvement (e.g., Winstone et al., 2017). Collectively, depicting structures as temporal and multi-agent facilitates a more nuanced analysis of the co-evolution of LFA and structure, emphasising their dynamism. 938 Synchronic: Bridging enacted and anticipated actions The synchronic pillar centres on synchronising diverse data streams, allowing an integrative analysis of learners’ enactments and their deliberations as they unfold during the feedback process. In each turn, enactments are coded as enacted actions, while d eliberations are coded as anticipated actions to capture the agent’s intent behind their actions. By synchronising enacted and anticipated actions, MATE ensures a comparable level of abstraction to offer a comprehensive depiction of how learners exercise LFA. Enacted actions are coded to detail the observable behaviours learners exhibit during the feedback process. MAIA builds on interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) adopted by sequence-of-LFA studies (Ajjawi & Boud, 2018) to identify turn-by-turn enacted actions within feedback episodes. Through recordings and observations, enacted actions are identified and coded based on two interrelated components of feedback: feedback information (e.g., content conveyed) and action taken (e.g., seeking, uptaking).  This coding provides a holistic account for feedback as both information and process (Winstone et al., 2022). Anticipated actions, layered onto enacted actions, reflect the outcomes learners aim to achieve through their behaviours in a given turn. Learners act with an anticipated outcome, seeking to influence the process to align with their objectives. For instanc e, a student’s enacted action of seeking feedback may arise from the anticipated action of their teacher providing more detailed guidance. Anticipated actions are coded from the semistructured interviews, where learners articulate the reasoning behind their behaviours. Like enacted actions, anticipated actions are coded for their content and process, facilitating comparisons between what others enacted and what learners anticipated. By synchronising anticipated and enacted actions, MAIA analysis creates a temporally aligned representation of learners’ behaviour and deliberations. Systemic: Visualising interplay through enacticipation graphs The systemic pillar accounts for the entangled aspect of LFA through visualising the interplay of structure and feedback agency. Unlike existing textual approaches, which struggle to capture the interplay between LFA and structures (e.g., Emirbayer & Mische, 1998; Biesta & Tedder, 2007), visualisations hold potential in illuminating complex patterns (e.g., Hmelo-Silver et al., 2008; Suthers, 2006), including how multi-agent interactions contribute to evolving structures and how learners adapt their actions within these interactions. Here, I draw upon the Enacticipation Graph to visualise the interplay (Lee & Chen, 2025). The Enacticipation Graph represents the structure’s multi-agent and temporal dimensions on the axis. The x-axis represents time, measured by turns, while the y-axis lists participating agents, visualising each agent’s involvement over time. Derived from “e nacted” and “anticipated,” the Enacticipation Graph maps enacted actions  (i.e., what agents do) and anticipated actions (i.e., what they expect to occur), revealing how agents exercise their agency to influence the feedback process. At each turn, the enacted actions of an agent and its corresponding anticipated action of others in the immediate structure are plotted to synchronise the actions and deliberations at the turn-by-turn level. These actions are coded similarly to reflect the actions taken (e.g., seeking, providing) alongside the corresponding feedback content  (e.g., historical narrative, argument). These synchronised analyses of actions and deliberations illustrate how agents envision their actions influencing subsequent feedback interactions, and illuminate the interplay between LFA and structure, offering a systemic view of how learners co-construct conditions for feedback within the structure. A critical feature of the Enacticipation Graph is its visual depiction of the structure-agency interplay through uncovering the enacted and anticipated actions of each agent in the immediate structures. There are three scenarios depicted in the visualisation: alignment, when one’s anticipated action matches the enacted action of others represented by a single grey box; misalignment, when one’s anticipated action differs from other’s enacted actions, shown as two boxes; and a lack of response, which appears as a single white box, indicating one’s anticipated action had no follow-up from others. This visual distinction allows for a clear analysis of how agents’ anticipated outcomes are either supported or hindered by the actions of others, revealing insights i nto how immediate structural conditions enable or constrain the exercise of LFA. The Enacticipation Graph encapsulates the entangled relationship between structural conditions and LFA, providing a visualisation to examine feedback episodes systemically. Reflecting the dynamism of structure and LFA, these entanglements can evolve across  time, revealing how students adapt their feedback involvement in response to dynamic contexts. For instance, learners may exercise more proactive feedback-seeking behaviour when conditions are enabling, but shift to a more reserved stance when conditions become restrictive. These shifts in involvement provide empirical evidence of how students adapt their agency in response to changing structural conditions. Over time, patterns of alignment and misalignment within the graph reveal the interplay between structure and LFA. The systemic analysis provided by the Enacticipation Graph aligns with broader calls to examine agency through systemic frameworks (Giddens, 1984). Despite encountering misalignment with B in Turn 2, agent A persists, redirecting B’s attention to historical narratives until alignment is achieved in Turn 4. In Episode 1, for instance, A’s proactive feedback involvement is hindered by B and C’s limited participation, whereas in Episode 2, B’s reciprocation enabled A’s efforts at progressing the feedback process. By illuminating learners’ underlying deliberations, the analysis reveals how individual expressions of LFA are simultaneously conditioned by and constit utive of the collective structure of the group.

Cross-episode interplay analysis The cross-episode analysis phase examines trends in LFA over a longer temporal timeline to depict changes in feedback practice as shaped by agent’s involvement. By comparing across episodes, this phase uncovers whether learners’ LFA persists or shifts in response to its evolving structure. For instance, C’s persistent delegation to others across episodes may reflect the solidification of roles within the group, positioning C as a passive participant. This sustained non-action shapes group dynamics by reinforcing a feedback norm that accommodates C’s minimal involvement. Over time, this could limit C’s opportunities for deeper engagement with feedback, underscoring how such persistent behaviours can entrench structural patterns within the group. On the other hand, Agent A’s shift from inclusive to selective involvement demonstrates A’s adaptation, redirecting efforts to focus on a single member after the unsuccessful attempt of engaging all members in Episode 1. Similarly, B’s transition from non-participation to active contribution signals an emergent recognition of the need for shared responsibility in sustaining the feedback process.

942 At a systemic level, these cross-episode comparisons reveal changes in the feedback norm. While Episode 1 is characterised by A’s solitary feedback involvement, Episode 2 transitions into a more collaborative environment, with A and B jointly driving the process. These findings underscore the interplay between multi-agent and temporal dimensions of structure, as agents continuously shape and are reshaped by their interactions. Recognising these broader trends is crucial for understanding the long-term impact of feedback practices and designing interventions that foster productive, adaptive feedback cultures. The MAIA analysis offers an alternative approach to examining LFA that accounts for its inherent complexity. Through the illustrative example, MAIA revealed how analysis can be conducted to uncover how each agent exercised their agency in the feedback proc ess. The analysis also demonstrated its capacity to depict a range of agentic involvement from passive to selective and proactive. Beyond the individual level, MAIA also illuminates how feedback practices evolve over time through the joint involvement of m ultiple agents. MAIA analysis highlights how agents’ actions are mutually enabling or constraining, thereby portraying LFA as a collective phenomenon shaped by interdependent contributions. Collectively, these analytical affordances underscore MAIA’s potential to generate deeper theoretical insights into LFA. Two caveats are worth noting. First, the current analysis delimits the multi-agent dimension to immediate classroom structures, focusing on agents directly involved in feedback episodes. This is not meant to ignore broader social or cultural structures (e.g., institutional norms or national culture) but rather assumes that such influences are embedded in and enacted through the actions and deliberations of these agents. While this establishes pragmatic boundaries that serve to ensure its analytical feasibi lity, it also limits MAIA’s capacity to systematically account for broader structural influences. Second, MAIA synchronises enacted and anticipated actions, operating on the assumption that all actions or non-actions have their anticipated outcome, while recognising that not every action may be entirely deliberate. Looking ahead, MAIA presents several opportunities for further development. First, future research could extend the analysis to trace enduring patterns of agentic involvement across episodes. Second, system-level analyses could examine how collective patterns of agentic involvement shape, reinforce, or disrupt feedback processes, offering insights into group-level dynamics that underpin productive feedback practices. Third, exploring how such analyses can inform pedagogical design may further enhance the practical utility of MAIA in fostering agentic feedback cultures.

In conclusion, while this paper demonstrated the application of MAIA for analysing LFA, its potential extends well beyond this context and can be applied across educational and social domains. Future research may employ MAIA to investigate agency in other education contexts or community settings. Future studies are encouraged to extend and adapt MAIA, further advancing its development as a methodological approach for examining agency in complex, dynamic systems.
