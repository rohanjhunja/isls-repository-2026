# Critical Thinking Habits of Mind in Media Arts Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Media arts programs offer youth opportunities to develop critical thinking through creative inquiry, yet little is known about how young people practice critical thinking in these learning environments. This research aims to create a framework for understanding the critical thinking habits of mind practiced throughout media arts learning. Using observations, reflective interviews, and creative artifacts from an exploratory case study at a youth filmmaking lab, we examined the production process for six young filmmakers. We used process coding to analyze the filmmakers’ reflections and artistic decisions and identified habits such as perspective-taking, adapting, synthesizing, and assessing. From our iterative analyses of filmmakers' data, we developed a codebook for Critical Thinking Habits of Mind in Media Arts Learning. This codebook is the first step in creating a framework for studying critical thinking in media arts learning and can be used to study this phenomenon across diverse settings.

## Introduction

For several decades, researchers have tried to provide evidence of the benefits of arts education by suggesting the arts increase academic achievement in other subject  areas or correlate with higher scores on standardi zed tests. Yet causal links between arts learning and academic outcomes have been challenging to find (Hetland & Winner, 2004). Arts education scholars suggest that instead  of measuring the benefits of the arts in relation to student performance in other content areas, we should focus  instead on the inherent benefits of arts education  (Eisner, 1998; Hetland & Winner, 2004; Marshall, 2016; Winner et al., 2013). In recent years, arts education research has evolved to focus on social-emotional outcomes (Freedman et al., 2022; Lashley & Halverson, 2021), including identity formation (Eglinton et al., 2017; E. R. Halverson, 2010) and empathy development (Goldstein & Winner, 2012; Kou et al., 2020; Montero, 2023). However, in  the learning sciences, the arts have primarily bee n studied in service of learning in other disciplines. At the time of writing  this research article, there are only five article s in the history of the Journal of the Learning Sciences (JLS) with the word “art” in the title, two with “media,” one with “video” (e.g., youth using video as a tool), a nd none with “filmmaking” or “media art.” However, there are 64 articles with “science” and 51 with “mathematics” or “math” in the title. In a recent special issue of the JLS, Halverson & Sawyer (2022) argue that “the arts are central to our understanding of learning and knowing and therefore of crucial importance to the learning sciences, even though our field is primarily known for its studies of learning in STEM” (p. 1). Four articles in the 2022 JLS special issue examined artistic practices and made empirical argu ments for the importance of understanding learning in and through the arts. Solomon et al. (2022) studied young Black dancers collaborating in an embodied physics learning lab to understand how the dancers’ existing funds of knowledge  could improve physics teaching and learning. They found that an embodied exploration of science concepts shifted not only the dancers’ perceptions of science but also provided them with new forms of sense-making. By highlighting how youth learn through da nce, the researchers give value to artistic learning spaces as sites that can inform educational research more broadly. However, while this study calls attention to the in herent benefits of learning through dance, as an ed ucational community, we need to be careful not to limit the i mplications of these findings to improving STEM education alone.

In another article from the JLS special issue, Sheridan et al. (2022) examined how instructor language supported student autonomy and artistic agency. The ir study stems from the well-established Studio Thinking Project, which considers the “studio thinking habit s of mind” young people are taught through visual a rts education. However, because the analysis focuses on  the instructor’s language and actions, fewer detai ls are provided about how young artists practice autonomy or agency. In this paper, we seek t o learn more from the young artists, through their reflections, about how  they are developing specific habits of mind. We sh ift the analysis to the language that young artists use and  the artistic decisions they make so we can better understand their think ing as they engage in creative production. We used observations, creative artifacts, final films, and reflective interviews to explore six young filmmakers’ habits of mind in a summer media arts program, guided by the following research questions: RQ1.  How do young artists practice critical thinking throughout the film production process? RQ2.  What are the unique affordances of media arts learn ing environments, specifically filmmaking programs, to cultivate critical thinking habits of mind? Through our analysis, we developed the beginnings of a framework to identify the critical thinking habits of mind that young artists cultivate through media arts production. We contribute this framework and discuss how it can inform learning design more broadly to suppo rt the development of critical thinking within and beyond media arts learning.

## Theoretical background

Critical thinking habits of mind Critical thinking can be defined as “reflective and reasonable thinking that is focused on deciding what to believe or do” (Ennis, 1996, p. 166). Critical thinking has been linked to reductions in cognitive bias (West et al., 2008), increased reflective judgment (Dwyer et al., 2015),  decision-making ability (Butler et al., 2017), and academic performance beyond general cogni tive ability (Ren et al., 2020). Lai’s (2011) liter ature review notes that across philosophy, psychology, and education, there is som e consensus that critical thinking involves specific abilities, including analysis, inferential reasoning, and eval uation. While essential for critical thinking, thes e skills alone do not necessarily predict that a learner will think critically when it is necessary. Research has shown that critical thinking dispositions, such as open-mindedness and flexibility, influence how we think and act—whether during an assessment or everyday situations (Ku, 2009; Perkins & Ritchhart, 2004; Sosu, 2013). A habit of mind is “a composite of many skills, attitudes, cues, past experiences and proclivities” (Costa & Kallick, 2008, p. 17). The term is rooted in a di spositional view of thinking that considers the pra ctical, intellectual, social, and emotional capacities of learners. While a definitive list of “critical thinking habits of mind” does not exist, several taxonomies of critical thin king dispositions have been generated, which includ e, among others, openness to multiple perspectives, being metacognitive or self-reflective, and being inquisitive (Alhamlan et al., 2018; Rubenfeld & Scheffer, 2014; Sosu, 2013).

The term “habits of mind” has also been used in arts education to describe the “studio thinking habits of mind” teachers impart through visual arts instructi on. The Studio Thinking Framework (STF) (Sheridan, Veenema, et al., 2022) has helped educators and researchers look beyond technical skills developmen t to understand how artistic thinking  is taught in studio art classrooms. The STF suggest s that through visual arts instruction, teachers encourage young people to (1) develop craft, (2) engage & persist, (3) envision, (4) express,

(5) observe, (6) reflect, (7) stretch & explore, and (8) understand art worlds. The creation of the ST F involved

rigorous documentation and coding of art teachers’ pedagogical strategies. However, research related to the STF is primarily focused on visual arts and teaching practices rather than how young artists demonstrate their thinking. By integrating critical thinking, habits of mind, and arts learning frameworks, we seek to understand the thinking processes that young artists engage in that might transcend artistic practice and help young people thrive beyond the proverbial “schoolhouse doors.” Media arts learning Often overlooked in critical thinking research, fil m and media arts programs offer young people opport unities to develop critical thinking through creative inquiry (Costantino, 2017). Filmmaking programs ask young people to explore concepts and solve problems by creating mul timodal work while engaging in analysis and reflect ion. However, more research is needed on the unique affordances of filmmaking programs to nurture critical thinking. Previous studies of media arts learning have examin ed the literacies young people gain through their interaction with new media (Jenkins, 2009) and how digital artmaking relates to visual art practices (Peppler, 2010; Sheridan, 2011). When researching creative pr ogramming environments, Kafai and Peppler (2011) identified critical and creative practices such as observing, deconstructing, evaluating, making artis tic choices, and connecting multimodal sign systems. Many of the practices they identified are inherent in the film production process, and their work has served as a foundation for this study on media arts learning. Halverson’s (2010, 2013) research on youth media ar ts organizations discusses how youth represent identity through multimodal composition and the “me ta-representational competence” (MRC) that filmmakers develop through the production process. Typically s tudied in scientific settings where students analyze representations such as graphs, Halverson (2013) us ed MRC to explain links between the media productio n process and higher-order thinking. By closely examining the young arti sts’ process, Halverson capture d how filmmakers engage in a “representational trajectory,” ultimately producing representations of themselv es that reflect a deep understanding of the relationship between a story and the tools of the medium. Keeping Halverson’s research in mind while observin g a summer media arts program, we considered how filmmakers produce something beyond representational. Filmmakers not only represent thoughts and feelings but also facilitate experiences for others. When a filmmaker combines i mages with music or manipulates time and tone, they evoke an emotional response. For ins tance, a “jump cut” can make the audience jump out of their seats, and a slow-motion sequence set to a particular so ng can make the audience cry. To achieve an emotion al reaction, the filmmaker needs to consider alternati ve points of view, self-reflect, assess their artistic decisions, and synthesize multiple modes of communication to c reate meaning. These critical thinking practices ar e embedded in the film production process. To better understand the young artist’s critical thinking pro cess, we engaged in a study with a teen filmmaking program to capture the filmmakers’ thinking in situ.

## Methods

Using an exploratory case study methodology (Merria m & Tisdell, 2015), we observed an urban summer filmmaking lab in which youth learn the basics of f ilmmaking and produce their own short experimental films. Participants were rising 9th and 10th graders, ages 13-15, identifying as male, female, Asian, Black, Latino/a, and White (self-reported data on application forms). Of the origina l 15 participants in the lab, we chose six as case studies based on their willingness to share their reflections (e.g., intentions, challenges, learning experiences) with the researcher. The program ran for four weeks, and  we recorded approximately 40 hours of field notes,  artifacts of youth-produced work (i.e., screenshots of editing timelin es), and audio recordings of informal  and semi-structured interviews.

## Data collection

The first author observed the program, gaining guar dian permission and participant consent at the onse t. She participated in discussions, activities, and field trips throughout the four weeks of the program to build trust with the participants. To understand the young artists’ thinking process in action, she used observations and field notes with thick descriptions (Tracy, 2010). We kept in mind Ennis’ (1996, p. 166) definition of critical thinking as “reflective and reasonable thinking that is focused on deciding what to believe or do” and focused on actions and decisions young artists made as they were confronted with challenges, presented with new tools, or engaged with peers and mentors. For instance, if a young filmmak er changed the soundtrack in their film, the researcher noted how and why the decision was made and who was invol ved. Additionally, she reflected on her “noticings”  daily by generating analytic memos (Miles et al., 2020) through audio recordings, which were then included in the field notes. In addition to the observations, artifacts such as final films, screenshots of video editing timelines, sketches, and peer feedback notes were collected.

The first author used reflective interviewing (Nardon et al., 2021; Roulston, 2010; Vossoughi & Zavala,

2020) practices during on-site conversations throughout the production proces s and in the semi-structured

interviews. This dialogic approach asks researchers to engage with participants as people with their own ways of knowing and understanding the world. The practice included giving time for the participants to consider their role in the research, asking participants to reflect on their process, using probing questions (e.g., “What makes you say that?”), and ensuring the researcher also reflected  on the meaning being built through the conversatio ns (Nardon et al., 2021). By reflecting on their artistic proc ess, the participants examined thoughts and feeling s, shared their intentions and challenges, and reasoned about their artistic decisions.

## Data analysis

A team of researchers engaged in qualitative data analysis of the informal and semi-structured interviews. In phase one, we openly coded using process coding (Miles et al., 2020) to identify the kinds of “thin king practices” the young filmmakers demonstrated throughout the produc tion process. Process coding uses gerunds (words en ding in “-ing”) to note what participants do or say they did when reflecting on th eir experiences. For instance, one researcher noted that a filmmaker practiced “perspe ctive-taking” when inferring how others would feel when seeing his work. After independently coding several  interviews, we analyzed recurring themes across th e cases, discussed discrepancies, and built consensus around  what qualified as a “critical thinking habit of mi nd.” Phase two involved iterating on and refining the agreed-upon codes to develop a codebook. After a 6-month iterative process looking across the six cases, we identified  12 Critical Thinking Habits of Mind in Media Arts Lear ning. In the third analysis phase, two researchers independently coded transcript excerpts from across the six cases, and the agreement rate was 98.23%. Researchers also ind ependently analyzed student films and field notes t o better understand the day-to-day activities, as well as each participant’s artis tic decisions, and data was triangulated by comparing these analyses.

Synthesizing When editing their films, we saw the filmmakers man aging tracks (e.g., video, audio, effects, text) and synthesizing modes (e.g., visual, aural, gestural) to create meaning. Combining these modes and making  artistic decisions about pacing, rhythm, synchronization, la yering, and juxtaposition is an advanced form of mu ltimodal composition. In the excerpt above, Daniel discusses  how he used “blank spaces,” or what appear as abru pt jump cuts to black, throughout his video timeline to cre ate a “feeling of suspen se.” Burn & Parker (2003) refer to this manipulation of time in cinema as “pause duration,” wherein discourse time continues, but story time stops. This artistic choice evokes a feeling of suspense throug h uncertainty. One researcher noted that these mome nts in the film evoke a feeling similar t o “the lights suddenly going out.” Removing images from the timeline is a visual choice; however, because the sequence was progressi ng, suddenly stopped, and then resumed, Daniel achi eved an emotional effect by also manipulating time (i.e., the temporal mode). Daniel also uses music to induce anxiety. He explains that “it starts low at the beginning and then it gets very, very intense…to keep the viewer at the edge o f their seat.” One researcher noted, “The rain, emp ty street, and grayscale image, combined with intense music, gives me a feeling of ‘something is about to happen.’” Daniel combined visual, aural, and temporal modes to creat e meaning and the experience of anxiety. Synthesizing multiple modes in editing is a form of multimodal literacy that requires the artist to think critically, notice nuance in variation, and understand how the combination of  modes creates meaning and an emotional impact for the audience. Through editing, filmmakers not only comb ine visual, aural, gestural, linguistic, and spatial modes but also use temporality to communicate concepts and evoke feelings. Manipul ating time in this way is unique to time-based art such as film and differs from tempo in mu sic and dance or how time is represented in theater  and literature. More research is needed to understand how th is process of synthesis that occurs through video editing simultaneously supports and demonstrates critical thinking. When asked how she defined a successful project, Kayla answered, “A successful project means, you feel confident about you, like you want to show it to people, or you just feel good ab out it while watching.” Kayla assessed her work bas ed on its cohesion and how well it evoked an emotional response: Kayla: It all works together, by having clips and the way that you transition over to another one. For instance, it is noted in the field notes that she c hose slower crossfades instead of abrupt jump cuts. Using the language “I was trying to go for a film with music” indicates some intentionality. However, it is unclear why Charlie feels that a “film with music” is bette r than a “music video with people talking,” and fur ther elaboration might help Charlie articulate his artistic reasoning. Charlie also shares in his interview that he considered the perspectives of his peers. Other filmmakers in the program gave him notes such as “add music,” “add more audio variations,” and “take out background audio.” After peer and self-assessment, Charlie combined footage of the city with an elaborate soun dscape of natural sounds and music. Evaluation is often cited as a critical thinking skill, and in this learning environment, evaluating work is an emergent, self-reflective, iterative, and often dialogic  process intended to improve the work; because of this, we have chosen the term “assessing” instea d of “evaluating.” In the example above, Charlie noticed that something did not “feel right,” made adjustments, r eceived feedback, considered other points of view, and produced a final product. Here, assessing is related to noticing, self-reflecting, and perspective-taking.

## Discussion

Assessments of critical thinking have taken many fo rms, including multiple-choice, constructed response, and performance tasks that use rubrics. Many of these m easures have been critiqued for failing to account for social desirability bias, measuring skill s but not dispositions, requiring learners to posse ss prior knowledge of specific content, or requiring the learner to simply recognize a correct answer rather than think critically (Ku, 2009; Sosu, 2013). These assessments also remove the learner fr om the  learning environment where unprompted critical thinking is necessary. Decades ago, Brookfield (199 7) suggested that to assess critical thinking, we s hould study specific contexts to observe the learning process, considering actions in addition to discourse, allow ing learners to document, demo nstrate, and justify their critical thinking becaus e when “viewing learning from the outside, instructors may miss entirely the critical dimensions of students’ thought and practice” (p. 20). This study examines “activities” and “externalizati ons” of critical thinking in the context of media a rts learning. We used an activity-oriented approach and analyzed the young artists’ p rocesses, decisions, language, and creative artifacts. The films are “objects-to-think-with” (Papert, 2020), and by engaging the artists in reflective dialogue throughout the production process, we soug ht to illuminate their critical thinking. Because o f these methods, we identified many critical thinking habits of mind  that might not be evident outside these learning spaces or in the final projects alone. We also shif ted away from language typically used in literature  on critical thinking (e.g., skills and dispositions) to “habits  of mind,” intending to encompass the practi cal, intellectual, social, and emotional facets of critical thinking.

This study differs from previous research into arti stic thinking in that we studied an art form that i s inherently multimodal and requires synthesis to create meaning. By examining how young filmmakers synthesize modes, we began to understand how they produce work  beyond representation. For instance, Daniel did not just show the feeling of “anxiety,” he created it. This multimodal literacy is valuable when producing media and when young people need to understand media influence (e. g., awareness that a polit ical ad makes them feel anxious). Through our analysis, we observed young people shifting perspectives and producing work intended to facilitate experiences for others. Media arts production is fi lled with opportunities to imagine other points of view (e.g., characters, audience, peers). This is an area that needs more research, focusing on how the consideration of other perspectives throughout the filmmaking process migh t cultivate a habit of “openness to multiple perspe ctives.” Young people in this lab also adapted and demonstra ted flexibility while problem-solving around naturally occurring constraints. This is valuable to learning  designers because media arts production affords yo ung people the ability to adapt and problem-solve throughout the learning process. The learners in this study also engaged in an emerg ent and iterative assessment process, critically reflecting on the success or effectiveness of their  work. This is valuable because the arts are a fiel d without definitive answers, and learners need to navigate a mbiguity, often constructing and defending their as sessments. By listening to the young artists’ reflections, we began to hear them form criteria for assessment, mu ch of which is based on feeling. This should not be discounted as too subjective b ut rather seen as an area worthy of further study. Future studies might investigate how the med ia arts production process cultivates “critical fee ling” or attunement to feelings and how we can better support young people in elaborating on their feelings through artistic reasoning and reflection.

The codebook we developed allows us to continue the study of critical thinking across media arts settings and to learn how these habits are practiced in different environments. Using this codebook across settings, we candevelop a framework for understanding critical thin king in artistic learning environments more broadly, potentially shifting our understanding of what is learned through the artistic process and informing learning design for critical thinking. Furthermore, in contrast to studies that use more traditional assessments of critical thinking, the methods used in this study center the young art ists’ language, reflections, and artistic products as externalizations of critical thinking. By embracing  the dialogic nature of the interview, this study a lso seeks to promote reflective interviewing as a means of asses sing critical thinking in naturalistic settings. Fu ture projects might integrate professional development for arts e ducators around using reflective interviewing to cr eate more space for young artists to practice critical thinking.
