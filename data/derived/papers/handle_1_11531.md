# Translanguaging Spaces: Exploring Scientific Meaning-Making Through Conceptual Metaphors

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Translanguaging beyond language allows researchers to examine how learners mobilize their full communicative repertoire, crossing languages, modes, and sensory inputs, to engage with and understand complex everyday phenomena. Using Conceptual Metaphor Theory and distributed social semiotics, this study explores how verbal, gestural, and material interactions in translanguaging spaces facilitate learners’ meaning making of everyday phenomena through conceptual metaphors. Findings reveal that, students’ use of Hindi and English, interwoven with gestures, their conceptual repertoire and physical engagement with materials in the translanguaging space triggers specific metaphors, anchoring scientific reasoning in sensory experiences. Collaborative interactions, mediated by objects like cups and tables, highlight how temperature differences and embodied actions support negotiating abstract concepts. The study restates the importance of multimodal interactions in connecting concrete sensory experiences with abstract scientific meaning making and calls for inclusive, multimodal approaches to scientific discourse.

## Introduction

Language is no longer seen as a fixed, isolated sys tem but as a dynamic interconnected structure of multilingual, multimodal, and multi-semiotic resources actively orchestrated in the meaning-making process (Hua et al., 2017). This perspective challenges traditional notions of language, which have often privileged verbal modes,  by positioning all communicative modes such as, linguistic, gestural, material, and semiotic as equ ally valuable in sense-making (Otheguy et al., 2015). Translanguaging, conceptualized by García and Li Wei (2017), offers a lens to explore how learners draw on these resources hol istically, treating them as a unified and fluid rep ertoire that adapts to the demands of context. Foundational work by Kress and van Leeuwen (2001) conceptualizes language as a material and spatial entity, while Lemke’s (2002) theory of distributed language situates meaning as emerging from networks of interactions among people, artifacts, and environments. Translanguaging builds on these ideas, reframing language as a distributed, situated, and embodied process that emerges through such interaction (Amin, 2015). This framework is particularly relevant for analyzing how students integrate verbal, gestural, and material resources to construct knowledge, going beyond mult imodal theory by treating resources not as discrete  modes but as part of a fluid, unified system. It foregrou nds the embodied, distributed, and extended nature of meaningmaking, shifting focus from individual cognition to  the broader ecology of interactions that include learners, spaces, and artifacts (Steffensen, 2011). While multimodal translanguaging has gained attention, a significant gap remains in understanding how verbal, gestural, and material interactions converge in the meaning-making process (Cheng, et al.,2020). This study seeks to address this gap by situating m eaning-making, at the intersection s of translanguaging practices and collaborative engagements with the material world, offering insights into how students’ repertoires evolve and adapt as they enga ge with complex, everyday phenomena. By doing so, it aims to extend the conceptual reach of translanguaging, highlighting its transformative potential to explor e the rich multimodal and distributed nature of learning.

A central aspect of meaning-making in translanguaging is learners’ intuitive kn owledge, which bridges everyday experiences and formal scientific understa nding. Students adapt language creatively to make s ense of scientific phenomena  and to connect their intuitive understanding. For instance, everyday terms like “hot” and “warm” can be transformed into abstract expressions  such as ‘the warmness’ (Rosebery et al., 2010). Second-language learners often contextualize abstract concepts in their first language while using scientific terms in their second, which expands linguistic flexibility and su pports conceptual understanding (Karlsson et al., 2 019). The interplay of disciplinary language, gestures, and material interactions bridges everyday experiences with abstract ideas, fostering motivation and engagement  (Jaipal,2010). The human conceptual system, shaped by embodied metaphors, organizes abstract phenomena through fam iliar, physical interactions (Lakoff & Johnson, 198 0). Building on Lakoff and Johnson’s cognitive turn in science education, research in Conceptual Metaphor Theory (CMT) highlights how abstract concepts are understo od through concrete, sensorimotor experiences (Broo kes & Etkina, 2015; Amin et., al,2015; Johnson, 1987). The theory of experientialism and embodied cognition supports this idea by  emphasizing that cognitive processes are rooted in bodily interactions with the environment, often through mental simulations and metaphorical express ions like falling or moving up /down (Niebert, 2012). However, the multimodal nature of these metaphorica l expressions, emerging from interactions with both language and the material world is explored to a lesser degree as opposed to individual modes, warranting further investigation.

The artefactual dimensions such as touch, sights, s mells, and material ity has shifted perspectives, emphasizing bodily engagements in meaning-making (Finnegan, 2015). Pierson (2021) highlights syncretic practices like modeling and translating, where learners use visual, verbal, and material modes to express scientific concepts (Priyadarshini et al., 202 3). Each mode offers different affordances, with verbal  language enabling detailed explanations and visual modes emphasizing key ideas (Gibson, 1979). Physical objects can trigger embodied intuitions in scientific reasoning, with gestures and tactile modes facilitating the discourse by supporting or demonstrating concepts (William, 202 2). Gestures, when connected with objects or events, re inforce metaphorical thinking and can be interpreted as non-verbal metaphors, impacting cognitive processes even in the absence of verbal expressions (Dreyfus et al, 2015; Cienki & Müller, 2008). Integrating gestures with m aterials offers a new dimension to understanding conceptual metaphors in scientific discourse involving spatial abstractions (Zdrazilova, 2018). While material practices are traditionally studied through laboratory work or hands-on objec ts, a more fundamental question lies in how materia l objects interact with other resources like gestures and language in social-material meaning-making, integrating 'hands-on' and 'minds-on' practices, pushing material into the conceptual space (Tang, 2024).

Research on multimodality, translanguaging, and CMT highlights the role of multiple modes in meaningmaking, yet little is known about how embodied inte ractions with everyday objects enable metaphorical understandings that connect abstract scientific concepts with intuitive experiences.  While multimodality in disciplinary concepts has been studied, its role in specific content areas requires a thorough investigation (Pierson, 2021). By thinking along the lines of these gaps, this study investigates the following  research question: How does translanguaging through verbal, non-verbal, and material interactions facilitate learners' meaning making of everyday phenomena through the use of conceptual metaphors? Theoretical lens The theoretical framework  for this study  combines multimodality, translanguaging, and C MT to explore how students make meaning of abstract scientific concep ts through embodied, semiotic practices, and materi al interactions. Translanguaging, as described by García and Li (2017), is a fluid practice where individuals use their entire linguistic repertoire, seamlessly blending l anguages rather than sticking to separate language systems. Although original ly used in linguistic contexts, Pennycook (2017)  and others have extended the idea of translanguaging by including shifts across social semiotic modes, highlighting how learners integrate  gestural, material, cultural, and spatial elements into their  meaning-making (Kress, 2011). Norris (2004) emphasizes the importance of context and situated interactions in multimodal analysis, highlighting that the affordan ces of different modes (e.g., gesture, language, material objects) play a crucial role in constructing meanin g. These different modes are not just separately understood but interact with each other in a way that influences the students’ cognitive processes and knowledge construction.

Lakoff and Johnson’s (1980)  CMT supports this understanding, explaining how abstract concept s are understood through metaphorical mappings based on c oncrete, everyday experiences. Image schemas, forme d through repeated sensory and bodily interactions wi th the environment, serve as the foundation for the se conceptual metaphors (Johnson, 1987). For instance, in understanding heat transfer, students may conceptualize heat as a ‘substance’ with heat seen as an entity moving from one object to another. In the co gnitive linguistic view, the choice of words for the predicates reflec ts different characteristics, interactions, and pro cesses of the phenomenon. The source of conceptual metaphors is r eflected in the display of these linguistic element s. For example, the containment schema is called upon by students to describe heat as being ‘held’ within an object or ‘contained’ by its material. Material interactions with objects thus contribute to the conceptualization of scientific concepts, linking students' concrete, embodied expe riences to abstract phenomena.  Kress notes, “Until ‘knowledge’ is ‘made material’ in a specific mode, it has no ‘shape’: we cannot ‘get at it’” (Kress, 2 011, p. 44). This aligns with the idea that metaphors and image schemas help materialize abstract concepts, turning them into tangible representations that can be ‘got at ’ by learners. Without material  and bodily  interactions, these representations would lack the necessary grounding to fully make sense of the phenomena.  CMT's application within a translanguaging framework provides a bette r understanding  of how metaphors influence students' cognitive models of scientific phenomena.  The theory of translanguaging and CMT suggest that as students engage physically with objects whether by touching a heated cup or manipulations, metaphors are reinforced and refined. These material experiences are integral in activa ting and shaping the conceptual metaphors that unde rlie their reasoning. The multimodal approach, then, allows us to see how  physical, verbal, and gestural expressions work together to create a full picture of how students make meaning. While translanguaging captures the fluidity and creativity of students’ expressions, CMT reveal s the cognitive structures that underlie these expr essions, demonstrating how metaphorical language and materia l interactions shape students’ understanding of abs tract scientific phenomena.

With the conceptualization that translanguaging inv ites a holistic view, integrating verbal, non-verbal, and material resources to construct meaning (Pennycook, 2017), the theoretical approach looks to expand the unit of analysis beyond the individual or the group of s tudents to that of the material environments  by following Hutchin’s (2000) standpoint that humans perform tasks not in isolation but rather in relation to other people  and in connection with the material world. Such an approach reifies the notion that cognitive tasks are deeply situated and distributed  among individuals, between the elements of the mate rial environment, and over time. This approach adds a further layer in the theoretical lens in und erstanding how individuals and groups utilize the materials elements of the environment along with and in relation to languages, gestures and other multimodalities in meaning making practices (Tang, 2024).

## Methods and analysis

Methods. The participants of the study were grade 11 students enrolled in schools in the suburban cities of Western India, who completed their formal education on the thermal properties of matter. This study, conducted outside of regular class hours, aimed to analyze students’  ideas in scientific meaning-making. Students were asked to build an explanatory model on “How does heat transf er occur in a coffee cup”. Heat transfer was chosen as the topic of choice for its phenomenological connection with students’ everyday life. The activities were about 3.5-4 hours and divided into different sessions (pertaining to macroscopic level, microscopic level and the  symbolic level), moderated by one researcher (first author) while three other researchers were present for assistance. During the sessions, each group (consisting of 3 students) discussed and developed an explanatory model of heat transfer in a coffee cup, using hot coffee poured in front o f the class as a stimulus.  Subsequently, they interacted with various cups to predict which would cool the fastest, updating their models accordingly. The groups then discussed and drew the molecular view of their chosen cup. Students were encouraged to use any language or semio tic resources, such as gestures and drawings, facilitating a translanguaging practice (Wei, 2011). Analysis. A multi-step approach to coding and analysis was undertaken  through different phases. Phase 1 involved segmenting and content logging the videos, to identify meaningfully rich episodes to guide our analysis of the metaphorical interactions. Anytime the students discussed any idea about hea t transfer or engaged in a semiotic action with each other and the materials a round them, these episodes were marked. Content log s were utilized for the multimodal interaction analysis (N orris, 2004) focusing on metaphorical instances with the talk, gestures and material interactions during their mea ning making episodes. Whenever there was a shift in  their interaction approach, topic or idea, we marked that  as the end of an episode. In phase 2, meaningful e pisodes containing multimodal data were identified and tran scribed, including data of verbal utterances, gestu res, and manipulation of the materials. Post-transcription, these meaningful episodes were looke d for metaphorical reasoning, using the Conceptual metaphor theory as the guiding analytical lens. The segmented episodes were read and watched repeatedly, and verbal utterances that had metaphorical units were first intuitively separ ated, followed by the process following the Metaphor Identification Procedure (MIP) by the Pragglejaz group (P group, 2007).

Since there is currently no standardized way to cha racterize non-linguistic modes for conceptual metaphors, we draw the analytical frameworks from m aterialism studies and gesture metaphor studies to guide our analysis (Tang, 2024; Cienki, 2008). There were three ways in which the role of conceptu al metaphors was analyzed for non-linguistic modes. One was by looking at how student’s interactions and gestural actions wit h materials represent an underlying reference to a conceptual metaphor, the other is how an interaction can influence the use or evocation of a conceptual metaphor and how this may in turn affect the material and gestural interaction. For instance, in one excerpt (see E pisode 1, Figure 1), the speaker states, "this no  (sic), the room temperature’s molecules will attack like this," while moving her hands in a wavy manner toward a cup in front of her. The use of the predicate "will attack" suggests a metaphor of HEAT TRANSFER AS AN HOSTILE PROCESS, with molecules depicted as acting violently, further reinforced by her bombarding ges tures toward the cup. After identifying these conceptual metaphors, their role in the meaning-making processes was analyzed, with non-propositional knowledge structures like image schem as (CONTAINER schema) being assigned to the metaphorical expressions. We specify these metaphors and image schemas using capitalized text. As previously mentioned in the theoretical approach, we looked to  understand how individuals and groups utilize the materials in the environment along with and in relation to languages, gestures and other multimodalities in meaning making practices. For this, we looked at  moments of explicit material considerations as stud ents engaged in meaning making. In the data logs, we considered moments where stude nts engaged in observable deliberations using materials, conversing with and through materials  (Gravel, & Svihla, 2021) as students physically interacted with these materials, for example tapping, holding etc.; gestures to materials, for example pointing; and gesturing with materials, for example, waving motions by holding aluminum foil etc. These moments were then connected back to the events where conceptual metaphors emerged in students’ meaning making practices and reanalyzed to come up with a coherent interpretation, individually and collaboratively with fellow researchers.

By imagining “cold air” as something palpable, this gesture also reinforced a MATERIAL metaphor, where air temperature felt like a tangible quality that surrounds objects. The phrase “leaving cold air surrounding it”, helped the students to imagine the cooling effect as something tangible similar to expressions like a trail of coldness. The statement “cold air takes energy”, followed by a pulling action (see Figure 2) is an idea that Roy works with by considering ENERGY AS A TRANSFERABLE OBJECT moving between heat and cold, giving aspects of agency, location and a physical  attribute. Each of the students’ phrase, gestures and interactions with the materials play with the metaphors of substance, agency, and movement, giving students cognitive tools in the semiotic space to frame invisible phenomena like heat transfer in terms that feel familiar yet scientifically grounded.

In this episode, the group's collaborative meaning-making is augmented by conceptual metaphors and image schemas, with  the languages  Hindi and English interwoven with gestures. The students used different modifications of HEAT AS A SUBSTANCE conceptual metaphor, where "hot ness" and "vapor" were treated as tangible entities that can ‘rise,’ ‘displace,’ or ‘move outward.’ For example, Rinu’s expanding hand gesture, like a blooming flower, and Tina’s pulling motion embodied HEAT AS SOMETHING THAT CAN BE MOVED OR EXTRACTED. Tina’s Hindi phrase, "hotness hi niklega, na (tr: hotness will only come out, right?)”, paired with her gesture moving away from the cup, reinforce d the idea of  HEAT AS A SEPARABLE SUBSTANCE. The group drew on CONTAINMENT and SURFACE boundary schemas, visualizing the cup as a bounde d container of ‘hotness’ and ‘coldness.’ Gestures, such as Rinu’s circular motions and Tina’ s container-like hand shapes, represented the cup as a space where ‘temperature entities’ were regulated and interacted, while Roy’s cupping motion mimicked the boundary of the cup's surface, helping them con ceptualize the cup as a container with controllable temperature borders.  The use of HEAT TRANSFER IS REMOVAL AND DISPLACEMENT conceptual metaphor  further shape d their explanations  by providing a framework through which they conceptualized and explained the movement of heat in terms of more familiar, tangible experiences. For example, when Rinu said "displacing the coldness" and gesture d by moving her hands, she use d displacement to describe how heat moves away from the cup. This metaphorical  framing helped her understand and explain how the heat is transferred from one area to another, as if the coldness is being pushed aside. Similarly, Tina’s s tatement "Hotness will come out" is a use of removal, where she imagined the heat escaping the cup and moving outward. Her question in Hindi, "kaise jayega jab tak hot air bahr nhi ayega (tr: how cold air will  go below until hot air doesn’t come out) ", conveys a sequence where  cold air only occupies space once hot air exits. Th is concept is visualized through Tina’s flowing gestures, represe nting air layers as moving in a defined order. Her  pulling gesture signify the DISPLACEMENT metaphor, where on e temperature layer replaces another. In this case,  the students’ ideas about heat ‘displacing’ or ‘making space’ for cold air are rooted in how they naturally interact with physical objects that can be moved or replaced by other objects. In the HEAT TRANSFER IS REMOVAL AND DISPLACEMENT metaphor, they think of heat as a phys ical ‘substance’ that can occupy space and must ‘move out’ for cooling to occur. This aligns with image schema s formed from their everyday embodied experiences of pushing and pulling  that help them organize complex ideas about energy in a way that’s intuitive  to them. Additionally, using both Hindi and English enabled the students to tap into the subtle conceptual diff erences of metaphorical expressions. For example, in Hindi, the fluidity of heat as some thing that ‘comes out’ or ‘rises’ is emphasized, while in English, the emphasis shifts t o spatial movement and energy transfer, such as "di splacing the hotness." This languaging between the two langu ages, each framing the abstract concept slightly differently provides a space for students to develop complement ary perspectives of the phenomenon.  This metaphorical framework simplifie d a partially observable  scientific process (heat transfer and cooling) by m apping it onto familiar, everyday actions of moving and replacing objects in physical space, thus making the concept of thermal exchange more comprehendible. It should be also noted that the variations and dis agreements in the conceptualization of heat transfer in these scenar ios were not  only resolved by the verbal and nonverbal externalization of the student ideas but also augmented by the materials present in the translanguaging space. The students’ verbal and nonverbal acts in explaining the phenomenon were in reference to the cup containing coffee and the table on which it was placed. The repeated interactions with the cup of coffee and table helped in resolving the conflicts, along with the other modalities. Argumentations and negotiations were also supported with the sensory inputs from the cup of coffee, table and th e rising vapors  as the students used the wordings “rise”, “displace”, “come out”, etc. along with the corresponding gestures.

## Discussion and conclusion

The analysis emphasizes how translanguaging through  multimodal interactions facilitates learners' meaning making of everyday phenomena, particularly through conceptual metaphors. The three episodes presented in this study highlight the complex nature of meaning-making in the context of heat transfer, where conceptual metaphors, gestures, and material interactions converge within  a translanguaging space. In Episode 1, the student 's engagement with the green ceramic cup reveals how  interactions with materials can evoke specific conceptual metaphors. Aaradhya’s gesture of leaning toward the  cup and tapping its surface prompted the metaphor of an “attack” by molecules, shaped by the SOURCE-GOAL-PATH schema. Such embodied interactions transformed the abstract phenomenon of heat transfer into a concrete process involving movement and collision. The embodied nature of the interaction enabled the student to an chor scientific reasoning in sensory experiences, a ligning with literature emphasizing how material engagement trig gers embodied metaphors that make abstract scientif ic phenomena tang ible (Brookes & Etkina, 2015). Episode 2 demonstrates the dual role of the term “absorb,” representing both a literal process and a metaphori cal image of the table as a sponge-like object. This anthropomorphizing metaphor facilitated intuitive u nderstanding of conduction and thermal absorption. The students’ gestures  wiping the table and touching its surface  reinforced the metaphors of SUBSTANCE and CONTAINER. The collaborative dialogue between students, mediated b y materials such as the table and cup, exemplifies how sensory experiences and embodied ac tions provide a shared space for negotiating and re fining abstract scientific concepts (Finnegan, 2015). These interactions highlight how the combinations of linguistic and gestural modes with the material usage enable students to expand their understanding of complex scientific ideas (Tang, 2024; Pierson, 2021). In Episode 3, the students’ integration of Hindi and Eng lish, along with gestures, reflect how translanguaging across languages enhanc es the understanding of scientific concepts. The me taphors in Hindi emphasized heat as something that "rises,"  while in English, the focus shifted to spatial mov ement and energy transfer, as seen in the phrase “displacing the hotness.” This shift in metaphorical reasoning demonstrates how multilingual translanguaging fosters complement ary perspectives on scientific phenomena, reinforci ng the cognitive flexibility outlined previously (Priyadarshini et al., 2025; Karlsson et al., 2019). The CONTAINMENT schema represented by gestures around the cup helpe d conceptualize heat as a substance contained withi n boundaries. This episode further demonstrates how  multilingual translanguaging coupled  with material engagement resolves conceptual disagreements and deepens scientific understanding (García & Li Wei, 2017). The episodes together demonstrate the fluid, multimodal nature of meaning-making, where gestures, material engagement, and verbal expressions come to gether to facilitate the construction of abstract s cientific ideas. Students' metaphorical mappings, embodied through g estures and material interactions, not only help students bridge everyday sensory experiences with a cademic scientific concepts but also enhance their ability to anchor these abstract ideas in lived experiences (C ranny-Francis, 2011). Prior studies affirm that students often rely on their first language to contextualize abstr act terms, whic h is evident in how students conceptualized thermal phenomena and processes such as chlorophyll absorption (Karlsson, 2019). Moreover, the importance of integrating material and multimodal interactions into formal scientific communication highlights the significance of sensory limitations when expressing abstract ide as. This study contributes to the learning sciences  by demonstrating how translanguaging, gestures, and material interactions shape students’ conceptual understanding of heat transfer through conceptual metaphors. It h ighlights the multimodal nature of meaning-making, showing how embodied experiences anchor abstract scientific  concepts in everyday sensory interactions. Moreove r, the findings extend research on embodied cognition by illustrating how multilingual translanguaging fosters cognitive flexibility in scientific reasoning, offering insights into the role of language and gesture in negotiating and refining abstract scientific concepts. However, instances where metaphorical expressions w ere not well received by students raise important questions about their appropriateness in formal scientific contexts. This suggests the need for further exploration, particularly regarding stu dents’ epistemological stance on using different mo des in scientific discourse.
