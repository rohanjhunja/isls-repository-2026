# Opportunities for Guiding Development: Insights from First-Year Life Science Majors’ Post-Pandemic Use of Metacognition

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Students with strong metacognitive skills are positioned to learn more than peers who are still developing metacognition. Yet many come to college without well-developed metacognition. Through interviews with 52 undergraduates at three institutions we identified when, why, and how first-year life science students use metacognitive skills. We found that current first-year students vary in their use of metacognition compared to first-year students our lab studied before 2020. We provide implications for instructors for supporting metacognition.

Metacognition in undergraduate life science students Metacognition is a powerful yet underutilized mecha nism for helping undergraduates succeed in life sci ence courses. Metacognition is defined as awareness and control of thinking (Cross & Paris, 1988). Three key processes comprise metacognitive regulation skills: planning,  monitoring, and evaluating (Schraw & Moshman, 1995). Planning involves deciding what strategies to use for a futu re learning task and when you will use them. Monitoring involves assessing your understanding of concepts a nd the effectiveness of your strategies while learning. Evaluating involves appraising your prior plan after a learnin g task and adjusting it for the future. Metacognition is known to improve learning outcomes, achievement, and problem-solving for college students. Metacognitive skills are critical for undergraduate learning. While many senior-level life science students use metacognition when prompted to evaluate their l earning (Dye & Stanton, 2017; Stanton et al., 2019), introductory life science students often fail to us e metacognitive skills when prom pted (Stanton et al., 2015; Stanton et al., 2019). We know many students did not need metacognition to be successful in high school and may need help using metacognition effectively in college  (Dye & Stanton, 2017). In this study, we aimed to characterize first-year life science students’ use of metacognitive sk ills for learning  when they joined college classrooms following the COVID-19 pandemic. We asked two research questions: (1) When, why, and how do students use metacognitive skills of planning, moni toring, and evaluating, and (2) How does  current first-year students’ use of metacognitive skills differ compared to first-year students before 2020? The knowledge generated from this study is being used to develop curriculum to accelerate metacognitive growth in first-year students.

## Methods

Participants. We collected data at three public institutions in the United States: Georgia Gwinnett College (n=8), University of North Georgia  (n=21), and University of Georgia (n=23). We selected institutions with different academic environments because learning context can affect metacognition. We recruited a group of 52 first-year undergraduate life science majors. Students provided written consent to participate in this IRB-exempt study. Procedure. We collected semi-structured interview data to help answer our research questions. The interview protocol was developed using the metacognition framework to examine students’ use of  three major metacognitive regulation skills. Interviews were  conducted o n Zoom, video and audio recorded, and professionally transcribed. Transcripts were checked for accuracy before data analysis. Analysis: We used holistic analysis to examine each participa nt’s use of metacognitive regulation skills in the context of that student’s overall data (Joss elson, 2011). We created a profile template that pr ompted us to determine how, when, and why a student used each metacognitive regulation skill. Our analyses involved iterative rounds of content analysis, profile creation, and g roup discussion. Each participants’ data was indivi dually analyzed by three researchers, and we synthesized our profiles into one final profile for each participant by consensus to ensure rigor. We used categorical analysis of the profiles to c ompare participants’ use of each skill and to identify commonalities as well as notable exceptions (Saldaña, 2013). We then compared our current data to our published data from first-year students before the pandemic (Stanton et al., 2015; Stanton et al., 2019).

## Results and discussion

Planning. Current first-year students showed greater openness to recommendations from instructors, peers, and online resources when they plan their study strategies than first-year students prior to the COVID-19 pandemic. Some students sought study advice from YouTube and TikTok where they learned about study strategies involving “active recall”. Students also planned to use strategies that worked well in high school, even when those strategies were not working in college. Interestingly, some participants selected strategies based on their preferred learning styles. Although the idea that a student learns bes t in a visual, auditory, or kinesthetic style has b een discredited (e.g., Pashler  et al., 2008), some participants asserted that they  learn best when concepts are taught in their preferred learning style. This belief misguided their study plans because some participants would not consider strategies unless they aligned with their preferred learning style. Monitoring. Current first-year students showed greater awareness of the value  of formative assessment for monitoring than first-year students prior to 2020. First-year students knew that they struggled to accurately monitor their understanding and therefore appreciated it when instructors helped them identify what they did not yet know. For example, participants appreciated when their instructors explained the correct answers to clicker-style questions in class. While studying on their own, first-year life science majors monitored in two ways: (1) they self-tested using instructor-provided resources, or (2) they explained a concept to themselves. Partici pants’ self-explanations were often constructed in their minds,  sometimes out loud, and rarely in writing. Some participants acknowledged the challenge of  being honest with themselves about what concepts th ey truly knew versus what concepts they were familiar with, especially when answering questions “in their head”. Evaluation. First-year students evaluate the effectiveness of their study plans at two point s in time: (1) using their predicted grade after taking an exam, and (2) using their actual grade after receiving their graded exam. When participants evaluated after an exam, they primarily used their feelings of confidence, fluency, and comfort, similar to first-year students prior to 2020. Some students evaluated their approaches to learning  as effective if the method, such as answering practice exam questio ns, was well aligned to the exam format. Other students deemed their approaches to learning as ineffective if they perceived the method to be time-consuming or “boring”. Participants in our study who had part-time jobs considered the efficiency of their strategies when evaluating.

## Conclusions and implications

By understanding how, when, and why first-year students use metacognition, we have gained ins ights on what can support them to be more metacognitive. Planning: Current first-year life science majors may be particularly open to advice on how  to study compared to first-year students prior to 2020. Instructors can capitalize on this openness by providing students with step-by-step instruction and practice on effective learning appr oaches. Yet instructors may need to debunk myths such as preferr ed learning styles to help first-year students make better planning decision s. Monitoring: Current first-year students  may better  understand the benefit  of formative assessment for monitoring compared to past first-year students prior to 2020. Instructors should con tinue to explain answers to  in-class questions because current first-year students  especially acknowledge the  need to develop their monitoring accuracy. Evaluating: Current first-year students may be primed to evaluate after taking an exam and when they receive a grade on an exam, similar to students before 2020. Instructors could focus their guidance on these timepoints to better help student s develop their evaluation skills.  Fostering metacognition in first-year life science majors will contribute to their learning in college and beyond.
