# Teaching and Learning Mathematics with Artificial Intelligence

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Novel technologies influence the curriculum, pedagogy, and learning in schools. My research explores the impacts of AI-based tools in mathematics education, attending to how these tools influence teaching practice and how students learn to think mathematically. My scholarly goal is to support mathematics and stochastics teachers in developing innovative and robust pedagogies that uphold cognitive demand in learning even as tools that automate humanistic thinking become endemic to schooling. Overview My research centers curriculum, pedagogy, cognition, and AI/LLM automation in mathematics. I am interested in how AI-based tools mediate students’ mathematical thinking and teachers’ development of expert teaching practices to support student thinking. My interests stem from my experience as a high school math teacher of seven years, where I learned that mathematics tools and curricular resources played a major role in lesson orchestration and what my students ultimately learned (Brown, 2011). I conceptualize teachers and learners as agentic, creative, and curious drivers of learning rather than recipients of societal changes influenced by developments in AI automation. I further envision teachers as lesson designers and orchestrators who, when faced with the latest technological disruptions, candesign effective instruction based in part on multi-faceted forms of teacher knowledge, including knowledge of how students’ interactions with tools impact their learning. To support teacher learning with insights about AI-tools’ impacts on students’ mathematical thinking, I have recently begun to research students’ AI-mediated problem solving processes (Delaney et al., 2025b). This work is in its second year and involves both well-defined and ill-defined mathematics tasks. I conduct video-recorded task-based interviews and observe the problem solving processes that surround students’ decisions, evaluations, AI trust, and epistemic judgments of mathematics output produced by chatbots. I have initiated this work in both clinical and classroom settings with algebra, calculus, statistics, data science, and numerical reasoning tasks. The findings from these studies inform my own teaching practice and AI adaptations, and with this knowledge, I continuously work to help other teachers develop design skills for an AI-integrated future. Establishing this program requires proficiency with the latest technologies. Under some conditions, technologies can create dynamic, inquiry-driven learning experiences that raise the cognitive demand required to solve nonroutine tasks, such as appraising the quality of a dataset or designing a facial recognition classifier (Delaney & Lee, 2024; Delaney & Selbach-Allen, 2025). In other circumstances, technology canderail students (or teachers) from their learning goals. My research program explores how teachers and students use novel technologies, the affordances and challenges that arise from their technology use, transformations on student and teacher thinking that are mediated by technology, and implications for learning. Theoretical frameworks I draw from theoretical frameworks that foreground the complexity of teaching and learning. I have several lines of research that build from different lineages of theory. When I conceptualize relationships between math curriculum and teaching, I draw from Brown’s illustration of teaching as adaptative, improvisational design work, akin to orchestrating a jazz concert (Brown, 2011). His pedagogical design capacity framework formed the basis of my dissertation, where I studied how statistics teachers adapted and enacted a machine learning curriculum (Delaney, 2025). When I conceptualize teachers’ mathematical professional learning with AI, I draw from teacher knowledge (Shulman, 1986) and teacher noticing (van Es & Sherin, 2008). What teachers attend to, interpret, and how they respond to students is directly related to their knowledge. Yet while teachers are still in the early stages of developing their knowledge, they may not yet have the multimodal forms of expertise to evaluate LLMs’ output and/or claims about students’ mathematical thinking. I am therefore developing theories about when and how mathematics teachers may prompt LLMs as cognitive supports, and what additional (human) supports they need to grow their pedagogical content knowledge and noticing abilities. From the student perspective, I have recently explored knowledge building with AI-based tools (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2020) and building expertise in mathematics with LLMs (Chi & Glaser 1985; Hiebert & Carpenter, 1992). Today’s LLMs can imitate humanistic mathematical reasoning, and can complete most of K-12 mathematics tasks with minimal prompting specifications. It is therefore necessary for students and teachers to discern how to use LLMs to build knowledge and foster their own expertise rather than bypass the cognitive work involved in mathematics problem solving altogether. Recent work has begun to explore what human learning a nd epistemic agency may look like when LLMs are involved in mathematics tasks, proposing that students’ uses of LLMs are indicative of how much problem solving agency they retain versus offload to intelligent agents (Wu et al., 2025). I intend to extend this work and study how students’ LLM uses mediate their moment-by-moment thinking and what agency they preserve as they develop problem solving skills.

I use a mixture of methods and research settings to enact my program. As a former high school teacher, I believe that observing teachers enact lessons with their own students can yield high-fidelity accounts of curricular and teaching interventions. At the same time, I use my own classroom teaching with pre-service mathematics teachers and aspiring undergraduate data scientists as a testbed for academic interventions. My methods build from designbased principles (Brown, 1992) that explore relationships between teaching, curricula, tools, and learning. Core to my work is preserving teachers’ design agency and decision-making authority in their classrooms. I use co-design methods (Delaney et al., 2025a; Severance et al., 2016; Xie et al., 2024) to position teachers as core decision-makers about the curriculum and methods that they use. I have sought co-design partnerships with industry partners to design mathematics and data science tasks with LLMs that are authentic to the work that practitioners do. I seek to position myself as a support for teachers during the curriculum design process who is respectful of the cognitive effort required to create high-quality materials (Potvin et al., 2024). As Brown suggests, classroom environments are ecologically noisy and chaotic, and it is sometimes difficult to discern why teachers make decisions about teaching, curriculum, and technologies. To this end, I use clinical interviewing (diSessa, 2007) to understand student and teacher thinking processes. I have used clinical interviews to study reasoning about machine learning (Delaney et al., 2023), algorithmic bias (Lee et al., 2022), and teachers’ selections of datasets for teaching statistics (Delaney & Lee, 2022; Lee & Delaney, 2021). I have learned to carefully design tasks and interview questions in these settings, and to consider my own positionality in relationship to the data produced. My analysis methods skew qualitative although I am trained as a data scientist and have recently begun to apply unsupervised clustering methods model patterns in students’ thinking. Research plans I am building a research program to advance knowledge on human-AI interaction in mathematics. I plan to closely study students’ thinking processes with LLMs during the next 1-3 years through a series of carefully-designed experiments so that I candevelop concrete, nuanced recommendations for teachers and curriculum designers. Two of my current funded projects reflect these goals. In the first, I apply external conditions and observe whether they influence students’ AI evaluations during mathematics problem solving with AI tools. I designed this variation because, in my exploratory round of AI and mathematics problem solving, I found that students’ AI evaluations of mathematics were more superficial than I had expected, because there was no goal or incentive to obtain a mathematically-sound answer. In the second, a colleague and I have trained a deceptive chatbot to consistently give an incorrect answer. In task-based video-recorded interviews, we will measure whether students’ epistemic trust and conference of mathematical authority is more robust toward the deceptive chatbot or a human tutor instructed to make the same mistake as the chatbot. Overall I am working to refine my study design and research practice so that I can recruit large enough student samples for randomized control trials. From these studies I aim to develop theories about human-AI mathematical interactions, including the degree to which any student-AI interaction truly qualifies as “collaboration” as the learning sciences conceive of it (e.g., Barron, 2003). In addition, I am continuing to partner with school districts and study teacher co-design with AI-based lessons using insights from my research. I am leading a professional learning in data science for late elementary school, where my colleagues and I will focus on building teachers’ co-designed lessons about algorithmic bias in school communities. With the same colleagues, we are working toward obtaining grant funding for AI literacy co-design that supports values and local ontologies in rural Borderlands California.
