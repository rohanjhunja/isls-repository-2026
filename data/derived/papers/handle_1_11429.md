# Answerable Research Practice Partnerships: Practicing Aloha ʻĀina, Cultivating Pilina, and Co-Producing Knowledge Accountable to Hawaiʻi

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Over the past 15 years, public and private investments in Research Practice Partnerships (RPPs), long-term collaborations that use research to improve education, have soared. But attention to how RPPs answer to Native places and peoples remains limited. In this critical, self-reflexive case study, we ask: How do participants in one RPP author meanings about “research,” “practice,” and “partnership” that answer to Hawaiʻi? Informed by Patel’s ethics of answerability and analytical tools drawn from the critical learning sciences, we analyze three years of fieldnote data generated through our efforts to transform ourselves and education in Hawaiʻi. We argue that daily practices of aloha ʻāina (devotion to caring for the land) forged lifelong pilina (binding, relationships). These reciprocal, land-based relations animated a research focus on (re)designing learning that enhances life and Kanaka ‘Ōiwi (Hawaiian) self-determination.

## Introduction: ʻA stranger for a day’

“Hoʻokahi nō lā o ka malihini,” Kimeona Kāne, a kumu (source, teacher, foundation) of uhau humu pōhaku (rock weaving), announced to a group of two-dozen volunteers. Kāne translated, “A stranger for a day —after the first day as a guest, you have kuleana [privilege, responsibility, burden ] to help with the work.” Ethan Chang, an assistant professor of education at the University of Hawaiʻi, stood in the warm, waist deep ocean water, dwelling with the kuleana he did not know awaited him upon accepting Kāne’s invitat ion to steward a loko iʻa (fishpond) in Kāne’s beloved hometown of Waimānalo. Over three years since this  initial workday in the winter of 2022, Chang has taken up Kāne’s invitation “to help with the work” by participating in activities not planned or directed by academics and by actively restoring Hawaiʻi hand-by-hand, rock-by-rock (Bang et al., 2018; Okuda et al., 2023). Kāne actively rebuilds Hawaiʻi. But more, he  has chosen to live into the kuleana that his Kumu, Kinohi Fukumitsu, set before him, that is, to “build builders”: to cultivate Native and non-Native leaders rooted in Kanaka ‘Ōiwi (Hawaiian) lifeways, who acknowledge and work to restore Hawaiʻi in service of our great-grandchildren’s grandchildren.

Community leaders and ʻāina (land, earth, “that which feeds ”) educators like Kāne have become increasingly sought-after partners of university-based researchers particularly a mid the growing popularity of community-engaged scholarship. As one example, Research Practice Partnerships (RPPs), long-term collaborations that use research to improve education practices, have soared over the past 15 years (Gamoran, 2023). RPPs aim to (co)produce knowledge in partnership with school t eachers and administrators and endeavor to make knowledge more responsive to immediate problems of school practice (Vetter et al., 2022). Despite promising examples of education RPPs, the rush to partner with leaders  like Kāne risks rehearsing extractive university-community relations (Chung-Do, 2019; Minthorn et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2025). On one occasion, Kāne remarked, “Suddenly, I’ve become everybody’s ‘community partner’!”  Moments such as these surface a need to examine how settler-colonialism—a persistent social structure and land-based project that seeks to maintain dominion over Native peoples, lands, resources, practices, and lifeways (Grande, 2004)—structures even more inclusive approaches to scholarship such as RPPs. Studying how act ors negotiate, make meaning, and co-produce knowledge in ways that answer to Kanaka leaders like Fukumitsu and Kāne holds promise for realizing the transformative potential of RPPs.

This critical and self-reflexive case study asks: How do participants in one RPP  author meanings about “research,” “practice,” and “partnership” in ways that answer to Hawaiʻi? We extend Patel’s (2015) call to shift education research “from ownership to answerability” by considering our re sponsibilities to place as a starting point for interrupting coloniality (p. 71). We also turn to conceptual  tools from the critical learning sciences to home our analyses of the ways Kāne and Chang co-constructed meanings, identities, and knowledge. Data for this project draws primarily on three years of fieldnotes Chang crafted chronicling our efforts to transfor m ourselves and education in service of Hawaiʻi.

We argue that the daily practice of aloha ‘āina (love of land)  and cultivation of lifelong  pilina (relationship, union, connection, adhering, fitting)  animated research processes and outcomes that answered to Hawaiʻi such as the perpetuation of ʻike Hawai ʻi (ancestral knowledges of Hawaiʻi).  Our work  challenges conventional RPPs that “feature engagement with research as a leading activity” (our emphasis, Farrell et al., 2021, p. iv). Turning our hands down to ʻāina as a matter of daily practice, opened possibilities for remembering, recovering, and reconstructing Hawaiʻi’s original classrooms of le arning. We offer our moʻolelo (story, tale, chronicle) as one case  that might afford generative openings for community-engaged scholars seeking to enact answerable research in  their own  land-based contexts. Put differently, our work is not intended to be selfaggrandizing but self-critical, not prescriptive but provocative; an invitation to ourselves and readers to redouble ancestrally guided commitments to building the worlds we want and need. In alignment with the 2025 International Conference of Learning Sciences Annual Theme, “Educating for world-making: Envisioning and enacting sustainable solutions to global crises,” we consider the look, feel, and texture of education research that tangibly services Native-led struggles in land reclamation and self-determination.

## Conceptual framework: Answerability and transformative agency

Patel’s (2015) call to shift education research “from ownership t o answerability” (p. 71) anchored our analysis. Patel argues that ownership represents a  taken-for-granted logic that governs education research; it structures researchers’ pursuits of (private) property such as the possession of more and more grants, data, awards, and publications. As an underlying logic, ownership can pull research projects away from land-responsive, ancestrally informed lifeways toward university-responsive, transactional modes of partnership. In contrast to ownership, answerability guides the formation of part icipatory, humanizing places for finding and perpetuating anti-oppressive knowledges. Patel (2015) put it this way:  “We should see ourselves as stewards not of specific pieces of knowledge but rather of the produc tive and generative spaces that allow for finding knowledge” (p. 79). Answerability facilitates a “radical restructuring” of researchers’ relationship to knowledge whereby researchers do not possess findings (e.g., intellectual property), but rather, honor, perpetuate, and apply ancestral knowledges to co-produce knowledge and co-construct experiments in freedom (Gilmore, 2022; Patel, 2021).  These dual (and dueling) concepts of ownership and answerability  guided our efforts to critique prevailing models of RPPs and propose more accountable, more answerable alternative pathways. To sharpen our analyses of the moment-to-moment actions and interactions of participants in one RPP, we turned to recent scholarship from the critical learning sciences. The critical learning sciences interrogates how inherited designs for learning (e.g., age-segregated, subject-specific, individually evaluated school classrooms) tend to reflect and reify broader oppressive social forces such as coloniality (Esmonde & Booker, 2017). It also sensitizes inquiry toward what Engeström & Sannino (2020) call, “t ransformative agency,” or “the human potential of transforming conditions and forms of collective life”  (p. 87). Critical learning scientists understand people as active producers of history and knowledge even as they are constrained by historically given cultural and material conditions  not of actors’ own choosing. From this perspective, one aim of scholarly inquiry is  to analyze how actors make meaning, take up new practices,  and author self-determined identities for themselves and one another (Mendoza et al., 2018). We were particularly interested in how actor s exercised transformative agency to divest from logics of ownership and co-create answerable spaces for finding knowledge.

## Methods

We used a critical and self-reflexive case study to synthesize multiple kinds of data (e.g. fieldnotes, images, texts) concerning how actors constructed meanings about research, practice, and partnership (Creswell, 2009). The “critical” nature of this case study attuned our inquiry toward the  broader social forces (e.g., coloniality, ownership) that shape, and were shaped by, the actions and interactions of participants. The “self-reflexive” dimensions of our case study countered extractive traditions of observing social others and productively redirected our analytic gaze onto the “subjective and often messy act[s]” of  our attempts to grow, learn, and co-produce knowledge (Patel, 2015, p. 76).

Positionalities: Kumu-Haumāna relations Our positionalities and pilina to one another as kumu and haumāna shape d every aspect of our research process. Kumu-haumāna bonds are mutually agreed upon relations in which kumu hold the responsibility to cultivate the ‘ike and personhood of haumāna, and haumāna (student s, pupils, apprentices) exercise responsibilities to learn and perpetuate knowledge in accordance with their kumu’s teachings. Fukumitsu is a Kumu Uhau Humu Pōhaku who chose to pour her time, energies, and ‘ike into Kāne. In turn, K āne has taken on the kuleana to become a Kumu Uhau Humu Pōhaku. Kāne chose Chang—as one among several dozen haumāna—to become a builder of both kuapā (wall of a fish pond) and kaiāulu (community, village).  A focus of our present work is the pilina between Kāne and Chang. Their pilina has pulled each other into complementary spheres of tho ught and action; pathways that Jacobs and colleagues (2022) might describe as those that  express “two-eyed seeing,” or the use and integration of Indigenous knowledges through one lens and Western-born scientific training through another to cultivate knowledge that perpetuates all living  peoples, plants, animals, lands, and waters (p. 274). Chang approaches learning under Kāne as one way of living in service of Hawaiʻi and one means of deepening his efforts to occupy spaces within the academy to better serve Hawaiʻi, and specifically, Fukumitsu and Kāne’s community of Waimānalo. Our written analys is that follows represents one way of weaving answerability across three generations of Kumu-Haumāna relations and amplifying our ongoing efforts to perpetuate Hawaiʻi. Study contexts: Writing with/from/for Hawaiʻi Hawaiʻi offers a strategic location for critically analyzing RPPs given historical legacies of nonviolent resistance to U.S. colonial occupation and colonial systems of K-16 schooling (Goodyear-Kaʻōpua et al., 2014). Our research directly confronts U.S.-led efforts to “possess” Hawaiʻi through ongoing military occupation of Hawaiʻi lands and through cultural discourses that exoticize Hawaiians and all things Hawaiʻi (including research) as seemingly natural, rightful possessions of whiteness (Arvin, 2019). Hawaiʻi’s distinctive multi-racial and multi-ethnic contexts—often mislabeled a “post-racial paradise ”—also hold potentially illustrative implications for RPP scholars interested in building equitable relations across social differences. Such an approach attends to the distinctive roles and responsibilities of settlers of color, or r acially minoritized peoples like Chang, who are not genealogical descendants of Hawaiʻi, but who are interested in accompanying and amplifying Native-led struggles for self-determination (Arvin et al., 2024).

Generating and analyzing data Analyses for this paper draws primarily on data generated through fieldnotes Chang crafted following ʻāina workdays and practices of  uhau humu pōhaku. To date, Chang has joined Kāne at 183 workdays since 2022 or approximately 2,000 hours of aloha ʻāina stewardship. Fieldnotes offered a glimpse into “the conditions  under which people actually invoke and apply such terms in interaction with other s” (Emerson et al., 2011, p. 167) and offered insight into how Kāne and Chang —alongside other haumāna and ʻāina stewards—authored meanings of “practice,” “partnership,” and “research.” Chang and Kāne regularly dialogued about these reflections as they wove rock. More formal pauses in our ‘āina work, such a s crafting this project proposal or presenting at other local, national, and international conferences,  afforded opportunities to deepen our analyses and revisit our distinctive roles and responsibilities in producing knowledge answerable to Hawaiʻi. We foregrounded answerability as our analytic frame and crafted a c odebook with three primary codes corresponding to each dimension of “research,” “practice,” and “partnership.” For each code, we asked, “How do participants author meanings about research/practice/partnership in ways that answer to Hawai ʻi?” Within these three primary codes, we then inductively generated sub-codes pertaining to the array of patterned themes through which actors made meaning, took up new practices, and/or authored identities for themselves and one another. As one example, under “partnership” we coded for “Human and more-than-human beings” to make meaning of how participants exploded conventional RPP  assumptions concerning legitimate partners to include kūpuna (elders), cultural practitioners, as well as the winds, skies, and rocks that constitute place (c.f., Meixi et al., 2022). In total, we generated 21 codes.

In the following section, we discuss major story lines we discerned within each dimension of RPPs. We begin our story with practices and trace how participants engaged in uhau humu pōhaku as one specific expression of aloha ‘āina. The practice of aloha ʻāina  formed the foundation for reciprocal pilina among people and place, which generatively  de-centered the school as a primary site of  learning and  research. From this ʻāina -based location, Kāne and Chang posed questions that answered to Hawaiʻi and K anaka families and youth and not necessarily to inherited, statewide school metrics of success. As one example, we began to pose research questions such as: How do we design learning that cultivates good people who ‘auamo (carry, as a burden) their kuleana to perpetuate Hawaiʻi?

## Findings

Answerable practices: Aloha ‘āina as healing the self and the land Participants constructed meanings about answerable practices that centered aloha ʻāina: an intimate, personal, and unrelenting care for ʻāina (“that which feeds ”; land) and devotion to perpetuating all that ʻāina represents as a source of physical, spiritual, mental, and collective nourishment (Case, 2021; Osorio, 2021). Chang and other haumāna of Kāne, expressed aloha ʻāina through ancestral practices of uhau humu pōhaku, or rock weaving. To uhau (place) and humu (weave) pōhaku (rock) was to actively restore  wahi pana (sacred, storied places) throughout Hawai ʻi. These wahi included  loko iʻa (fishponds), hale (houses), or kuahu (alters). Uhau humu pōhaku nudged Chang to put down his own books and pick up  different ones—texts that were not etched in ink or bound pages  but encoded in the texture and shape of pōhaku, the oral traditions of oli (chanting), and the fragrance of lepo (mud). These practices were intimate and personal and, unexpect edly for Chang, healing. To exercise aloha ʻāina was to heal the land, and in doing so, open oneself to healing and learning to live a  life in service of place.

Early in Chang’s relationship with Kāne, he puzzled over how to uhau humu pōhaku. Kāne would often describe the practice this way: “Our job is to find out what each pōha ku’s kuleana is—what role in the wall each pōhaku serves.” But uncovering each pōhaku’s kuleana was an immensely frustrating process. “I am trying to let ‘āina move through me,” Chang jotted in an early fieldnote, “But the more I try, the more I think.” Chang described his early kuapā constructions as “ugly,” “weak,” “unstable.” In response, Kāne persistently reminded, “Be kind to yourself. Listen to the pōhaku.” The directive, “Be kind,” was a namu haole (English) mode of expressing the foundational concept of aloha; what Aunt ie Pilahi Paki defines as: Akahi (kindness), Lokahi (unity), ʻOluʻolu (gracious), Haʻahaʻa (humility), and Ahonui (patience). Expressing aloha for pōhaku required Chang to practice aloha for himself. It was impossible to weave rock without holding oneself with akahi and ‘oluʻolu. Kahiau Wallace, a kahu (caretaker, guardian) of Huilua Loko Iʻa, poi gnantly observed, “It [Huilua] is not a working fishpond yet but it’s working our minds and spirits” (Hawaiʻi State Department of Education, 2021). In precisely this way, uhau humu pōhaku worked on Chang, even as Chang arrogantly thought he was working on Huilua in a unilateral way. E arly deficit reflections of his rock weaving slowly transformed into m ore assetbased accounts. A year into his relationship with Kāne, Chang reflected, “The wall curves slightly to the right but it is paʻa [firm, solid].” The wall was not perfect but it was paʻa. Terms such as “ugly” disappeared from Chang’s fieldnote descriptions of his wall, and subconsciously, occupied less and less of  Chang’s own understandings of himself and his capacities to contribute to Hawaiʻi. Kāne described the reciprocal relationship of aloha ‘āina this way: “As we rebuild the wall, we rebuild ourselves.” Conversations between Kāne and Chang centered on but far exceeded rock. Fieldnotes included learnings about  how Chang attempted to unsettle and (re)build long-sedimented ways he upheld his marital, familial, and educator responsibilities. These processes of personal growth mirror Hemphill’s (2024) definition of healing as, “the process, often lifelong, of restoring and reawakening the capacities for safety, belonging, and dignity on the other side of t rauma” (p. 40). Uhau humu pōhaku was a way of practicing healing—of restoring wahi pana (sacred, storied places) as a means of restoring oneself. Valued practices in this RPP did not begin with activities led or directed by researchers, but instead, involved activities led by the needs, stories, and dreams of place and stewards of those specific places. Uhau humu pōhaku offered one restorative, culturally sustaining way to practice aloha ʻāina. Beginning with practices of aloha ʻāina challenges patterned ways  in which  settler institutions so often  disconnect words from deeds: to acknowledge the land without taking concrete steps to return or restore stolen lands and  resources to  Native peoples (Salis Reyes et al., 2023).

Answerable partnerships: Familial, lifelong pilina to place and people From a Kanaka worldview, place guides and governs behavior (Oliveira,  2014). Place also shapes the very research questions worth asking (Kūlana Noiʻi Working Group, 2021). To be “pili” with a place is thus much more than a “partnership.” Pilina involves a deep, intimate, lifel ong devotion to perpetuating right relations with place and people, which in turn, feeds and perpetuates the capacitie s of individuals and families to (re)dedicate their lives in service of place.

To choose pilina over partnership, however, required  interrupting what TallBear (2019) calls a n American “animacy hierarchy” that places some beings below others such as m en above women, able-bodied over disabled people, humans over plants and animals  (p. 25). Unlearning this deeply sedimented animacy hierarchy was evident in one fieldnote reflection from Chang. During closing piko today, I thanked Chris [Cramer], kahu of Kānewai  Loko Iʻa. I shared that it was humbling to see Chris “all by himself” as we oli kāhea’d into space [requested permission to enter place]. I intended my words to elevate Chris for his devotion to perpetuating Kānewai without the funds or staff that this wahi deserves. But Kumu interjected, “What do you mean ‘all by himself’—what about all these people [pointing to the plants and animals of Kānewai]. The oʻopu, the lāʻī, the hapawai, the hihiwai, the ʻamaʻama…” Kumu went on and on. With a smile and a dose of sarcasm, he listed every living winged, scaled, plant, and more-than-human being by their inoa [name]. I felt a sting of embarrassment  and mahalo’d Kumu with an uncomfortable laugh.

This example illustrates one moment in which the practice of aloha  ‘āina ushered in Kanaka understandings of being and being in right relation. Kāne guided Chang to acknowledge  what Deloria and Wildcat (2001) call the “personality” of rock, winds, fish, trees, and the multitude of more-than-human beings that have made and remade Kānewai for generations (p. 27; c.f., Meixi et al., 2022). Moments of embarrassment, however, can open portals for lifelong pilina. Pilina to place shatters statecentric assumptions of legitimate “partners” in RPP scholarship, namely, those individuals who the state certifies or who formal institutions of learning credential as authoritative knowledge holders (e.g., individuals who hold a PhD). As Ishimaru et al. (2022) productively critiqued, the vast majority of RPPs take place within formal school settings. They argued that conventional approaches to partnership  tend to ignore the contributions of families, youth, community leaders, and other non-formal educators and organizers from predominantly Black, Native, or minoritized communities; “partners” like Kāne who hold the equivalent of multiple PhDs but none recognized by settler institutions of learning. As a Kumu, Kāne facilitates haumā na engagements with place. He guides our unlearning of violent animacy hierarchies to recognize place-as-partner—to understand where we are, given that, as Jacob and colleagues (2021) rightly point out, “From an Indigenous perspective, if you do not know where you are, you are lost” (p. 2).

When we consider place-as-partner, we open ourselves tonew intimacies and horizons of being in relation. Kāne often remarked, “You breathing Hawaiʻi air. You have kuleana to Hawaiʻi.” Paying attention to place elevates the wisdom and insight of those kahu, kūpuna, and kumu who, often daily, care for and perpetuate Hawaiʻi beyond school or university walls (among others). Pilina also challenges conventional RPP horizons of partnership (e.g., academic calendar years, grant-funded timelines). On one occasion, Kāne attended a virtual presentation Chang gave on out-of-school learning designs. When an audience member asked Chang how he plans to “exit” from community, Kāne recalled thinking, “Whatchu mean, babe s, this relationship is lifelong!” Kāne admitted tonearly unmuting his mic rophone and interrupting this questioner. This occasion  also offered a reassuring moment of reflection for Chang, who could better discern how the pilina he shared with Kāne rendered short-term, transactional modes of partnership unthinkable. Elsewhere, Kāne would express a similar lifelong understanding of pilina by noting, “We will be a student of this practice [uhau humu pōhaku] until we die.” These long-haul understandings of pilina to place and people speak of responsibilities, not rights, and whereby notions of “exit” are not a part of the grammar of relationships (Vaughan, 2018). Answerable research: (Re)designing education with ʻāina to build ‘good people’ Daily practices of aloha ‘āina enfolded into lifelong pilina to place and people, which in turn, constituted the kāhua (foundation) from which  Kāne and Chang constructed meanings about research. Specific to education, Kāne and Chang began with big philosophical questions such as, “What counts as an educated person in Hawaiʻi?” Kāne and Chang thus made meaning about research that both (a) de-centered formal schooling as a privileged site of learning, and accordingly, (b) pushed Chang to address the ways universiti es and schools have historically colluded with the ongoing colonial erasure of Kanaka knowledges and disconnection of youth from ‘āina. A number of scholars have named the ways educators and education res earchers tend to conflate schooling with education and narrow our imaginings of what rigorous learning can and could look like (Shield et al., 2020). Etched in one of Chang’s early fieldnotes was a line from Kāne: “You can be successful in school and still be an asshole.” One patterned theme in our discussions of a ʻo (teaching and learning) was the idea that the object of ‘āina-based learning was, to quote Kāne again, “to be a good fuckin’ person—someone who knows their kuleana, cares for themselves and their community.” Salis Reyes’ s (2019) analysis of Native college graduates echoes this very idea. She elaborates a question that one Native interviewee consistently posed to herself: “What am I doing to be a good ancestor?” (p. 627). To be a “good fuckin’ person” or “to be a good ancestor” challenges narrow definitions of school success  that hem in our understandings  of learning to prevailing objectives and models of colonial schooling.

From this expansive understanding about the purposes of education, Kāne and Chang then crafted more specific empirical research questions: “How do Hawaiʻi public schools (dis)connect young people from ʻāina? ” “How do carceral logics ʻseep’ into youth-adult relations even in ʻāina settings beyond the school?” (c.f., Serrano, 2024). “How might we (re)design learning beyond schools and in ways that are guided by and devoted to aloha ʻāina?” Given the ways schools have historically absorbed well-intended reforms (Tyack & Cuban, 1995), our dialogues shifted from what Ogawa (2015) calls studies in “reform” to “redesign”—from tinkering with schooling to (re)designing learning ecologies “rooted in different institutional histories from that of schools” (p. 5). Glimpses of (re)design are evident in Kāne’s description of ‘āina  as “the classroom of the future as they were in the past.” Elsewhere, Kane mentioned, “It is long overdue that they [ʻāina classrooms] be given the proper time in our lives.” Our research pathways have propelled joint efforts to think systema tically about ‘āina education while refusing colonial invitations to “package” knowledge into a discrete curriculum the state might own, possess, and put on a digital shelf. Answerability thus lived in the practice of designing research as an expression of aloha ‘āina and our lifelong pilina.

Our research focus on education redesign also contributed to a growing, shared urgency to address the ways the University of Hawai ʻi normalizes colonial attempts  to possess knowledge. During one rock weaving session, Kāne gifted Chang  the book, Ka Lama Kukui: Hawaiian Psychology An Introduction. The text was an invitation for Chang to re-examine some of his personal, internal struggles through  a Kanaka lens. Kāne added with a laugh, “I buy some books from UH Press sometime, but fuck U H…” A member of the hui, Kaho ʻokele Kea, asked sincerely, “Why ? Do you not like UH, Kumu?” I chimed in, “Yeah, I know UH is involved in the genetic modification of kalo and TMT [Thirty-Meter Telescope]—but what else?” “That’s not enough?” Kāne stammered. I shook my head in disbelief of the wor ds that came out of my mouth. But our pilina made possible moments such as these  whereby honest, uncomfortable conversations furthered researchers’ reckonings with the violence UH has exacted upon Kanaka relatives like kalo, Mauna Kea, and Kanaka families and Hawaiʻi broadly (Goodyear-Kaʻōpua, 2013; Hobart, 2019). Informed by Kāne, we continue to confront the ways UH and other formally trained researchers enter  Kanaka communities, take data, publish, and grow their research teams without answering to community  members’ hopes, dreams, and/or concerns. In the words of Patel (2021), Kāne and the Waimānalo community have re sponded to  researchers’ inquiry, “How do I gain access to communities?” by asking  a question of their own: “Why should you have access?” (p. 65). Over the course of our still growing pilina, Kāne has placed more and more kuleana before Chang. “How is the University of Hawaiʻi, Hawaiʻi?” Kāne often asks Chang. These nudges have pushed Chang to build pilina with other community-engaged scholars at the University of Hawaiʻi to transform how UH prepares researchers to answer to Hawaiʻi and Hawaiʻi families and young people.

## Discussion: Toward answerable research-practice-partnerships

Gilmore (2022) reminds that “freedom is a place” (p. 6). The essential question, Gilmore adds, is: “[H]ow do we make such a place over and over again?” (Gilmore, 2022, p. 93). Our work centers the making of place through aloha ‘āina and  the perpetuation of pilina to sustain those very places. Co-creating knowledge is central to the perpetuation of place-based relations, as we  strive, imperfectly, to cultivate “productive and generative spaces that allow for finding knowledge” (Patel, 2015, p. 79). We conclude here by offering lessons emanating from our practices of aloha ‘āina, privileging of pilina over partnership, and  engagements with education research for learning (re)design.

First, our findings trouble  conventional RPP framings of a “research-practice” divide. We see value in producing knowledge for “the practice side of the partnership” (Gamoran, 2023, p. 35). But at the same time, we urge researchers to confront their own practices —those taken-for-granted understandings of scholarship that so often reify logics of  ownership. Kāne and Chang enacted aloha ʻāina as a way of being and being-in-relation to place, people, self, and ancestral traditions of knowledge. As O sorio (2021) states, “It [aloha ‘āina] is the central and orienting framework for any attempt to understand what it mea ns to be Kanaka Maoli” (Osorio, 2021, p. 9). To enact aloha ʻāina thus pushed Chang to consider his own practices and his own (limited) streams of knowledge through which he might show up in place to (co)produce responsible research alongside Kāne. Second, our findings prioritized pilina over partnership. Pilina replaced relations guided by conventional, state-legitimated partners and grant-funded timelines with more expansive notions of binding, lifelong relationships to place, elders, and stewards of place  (Minthorn et al., 2023). Pilina reminds us that, in the words of Niiyokamigaabaw Deondre Smiles (Leech Lake Band of Ojibwe, personal communication, April 2024), “Settler colonialism is a momentary aberration in the history of the world.” When we examine social science from a Kanaka paradigm, we can better grasp how conventional terms of partnerships express thin, and often, “WEIRD” ways of being in relation  (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic; see Kurtiş & Adams, 2015). Rather than researchers presuming that “the researched need change and that social science will compel it” (Tuck & Yang, 2014, p. 236), pilina honors  long-term, intimate, familial relations. It insists that researchers answer to Native places and cultural practitioners like Kāne and Fukumitsu, whose wisdom and insight  researchers might have the privilege to share in. Finally, our analysis confronts  histories of extractive university-based research to consider the fraught contexts of doing research from within settler institutions (Glass et al., 2018; Salis Reyes et al., 2023). Chang and Kāne did not jump into efforts to “improve” or “reform” schools. Rather, they began with philosophical questions about the purposes of education and the roles and responsibilities of re searchers to advance community-defined ends. Given the patterned ways public schools in Hawaiʻi have colluded in the ongoing erasure and disconnection of young people from ʻāina, research then became a means of exper imenting with ancestrally guided designs for learning that answered to Hawaiʻi. We argue that research has a role to play. But research is only one tool among many that scholars might draw on to build community power and transform the cultural and material inequities in the very places they live, work, and play.  Kāne and Chang sidestepped conventional RPP tendencies that “feature engagement with research as a leading activity” (Farrell et al., 2021, p. iv) to consider how research might build builders, gro w Hawaiʻi consciousness, and expand life-affirming spaces for finding knowledge (Patel, 2015).

We conclude our moʻolelo with questions we continue to ask ourselves and that, we hope, might provoke more answerable ways of enacting RPPs in distinctive land-based contexts. We invite readers to join us in asking: How have I remained “a stranger for a day”  in my land-based contexts? How is my daily lifestyle (not only my research) contributing to the flourishing of interwoven human and more-than-human relations? And finally, how might I transform myself in the process of building transformative spaces of learning, specifically those that divest from logics of ownership and nurture answerable spaces for finding knowledge?
