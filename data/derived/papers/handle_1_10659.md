# Reimagining Learning Contexts and Learner Agency in K-12 Science Education: A Critical Review

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In the last few decades, the purpose of science education has been called into question mainly for not being impactful enough for students’ everyday lives. In part, this has to do with the framing and use of learning contexts; what educators consider as influential contexts are external to the students thereby providing learners with limited agency to modify and work with them. In this paper, we examined how contexts were articulated in K-12 science education research. Based on our preliminary results, we created a four-category continuum ranging from contexts as indexical factors to contexts as constitutive elements. The study has relevance for educators in terms of reconceptualizing our understanding and use of learning contexts in research and learning design.

## Introduction

Despite increasing recognition of the importance of contextualizing  science education to the everyday life of students and their communities (e.g. Na & Song, 2013; Keifert & Stevens, 2019), there isn’t a considerable shift in the contents of learning materials and the conte xts of learning design. That is, contexts are often discussed in terms of their relevance for students to understand  specific, often prespecified content or concept such as force, acceleration, or photosynthesis. Secondary school science education has struggled with declining student interest as they progress through the curriculum, which is p artly explained by a perceived lack of relevance and the way science is taught (Basu & Barton, 2007; Barmby et a l., 2008; Steidtmann et al., 2023). This lack of sustained interest in STEM subjects is leading to a decrease in the number of students choosing science-related subjects in upper secondary school or pursuing non-compulsory higher education (Subotnik et al., 2010). Furthermore, Indigenous and Ethnic minorities are especially underrepresented in non-compulsory science courses (McKinley & Gan, 2014). Using individual interest-evoking contexts have been suggested as interest-enhancing interventions (Harackiewicz et al., 2016). However, to foster rel evant contexts for a diverse group of learners, we must look critically at how contexts are used and designed in science educa tion research. In this paper, we examined the way learning contexts are articulated in science education studies and the extent to which learners have the agency to modify or shape the stated contexts. Mor e specifically, we answer the research question, “W hat is considered as learning context in K-12 science education studies and what level of agency do learners have to modify learning contexts?” The results of the study will provide insights for educators to  reconsider their way of using learning contexts in designing learning environments for students with diverse backgrounds. Science education: Purposes, practices, and representations In the last few decades, science education has seen  a “practice turn” (Ford & Forman, 2006), during wh ich the focus shifted from learning about science to developing a scientific understanding through performing science. More recently, the words ‘relevant’ and ‘science ed ucation’ have been put together, although the way r elevance is defined varies greatly (Stuckey et al., 2013). Berland et al (2016) argue that there is a need for science learning that is both meaningful for the scientific communit y (e.g., preparing for a career in STEM) and for th e student and their communities. To make school science meaningful, there is a need for the learners to be able to use their everyday experiences; the ideas and interests they take from their home lifeworld and bring them into the classroom. Perceived boundaries between students' everyday life and the (science) classroom disrupt needed continuities for students’ learning (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011). Often, the crossing of boundaries between the diffe rent worlds a child participates in (e.g., science classroom, home environment) leads to internal conf lict, resulting in low motivation and poor knowledg e integration (Bronkhorst & Akkerman, 2016). Besides,  when teachers use issues from everyday life in the ir teaching, there is an unjust representation of the experiences and values of communities. This is beca use “many science learning materials present phenomena and ex amples that are only familiar to students from main stream cultural backgrounds in affluent countries” (Tapia,  2020, p. 9). Challenges of discontinuity are there fore even more pronounced for indigenous and ethnic minority students. The way contexts are used in science educ ation research and learning design can have a powerful im pact on the motivation, interests, and effective le arning of students from diverse backgrounds. We aimed to capt ure the range of ways by which contexts are articul ated in intervention-based K–12 science education studies and suggest ways in which they could be reconceptualized.

## Method

Using “Web of Science”, we conducted abstract searc h for “context” OR “learning environment” and “scie nce education” OR “science learning”, which resulted in  1702 studies. By focusing on intervention studies,  we narrowed the pool to 200 peer-reviewed paper s for full-text analysis. We then focused on studies where the participants were K-12 students. So far, we extracted data from 59 stud ies and this paper is based on our preliminary findings. We focused on sections that described the contexts, learning envi ronments, and learning activities. From these, we thematically clustered the codes to create the categories discussed in the next section.

## Results

Using our preliminary findings, we constructed  and labeled  four categories of learning contexts in science education. This is based on the way researchers articulated contexts and the extent to which students can change or modify the specified contexts. In other words, the categories are based on the extent to which the stated contexts are “provided” (external) to students, or if students c an influence/create the contexts. We also considered the dynamic or relatively static nature of the stated c ontexts as participants interact with them in their  learning activities.

Contexts as indexical factors Studies in this category use pre-existing factors that the researchers believe influence students’ learning. Examples of such contexts include the nature of school neigh borhoods, the SES of students, gender/racial composition of the classroom, or profiles of teachers  (e.g. Chao et al., 2017; Pierson et al., 2023). Contexts in this case relate to who the students/teachers are or where the learning  happened. The challenge with such framing is that contexts are often determined for students, and they don’t have the agency to change them. Besi des, these factors are presented as indexical descriptions to point out where the research or learning happened; not much is said in terms of how these factors interact with students’ learni ng processes. The results section of studies rarely  includes variations in learning processes related to these variables. Contexts as designed learning technologies Most of the studies (75%) described designed (often technology-based) learning environments as contexts. These are generally designed by researchers/teachers and made available to students. Compared to indexical c ontexts, designed learning environments provide better inter action for students where they manipulate different  variables and interact with technological resources as opposed to just operating given the contexts. Lai et al (2018) designed a multimedia learning environment in which students  read a textbook and use an AR application to project 3D images of topography. The students can choose to scan the image based on their interests, but the result is always the same – a 3D representation of the topography. Working in technology environments can be useful and engaging but the degree of student agency is limited as the available opportunities of interaction are predetermined and follow prescribed paths created by educators.

## Contexts as authentic scenarios (context-based learning)

Context-based learning environments aim to use everyday lif e experiences and scenarios as examples and make it clearer for students why they learn a given scie nce topic that is covered in class (Pilot & Bulte, 2006). In this case, there is a greater focus on choosing and designin g contexts that are grounded in authentic practices  and examples from everyday life albeit teachers and researchers determine what is relevant and useful. Although there are limited student inputs in framing, these contexts provide opportunities for interaction with different elements through learning activities that do not generally follow a prescribed path. A good example is a debate-style learning activity within a context-based lesson on protein-rich food (Ummels et al., 2015). Contexts as constitutive elements Different from the previous categories, the se contexts are not provided for the students but are brought forward and appropriated by them. These contexts are not external or technological factors that are assumed to influence students learning from the outside, they are rather  constitutive elements that make learning possible,  thinkable, and achievable (Clarke, 2013). These contexts are what students bring to the learni ng environment in terms of who they are, what their interests are, what issues they want to address through their learning, and what they want to take from their learning experience. As interactions change over time, the contexts develop in response, thereby creating a dynamic whole.  Harper (2017) created a cross-cultural learning community consisting of first-generation refugee children, their parents, non-refugee children, and the researcher to embed scien tific inquiry within cultural knowledge from all participants. Pa rticipants brought their everyday experiences and h istorical cultural background into the le arning space, which included taking pictures of their culture a nd science in their homes and the creation of moon stories combining scientif ic and cultural knowledge. Studies in this category reported increased perceptions of the relevance of science, an increase in interest, and the creation of a space for students from minority groups to express their expe riences and give a voice to those who are often sil enced (e.g. Gallay, 2021; Solomon et al., 2022).

Based on these categories, we created a continuum r anging from “indexical” to “constitutive” and the degree to which the context is dynamic or interactive. Figure 1 places the categories on the visualized continuum. Our observation is that the more science education is connected to the students (internal), the more d ynamic and relevant it is for students' active learning and engagement. The place of the categories on the continuum represents where most of the studies can be located and does not consider outliers. ## Discussion

Science education has seen many changes and turns over the years, but there remain problems of declining interest and inclusivity. The relevance of science learning in everyday life has also been called into question. Our analysis shows that most of the empirical K-12 science education studies are located on the external and less dynamic side of the continuum, with contexts provided for, as opposed to constructed by the students. This also raises questions about the purpose of science education as to whethe r we are focusing too much on producing young scientists who would eventually look and act like experts or making science learning relevant for the everyday lives of learners even if students don’t end up in science-related jobs. Following the studies that created contexts through  interactions, we suggest reconceptualizing context s as “becoming” instead of “being”—the difference being in the agency of the learners. When thinking in terms of constitutive elements, contexts can inhabit differe nt social worlds (Star & Griesemer, 1989) thereby m aking the science classroom a place where boundaries are blur red facilitating the crossing between (westernized)  science and the students’ everyday lifeworld. Within this, context is not fixed, but something to develop and act with, “its materiality derives from action” (Star, 2010, p. 603). A context does not have to be static and, for t he student, an externally provided “thing”. It can be dynamic, and student-created, becoming through the interaction between students, teachers, community members, and learning  materials such as textbooks and laboratories. By m oving further up the continuum, contexts can become more familiar, interesting, and relevant for a wide variety of students as they become the main creators.
