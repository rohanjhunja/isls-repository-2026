# Youth as Designers of Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning Technologies: What Do We Know About the Opportunities and Challenges of K-12 Students Creating Their Own Applications?

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Over the past decade there has been exponential growth in artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML) applications moving out of the lab and into the world, impacting everyday lives. To date, most AI/ML and learning research efforts have centered learners as recipients of instruction from, or collaborators with, AI agents. This symposium brings together a group of scholars who will discuss how learners and teachers can engage with AI/ML technologies as creative resources, or CreateAI. CreateAI refers to the use of AI tools and methods for creative expression, that is, involving students not just in using AI for pre-determined productive ends, but also as creators who candesign and build projects with AI/ML.

## Overview

We are participating in  exponential growth in arti ficial intelligence and machine learning (hereafter: AI/ML) applications, using them, or being data subjects of  them, as they move out of the lab into the world, impacting everyday lives. Consequently, there has also been increased attention to algorithmic bias and harm enacted by AI applications (Benjamin, 2021). There is now a growi ng call for AI literacy (e.g., Long & Magerko, 2021; Touretzky et al., 2018) and for all K-12 students and teachers to be prepared to understa nd, use and critique AI applications. However, to date, most AI in education efforts have concentrated on what has been called artificialteachers, artificial-tutors, and artificial co-learners, positioning learners as recipients of instruction from AI agents or collaborators with AI agents (Ouyang & Jiao, 2021). In current discussions, one perspective has received much less attention: how learners create, train, test and research AI applications. Like Touretzky, Gardner  McClune & Seahorn (2022), we see the construction of computat ional artifacts of AI/ML-based applications as a premier opportunity  for students to see themselves as “not  just programmers, but AI application developers.” (p.10). While K-12 computing education research (CER) has recognized  youth potential for consequential computational action with a variety of  curricular activities and programmable tools, much less is known about what this means in the context of developing applications involving AI/ML. In this symposium, we bring together an internation al group of presenters who will address different dimensions of what learners can gain from designing AI/ML applications, examine in which ways critical agency can be developed, and what kind of challenges they observed. The presentations in this symposium will focus on a set of intersecting ideas: ● Tools: What kind of tools already exist or need to be designed to support creative AI design activities? What are the design features that support students in di fferent grade bands and with different motivations to work in Creative AI?

● Ethics: What are critical issues in Creative AI? How best to engage students in discussions around algorithmic justice and fairness when creating AI applications?

● Learning: How do we define this approach of Creative AI? In  which ways do current discussions about generative AI add/contrast/extend the possibilities of Creative AI? What do we need to know about students’ prior understanding at different age levels? What m ight students learn about AI, CS, and other discipl ines through Creative AI? What are the intersections of practices and concepts in Creative AI vs CS framed by current CS standards?

● Teaching: What are promising pedagogies for teaching Creative  AI in K-12 schools and informal settings? What frameworks are in development or already in pl ace? How can AI/ML be integrated into current computing education pedagogies and frameworks? What  are appropri ate activities that are accessible and feasible for K-12  students and their potential teachers? and ● Assessment: How can teachers assess student learning of AI ke y concepts and practices in creating AI applications? Our goal is to develop a better under standing of the opportunities and challenges associ ated with creative AI/ML applications and how we can pre pare students and teachers for a world in which AI-based solutions to problems in health, energy, and the environment will be a ubiquitous part of their 21stcentury education experience. The symposium is organized in four sections: (1) the chairs will introduce the topic; (2) each of the panelists will have six minutes to share their approach, (3) follo wed by our two invited discussants, and (4) a Q&A w ith the audience and presenters.

Making AI-Based Apps Strengthens Children's Data Agency and Understanding of AI Matti Tedre, Henriikka Vartiainen This socioculturally informed  study introduces  a pedagogical  framework, design principles, and a cr ossboundary co-design process involving teachers and researchers t o co-develop new educational practices, curriculum materials, and technology for teaching A I in Finnish basic education. In a multidisciplinar y, designbased research project, 213 students in 4th and 7th grades explored the basics of AI by co-designing and creating their own AI-driven applications while considering AI’s impact a nd ethics. Grounded in  design-oriented pedagogy, the aim was to  position children as desi gners and knowledge creators in AI (Kahila et al., 2024). The intervention started by introducing learners to uses of classifiers in contexts familiar to them. Following that, our meta-design approach used open-ended tasks and scaffolding to engage students in collaboratively exploring key classifier concepts through their own interests and app ideas. Using our own AI app development studio that does not require programming, children followed the basi c epistemic functions related to ML workflows and c onnect their design ideas and evolving understanding to real-world contexts (Pope et al., 2024). Fig. 1 presents classroom photos from different stages of the co-design process. At the end of the project, children reflected on the potential risks and harms of AI through their own app-design process  and  explored algorithmic bias usin g generative AI (Vartiainen et al., 2024).

Qualitative content analysis of pre-and post tests, children's design ideas, and reflec tions of the app design process revealed that co-design provided promising entry points for children  to develop conceptual understanding of AI principles, workflows, and  its role in everyday life. Taking on agentive, cent ral roles in personally meaningful, scaffolded AI design project s cultivated children’s data agency and ownership o f their learning process. Children’s pre-and post tests showed that their data-driven exp lanations of algorithmic bias improved significantly,  indicating a more critical understanding of how AI-based systems should be designed or redesigned (Vartiainen et al., 2024). The study's t heoretical and pedagogical insights, alongside its empirically validated framework for scaffolding data-driven design, offer research-based guidance for advancing culturally relevant, equitable, and inclusive AI education. We  suggest that such AI education will help children build their data agency: their ability and volition to be activ e, contributing members and critical makers of the data-driven society.

Given the immense impact of AI/ML systems on our daily lives, this includes empowering students to creatively design and de velop such systems themselves. To this end, relevant AI/ML-related competencies are being discussed and introd uced in K-12 computer science curricula around the world (e.g., Touretzky et al., 2019; Tedre et al., 2021; Michaeli et al., 2023). Also, a plethora of educational resources and approaches has been de veloped in recent years (Morales-Navarro et al., 2024). However, we still lack an understanding of problems  teachers and students face in the classroom, in particular when designing AI/ML systems.

To address this gap, we conducted an action researc h project with the goal of developing theories abou t learning and teaching processes in the field of AI/ ML in order to improve teaching on an empirical bas is. In our national context (Bavaria, Germany), AI was introduced within a mandatory computer science subject for all high school students in year 11 (Jetzinger et al., 2024). Within the curriculum, in about 16 lessons, the s tudents learn about basic ideas of AI methods, specific algorithms (such as decision tree learning and artificial neural networks), discuss challenges for our society, and train their  own AI/ML models. In our first action research cyc le, we particularly focused on students’ (AI-specific) learning difficulties. To this end, the 10 computer science teachers who participated in the project reported their lesson plans and brief reflections on each lesson. After teaching the entire AI sequence, the students' learning progress was assessed through a test. Furthermore, we conducted semistructured interviews with the teachers. The data was ana lyzed qualitatively using an inductive approach in line with grounded theory, triangulating the different data sources. In addition to general barriers in line with existi ng literature, such as issues with mathematical and programming skills, we identified several AI/ML-specific learning difficulties. First, our results suggest that students have difficulties in evaluating whether ce rtain AI/ML methods can be applied to a given probl em. One reason for this may be a more general problem with assessing the capabilities of AI: on the one hand, students overestimated the capabilities of AI systems. On the other hand, they generally assumed that AI/ML methods can only be used for very complex tasks. Furthermore, t he teachers reported difficulties in training AI/ML  models with unknown datasets. In contrast to the small and  generally “well-prepared” data sets that are commonly used within AI education, students face various difficul ties in preparing the data (e.g., splitting into a training and test data set), applying a particular ML algorithm and e specially evaluating a trained model with the purpo se of improving it. These findings  align with the more general difficulty we found in our data of bridging the gap between pedagogically reduced and real-world examples to apply AI-related concepts from the classroom to AI/ML systems that students use in their everyday l ives. Additionally,  our results show that working with authentic tools that allow for solving “real-world problems” poses a lot of challenges for stude nts and teachers alike. Similar to other areas of computing education, this raises the question of design principles for adequate tool support that foster students’ creativity and agency.

The next step in the first action research cycle is  to discuss these difficulties with the participati ng teachers in order to co-design strategies and approaches to address them fo r the second cycle. In this way, the results of our study contribute to the identification of learning difficulties in K-12 classrooms and provide valuable insights for improving AI education on an empirical  basis-laying the foundation for empowering students to shape their future.

Critical, ethical, empowering design of AI/ML systems for social good Netta Ilvari Children are living in a world dramatically transfo rmed by emerging AI/ML systems. Moreover, their eve ryday life is transformed by numerous crises, pandemic, on-going wars and climate change as notable examples. All this is causing anxiety, sorrow and fear among children, concerning their future as well as the future of the globe and humankind. Now, more than ever, we need to engage c hildren as changemakers for a better – more equal, just, sustainable – future. Computing education, on its part, should foster children’s agency regarding their (digitalized) futures. We should invite children to approach comp uting and its consequences critically and to shape the trajectories of computing of the future. Moreover, we should invite children to view the world and t he crises within with a critical yet constructive stance, try ing to make the world a better place for us all. Na turally, computing alone can never solve complex societal problems, but it can contribute on its part. We report on a design research project in which we explored critical, ethical and empowering design methods together with children. The methods, in dif ferent ways, invite critique of the status quo with  injustice, inequality and oppression; and taking action to transform it (Iivari et al. 2022). We particularly derived inspiration from the Scandinavian political Participatory Desig n tradition which prioritizes empowerment and the r ight to have a voice and a say in design (Iivari et al. 202 2). We also relied  on speculative and critical design traditions that invite critically probing, questioning and dre aming about (alternative) futures, using design as a form of critique or a political tool (Iivari et al. 2022). Such approaches enable both critique and action taking for a better world. They entail ethical consideration of right a nd wrong and foster empowerment – of individuals and collectives, in relational and motivational senses (Iivari et al. 2022). In the project, children designed diverse AI/ML systems with advanced surveillance, sensing, reasoning and adaptation abilities to tackle a wicked societal problem of bullying. Based on our explorations, we recommend four methods for critical, ethical, and  empowering design of AI/ML systems: (1) Miracle met hod (Iivari et al. 2024a) invited children to imagine a bullying-free futures, revealing rich insights into the oppr essive status quo but also desirable futures to design for (Iivari et  al. 2024a).; (2) Critical reflection on underlying  assumptions, values and best-and worst-case consequences of design invited children to cri tically consider their own role and responsibility as designers shaping the future, and  the consequences of AI/ML systems on people’ s lives (Iivari et al. 2024b); (3) Theatre of the Oppressed method (Iivari et al. 2024a) invited children to discuss t heir designs within their school community and to mobilize the c ommunity for collective action taking against bully ing, fostering empathy towards and the necessity to work  on behalf of the bullying victims.; and (4) Design  activism (Iivari et al. 2024a), finally, invited children to  engage their school community through activism cam paigns and anti-bullying designs to mobilize everyone to take actio n against this vicious problem, making visible pers onal responsibility to do so.

Criticality in the sense of critique and radical ch ange picture in these methods, as well as reflectio ns on right and wrong, desirable and undesirable futures – including with AI/ML systems. Relational and motivational empowerment of child-designers and bullying victims picture strong in th e design, but also collective empowerment of entire school communities. Our insig hts enable renewing children’s computing research a nd practice in several respects. We wil l continue tackling wicked societal problems of int erest to children, collaboratively with them exploring the potential of AI/ML systems for social good. Empowering teachers as users and creators of specialized AI tools for diverse classrooms Safinah Ali Current AIML curricula are primarily designed for S TEM classrooms with AI concepts, practical applicat ions and ethical implications as the primary goal (Casal-Otero, et al., 2023). However, AI literacy today is relevant to non-technical classrooms, and is not just a technical skill, but a socio-technical skill (Ali et al., 2021). Diverse fields of work such as art, law, education, policy,  and sports are all impacted by AI. To make AI lear ning accessible within these contexts, AI pedagogy must be adapted to non-technical classrooms, and be designed with educators from diverse classrooms. We situate the c urrent work in the context of art classrooms, where  we integrate AI learning materials and tools in art le arning. We exploit multiple ways of knowing about t echnology-epistemological pluralism-as a framework to understand art making as a way to learn with and about AI.

In this work, we co-designed AI and Art learning materials and tools wi th 21 creative educators in four languages. Teachers gained foundational AI knowledg e, practical experience with AI tools, and AI proto typing skills. Teachers described AI and art learn ing objectives for their students, developed lesson s to use AI tools in creative learning,  designed AI tools for supporting art learning in their classrooms, and reflected on the usage of AI in creative classrooms. We utilized the use-modify-create framework (Lee, et al., 2011), where teachers gained experience with AI skills, and used them to create their own AI tools and lessons. Teachers demonstrat ed higher confidence in teaching with, explaining and making with AI. For instance, teachers used the AI Art Explorer tool that helps creative learners reflect on the artwork and gain personalized feedback during art creation, and develop lesson plans around using AI Art Explorer in their own classrooms. Teacher-defined learning objectives ranged from using AI for personalized art feedback, investigating studen ts’ own art style (4 teachers), evolving own art style (4 teachers), expressing emotions through art (3 teachers), creative ideation (3 teachers), gaining knowledge in art history (3 teachers), identifying emotions in art (2 teachers), and art for social justice (2 teachers). Teachers designed tools tailored for their classrooms, such as, a socio-emotional learning tool, a language learning through AI character personality design, and a social justi ce art reflection tool to promote creative inquiry. A them atic analysis of pedagogical practices emerging in teach ers’ lessons revealed the use of reflective practic e, inquirybased learning, iterative learning, representation of self, project-based learning, eth ical reflection in art making, aesthetic and emotional management (see Figure 2).

In this work we suggest a three sta ge approach-learning about AI, using AI tools in lessons, and developing AI tools for their own classrooms, that involves educators as key stakeholders in designing  learning materials and tools, and empowers them to be inform ed users, creators and educators in an AI-driven future. Understanding and creating with AI: Tools and strategies for computing education Line Have Musaeus, Ole Sejer Iversen Over the last few years the availability and usabil ity of generative AI systems have increased tremend ously, impacting everybody’s lives, also the everyday lives of students. Hence, there is a growing call for teaching about AI in computing education (Tedr e et al., 2021). Research on how to teach about gen erative AI systems and not only about the use of generative AI is increasing in computing education research, and there are now an interesting array of approaches to teaching about AI. This body  of research allows us, as researchers, to focus ou r concentration on questions such as: What kind of tools can support students in being designers and creators of AI systems? What and how can students learn about gene rative AI systems when interacting with such tool s in various subjects? And most importantly, can students transfer their creative approaches and understanding of how generative AI systems work from a computing educati on setting to other subjects in school, or even to their everyday lives outside school?

As the questions posed above indicate, there is a research gap on how to teach generative AI with students as creators and designers, and how to foster studen ts’ ability to transfer these competences to the wo rld they encounter outside school. Hence, future research sh ould address whether students are able to retain their knowledge and competences about generative AI syste ms and if they are able to apply them in new contex ts. Transfer of knowledge is crucial to education since  knowledge about and interactions with AI system s generally aspires to have impact on contexts, such as students’ lives outside school, which is quite different from the specific context of learning in computing education. When in vestigating the nature of students’ transfer of kno wledge, both the concept of near transfer (learning transfer to closely related contexts and performances) and the concept of far transfer (learning transfer to rather different contexts and performances) are relevant (Barnett & Ceci, 2002; Musaeus & Musaeus, 2024).

At the Center for Computational Thinking & Design, we are concerned with developing critical AI literacy in K-12 education using a design based research approach  and participatory design to work closely with teachers during the entire research process. We present research investigating how to design tools for teaching about the underlying models of natural language pro cessing and about the training of large language mo dels (LLMs) in high schools, while exploring what creative expressions students engage in when interacting with these tools, see Figure 3 (Musaeus et al., 2023). Also, we are studying how students’ and teachers’ creative interactions with AI systems change after participating in teach ing interventions in which these tools are applied,  and if knowledge is retained. Finally, we are investigating if and how knowledge and interactions transfer to o ther contexts outside formal education, leading to computational competent, critical, and creative students. Building your own generative language model: High school students’ engagement with data practices and ethical considerations when designing babyGPTs Luis Morales-Navarro, Daniel J. Noh, Yasmin B. Kafai As generative language models (GLMs) have gained po pularity, youth are increasingly using them in thei r everyday lives. However, most research has centered  on supporting youth as users of GLM-powered systems (e.g., Solyst et al., 2024). At the same time, r esearch on how youth engage with the design of ML m odels has mostly centered on classification tasks with little  attention given to generative tasks. Thus, we know  little about how to engage youth in the design of these models. We investigated how youth engage in designing small GLMs by building on the rich tradition in learning scien ces and child-computer interaction of positioning young people as designers of applications. We conducted a worksh op spanning five class sessions (each 90 minutes long) with 35 high school students (ages 14 –15) in which they designed very small GLMs (between  30K-600K tokens), which we call babyGPTs, using the nanoGPT framework. In this class activity, we took a data-driven approach that glassboxes, or makes transparent, how data  shapes model performance and blackboxes the role of  learning algorithms in the ML pipeline (Morales-Navarro & Kafai, 2024). Building on research on ML data practices (Olari & Romeike, 2024) and ethical sensemaking (Landesman  et al., 2024), we analyzed the  students’ design process, discussions, artifacts, and interviews, to address the following question: What data practices and eth ical considerations did participants engage with in the construction process of their own GLMs? Figure 5 Fragments from Outputs of the Marvel babyGPT Model when using Different Prompts We present a case study of team of three students w ho designed a babyGPT model to generate Marvel-like screenplays (see Figure 5 for examples of outputs f rom the model). The team identified that the latest  Marvel films were of lower quality and decided to build a screenplay generator that, trained on the scripts of their favored films, would generate new scripts. In doing so, the y engaged with data practices (Olari & Romeike, 202 4) such as understanding a task  and defining success criteria. While building their model, the team collected data, controlled the quality of the data, prepared the data  by tokenizing it and evaluated the performance  of their model. Engaging in these practices iteratively prov ided opportunities for students to consider how dat a quality impacts model outputs and to participate in complex  discussions about copyright, authorship, and attri bution. After creating these mod els, participants reflected on ethical implications  related to the trustworthiness of generative models, copyright issu es, and environmental impact. They also reflected o n the importance of data quality and quantity in the training dataset and vo iced a more refined understanding of the functional ity of generative language models. Our study demonstrates the feasibility of engaging young people in the des ign of GLMs. Compared to other studies in which youth lear ned about the functionality of generative models and explored their outputs as users (Solyst et al., 202 4) engaging youth in construction activities made m ore transparent the process of building models. Building babyGP Ts supported youth to question the “aesthetic legitimacy” of outputs (Solyst et al., 2024) and develop nuanced understandings of the functionality and ethics of GLMs.
