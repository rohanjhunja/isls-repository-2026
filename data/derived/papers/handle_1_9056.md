# A Comparison of Refutational and Rhetorical Aspects in Science Arguments by Grade Level

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Engaging students in the process of argumentation supports student understanding of scientific knowledge. We present a comparison of arguments from 7th and 11th grade biology students from modeling tasks using an argumentation framework to investigate differences in student approaches during two comparative tasks. While students at both grade levels constructed arguments that contained multiple reasons and epistemic ideals, we identified differences in rhetorical statements and refutational strategies by grade level. High school students attended to the underlying model-evidence relationship to explore differences between competing models and to make comparisons and refute the competing model at higher instances than middle schoolers. Refutational strategies by high schoolers connected multiple pieces of evidence, or made connections between provided models and evidence, while middle schoolers made use of epistemic ideals to refute a competing model. These differences are discussed in conjunction with implications for design.

Argumentation engages students with constructing and critiquing claims about phenomena using appropriate and sufficient evidence (e.g., McNeill & Krajcik, 2011). Students can leverage evidence in arguments in mu ltiple ways to make sense of phenomena and to articulate evidentiary relationships of the natural world (e.g., Nussbaum, 2021). Sometimes to increase the challenge of the task, students are presented with models in lieu of claims. These models include mechanisms and serve to explain some  phenomena. Students can be tasked with constructin g, evaluating, or revising such explanatory models (e. g., Passmore et al., 2009). Scientific argumentatio n plays a central role in evaluating competing models based on evidence, or identifying the best model or explanation (e.g., Krajcik & McNeill, 2012; Nussbaum et al., 2019). Re search on student argumentation relies on argumenta tion frameworks to provide insight about how students evaluate and use evidence to articulate claims and revise their own and others’ claims.

These argumentation frameworks tend to focus on argumentation structure with less focus given to the role of epistemic knowledge about what qualifies as  a good argument, a good model, good evidence, or t he relationship and interaction of models with evidenc e. Some of these frameworks focus on a specific asp ect or aspects of arguments in order to conceptualize student understanding. Paying specific attention to these structural aspects is critical but tends to hide other aspects such as the rhetorical nature of arguments. To better bridge this gap we use the Integrative Argumentation Framework (IAF) which examines arguments as a series of argument moves (Cavera et al., 2018a). This framework attends to structural aspects of arguments (e.g., capturing the claims made, supporting or refuting statements), appeals to epistemic criteria of model quality, and rhetorical nature of arguments as argument moves. These moves serve to capture how students convinc e a reader of a standpoint as they move through the pieces of their argument.

Scaffolding and task structure play a major role in the kinds of arguments that students can construct and critique (e.g., McNeill & Krajcik, 2012). These arg uments tend to contain increasingly robust aspects of arguments (e.g., a refutation, more reasons) when e xplicit scaffolds help students attend to those fea tures of arguments. While we know that students tend to cons truct increasingly robust arguments as they move th rough different grade bands particularly with scaffolding (e.g., Kuhn & Udell, 2003; Nussbaum et al., 2019) we know less about the nuance between those differences. This paper intends to address the impact of scaffolding on student argumentation and evaluate the kinds of complexity and nuance that students develop at two grade levels (middle school and high school) using the same argumentation framework, IAF.

## Theoretical Framework

Argumentation is the process of using data to defend or refute a standpoint (Toulmin, 1958; 2003), and is a critical knowledge-building process that appears in science classrooms as a way to support student understandin g of science concepts (e.g., Driver et al., 2000). Stude nts can be presented with claims that describe scie ntific phenomena or scientific models that include an unde rlying mechanism, but students find evaluating comp eting mechanistic models to be cognitively demanding (e.g., Krist et al., 2019; McNeill & Krajcik, 2012). Sc ientists ICLS2022 Proceedings 504 © ISLS make these kinds of evaluations about complex argum ents of phenomena (Popper, 2014). Students can crit ique and argue about why one argument is better supporte d by evidence oftentimes by demonstrating that more evidence supports evidence over another. This kind of weighing strategy has been described before (e.g., Nussbaum et al., 2019) as a way of either counting the amount of supporting evidence in support of a c hosen model or by integrating evidence into the argument or by integrating evidence into the model and showing how that evidence relates to the chosen model, either as warrant or backing for a chosen model (Cavera et al., 2018a). However, we know that students find difficulty in c onstructing and critiquing competing claims and models of competing phenomena, often developing sup porting statements of their chosen claim without discussing a competing idea. This idea of discussing the opposing side, or refuting competing claims, evidence, or discussing the contra argument, is often considered one of the more difficult aspects for students to attend to in argumentation. This process of identifying differences between competing ideas is central to the argum entation process. Osborne and colleagues (2016) discuss arguments that include a counter claim with justification as one in which a student can explain why the competing claim is wrong by leveraging a warranted comparative argument. This kind of sophisticated reasoning pattern is a d ifficult move for students to tackle and appears at  the highest level of their argumentation learning progression. McNeill & Krajcik (2012) discuss scaffolding studen ts’ rebuttals last in class as part of their claims, ev idence, reasoning, and rebuttal (CERR) framework in  order to reduce cognitive load. Successfully navigating and discussing their own argument as well as the counterargument in both of these instances is seen as an achievemen t. Often the idea rebuttal and refutation are used interchangeably in the literature but are distinct ideas. Toulmin (1958) defined rebuttal as “conditio ns of the exception”, whereas refutation is a contradictory idea to the chosen claim (van Eemeren & Grootendorst, 2001).

When students identify juxtaposing ideas between co mparative models, they are refuting such as in the case, I believe in model 1. It is better supported by the e vidence unlike model 2 which is supported by less, low quality evidence. A rebuttal could be posed as, model A is always right except when it is supported by less evidence. We know that students struggle to form refutations and without sufficient scaffolding struggle to form refutations on their own. For example, Nussbaum and  colleagues encouraged students to juxtapose compet ing arguments as part of a scaffold known as the argument vee diagram (AVD) at the middle school and college level which supports students to make comparisons to integrate competing arguments (Nussbaum, 2021; Nussbaum et al., 2019). This kind of comparative strategy allow ed students the planning space to identify flaws in  the counterargument. Essentially, refutation in this case was defined as the identification that the concl usion of the counterargument is false or that the premises of the competing argument is flawed. By allowing students the space to build an integrative solution, or a design claim, students can identify flaws and explore both an argument and counterargument using the AVD scaffold.

These and other approaches attempt to capture the n uance and complexity of student argumentation at the middle or high school level (e.g., Grooms et al., 2018; Nussbaum, 2021; Nussbaum et al., 2019) though these often focus on analyzing how students construct and critique arguments and the kinds of structural and epistemic ideas that students have about a topic. In this, we focus on argumentation moves across two grades (7th and 11th grade) in order to understand more about the differ ences in argumentation across middle and high schoo l. We apply the Integrated Argumentation Framework (IAF) which focuses on an analysis of nuance of argumen ts through the lenses of structural, epistemic, and rhetorical perspectives of arguments (Cavera et al., 2018a). These moves aim to capture how students articulate their standpoint by capturing three lenses of argumentati on: structural aspects (e.g., supporting claims, refuting claims), appeals to epistemic criteria, and rhetorical features. We applied IAF to student arguments to identify the kinds of arguments that students develop in middle and high school. We developed similar assessments about a topic in biology that aimed to evaluate students’ competencies and reasoning with models, and the construction of evidence-based arguments about core science topics.  Our research questions are: What are the kinds of arguments that students develop at the middle school and high school levels? What are the types of refutational and rhetorical features identified in student arguments?

## Study Context

The study took place in two suburban schools in the United States—a middle school (7th grade) and a high school (11th grade) in which students were learning biology top ics in the same school district. The schools were considered academically average in mathematics, language arts, and science performance. The district served 79% White, 9% Hispanic, 6% Black, 1% Asian, and the rem aining students identify as mixed race. Approximate ly 34% of the students were classified as economically disadvantaged, with 27% of students qualifying for free or reduced lunch in the middle school while 34% qualified in the high school. ICLS2022 Proceedings 505 © ISLS In both grades, students participated in a modelin g-based biology curriculum which engaged students in the development, evaluation, and revision of models of scientific phenomena, and provided students with multiple opportunities to develop evidence-based arguments in support of a chosen model (from 2-3 competing alternative models and multiple pieces of evidence that varied in quality). In both high school and middle school levels, students were assigned either a task called Falling Leaves (FL) or Muscle Pain (MP) at the close of instruction.

## Instrument Design & Data Collection

Students were provided with 60 minutes to complete the task. The assessment tasks included five pieces of evidence that varied in quality and two competing explanatory models, and students were asked to build an argument in support of the explanatory model they thought best explained a phenomenon they had not studied in class around the topics of muscle pain (e.g., Why do you think muscle pain occurs after heavy exercise?) or leaves falling off trees (e.g., Why do you think leaves fall off trees in the fall?). We will be discussing posttest arguments written by students at both grade levels. The evidence provided to students were either of high quality (e.g., scientific studies), provided information on the phenomenon (e.g., description of the phenomenon), or was an anecdotal account.

## Data Analysis

We analyzed student arguments using the Integrative Argumentative Framework (IAF)  (Cavera, 2018a). This argumentation framework analyzes student arguments by segmenting student arguments into coherent propositions or argument moves. These moves are categorized into three major pers pectives: structural, which captures the presence of a Claim statement (e.g., I believe model A is best.) which can either open or close the argument, and Support and Refute statements which provide credence (e.g., I think the model is correct because evidence 1 and 2 support it.) or demonstrates how the competing model is false (e.g., The only evidence that supports model B is evidence 4 and that isn’t a goo d quality piece of evidence.).  Epistemic moves are those in which a student appeals to epistemic ideals to satisfy an epistemic aim (e.g., The model has good pictures. It shows a step-by-step process.). Rhetorical moves include the Setting the Stage moves, which are those that provide a strategic organization of the argument. We identified two major types of Rhetorical moves: (a) Overview moves where students identify differences between models (e.g., Model B talks about damaged cells which Model A doesn’t even mention.) and (b) Preview where they provide a summary or layout their argument (e.g., I think [the] lactic acid [model] is better. There are more evidence in support of it and it’s clearer organized.). We took this framework and expanded the Refute move category based on a prior coding scheme (Cavera, 2018a) in order to capture the kinds of refutationa l strategies that students applied during their arg uments. We ICLS2022 Proceedings 507 © ISLS identified three major refutational strategies: (a)  refutation based on congruence with existing theor ies, (b) refutation based on evidence credibility, or (c) re futation by evaluating the quantity of evidence tha t supported each model in conjunction with (a) or (b). Three in dependent coders coded 22% of the middle school  data and 38% of the high school  data. Interrater reliability was 86% and 91%, MS, H S respectively. All disagreements were discussed.

## Discussion

Prior to students completing the post argument asse ssment, which was examined in this study, students were provided with scaffolds that were embedded into the curriculum. Due to students’ use of these scaffolds, we have found that students developed extended arguments th at contain rhetorically complex ideas and refutatio ns. Not only do students generate these kinds of arguments at each grade level they attend to different kinds of strategies to refute the competing model. Students at both gra de levels are also able to attend to the rhetorical  nature of arguments and appeal to epistemic qualities of evidence and models. Refutational strategies and complexity Appeals to epistemic ideals, rhetorical ideas, and refutations are evident, identified here as argumentation moves, both middle school and high school students developed arguments that contained features but did so in different manners. Previous researchers have identified that with scaffolding students can take the tactic of re futing a competing argument by evaluating the amount of supp orting evidence for competing arguments (Nussbaum, 2021; Nussbaum et al., 2019). This approach is similar to the strategy discussed here though many of our students additionally combined this approach with an additional appeal to epistemic quality. Middle school students who refute tend to focus on the epistemic qualities and features of the evidence in order to refute the competing model. High school students tend to focus on the relationship between the models and evidence, attacking the connection as compared to middle schoolers who attack qualities of the model and evidence. In each instance studen ts are refuting; however middle schoolers tend to focus on their model quality criteria list and describing how each of the evidence support their own model and not the co mpeting model based on the provided scaffold. This demonstrates the difference in what is important to  tackle and differentiate between—the model evidenc e connection or the epistemic qualities of the evidence. Students can evaluate the quality of the evidence that support their own model or claim or the quality of the connection between the model and evidence. Arguments written by high schoolers contained more  variation in argument moves and were more likely to contain both types of Setting the Stage move in the MP task. These rhetorical moves were no ted at a much lesser degree in the competing FL task. High school  arguments were, on the whole, longer than the argu ments written by middle schoolers which lends themselves to the increased level of nuance. However, at both grade levels students could include a Setting the Stage  move, often cueing the reader of how their argumen t was organized or of what features they would discuss throughout the argument. This idea of setting up the argument is an important rhetorical strategy that alerts the reader of important features of the competing argument but also tied into a refutation it demonstrates a powerful approach to argumentation. The role of scaffolding on argument complexity A major contributing factor to the development to t he nature of the arguments that students developed was a feature of the task design. During instruction, stu dents were provided with the Model-Evidence Link (M EL) matrix scaffold and expected to discuss the competing models and relationship between the models and evidence. For the assessment the scaffold was removed, and we found that at both grade levels students struggled to discuss the competing models, a finding consistent with previous work (Cavera et al., 2018a; 2018b). This work further supports the idea that students can argue  in robust ways with respect to rhetorical and refutational aspects of argumentation. Previous res earchers have demonstrated that taking students to make comparisons between competing claims is difficult, challenging students with competing multistep model s increases the difficulty—even with sufficient scaff olding. This work contributes to the body of work t hat has shown that students at both the middle and high sch ool levels can form refutations; this work demonstrates that students at each of the grade levels go about the process in different ways dependent on task and grade level. We also find task dependent differences with respect to rhetorical approaches. We hypothesize that students responded ICLS2022 Proceedings 510 © ISLS to each task differently because this assisted in how they made sense of each task. As these tasks were completed by student populations that seen the phenomenon of leaves falling off trees before this, coupled with the provided evidence may not have required the set up for the u nderlying mechanism. Comparatively, the complexity of muscle pain (a phenomenon students have experienced but cannot physically see) may have required more mental effort to unpack and explain lending itself to more rhetorical maneuvering. Students at both grade levels can argue rather com plexly especially when it comes to refuting. They just need to be provided complex tasks that ask them to develop extended evidence-based arguments. The refutations attended to are powerful but the strategies at each level were different with more attention at the high school level paid to the connection between the model-evidence connection while middle schoolers paid more attention to the qualities of the evidence that support their own mo del. These approaches differ from what Anderson and colleagues (2001) and Nussbaum and colleagues (2019) have previously found. We also know from previous work and others in the field that discussing the co mmunicative features of arguments (e.g., pictures, titles, etc.) are what students’ value before instruction (e.g., Pluta et al., 2011; Cavera et a l., 2018b). Even with scaffolding students at the middle school level rely on these c ommunicative features to refute the competing model  and its evidence. Students at the high school level begin to tackle that relationship between the model and evidence noting the strength of the model is only as good as the evidence that supports it.
