# Exploring the Roles of Silliness and Play in Student Group Work Using Epistemological Frames

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how humor and silliness function as productive forms of engagement in collaborative, inquiry-based science learning. Drawing on the framework of epistemological framing, it explores how first-generation and deaf and hard of hearing students participating in a metacognitive, studio-style summer program made sense of their group work through talk and body language. Video data from four student groups were coded along two dimensions – task (on/off-task) and seriousness (serious/silly) – and visualized to identify intersections between on-task and silly framing. Qualitative interaction analysis revealed four roles of humor: directing attention, transitioning from off-task to on-task engagement, epistemic distancing, and relieving tension. Findings demonstrate that playful behavior can support learning by fostering collaboration, mitigating uncertainty, and sustaining engagement. By reconceptualizing silliness as a form of epistemological participation, this work extends framing theory and highlights the value of humor in creating inclusive, human-centered science learning environments.

Over the past several decades, science education reforms have emphasized a shift from traditional, lecture-based instruction toward inquiry-oriented, student-centered approaches (NRC, 2012). This pedagogical shift reflects a broader reorientation of science teaching from the transmission of knowledge to the co-construction of understanding through dialogue, experimentation, and reflection (Windschitl et al., 2012; Osborne, 2014). As instruction becomes more activity-based and inquiry-driven, new or revived instructional models have emerged to support these goals in both the K-12 science setting as well as the postsecondary science setting (e.g. Michael, 2006; Reiser et al., 2021). Among them, studio-style teaching has gained prominence in postsecondary science education as a framework that integrates laboratory and discussion components, reduces lecture time, and emphasizes student collaboration and inquiry (e.g. Beichner et al., 2007; Foote, 2016; Pomian  et al., 2017). In these settings, learning occurs through collective sensemaking, positioning students as active participants in constructing scientific understanding.

Humor, silliness, and play have traditionally been viewed as distractions in academic settings, but recent research suggests they may serve valuable cognitive and social functions  (e.g. Berge, 2016; Reddington, 2015; Pomian, 2018). In collaborative, inquiry-oriented classrooms, participation is mediated not only by cognitive demands but also by social and affective dynamics  (Windschitl et al., 2012; Osborne, 2014). However, everyday interactional practices such as humor and silliness remain underexamined in studies of active-learning group work. Therefore, I ask: How can humor be productive during on-task interactions in classroom group work that centers active learning through studio-style engagement?

## Theoretical framework

This study draws on epistemological framing  as a lens for understanding how students make sense of their participation in collaborative science activities  (e.g. Hutchinson & Hammer, 2010; Hammer et al., 2005). The construct of framing originates from anthropology and linguistics, where it was used to describe how individuals or groups interpret “what is going on” in a given context (e.g. Bateson, 1972; Goffman, 1986). In classroom learning environments, framing captures how participants define the nature of  their activity; what counts as appropriate behavior, what kinds of reasoning or contributions are valued, and how they position themselves in relation to one another and to the learning task. These interpretations are not static but shift dynamically as students respond to cues from peers, instructors, and the evolving flow of interaction. Framing is expressed and negotiated through multiple modalities, including discourse, gesture, gaze, and posture (Goodwin, 2000; Frank & Scherr, 2012; Irving et al., 2013).

## Instructional context

The context for this study was the Integrating Metacognitive Practices and Research to Ensure Student Success (IMPRESS) summer program at the Rochester Institute of Technology (RIT). IMPRESS is a two-week, precollege program designed for incoming STEM majors who identify as first-generation college students and/or are 1712 deaf or hard of hearing (DHH) (Franklin et al., 2018). The program supports students who have recently graduated from high school as they transition into university-level science coursework. Its design integrates metacognitive practice with studio-style in struction, combining inquiry-based laboratory work with structured reflection on learning processes.

The observed classroom included twenty students organized into five groups of four, seated at separate tables. The program spanned nine consecutive days, running approximately eight hours per day. For consent reasons, this study focuses on three of the five groups, which were video-recorded using cameras placed on mini tripods to capture group talk, gestures, and overall physical engagement – key data for this study’s focus on both discourse and body language. During activities, the instructor and two stude nt leaders (former IMPRESS participants) circulated around the room, providing guidance and interacting informally with the groups. This study focuses on a morning session on the program's last day. This session included three integrative activities involving symbolic modeling, diagrammatic representation, and critical evaluation of climate change representations. Together, these activities provided a rich context for examining how students interacted, negotiated meaning, and used humor or silliness during metacognitive, inquiry-based science learning.

## Data analysis

This study employed qualitative video analysis methods (Knoblauch, 2012) grounded in the theoretical framework of epistemological framing (Scherr & Hammer, 2009) to investigate how students’ discourse and body language reflected their sensemaking and engagement during group science activities. The primary data consisted of video recordings from three student groups participating in the ninth-day of activities. These multimodal data captured students’ spoken interactions, gestures, facial expressions, and posture – elements central to understanding how they framed their participation and how humor or silliness emerged during collaborative work. Each intersection was then analyzed using interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995), an approach well-suited for examining learning as a social and multimodal process. Multiple analytic passes were made through the data, during which episodes were revisited, compared across groups, and contextualized within classroom activity. Memos were written throughout to document interpretive insights, emerging patterns, and theoretical connections. Humor was treated not as an isolated behavior but as a contextually meaningful phenomenon, a resource through which students negotiated understanding, maintained group cohesion, or re-engaged with the scientific task.

Through these iterative analyses, the study sought to reveal how moments of humor and silliness contributed to students’ epistemological framing. In doing so, it extends previous work on framing by illustrating how affective and social dimensions of learning can shape, and be shaped by, students’ interpretations of “what kind of activity this is” within inquiry-based, metacognitively structured classrooms. Coding procedures The task dimension captured how closely students’ talk aligned with the assigned classroom activity. Discourse was analyzed for relevance to the task prompts introduced by the instructor. Two frames were identified: on-task and off-task. Conversations directly related to the problem or concept under discussion were coded as on-task; unrelated discussions were coded as off-task. Two researchers independently coded thirty-minute video segments from each group, with agreement increasing from 88% to 98% after refinement of coding definitions. The seriousness dimension was coded primarily through body language and tone. Two frames were identified: serious and silly. A serious frame was characterized by focused attention on materials, forward-leaning postures, and minimal laughter or smiling. A silly frame was marked by laughter, grins, playful gestures, or lighthearted teasing. In some cases, a sarcastic tone, validated through peers’ reactions, also indicated a shift into the silly frame.  Students in the silly frame often displayed relaxed postures, distributed attention between peers and materials, and increased social responsiveness.

To better understand how students framed their group activities across the three classroom tasks, I created timeline graphs that visualized shifts between the task (on-task vs. off-task) and seriousness (serious vs. silly) dimensions for each group. These graphs allowed me to identify points of intersection, moments when students were simultaneously on-task and silly. Examining these intersections provided insight into how humor and playfulness were embedded within productive engagement.

Across groups, students spent the majority of their time in the serious and on-task frames. However, ontask/silly intersections appeared periodically throughout the activities, revealing moments when humor accompanied active engagement with the task. No consistent pattern was observed across groups regarding when 1713 these intersections occurred within or between the three activities.  Nonetheless, on-task/silly interactions were most frequent during Activity 2, suggesting that this open-ended, creative diagramming task may have provided conditions conducive to playful yet focused collaboration. This finding points to a potential area for future research: identifying task designs that naturally invite productive forms of humor and play.

Using timeline graphs of activities sorted by silliness, I identified specific moments at which on-task/silly states intersected. Each of these cases was analyzed using iterative qualitative analysis to examine how humor emerged, how participants responded, and what role it served in the interaction. From these analyses, four recurring roles of the on-task/silly frame were identified: directing attention  to an area needing clarification, transitioning from an off-task frame into on-task engagement, e pistemic distancing, and r elieving tension, see Table 1 below.

Directing attention The on-task/silly frame served to direct attention when humor or playfulness highlighted a part of the task that required clarification or further elaboration. These moments often arose when an instructor or student leader introduced a humorous comment that prompted renewed focus and collective sensemaking. For example, in Group 1 during Activity 2, the instructor, Corey, stopped by a group’s table and jokingly asked a student, “Why is that throwing up into a beaker?” while pointing to an ambiguous drawing. The group immediately burst into laughter. This humorous prompt led Justin, the student who drew the figure, to explain his idea more clearly, after which others elaborated on their own drawings. Corey’s lighthearted question, framed playfully but tied to the task, successfully directed the group’s attentio n toward clarification and refinement of their work.

Transitioning from off-task to on-task The on-task/silly frame also acted as a bridge between off-task conversation and renewed task engagement. In these cases, humor created a smooth transition that reconnected students with the activity without a disciplinary shift.

An instance of this occurred in Group 3 during Activity 1. The group had been chatting about their upcoming college classes, an off-task/serious frame, when Jose, a student leader, jokingly asked, “So how’s it going?” with a smile and laugh. The group responded with laughter, and Jill and Grace (2 students in the group) playfully replied, “We expanded it even more,” gesturing toward their equation. The humor reoriented the group to the task, moving them into an on-task/silly frame. When Jose left, the group gradually drifted back to offtask/serious talk. In this case, humor initiated by a peer leader served as a short-lived but effective mechanism for transitioning from social conversation back to focused engagement. Epistemic distancing A third role of the on-task/silly frame involved epistemic distancing, a process described by Conlin and Scherr

(2018) in which students use hedging, quoting, or joking to soften their stance and protect themselves from

potential disagreement or error. Within this study, students often used humor to propose uncertain ideas without fully committing to them, allowing exploration without social or intellectual risk. For instance, in Group 2 during Activity 1, Herb, a student, examined the group’s equation on a computer and suggested simplifying it. Speaking in a sarcastic tone, he turned his uncertainty into a joke, prompting laughter from his peers. This humorous delivery allowed him to test an idea while signaling that he was not overly attached to its correctness. The group briefly moved into an off-task/silly frame before returning to an on-task/serious 1714 intellectual risk-taking through epistemic distancing.

Relieving tension Finally, the on-task/silly frame frequently served to relieve tension that arose from uncomfortable social or instructional moments. Humor provided a way for students to ease interpersonal pressure and restore a positive group atmosphere, often enabling renewed participation. A representative example occurred in Group 2 during Activity 2. Corey, the instructor, encouraged everyone to contribute to the group’s CO₂-cycle diagram, indirectly prompting Herb, a student, who had been less involved, to take part. The group fell silent  as Herb begandrawing, and the atmosphere became tense. After a brief pause, Brett looked over the table, laughed softly, and asked, “What is that?” prompting group-wide laughter. This playful exchange shifted the group into the on-task/silly frame, diffusing the tension and reestablishing a comfortable dynamic. Herb visibly relaxed and began explaining his drawing in more detail, transforming a potentially uncomfortable moment into renewed engagement These findings demonstrate that humor and silliness were not peripheral to learning but integral to how students managed social relations, negotiated uncertainty, and sustained engagement during inquiry-based group work. The on-task/silly frame, in particular, functioned as a productive space where students balanced playfulness with intellectual effort, blurring traditional boundaries between “serious” science and social collaboration. Interactions between the dimensions of seriousness and task-oriented epistemological framing revealed that silliness often coincided with constructive cognitive and social work. The identification of four distinct roles of the on-task/silly frame – (1) directing attention to an area that needs expanding or clarification, (2) transitioning from off-task talk to task engagement, (3) epistemic distancing, and (4) relieving tension – illustrates the diverse functions of humor in sustaining productive collaboration. These roles demonstrate that humor operates as a subtle mechanism through which students  manage both authority and equality in group dynamics. By using silliness strategically, students positioned themselves as competent peers and co-investigators, creating a space where scientific ideas could be explored in a relaxed and inclusive atmosphere. This work extends prior studies that have examined humor in classroom framing. Scherr and Hammer

(2007) first introduced the joking frame, describing it primarily as off-task, while Irving et al. (2013)

reconceptualized it as part of a continuous axis between seriousness and silliness, still retaining its non-productive connotation. The current study builds on and refines this framework by treating seriousness and silliness as discrete frames, enabling clearer identification of moments when humor and learning meaningfully intersect. Through independent coding of discourse and body language, this study shows how the silly frame can actively support learning by making complex tasks approachable and sustaining engagement in inquiry-based contexts.

## Significance and conclusion

The significance of this study lies in providing empirical evidence and theoretical grounding for the productive role of silliness in collaborative science learning. By examining moments when students were both on-task and silly, this research challenges the assumption that playful behavior indicates disengagement. Instead, it reveals that humor can align with epistemological framing to support sensemaking, collaboration, and identity development within groups.

This work also offers a practical framework for educators to better recognize and interpret students’ playful engagement. By identifying when students’ epistemological framing intersects with silliness, teachers candistinguish between off-task distraction and productive, affectively charged participation. Such r ecognition can help educators make informed, in-the-moment decisions about when to intervene, when to allow space for humor, and how to use these moments to reinforce learning and community. Furthermore, attention to the interplay between discourse and body language provides an additional diagnostic tool for understanding classroom dynamics. The ways students lean, gesture, smile, or change tone offer important cues about engagement and group comfort. These multimodal signals, when considered alongside discourse, can help teachers identify productive moments of play and evaluate how students are positioning themselves relative to the task and to one another. In this way, attention to the on-task/silly frame contributes not only to research on epistemological framing but also to pedagogical practice aimed at cultivating classrooms where students develop scientific identities and collaborative competencies through humor, play, and shared inquiry.
