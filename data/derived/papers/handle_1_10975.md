# Using Teacher Feedback to Refine an Educational Video Game Evaluation Model

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As part of an ongoing project, our research team worked with three social studies teachers to refine the Teacher Lens for Digital Relevance (TLDR) model for evaluating educational video games (EVGs). We found that the model was useful to teachers for focusing their attention on important aspects of EVGs. Our findings suggest that the TLDR model is a promising tool for helping teachers but may benefit from further refinements to enhance its usability and adoption.

## Background

Research shows that video games can benefit learnin g. However, Foster and Shah (2020) reported that teachers would like easier ways to find and incorporate good  EVGs, noting the need for systematic approaches to  aid in this goal. One solution: researchers and practitioners collabo rate on models for evaluating educational video games (EVGs). We designed the Teacher Lens for Digital Relevance (TL DR) model to evaluate EVGs based on three aspects: (1) player experience, (2) design fo r learning, and (3) culturally responsive instructi on (CRI). We worked with three social studies teachers to address the question: Would teachers use the TLDR model, and what factors affect this decision?

## Theoretical perspectives

The TLDR model starts with the premise that EVGs sh ould be engaging for the players and offer a techni cally functional learning experience (Ibrahim, 2012). In addition, EVGs should have well-designed learning opportunities embedded in the gameplay itself. Gee (2005) outlined three broad areas where games can become engines for learning: (1) empowering learners, (2) offering problem-solving opportunities, and  (3) promoting deeper understanding. The TLDR model incorporates these learning opportunities in its evaluation criteria. CRI, based on work by Ladson-Billings (1995), involves creating inclusive learni ng environments that validate and incorporate students’ diverse experien ces and perspectives. While CRI has been recognized  as an important consideration for teachers (Powell e t al., 2016), models for game evaluation often over look it. At the same time, researchers note that EVGs and the ways they address diversity, equity, and inclusion significantly impact the students playing them (Acosta & Denham, 2018). The TLDR model explicitly uses a CRI lens. Finally, the Technology Acceptance Model (Davis, 19 89), or TAM, has been widely used to evaluate how likely people are to adopt technology. Despite ongoing debates about the predictive value of this model (Marangunić & Granić, 2015), it is generally regarded as effective for ed tech (Scherer & Teo, 2019).

## Methods

The case reported in this paper is part of a larger, ongoing design-based research (Barab & Squire, 2014) project. We are developing a model for teachers to evaluate EVGs for their playability, learning opportunities,  and CRI (Duvall et al., 2023). The prior phase of the proje ct demonstrated the potential of the TLDR model for  EVGs in general. For this paper, we report on the second ph ase of our study: understanding whether educators f ind the model useful and easy to use.

Three social studies teachers participated in the p roject: a female teacher from the US with 16 years of experience, a male teacher from the US with three years of experience, and a female teacher from China with two years of experience. We selected A Cheyenne Odyssey: Westward Expansion  from the Mission US organization as our exemplar game because it is part of an award-winning educational media project (Mission US, 2021).  This game addresses national transformation and conflict s between white settlers and indigenous tribes in the US in the 1860s and 1870s, as players take the role of a Cheyenne boy. The participants independently played the game and recorded their responses to the TLDR model. Then they responded to a TAM survey indicating their likelihood of using the TLDR model in their ongoing practice. We also conducted pre-and post-interviews via Zoom. The TLDR model has three sections, as follows: (a) Test Play, after 5-10 minutes of gameplay, has eight questions about the game’s playability and function ality; (b) Deep Play, after 30 minutes-1 hour of gameplay, has thirteen questions about the learning opportuni ties in the game; and  (c) Reflective Evaluation, thinking holistically about the game, has eight questions about cultural relevance in the game. The TAM survey had twelve questions, with six targe ting perceived usefulness (PU) and six targeting perceived ease of use (PEU). The questions use a 7-point Likert scale. For PU and PEU, the total score provides a number between 1 and 100, with a higher score ind icating a greater likelihood of adoption. We analyz ed the results from the TAM survey (Davis, 1989; Lewis, 2019), as well as transcripts of the teacher interviews that were coded using open, axial, and selective coding (Charmaz & Thornberg, 2021).

## Results

Our participants indicated a high preference for using the model, with an average PU score of 72 (range: 9) and a PEU score of 75 (range: 33). Overall, the teachers’  comments revealed a theme of simplification. Participant 1 said, “The DEI is definitely my favorite part…that' s just a really big push right now…How are we incor porating marginalized voices in a non-token manner?” But, “When you have a model for any educator, you don't want it to be too complicated…like…20 ideas into one model…” Participant 2 noted  another way to simplify the model would be to declare “a range as to what is a good n umber” for each of the sections. Similarly, Partici pant 3 said she would recommend the model but more as a conversation starter than a tool to use exactly as prescribed.

## Discussion

The teachers recognized that the TLDR model might not be predictive due to context. Grade level, subject matter, student demographics, and more may result in a diff erent overall score for the same game. Teachers may require additional simplification, but the model was a usef ul evaluative tool to understand  how and why to use EVGs in the classrooms. The challenge to refinement will be maintaining theoretical rigor while increasing the ease of use. The next step is redesign based on this feedback and evaluate the TLDR model with a larger group of teachers.
