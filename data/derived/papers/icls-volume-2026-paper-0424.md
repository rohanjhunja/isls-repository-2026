# Infrastructuring Inquiry and Inclusion at Scale in (Re)Design of Teacher Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study explores how colleges of education can support, at scale, faculty design practices that enact values of inquiry and inclusion within (re)design of teacher education. Through longitudinal analysis of a networked improvement community (Bryk et al., 2011) of 15 colleges of education focused on integrating computational and digital literacies, we explore diverse approaches utilized by network organizers to infrastructure (Penuel, 2019) a culture of inquiry and inclusion in teacher education (re)design.

Through longitudinal analysis of 15 colleges of education focused on integrating computational and digital literacies (CDLs), this study explores how colleges of education can support, at scale, faculty design practices that enact values of inquiry (formal  processes of utilizing evidence) and inclusion (formal processes to include stakeholder perspectives) within (re)design of teacher education programs. We explore the variety of approaches utilized by network organizers to scale a culture of inquiry and inclusion in teacher education program design.

## Literature review

Scholarship has established the importance of both utilizing evidence-based approaches (Goldhaber, 2018) as well as of incorporating perspectives from a variety of stakeholders (Warr et al., 2023) in the design of teacher preparation. This inclusive approa ch is in line with the principles of liberatory design laid out in the National Equity Project, such as increasing agency for those involved in systems change initiatives (NEP n.d.). However, few teacher education efforts seek to understand how these values of inquiry and inclusion might be enacted at scale within the context of ambitious instructional reform. Following Coburn (2003) we conceptualize the scale of institutional change initiatives across multiple dimensions: shifts in ownership, deepening of instructional practice, spread across a network, and sustainability over time. Our study roots itself in the theoretical perspectives of infrastructuring within education; “activities that aim to redesign components, relations, and routines of schools and districts that influence what takes place in classrooms” (Penuel, 2019, p. 1), highlighting various efforts undertaken by network organizers to create new infrastructural supports related to inquiry and inclusion within teacher education design.

This study’s guiding research question is: How might values of inquiry and inclusion be supported at scale through infrastructuring practices focused on the (re)design of teacher education programs? To address the question, we explore the Computing Integra ted Teacher Education initiative at the City University of New York, a multiyear institutional change effort to spread and scale computational and digital literacy (CDLs) pedagogies across 15 colleges of education. We analyze particular infrastructures that network leaders enacted across four years to support inquiry and inclusion, seeking to understand how these infrastructural interventions evolved over time. Specifically, network leaders designed a constellation of over 10 infrastructuring touchpoints which were analyzed along three dimensions: the major activities stakeholders engaged in and the resulting impact to institutional change vis-a-vis teacher education (re)design efforts, the values enacted in the infrastructuring intervention (e.g. inquiry and/or inclusion), and the timeframe of the infrastructuring intervention. A longitudinal analysis of the infrastructuring process reveals that the lifecycle of infrastructure is dynamic rather than static, responding to initial needs of an institutional change project, evolving as those needs shift, and in some cases sunsetting when those needs are resolved. See Table 1 for a detailed view of these dynamic shifts. Initially, the network had two central needs: network-level imperatives focused on establishing a highlevel vision of “what counted” as valued, equitable CDL integration, and faculty-level needs around building 2701 capacity for designing meaningful CDL instruction. Inclusive inquiry practices were leveraged towards those ends. For example, to meet the network level-need of equitable CDL integration, network leaders conducted landscape reviews to understand implications of CDLs for teaching and learning in higher education as well as to create guidance documents around an equitable vision for CDL integration in teacher education. To meet the faculty-level needs around capacity building for implementing meaningful CDL-related instruction, faculty were able to receive structured feedback on their teacher candidate-facing activities and assignments as well as to apply for research grant funding to study the implementation of their own teaching lessons. In later years these needs had evolved to include a greater focus on supporting strategic planning efforts around comprehensive instructional change in teacher preparation programs including in students’ clinical fieldwork experiences. The initiative created new structures to help network leaders and stakeholders engage in inclusive inquiry practices towards those ends. These intervention touchpoints include student feedback mechanisms (common experience surveys and focus group protocols based on co-generative dialogues (Noble, Powietrzynska, & Figueroa, 2025) designed to bring other stakeholder voices into the program redesign process) and a clinical pilot that supported collective inquiry around how to provide students with opportunities to practice their computational and digital literacy skills in the field. At each stage, network leaders grappled with challenges collaboratively with faculty, promoting collective ownership over the work. Furthermore, faculty’s inquiry projects were spread through multiple channels that celebrated and communicated their findings including annual research fairs, network retreats, and a community summit. Taken together, faculty had robust opportunities to deepen their instructional practice.

The findings demonstrate how intentional partnership between network leaders and network members can create a culture of inquiry and inclusion that is both responsive to and generative for evolving instructional systems change work. Critically, it finds that any individual structure was not valuable in and of itself, but rather that different structures might be introduced, iterated, or sunsetted in response to contextual needs. Additionally, the full poster will detail concrete, replicable levers, such as participatory framework development and inclusive student feedback mechanisms, that can help institutionalize values of inquiry and inclusion at scale.
