# Investigating Melting And Freezing: How Different Scalar Levels Of Mechanistic Reasoning Look In Modeling-Based Learning In Kindergarten Science

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Modeling has been recognized as a crucial element of science education. Following research on using Modeling-based Learning (MbL) in kindergarten science education with an emphasis of mechanistic reasoning, this case study provides detailed descriptions of the implementation of MbL with a group of 21 kindergarten children studying (a) ice cube melting and (b) water freezing. Data included transcribed lessons and child-developed models. We coded the discourse data for mechanistic reasoning, and child-constructed models using artifact analysis. Findings suggest that participating kindergarten children (a) can think productively across various scalar levels of mechanistic reasoning, although support to move across these levels is necessary; (b) working in a step-by-step process of progressive MbL is crucial for children before moving to lower scalar levels of reasoning; and (c) multiple ways of analyzing the data from children of these ages provide better insights into the MbL process.

## Introduction

While modeling has been recognized as a central practice to both science and engineering (e.g., Grandy & Duschl, 2007; Nersessian & Patton, 2009), a growing number of studies have advocated for the effectiveness of modelingbased learning (MbL) as a productive approach to te aching and learning science (e.g., Clement, 2008; G ilbert & Justi, 2016; Halloun, 2016; Jackson et al., 2008; O sborne, 2014; Schwarz et al., 2009; Windschitl et a l., 2008; Baumfalk et al., 2018; Tobin et al, 2018; Zangori, et al, 2017; Acher et al., 2007; Lehrer & Schauble,  2010; Schwarz & White, 2005). This approach provides learners with opportunities that may enable them to  construct and communicate theories and ideas using a variety of representational means (e.g., Lehrer & Schauble,  2006; Passmore & Stewart, 2002; Schwarz et al., 2009; Wil ensky & Reisman, 2006). MbL is a “multi-phase process” during which learners construct and subsequently us e models “as explanatory tools for sense-making and engagement in model-based reasoning” (Zangori et al., 2017, p. 1250).  When learners engage in this process, the learner-constructed models become tools for learning, enabl ing a comprehensive exploration and understanding of the underlying mechanisms of natural phenomena (Schwarz et al., 2009). To create models, learners must effectively transfo rm and integrate their observations, experiences, knowledge, and theories into tangible representations. The MbL cycle starts with observations of the phenomenon under study, and then lead to the construction of m odel(s) representing the phenomenon. Then, the lear ners evaluate these models, which in turn drive the identification of any revision possibly requiring further observations and/or revisions of the initial model.

Recognizing modeling as a crucial element of scienc e education, the National Research Council [NRC]

(2013) suggests that learning science should encomp ass understanding natural phenomena through model

construction and refinement (also see Louca & Zacharia, 2015). This approach includes grasping the processes of model development and refinement (also see Louca et al., 2011a; White & Frederiksen, 1998). Despite its value, and an increasing number of stud ies of MbL in higher education, middle school, and less in elementary school science education (e.g., Baumfalk et al., 2018), a clear understanding of Mb L's role in kindergarten science education remains vague. Additionally, modeling-based reasoning and practices are often insufficiently emphasized in school science educati on, particularly in younger grades (Forbes et al., 2015; NRC, 2012; Schwarz et al., 2009; Windschitl et al., 2008). Considering the complexity of these practices, it's reasonable to wonder about the engagement of kindergarten chil dren in similar practices. Implementing MbL in these early years necessitates supporting teachers, developing appropriate curriculum materials, and understanding  what Louca & Zacharia (2023) referred to as the young children’s MbL resources.

## Theoretical framework

This study explores the implementation of MbL with kindergarten children. The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2022) proposed that the development and utilization of models, along with t he construction of explanations for physical  phenomena, should be integral components of early s cience education. Additionally, child-constructed models can be perceived as tools throug h which children externalize their ideas about natural phenomena, allowing them to communica te their scientific thinking by generating, testing, and revising models. These child-constructed models can take diverse forms, including everyday language, drawings, diagrams, gestures, dramatic play, 3-D structures, and computer simulations (Giere, 1990; Schwarz et al., 2009; Windschitl et al., 2008; NASEM, 2022; NRC, 2012). M odel development necessitates the representation of elements relevant to the phenomenon and the corresp onding underlying mechanism s that dictate how these elements interact, thereby yielding the observed phenomenon (NRC, 2012). MbL involves the process of constructing models dur ing which learning occurs. Rather than solely focusing on the model as the end product, MbL empha sizes the modeling processes and journey. It includes the development, revision, use, and evaluation of model s aimed at predicting and explaining physical pheno mena (e.g., Louca & Zacharia, 2012). Externalized in various ways, a learner-generated model becomes a tool that enables learners to understand how a phenomenon occurs and to make predictions about it (Schwarz et al., 2009). Thus, MbL extends science learning beyond acquiring  facts by engaging learners in reconstructing the mechanisms underlying natural phenomena (NRC, 2012; Schwarz, Passmore, & Reiser, 2017; Krist et al., 2019). NASEM (2022) suggests that developing and using models and constructing explanations should be part of science learning from early grades. NASEM (2022)  points out that pre-school children exhibit substantial representational proficiencies within the contex t of MbL, possessing what Louca & Zacharia  (2023) called modeling resources from areas such as free play (ma nipulating objects), storytelling (describing seque nces of events), and drawing (making, recognizing, and interpreting representational choices and intentions) (DeLoache, 2004).

Louca & Zacharia (2015) undertook a detailed analysis of learner mod eling processes, comparing how young modelers work during MbL. They reported that young modelers, including kindergarteners and elementary students, follow similar practices but with distinc tions. Developing a model involves decomposing the phenomenon, identifying components, and synthesizin g—requiring learners to mentally combine content for the model to take shape. Krist et al. (2019) also highl ighted the transition of learners between di fferent scalar levels of mechanistic reasoning, showing more advanced des criptions of mechanistic reasoning in lower scalar levels, which is directly related to modeling-based reasoning when children develop and revise models. Being central to scientific work (e.g., Machamer, D arden, & Craver,  2000), a significant part of MbL includes representing the mechanism underlying a ph enomenon. This process evokes  and facilitates the development of mechanistic reasoning in children. I n the context of MbL, mechanistic reasoning is defined as explaining how factors interact to result in a phenomenon (Louca et al, 2011a; Machamer et al., 2000; Russ et al., 2008; Springer & Keil, 1991). Mechanistic reasoning is crucial in construc ting scientific explanations of natural phenomena (Krist et al.,  2019), providing theoretical accounts of the regula rities behind empirical observations. Ke and Schwarz (2019) argued that the construction of models that include the underlying mechanism(s) should be considered a “productive epistemic consideration that is aligned to the values of both disciplinary science and the classroom knowledge building  community” (Ke & Schwarz, 2019, p. 184). Developing  such models is, of course, both challenging and demanding, primarily f or young learners (Louca & Zacharia, 2015). Researchers have argued that the role of the teachers heavily i nfluences learners’ development of causal models (e.g., Khan, 2011; Manz & Renga, 2017).

Purpose Following an inquiry learning approach using models  (Seel, 2017), grounded in social constructivism th at encourages children to create, evaluate, and discus s their models, fostering a deeper understanding (L in et al., 2018; Gilbert & Justi, 2016), this case study aims to contribute to MbL research in kindergarten science education.

The study investigates the implementation of MbL with a group of 21 kindergarten children engaged in the study of (a) ice cube melting and (b) water freezing. With an emphasis on  the representation of the mechanism of the phenomenon (Baumfalk et al, 2018), we analyzed evidence to describe how different scalar levels of mechanistic reasoning (Krist et al, 2019) look in MbL in kindergarten science.  We use this evidence to argue that participating kindergarten children (a) can think productively across various scalar levels of mechanistic reasoning (Krist et al, 2019), although support to move across these levels  is necessary; (b)  working in step-by-step process of progressive MbL is crucial for children before moving to lower scalar levels of reasoning; and (c) multiple ways of analyzing the data provide better insights into the MbL process. Design and procedures Following a descriptive case study approach (Yin, 2017), this study involved a group of 21 kindergarten children (ages 5-6) in Cyprus, taught by the second author at a publ ic kindergarten school. The teacher participated in  a professional development (PD) program for MbL in ki ndergarten science organized by the first author ov er the school year. The teacher developed the unit through  a series of online bi-weekly meetings and data-driven reflections (video and photos of children's work). All lessons (about 40 minutes each) were videotaped  and transcribed for analysis, and children's work was c ollected for analysis. For this study, we adopted t he drawingbased modeling approach where children use annotate d drawings to represent their models (Fretz et al.,  2002). We analyzed child-developed models (artifact analysis) and their presentation and discussion (discourse analysis) to provide rich, detailed descriptions of the characteristics of children’s MbL engagement. Our analysis of MbL discourse employed two compleme ntary approaches. The first analysis used transcribed children’s conversations to explore their thinking and learning experiences (e.g., Criswell et al., 2021; Tang et al.,  2021; Sandoval et al.,  2021). We used discourse to develop detailed accoun ts of the context and content of MbL conversations. To support this analysis, we also coded the discourse data using Russ et al.’s (2008) coding scheme of seven components of mechanistic reasoning (see codes in Table 1). ## Findings

We start the presentation of the findings, by detailed overviews of each of the two case studies and then turn into a discussion about common emerging themes. The desc riptions of the findings below seek to cover the complete unit as it was enacted by the teacher and the children, seeking to give a detailed picture of how the children reached their final model and then use that as a tool for further learning. ## Discussion

The case study investigated the implementation of MbL with a group of 21 kinder garten children engaged in the investigation of (a) an ice cube melting (sub-case 1) and (b) water freezing (sub-case 2). With an emphasis on the representation and explanation of the mechanism underlying the phenomenon (Baumfalk et al, 2018), we analyzed evidence to describe the variation of mechanistic reasoning in MbL in kindergarten science.  Our study’s findings align with Krist et al. (2019), suggesting that lea rners as young as kindergarten  can think across various scalar levels. For example, children’s model 1.3 represent s a scalar level lower than their observations of t he target phenomenon (the size of the ice cube becoming small er) at the substances level (mass and weight). Mode l 1.4 represents an even lower scalar level of mechanistic reasoning, at the molecular level, describing physical objects (water molecules), physical entities (water molecules in motion when detached from the ice cube), and interactions between physical objects and entities representing the melting process. Although used to represent the  opposite phenomenon, model 2.3 corresponds to similar scalar  level as model 1.3, and models 2.4 and 2.5 corresp ond to similar scalar level as model 1.4.

Our study, however, adds useful insights to Krist et al.’s (2019) concept of mechan istic reasoning structured in scalar levels. Firstly, we suggest th at analyzing mechanistic reasoning with a single an alysis (e.g., scalar levels) provides only a partial understanding of children’s reasoning. Multiple analyses, including those of the components of mechanistic reasoning (Russ et al., 2008) and child-constructed models (Louca et al., 2011b), better support the findings by providing a better detailed picture of the content of the mechanistic discussions that took place in the classroom. Secondly, we propose t hat children as young as kindergarten can also thin k across various scalar levels, although support is clearly necessary. This aligns with Krist et al. (2019), wh o suggest that thinking across scalar levels is a nontrivial task,  especially for kindergarten children, who must fir st identify the need to include and describe nonvisible physical entities and interactions to explain underlying mechanisms. Our findings shows that although capable of reasoning about the mechanism in various scalar levels, this was not done automatically, simply because the children were abl e to develop a model (for water cooling) for a simi lar phenomenon in a previous unit (i ce melting). Support may include providing children with modelin g tools to assist in transitioning to lower scalar levels of mechanistic reasoning, linking their work with experiences of similar phenomena, and prompting specific thinking directions as we described in this study. For insta nce, the need for a dynamic model showing changes in physica l entities over time in sub-case 1 prompted the use of a different modeling medium (theatrical play) that supported developing a model in a lower scalar level. Thirdly, the development of models of higher scalar levels of mechanistic reasoning is a crucial step for children before moving to lower scalar levels. This  agrees with Wilensky & Resnick (1999), who suggest ed that thinking in levels is crucial for building explanat ions step by step. Louca & Zacharia (2023) also highlighted the role of higher scalar level child-constructed models in helping children understand t he observations of the phenomenon first, and then develop the mechanism ca using the observed changes. We concur with Krist et  al.

(2019) that these are important steps in MbL. Fourt hly, although models 1.4 and 2.5 have similar scala r depth in

terms of mechanistic reasoning, they address differ ent aspects of the phenomenon (molecular structure vs. temperature-related behavior). We contend that investigating th e opposite phenomenon through MbL supported the children in creating additional models of simil ar scalar level that included new ideas/concepts/en tities not previously considered. We suggest that future inves tigations focus on how to support this more complex mechanistic thinking across scalar levels in kindergarten children.
