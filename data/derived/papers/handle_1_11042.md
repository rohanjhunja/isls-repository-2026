# “We Appreciate That Reviewers Pointed Out This”: Exploring Knowledge Co-construction in the Process of Peer Review

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Knowledge co-construction within the academic peer review process is an area yet unexplored empirically. This study analyzed peer review reports. Preliminary findings reveal that Negotiating Meaning is the most frequent phase, contrary to previous assertions of dominance by lower complexity thinking phases in knowledge co-construction. This analysis of expert dialogues offers insights that could enhance student peer review processes.

## Introduction

Knowledge co-construction is a collaborative process involving i ndividuals not just sharing their knowledge but also collectively examining and refining each other’s ideas, which leads to enhanced collective understanding and knowledge building (Scardamalia, 2002). The essence  of knowledge co-construction lies in its interactive nature (Chapman, 2013). As discussed by the social constru ctivist theory, learning is more effective when stu dents can actively exchange ideas and negotiate meanings with their peers (Scardamalia, 2002). Prior research has underscored the value of student peer review as an effective strategy for facilitating a productive knowledge co-construction process. For instance, Reddy et al. (2 021) shed light on how peer review improved student knowledge. However, the efficacy of student peer review in fostering knowledge improvement has been a subject of debate. According to Alqassab  et al. (2018), domain knowledge is a crucial limit ing factor in the effectiveness of student peer review. The gap in knowledge and skills between novice and expert reviewers often leads to skepticism about the value of student feedback. In light of these concerns, examining the academic journal review process, in other words, the interactions between expert reviewers, could offer valuable insights. By analyzing the knowledge co-construction process in these expert dialogues, we can glean bes t practices that could be adapted to enhance the ef fectiveness of student peer review and structured peer review training. To the best of our knowledge, there is no empirical evidence investigating knowledge co-construction in academic journal peer-review comments and responses. This paper aimed to bridge this gap by offering empirica l evidence to determine whether peer review charact erizes knowledge co-construction.

## Methods

This research follows the qualitative research case  study paradigm, collecting peer review reports from a peerreviewed journal and analyzing the data following the framework of k nowledge co-construction modified from Gunawardena et al. (1997), as shown in Table 1. The  coding framework was pilot-tested by two co-first authors, and they coded the studies independently. Any disag reements were resolved as a group until a satisfyin g interrater reliability score was achieved. ## Results

In this pilot study, we identified 5 phases of know ledge co-construction in the peer review proc ess. As shown in Fig. 1, the Negotiating Meaning phase occurs most frequently (n = 150), followed by the Sharing or Adding phase (n = 77) and the Applying New Knowledge phase (n = 38). In contrast,  the Building Consensus and Identifying Inconsistency phases are less frequent in the peer review process. There is an imbalance in participation in the knowl edge co-construction. Reviewers contribute more to the early phase of knowledge co-construction, such as sharing or adding (75.3%), id entifying inconsistency (81.5%), and negotiating meaning (48.7%). On the other hand, authors tend to put more effort into the latter phase of knowledge co-construction, such as negotiating meaning (51.3%), building consensus (92%), and applying new knowledge (100%).

## Discussion and conclusion

In our analysis of peer review comments and respons es, we discerned five phases of knowledge co-construction within this process. This finding contrasted with e arlier empirical studies, which suggested that lowe r levels of complex thinking (i.e., the first or second phases) predominated in knowledge co-construction (Lucas et al., 2014). Our study revealed that the majority of operations we coded were concentrated in the third of these on e-on-one expert dialogues. Typically, reviewers generously s hared their concerns and offered solutions to authors. When authors concurred with these suggestions, a consensus was reached, leading to the application of new knowledge. Conversely, in instances of disagreement, authors e mployed various strategies to challenge and rebut reviewers' comments. However, consensus was not achieved in a few cases, possibly due to data unavailability or other cases of force majeure, despite authors acknowledging the value of reviewers' suggestions. We acknowledge the presence of biases and limitatio ns in our research due to the small size of our dataset, survivor bias, and the constraints inheren t in analyzing written text. In future studies, we aim to expand our dataset and further investigate the p atterns and dynamics of knowledge co-construction in this expert-driven collective discourse.
