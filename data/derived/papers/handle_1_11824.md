# Justice-Centered Environmental Learning: A Design Tool

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Climate change and other environmental harms disproportionately impact communities of color and low-income communities, and therefore represent existential social justice issues for science classrooms to address. Although learning scientists and science educators have theorized sociopolitical dimensions of environmental learning, there are few practical tools for justice-centered design. To this end, we developed a design tool that consists of four environmental justice lenses: address positionality, engage multiple ways of knowing, analyze structures of oppression, and engage in justice-focused action. We illustrate these lenses with vignettes from an institute on air quality for in-service teachers. We discuss the potential of this tool to guide environmental learning and inform future research.

## Introduction

The impacts of climate change and other environment al stressors are disproportionately concentrated in  low-income communities and communities of color (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, 2019). Climate and environmental education are therefore essential to our collective wellbeing and to building a more jus t world. Learning scientists and science educators increasin gly express the need to address climate and environ mental learning from a justic e-focused perspective. A growing body of research sho ws how science classrooms can address issues of social and environmental justice (Morales-Doyle, 2017). Recent work has focused specifically on climate change, calling for the politicization o f climate education (Iyer & Brett, 2024)  and offering commitments to climate justice teaching (Nxumalo & Montes, 2021). While systematic reviews of research on climate and environmental education have identified  effective interventions, such as deliberative disc ussions or implementing school projects (Monroe et al., 2019), new forms of climate and environmental learning that center sociopolitical perspectives are needed. For example, some instructional designs engage students in col lecting and/or publicly sharing quantitative data on geospa tial factors, representing the st ance that democratizing data production and empowering students to be knowledge creators is a form of justice-focused learning (Van Wart et al., 2022). We agree that such engagement is powerf ul, and also emphasize the need for students to exp licitly address the sociopolitical dimensions of environmental inequities and science learning.

In this paper, we build on existing literature to o ffer a concrete design framework for climate and environmental justice learning that centers sociopo litical perspectives. Our learning designs are deve loped in collaboration with community-based environmen tal justice organizations and focus on local climat e and environmental phenomena. Within these designs, we engage four literature-based lenses that we argue are central to climate and environmental justice learning: addr ess positionality, engage multiple ways of knowing,  analyze structures of oppression, and engage in justice-focused action. We illustrate these lenses with examples from our professional learning design on air quality for in-service teachers and reflect on the affordances and limitations of the tool for supporting justice-focused climate and environmental learning.

## Theoretical framework

We adopt the stance of others who have called for s cience education not to merely transmit knowledge, but to work towards social and political transformation (Morales-Doyle, 2017; Mutegi, 2011). Socially transformative learning includes not only mastery of science content, but also critical examination of the limitations of traditional forms of scientific knowledge, which uphold colonial structures, center Western and European ways of knowing, foster extractive relationships with land, and concentrate environmental harms in minoritized communities. Thus, socially transformative curriculum aims to build awareness of the current limitations of scientific knowledge and the historical, systemic causes of current inequities.

Socially transformative science education is supported by investigating local climate and environmental phenomena (Meyer et al., 2024). Centering community knowledge and local datasets,  both qualitative and quantitative, represents a deviation from tradition al science education, which often focuses on large climate datasets that have been stripped from their place, culture, and context. Focusing on local phenomena s upports scientific sensemaking and can instill a sense of c ommitment to and pride in one’s community and its h istory. Deeply exploring local issues allows students to look at similarities and differences across other local contexts, or engage in “translocal sensemaking” (Meyer et al., 2 024). In doing so, they can comprehend the deeply s ituated nature of climate and environmental phenomena as a foundation for understanding the global context of change. Importantly, addressing local issues also allows students to develop a sense of agency and to engage i n transformative action in solidarity with others from their communities. In our work, we address local issues through collaboration with community-based environmental and climate justice organizations. We draw elements fro m Social Design-Based Experiments (Gutiérrez & Jurow,

2016) to reorganize activity systems in ways that center the experiences and knowledge of our community

partners. These partnerships create new learning ecologies (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016) that connect teachers (and by extension, their students) to the work of environmental justice activists. Such engagement allows t eachers to critically reflect on current environmental inequities, envision more just futures, and think together about concrete actions they can take. Importantly, we co-design our teacher learning experiences with our co mmunity partners to center their experiences, questions, and concerns. In the case of this particular learning design, we focus on the local environmental issue of air quality, with a focus on the clustering of p olluting industries in a community of color and the resultant health impacts. Rather than  center our design on specific STEM ideas associate d with climate change, like the carbon cycle and carbon fo otprints, we focus on justice goals, including examining our own relationship to the issue, examining diverse forms of evidence, analyzing the structural and historical factors that caused the inequities, and advocating to individuals and organizations in power. Environmental justice lenses We draw from a range of literatures, including lear ning sciences, science education, and teacher educa tion, to articulate a set of justice principles. In our desi gn tool (see Table 1), we frame these principles as  a set of lenses that we apply to local iss ues in collaboration with our community partners. T he first justice lens is acknowledge positionality, which asks that individuals locate themselves histo rically in the work of environmental justice and education to recognize the limitations of their kno wledge. It is crucial to acknowledge the wide-ranging relationships to environmental issues that differen t learners might have, including the extent to whic h they are impacted by environmental harms and/or are complici t in creating them. The modern environme ntal movement originated within colonial projects that center Whi teness (Curnow & Helferty, 2018). In California, John Muir and the larger conservation movement were responsible for forcibly removing indigenous peoples from their land and forwarded the idea that wilderness should be “protected” leaving it untouched, thus erasing the longstanding indigenous stewardship practices that supported ecosyste m health. The modern environmental movement continues to be racialized, largely shaped by White  leadership and European colonial ideologies. The l ived experiences of impacted groups are often not recogn ized in these spaces. As a result, environmental and c limate solutions may be imposed by outsiders in ways that do not reflect frontline communities' interests and goals, thus undermining their self-determination.

An individual’s relationship to environmental issue s is connected to their intersectional identities, including—but not limited to —racial and ethnic identities, socioeconomic status,  gender, documentation status, educational background, ability, and language practices (Annamma & Winn, 2019). These different identities also impact the extent to which their personal experienc es and ways of knowing are represented and valued in STEM spaces (Nasir & De Royston, 2013). Teachers who address climate and environmental is sues in their instruction must consider their own positionality and that of t heir students. For example, a White teacher who is new to the community in which they teach may need to work to u nderstand how issues of social justice intersect with local environmental phenomena and to make space for community perspectives. A teacher of color who grew up in the community where they teach may still experience pri vilege based on socioeconomic status, educational background, and other identities that influence their  experiences of climate and environmental phenomena  and sense of belonging in STEM spaces.

The second justice lens is honor multiple ways of knowing. Many STEM practices are critical for climate and environmental learning, such as analysis and interpretation of quantitative data, argumentation from evidence, causal explanation, and predictive modeling. At the  same time, there is a clear limit to the e xtent to which “technical” knowledge can address a socioscientific  issue like climate change (Morales-Doyle, 2023), which requires political solutions that are tailored to t he social and cultural context. Thus, there is an u rgent need to “desettle” the tightly controlled boundaries of acc epted meaning-making practices in STEM, which often marginalize the diverse ways of knowing of racialized communities—including Black, Indigenous, and People of Color (BIPOC)—particularly when addressing issues at the intersection of nature and culture (Bang et al., 2013). Traditional Eurocentric science eschews many forms of knowledge that are central to individuals’ every day sensemaking about the world, including embodied and  relational knowledge, intuition, emotion, and narr ative styles. Many of these forms of knowledge represent valued ways of knowing and being for BIPOC communities. Yet, their practices are often framed as “unscientific” in STEM learning contexts, despite b eing present in the work of professional scientists. These boundaries m arginalize BIPOC students within STEM contexts, res ulting in their disengagement from learning.

Understanding and advocating for climate and environmental injustices requires multiple ways of knowing, including those that are often not recogni zed in STEM spaces. Narrative and storytelling can be a powerful way to draw conclusions from data related to climate and environmental phenomena and construct visions of a more just future (Reigh et al., 2022). Furthermore, BIPOC communities bring specific for ms of community cultural wealth (Yosso, 2005) that are important for addressing issues of climate  and environmental justice. They bring “familial capital” in the form of cultural knowledge and community history and mem ory, which can provide a powerful form of evidence about  how climate has changed over time. They may also b ring “aspirational capital” to maintain hopes and dreams  in the face of barriers and “resistance capital” t hrough oppositional behavior that has challenged inequity; these skills are necessary to envision and work towards more just climate and environmental futures, even in the face of inevitable challenges and setbacks. Teachers may need support to identify their students’ ways of knowing  and community cultural wealth, particularly in connection to climate and environmental issues. Moreover, teachers may need support to engage these  ways of knowing alongside STEM practices in ways that uphold their value and integrity. The third justice lens is analyze structures of oppression. Socially transformative climate and environmental education must “emphasize the centrality of race and racism, gender and sexism, and the economic exploitation of capitalism in structuring society a nd schools” (Morales-Doyle, 2017, p. 1037)  Understanding environmental inequities requires understanding rac ism and classism as constituted not only in interpe rsonal interactions but also in historical structures and policies that systematically disadvantage racialized communities (Bonilla-Silva, 2017). Environmental injustices often have spatial quali ties, meaning that they are located in particular places. Critical race spatial analysis (Vélez & Solórzano, 2018)  is a method that uses maps and corresponding forms of quantitative data to show the geographic dimensions of inequities. However, quantitative data alone is insufficient for analyzing racialized  phenomena (Gillborn et al., 2018); processes of racialization require analysis of the historical decisions by ind ividuals and institutions that created and reproduc ed inequities. Thus, addressing environmental injustices requires a transdisciplinary perspective, including a clear understanding of the history of political, economic, and so cial structures. One example is redlining, a histor ical government policy that caused systematic divestment  from neighborhoods where communities of color resi ded. Studying current data about environmental inequities with attention to this racist policy helps learners to develop an understanding of how these inequities came to be.

Teachers may need considerable support to undertake  such transdisciplinary work, which is not well supported in curricula and is undermined by schools ’ disciplinary silos. Science teachers may need to develop knowledge of how historical policies connect to  current environmental realities, in addition to lea rning new pedagogical strategies to bring historical and poli tical analysis into the science classroom. Converse ly, teachers in other disciplines may need support in understand ing how STEM practices, such as the analysis of quantitative data, can inform the analysis of historical patterns related to climate and environmental issues. The fourth justice lens is take action towards more just futures. Socially transformative learning emphasizes the need for students to engage in praxi s—actions that ameliorate structures of oppression (M utegi, 2011). Action is an essential element of justice-centered environmental education; learning about injustice without opportunities for action may reinforce students’ fe elings of despair about the future (Lewandowski et al., 2024), an effect that may be particularly pronounced in im pacted communities. Students need opportunities to envision more just futures for themselves, their families, a nd their communities, and must come to see themselv es as change-makers who can impact local circumstances and policies (Morales-Doyle, 2017). Dismantling systems of oppression cannot be solitary work; by definition, it must be done in community (Curnow & Jurow, 2021). Justicefocused action must center the experiences and ways  of knowing of impacted communities, with teachers and students acting in solidarity to support these comm unities’ self-determination. Such action requires continual reflection on one’s own positionality and the limits of one’s own knowledge. For most teachers, centering action represents a di stinct shift in their approach to teaching about th e climate and environment. Although science standards and curricula often call for students to propose and evaluate solutions to environmental impacts, taking action o utside the classroom is rarely suggested by curricu la or supported by school resources. When instruction does focus on action, it often emphasizes individual and practical steps, such as recycling and reducing single-use plastics, rather than  collective or political engagement, such as advocating for policy changes (Clark et al., 2020). Even teachers who focus on collective action in t heir work with students may take a bureaucratic orientation, working within existing systems, rather than an act ivist approach that questions, reimagines, or shifts exis ting systems (Fitzmaurice & Wilkerson, 2024). Many teachers we have worked with have volunteered for environmen tal causes, but do not see themselves as leaders or organizers in this realm. Thus, teachers may need o pportunities to gradually move beyond individual ac tions towards collective advocacy.

In the design process, WO EIP staff emphasized the need to center community i n research and advocacy and forwarded a collaborative  approach to problem solving that brings all stakeholders to the table, including polluters.

Teachers’ conversations tended to focus on their po sitionalities vis-a-vis a range of climate and environmental issues. Multiple ways of knowing: Personal experiences and stories as data In the walking tour, Bryan, a co-founder of WOEIP, emphasized the importance of not only quantitative measurements but also personal experiences and stories for understanding environmental inequities. In one story, he described how another co-founder of the community organization, Ms. Margaret, challenged the data presented by a scientist that showed that air quality in their neighborhood was within acceptable ranges. She shared that she saw black soot on her car each morning, but the reg ulatory sensor was too far from her home to detect this pollution. WOEIP later hired residents of the neigh borhood to walk around with backpack monitors to co llect quantitative evidence of the poor air quality that they experienced in their daily lives. Throughout the tour, Bryan also asked the teachers to observe people and the a ctivities they were engaging in (e.g., “Who shares this space? What are they doing here?”). For example, they observed a daycare next to a foundry and discussed the impact of toxic emissions on the children playing outside. Im portantly, Bryandrew attention to the land-use decisions that resulted in this situation, rather than treat them as a coincidental occurrence, foreshadowing the att ention to structures of oppression later in the institute.

In the subsequent walking data collection on the un iversity campus on Day 2, teachers were asked to collect not only quantitative data on air quality b ut also qualitative observations of land use and pe ople’s experiences and stories. They were specifically  asked to consider “What would Bryan say?”, bringing  his perspective to their observations. One group attend ed closely to people’s stories as they collected qu antitative data. They took measurements at the university haza rdous waste facility, which was loc ated near a gym and exposed athletes to toxic emissions. When a middle-aged Black worker approached them to ask what they were doing, they took the opportunity to pose a number o f questions, such as how safe he feels in his job, if he thinks that he is exp osed to dangerous substances, and whether he has su fficient access to health care. He seemed somewhat reluctant to share his experiences, giving  only short answers. The group later asked similar questions to a group of White contractors working near a manh ole who more readily shared examples and stories. T he teachers discussed that the stories of impacted ind ividuals might be more valuable than quantitative d ata. They also connected the difference in responses to the different positionalities of the people they encountered, this time making specific reference to racial identities; they thought that the Black worker likely had strong opinions about workplace safety, but might not feel safe to expres s his views. In doing so, they recognized how inter sectional identity can impact what information can be gathered about environmental phenomena. Structures of oppression: Using historical texts to explore the unequal present In the “historical texts” inquiry activity on Day 3, teachers were asked to explore a question related to air quality in small groups (e.g., construction of stationary a nd mobile sources of air pollution, impact of air q uality on health). They explored a wide range of texts from different disciplines–including modern maps, past and present news articles, and historical and current data from  social and natural science sources –that described city-wide patterns of social inequality and disproportionate pollution burdens. Teachers overlaid excerpts from the texts on the redlining map, using a spatial framework to connect historical decisions to current environmental inequities. Most groups drew clear connections between the hist orical redlining policy and current environmental conditions. In doing so, they directly addressed the structural nature of environmental inequities. One group made annotations that directly attended to ra ce, writing “Red zones [are] primarily BLACK & BROWN.” They also connected this demographic data to quantitative data on emissions and particulate matter from a scient ific article and a news source. The historical nature of the map  and some of the news articles allowed teachers to see a correlation between race and exposure to pollutants and, importantly, to ask critical questions about the historical causes of the current racialized inequities. While the activity successfully opened space for teachers  to begin historicizing the current conditions, teachers may have needed additional opportunities to explore cau sal factors. This might be accomplished by including additional texts about land-use policies and political decision making and adjusting the activity prompts to focus more explicitly on causal explanations. Engage in justice-focused action: Mock public comment As a culminating activity, teachers had the opportu nity to work in groups to practice making a public comment about an environmental injustice to an individual o r group in a position of power, such as the city co uncil or a school board. Through this “mock”  comment, they were able to think about action as co llective rather than individual. Prior to developing the comments, we le d teachers in a discussion of what constitutes (and  does not constitute) justice-focused action. Teachers described justice-focused action as “community-led” and “acknowledging histories and looking towards the fu ture.” They described non justice-focused action as “not getting community input,” “collecting data and not doing anything” and “savior complex.” Teachers’ public comments were informed by this dis cussion. Each group selected an issue that connected to either their students’ lived experienc es or those of their own families. For example, one  group of teachers described the inequities in the schools i n their districts; the schools that served minoriti zed groups were in a state of disrepair and plagued by environmenta l toxins, while the schools that served more afflue nt students were well-maintained. One teacher described a personal experi ence of movin g from a school serving primarily low-income Latinx students to a school serving more pri vileged students. She was shocked that her new scho ol, which was in the same district, did not have moldy walls or broken ceiling tiles. Teachers also identi fied the influence of one petrochemical company that was both a major polluter and major employer in their commu nity. They highlighted inequities that were propagated by  this company. For example, a few schools very clos e to the refineries that served minoritized students had received major donations for upkeep. They saw these payments as “hush money” that attempted to mask the company’s e nvironmental impacts. This group focused mostly on personal experience in their mock public comment, but expressed a desire for more formal data collection at their sites to hold leaders accountable for taking their lived experience seriously. Many teachers found this mock public comment to be challenging. They reported initial discomfort to speak publicly about the inequities their students face, but appreciated that the activity pushed them  to articulate their concerns. One teacher commented, “I still feel like I’m not good at public speaking,  even though I’ve been teaching for sixteen years,” and another said, “I n eeded to say that out loud.” Overall, the activity supported teachers in taking a justice-focused view of action and helped them to develop skills for future public advocacy.

## Discussion

This paper presents a concrete design tool for soci ally transformative environmental learning experien ces (Morales-Doyle, 2017; Mutegi, 2011). We applied this tool within a week-long institute focused on air quality for in-service teachers, with activities focused on the lenses of positionality, multiple ways of knowing, structures of oppression, and justice-focused action. Our institute represents a signific ant deviation from more commonplace approaches to climate and environmental learning th at are organized around specific scientific understandings. Rather, we forwarded a socially transformative appr oach that recognizes the limitations of mainstream scientific knowledge in addressing environmental crises, which  are fundamentally political in nature. Throu gh deep engagement with our community partner, WOEIP, we bu ilt a learning ecology that centered the knowledge and experiences of frontline communities and brought teachers and community activists together to envision and work towards a more just future (Curnow & Jurow, 2021; Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016). The final product of the institute was not an explanatory scientific model, but rather  an opportunity to advocate about a local issue, re presenting a form of praxis.

Our examples of teacher engagement show that the different environmental justice lenses in our tool are inextricably linked; engaging in one lens can promp t consideration of another. As teachers reflected o n their positionality and relationship to environm ental issues, they asked critical questions about s tructural factors that concentrated environmental impacts in minoritized c ommunities. Although many teachers initially shied away from discussing race when considering positionality, collecting stories and experiences to supplement quantitative data, a practice that was highlighted by our commun ity partner, prompted deeper consideration of how r ace and racialization can impact what we are able to learn about environmental issues. As teachers engaged in practices related to justice-focused action, such as the mock public comment, they shared personal experiences and reflected on the positionalities of their students, in additi on to highlighting historical and structural forces  that shape their experiences. Th ese mutually reinforcing lenses provide multiple en try points for teachers from different backgrounds to view climate and environmental issues from a justice-focused perspective. The design tool helped us to improve our own teacher professional learning designs to increasingly center the sociopolitical nature of environmental phenomena (Iyer & Brett, 2024). For example, the tool prompted us to expand on the traditional quantitative data collection often privileged in STEM spaces. We enriched the air quality data collection sheet to engage multiple ways of kn owing by explicitly asking teachers to engage in p eoplewatching, collect personal narratives, and consider  land-use decisions. The tool also supported us in our in-themoment interactions with teachers. We prompted teachers to consider structures of oppression through questions like, “What historical dec isions do you think caused it to be this way?” and to consider transformative action through questions such as “Who in our community is impacted by this and how?” and “How can we collabor ate with the community rather than speak for them?” Importantly, the tool has allowed us to identify shortcomings in our previous designs. Many of the teacher educators on our team have been working for more than a decade to design climate learning on local issues. This tool pushed us to recognize that our past designs have often led teachers to address cli mate change through individual actions rather than collective solutions. In many cases, teachers connected the curriculum to students' experiences of environmental impacts, but did not highlight or build on community activism that supports justice and self-determination for frontline groups. Importantly, hi ghlighting impacts and focusing only on individual solutions may perpetuate students’ sense of despair about climate change (Lewandowski et al., 2024). In contrast, a focus on collective action can foster a sense of agency and solidarity. Moreover, the tool has allowed us to identify aspec ts of our current design and facilitation that can be improved. We used the tool to reflect on the strengths and weaknesses of our own designs and iterated the language in the tool under our own evolving understandings o f climate and environmental justice. For example, w e recognized that we needed to give more attention to  intersectional identities, including race, ethnici ty, and class, and support teachers in considering how these facto rs related to environmental issues for both themsel ves and their students. After discussing this issue with ou r facilitation team, we refined the language of the  positionality lens to include a greater focus on intersectional i dentity. Similarly, we recognized that our initial articulation of honoring multiple ways of knowing required additional intersectional analysis. We reworded the lens to explicitly uplift the BIPOC knowledge traditions that are not often recognized in STEM spaces, which we consider a crucial justice move. Revising the tool provided a practica l way for us to make race more explicit in our own understandings of climate and environmental justice and to center it in our design and facilitation. Overall, our experiences using the tool to design t he air quality professional learning institute have reinforced our view that justice-focused climate and environmental learning is best supported by grounding learning in local issues (Meyer et al., 2024).  We chose the local issue of air quality, which was important to our community partner, even though it does not directly  involve traditional climate topics like carbon emi ssions and global warming. However, focusing on an issue connected to community activism allowed us to center our justicefocused goals and sociopolitical perspectives, the aspects of climate and environmental learning that are most often overlooked. We also found that climate change naturally emerged as an important part of the conversations about air quality in the institute. For example, te achers were struck by the fact that carbon dioxide is not treated as a pollutant and is therefore not regulated as su ch. Teachers also consistently surfaced climate cha nge in their discussions of justice-focused action. We believe that teachers’ deep engagement with a local environmental issue provided a foundation for understanding the global context of climate change. Yet, we also identified a number of ongoing vexatio ns that underscore the complexity of climate and environmental justice learning. The importance and interconnectedness of each justice lens create a challenge for instructional designers in prioritizing th eir focus within time constraints and in determinin g what sequence of learning will be most impactful. In future iteratio ns of the air quality institute, we may consider in troducing structures of oppression earlier to give teachers m ore time to connect his torical and political factors to current quantitative data. However, this attention must be balanced with the need to dedicate time to teachers  unpacking their own positionality vis-a-vis environmental phenomena through intersectional analysis and engaging multiple ways of knowing. Similarly, we are considering how to work towards collective action within the constr aints of a week-long institute. We were able to connect teachers wi th successful community activism and allow them to rehearse advocacy, but we recognize that these do not represent genuine engagement in environmental action. We are currently working to identify the necessary ski lls and dispositions to engage in action and determ ine which ones might reasonably be introduced and developed in a short time frame. In addition to using this tool to design teacher learning experiences, it can guide research on teacher and student learning. In our future work, we plan to an alyze teachers’ engagement in professional learning  activities to explore which lenses different teachers adopt most easily and how their engagement connects to their previous experiences and identities. Moreover, we plan to ex plore which lenses teachers eventually employ in th eir instruction with their K-12 students. For example, we have observed teachers eagerly engage with redlining maps in professional learning, but ultimately decide not  to use this in their classrooms because they teach  privileged students. We also plan to explore how teachers migh t use the tool to design climate and environmental  justice learning experiences for their K-12 students. Teachers might choose lenses to emphas ize in their curricular designs, and the language in the lenses could support teacher reflection on the lesson and feedback from peers and coaches. Using the tool to analyze K-12 learning experiences may highlight complexities of climate and environmental justice work that we should address in our professional learning for teachers. To effectively address climate and environmental justice issues with their  students,  teachers may need specific support to navigate diverse experiences of marginalization, privilege, and complicity.

## Scholarly significance

Although the learning sciences largely agree that climate education should be justice-focused, the discussion often remains theoretical. While there is some agreement on the STEM content that should be covered in clima te education, there is less guidance r egarding the sociopolitical aspects of this work. T eacher educators and K-12 teachers will benefit from concrete tools to translate our aspirations and commitments into practice. We contribute one such tool, which specifies learning goals and g uides instructional design. Our tool is grounded in  the view that curriculum should support social transformation and centers community partnerships and local issues towards this end. The design tool offers a framework for teacher educators to reflect on the strength of their justice-focused designs and identifies important directions for future research.
