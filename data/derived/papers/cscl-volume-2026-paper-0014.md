# Supporting Students’ Shared Epistemic Agency Discourse with GAI via Learning Analytics Augmented Meta-discourse

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates the effectiveness of a learning analytics-augmented meta-discourse environment in fostering students shared epistemic agency discourse in a Generative AI (GAI) learning context. Two types of learning analytical tools and meta-discourse were utilized to support students ’ epistemic understanding of critical discourse, thus, to support students’ epistemic agency discourse. A total of 48 project management students participated in the 18-week course, engaging in four online discussions and two meta-discourse sessions. Findings show that the intervention significantly improved students' epistemic understanding of critical AI discourse. Furthermore, students demonstrated progress in their shared epistemic agency discourse, moving from inform ation sharing to more projective and regulative activities across the three phases of discussion. Students with high epistem ic understanding showed a stronger tendency to build on, evalua te AI information, and create joint action plans, in contrast to low-understanding students who primarily used AI information for idea negotiation. These results underscore the critical role of pedagogical design in utilizing GAI as an epistemic tool to support collaborative knowledge advancement.

With the advent of Generative  AI (GAI) tool, researchers  have been investigating  the effectiveness  o f GAI in supporting students’  higher-order thinking sk ills compared  with traditional non-AI learning environment. Existing research  has  shown that GAI enhances  students’ creativity  and critical thinking abilities  (Liu et al., 2024), facilitates  reflective  thinking  and the acquisition  of conceptual  knowledge (Yang et al., 2025), and promotes knowledge  comprehension  while reducing  free-riding  behavior in collaborative  settings  (Gong et al., 2024). Among the  various skills that studies aim to enhance, few have investigated whether GAI supports students shared  epistemic agency, and even few er have explored strategies for promoting it. Shared epistemic agency refers to the  dynamic  process  through  which  students collaboratively  and intentionally  engage with individual  ideas and external  information  sources, constructing  collective  shared understanding that actively  advances knowledge  rather  than passively accepting  given information  (Damsa et al., 2010). Supporting shared epistemic agency becomes important especially in the age of  GAI, where information is easily accessible. Without epistemic agency,  students would treat GAI-generated  content  as an authoritative, unquestionable  entity  and c onsume information  without thoughtful consideration.  Therefore,  a significant challenge lies  in designing learning  environments  that effectively utilize GAI as an epistemic tool  supporting students shared epistemic agency. While research on the use of Generative AI (GAI) to enhance shared epistemic agency is limited, existing studies have delved into this concept within traditional knowledge-building environments. Researchers utilize design components such as learning analyt ics and meta-discourse to support students ’ epistemic agency (Tong & Chan, 2023; Yang, 2019; Yu et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025).  Tong and Chan (2023) utilized a learning analytics-augmented meta-discourse design to promote students’ epistemic discourse understanding. The study, conducted within a knowledge-building context, showed that the experimental group developed a more sophisticated epistemic discourse understanding and greater domain knowledge than a comparison class. The authors argued that this me ta-level understanding is a critical  foundation for engaging in productive inquiry. Yang (2019) designed a reflective assessment framework to foster shared epistemic agency among low-achieving students in a knowledge-building environment. This research demonstrated that reflective assessment helped students set goals, monitor their progress, and regulate their inquiry, thereby promoting high-level epistemic agency. Yu et al.(2024) examined the effectiveness of learning analytics-augmented meta-discourse in  supporting shared epistemic agency. The findings showed that the approach was effective in helping college students develop shared epistemic agency discourse, characterized by their engagement in a comprehensive discourse trajectory that included projecti ve planning, information sharing, and creating shared understanding. The dynamics of  using learning analytics to augment teacher and students meta-discourse sessions could be concluded from the following perspectives. Learning analytical tools,  such as Knowledge  Building Discourse  Explorer (KBDeX), function as intellectual artifacts, synthesizing students  fragmentary online conversations  into coherent representations and made  students’ invisible interaction levels and discourse structures visible  (Chen & Zhang,  2016).  The tangible  representation  of students  ’ progressive  inquiry 42 becomes  the collective object  of inquiry for subsequent reflection in a meta-discourse session, which is defined as discourse about discourse (Tong & Chan, 2023; Lei  & Chan, 2018).  During these sessions, students and teachers collectively review and analyze representations  that demonstrate students  ’ different levels of group interaction,  participation, and discourse  structures.  This process  of reflecting  on the goals, characteristics,  and processes  of discourse  ultimately allows the community  to co-construct  epistemic understanding  of discourse,  establishing  the communal  standard  for productive inquiry and advancing knowledge (Tong & Chan, 2023; Tong  et al., 2023).  Epistemic understanding of discourse  refers  to a student’s meta-level comprehension of the goals, standards,  and strategies  that support their engagement in productive discussions (Tong  &  Chan,  2023). According to Chinn’s AIR model, this conceptualization encompasses  a student’s perception  of three  main constitue nt components:  Aims and Goals, Standards and Ideals, and Reliable Processes and Strategies. The first component, Aims and Goals, focuses on the fundamental purpose of  the discu ssion, which might include information sharing, collaborative problem-solving,  or the collective advancement of  knowledge. Building on this foundation, Standards and Ideals outline the criteria  for a successful discussion,  such as the maintenance of sustained inquiry, the  generation of new ideas, or a focus on productive dialogue as opposed to a simple lack of arguments. Finally, Reliable Processes and Strategies detail  the specific  methods used to achieve  the established aims and adhere to the ideals, involving p ractices like building on each other’s contributions, synthesizing  diverse  ideas,  and identifyin g knowledge  gaps within the group’s current understanding. The present study This study identifies two key research gaps. First, among the studies, there is a lack of research that investigates the effectiveness of a learning environment that integrates learning analytics and meta-discourse to foster students shared epistemic agenc y in a GAI learning context. Second, the underlying mechanism of using learning analytics-augmented meta-discourse to support students’ shared epistemic agency in GAI learning environments is yet to be explored. Tong & Chan (2023) identified that learning  analytics augmented meta-discourse session supports low achieving students’ development of epistemic understanding of discourse and the positive correlation between epistemic understanding and productive knowledge building discourse. However, it remains u nclear  whether these findings hold true within the GAI learning environment.

To address these research gaps, we adopted the existing pedagogical design approach of using learning analytics augmented meta-discourse to support students shared epistemic agency. Rather thandirectly targeting the concept of shared epistemic agency, we opted to support students’ epistemic understanding of critical discourse for following reasons. First, the concept of shared epistemic agency is rarely studied in China which made students feel unfamiliar with this notion. Second, the concept of critical discourse which is conceptualized by Newman et al (2002) involves similar collaborative process as shared epistemic agency (Damsa et al., 2010). Both frameworks highlight the importance of social and collaborative actions. Shared epistemic agency is defined as “collaboratively and intentionally” engaged process of constructing collective understanding, which aligns with the view in Newman et al. (2002) that critical thinking process is a dynamic activity developing through social interaction where meaning is  socially constructed.Thus, promoting students’ epistemic understanding of critical discourse serves as an effective way to support their engagement in the processes of shared epistemic agency. The study systematically supported students’ different dimensions of epistemic understanding with different design sessions. The first session fostered students’ meta-level comprehension of the Aims and Goals and Standards and Ideals of productive discourse, focusing on the importance of sustained engagement with AI-generated information. The second session utilized the same design features to develop students’ knowledge of Reliable Processes and Strategies, enabling them to grasp the specific techniques required for engaging in critical discourse. In this study, we investigate the effectiveness of using learning analytics-augmented meta-discourse to support students epistemic understanding of critical discourse, thus to support their engagement in shared epistemic agency discourse. We ask following research questions. 1.To what extent does the learning analytics-augmented meta-discourse support students’ development of shared epistemic agency discourse? 2.To what extent does the learning analytics-augmented meta-discourse support students’ epistemic understanding of discourse?3.How students’ with different epistemic understanding demonstrate different levels of shared epistemic agency discourse features?

## Participants and instructional contexts

This study involved 48 students, including 40 males and 8 females. The course, titled “Evaluation of Engineering Investment and Financing”, was attended by students majoring in project management. All participants were in their second year of study and had successfully completed the prerequisite courses prior 43 to the class. The instructor had 15 years’ teaching experience and knew the students well. The semester spanned 18 weeks and was structured into three sections, each lasting six weeks and concentrating on different topics. The first section (weeks 1-6) introduced fundamental concepts of project investment and financing strategies. The second section (weeks 7-12) concentrated on project investment, while the third section (weeks 13-18) focused on financing. During the first and third sections, the instruct or employed lecture-based instruction, whereas the second section emphasized inquiry-based learning. Throughout the second section, students acquired foundational knowledge in writing investment plans during class and collaborated in small groups to draft their investment plans outside of class. Students voluntarily formed groups, with each group consisting of six members, resulting in a total of eight groups. From the eighth week, each group presented their milestone achievements in drafting their investment plans during class. Before these presentations, students participated in online asynchronous complete their investment plans. Each group established a WeChat group visible only to its members, resulting in a total of eight groups created. Students were encouraged to engage with GAI to ask questions, summarize k ey concepts, and identify issues or errors in their calculations. Furthermore, students were required to document their interactions with AI throughout these discussions. In total, four online discussions were conducted, each addressing essential elements of an investment plan. The first discussion, held before the 8th week, focused on the project overview and market analysis. The second discussion took place before week 10 and dealt with project planning and some financial analysis. The third discussion, o ccurring before week 11, concentrated on both financial analysis and risk assessment, while the final discussion explored the topic of proposing countermeasures. There was a break during the 9 th week for a fall recess, and interventions were implemented during the 8 th and 11 th weeks of class. The instructor hosted two learning analytics-augmented meta-discourse sessions to enhance students’ shared epistemic agency. The two sessions divided the online discussions into three phases: the first phase encompasse d the initial discussion, the second phase included both the second and third discussions, and the third phase comprised the final discussion.

## Pedagogical design

Meta-discourse session I The primary goal of the first design session is to help students develop an epistemic understanding of the importance of critically engaging with AI. To effectively support this, we implemented following design components. First, the teacher selected onlin e discussions from two contrasting groups: one group where students actively built upon each other’s ideas, including AI information and another where responses predominantly came from students’ agreements with AI information. Second, learning analytics visualizations were utilized to illustrate the levels of engagement in critical AI discourse. Twonetworks were generated from the raw discussion data: one displayed a centralized structure, indicating that most students were responding exclusively to the AI  information, while the other showcased a decentralized structure, with multiple students actively engaging and developing each other’s ideas (see Figure 1.a; a clearer version of the figure could be obtained by visiting following link: https://drive.googl e.com/file/d/1QtRt9z1se-fuiRQsHA5cfAvzr8ubQP5F/view?usp=sharing). Third, the teacher facilitated meta-discourse session, encouraging students to identify the contrasting features of the twonetworks, including the shape of the network and the thickness of the connections between students. Additionally, the teacher art iculated what these patterns reveal about students’ online discourse. To enhance comprehension, snippets of the discussions were presented, and network features were mapped to specific discours e segments (see Figure 1.b; a clearer version of the figure could be obtained by visiting following link: https://drive.google.com/file/d/1QtRt9z1se-fuiRQsHA5cfAvzr8ubQP5F/view?usp=sharing). Meta-discourse design session II In the second design session, students’ epistemic understanding is supported from processes and strategies of critical discourse engagement. One major aim is to support students’ understanding of what types of strategies they could engage in conducting cri tical discourse. We employed similar design components as the first design  session, namely, contrastive group discussions, learning analytics and meta-discourse. In this design session, the teacher first selected two contrastive group discussions which are group A and B (see Figure 2a-b; a clearer version of the figure could be obtained by visiting following link: https://drive.google.com/file/d/1QtRt9z1se-fuiRQsHA5cfAvzr8ubQP5F/view?usp=sharing). Students from the two groups showed different levels of critical use of AI information. In this session, ENA was employed to create learning analytical visualizations to support students' reflective practices in following meta-discourse sessions (see Fi gure 2c; a clearer version of the figure could be obtained by v isiting following link: https://drive.google.com/file/d/1QtRt9z1se-fuiRQsHA5cfAvzr8ubQP5F/view?usp=sharing). To create ENA figures, the teacher made similar attempts as data analysis, including following steps. First, the teacher coded students’ online data with the coding scheme from Newman et al (1995) which aimed to measure how college 44 students demonstrated critical thinking in computer-supported group learning with adaptations (see table 1). The teacher coded segments of students’ discussions from both groups. In total, the teacher coded 156 notes, in which 76 were from group A and 80 were from group B. The teacher then explained what different components in one ENA graph indicate. For example, what do the dotted squares, the squares, the codes and the edges that connected the codes indicate. The teacher then guided students to notice di fferent locations of the centroids and explained to the students the meanings of different centroids locations. The teacher firstly explained that the centroids of the two groups located in different directions of the X-axis showed students’ different disc ourse patterns. Furthermore, the teacher explained that in group A (shown as red lines and squares in figure 3c), students engaged in discourse types as evaluating solutions, providing information related to the question, and sustaining the inquiry by asking more questions while in group B (shown as blue lines and squares in figure 3c), students engaged in extending AI information, providing initial solutions to the problem and planning for the inquiry process. The teacher also showed snippets of students’ online referred to AI information, then evaluated AI information. Students then evaluated how they should change their information searching strateg ies based on the information they have searched. Similarly, the teacher also referred to the discussion snippets from group B and explained that the discussion dynamics showed that students appeared toneglect incorporating information from AI nor asking f urther questions from AI to improve their current answers (see Figure 2). From this, students developed their epistemic understanding of critical AI discourse further, identifying that critical AI discourse involves evaluating, identifying information obtained from AI resources, monitoring object advancement, adjusting group strategies and checking validity.

To explore students’ epistemic understanding of critical AI discourse, three questions were designed to measure students’ understanding. In total,  46 students participated  in the interview.  All the interviews  were recorded  and transcribed  for future analyses.  In addition to the interview  data, students’ learning  artifacts  were collected, comprising their investment  plans and daily reflections. In  the investment plans, students  were prompted to evaluate the degree to which their interactions with generative AI contributed to the process of  crafting the plan.

## Analytical methods

To address the first question, we conducted following analyses. First, we exported students' online discussion notes into a spreadsheet and then imported them into NVivo 12 for further examination. Next, we carried out content analysis, adapting Damsa’s di mensions of shared epistemic agency to characterize students’ discourse (2010). In addition to the original 24 actions, which were  grouped into  four categories to reflect students’ engagement in shared epistemic agency discourse, we included new actions  that indicated students' interactions with AI. These two dimensions involved sharing information from AI and criticizing AI-generated information (see Table 2). One researcher coded the data, while a second researcher performed reliability checks. We achi eved a reasonable reliability score of 0.897 and discussed any inconsistencies found in our coding results. Following this, we conducted an epistemic network analysis (ENA) based on the developed shared epistemic agency discourse over the three phases. Therefore, we used whole conversations as the stanza size, with “phases” serving as the activity unit. Our findings demonstrate that a Learning Analytics (LA) -augmented meta-discourse environment significantly improves students’ epistemic understanding of critical AI discourse. This aligns with Tong et al. (2023) regarding the utility of meta-discourse in fostering productive inquiry. While Tong noted high epistemic understanding correlates with traditional knowledge-building (e.g., synthesizing), our study reveals its specific role in the GAI context: students with high understanding evaluate AI outputs to create joint action plans, whereas those with low understanding negotiate ideas without substantive integration. Furthermore, consistent with Yu et al. (2024), we observed a shift from information sharing toward projective and regulative activities. Notably, engaging with GAI appears to catalyze these higher-order moves more intensely than traditional environments.

This work enriches the notion of shared epistemic agency for the GAI era. We establish that supporting students' epistemic understanding of AI discourse is a primary mechanism for promoting agency. Methodologically, we demonstrate that integrating tools like ENA —not just for post-hoc assessment, but to generate "objects of inquiry" for reflection —enables students to visualize and refine collective discourse patterns. Practically, we offer a clear pathway for cultivating shared agency by establishing critical AI discourse as a foundational collaborative practice.
