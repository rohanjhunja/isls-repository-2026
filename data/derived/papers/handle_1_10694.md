# How Gesture Protects Against Failures in Spatial Problem-Solving

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Student-generated gestures improve their spatial problem solving. Embodiment perspectives theorize that the mechanisms that underpin gesture’s role in spatial reasoning lie in the motor system. Some embodiment theorists, however, jettison working memory models while many information processing theorists assimilate embodiment into their working memory models. We therefore leveraged innovations in neuroimaging technologies to begin addressing this case of underdetermination of theory by data. Using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) we determined that students who learned to solve spatial problems successfully recruit brain regions associated with cognitive effort more so than students who learned to solve problems with non-gesture-based strategies. We discuss the theoretical implications for future educational research on gesture-based learning strategies.

## Introduction

People move their bodies when they think. Some embo died cognition perspectives maintain commitments to information processing theory. From this view, the body buffers or “off-loads” information from working memory systems onto the motor system (Hostetter &  Alibali, 2019). Some scholars have critiqued such a rguments on the grounds of underdetermination —i.e., the standard evidence (e.g., performance) is insufficient to falsify the theoretical entailments of working memory “load” (d e Jong, 2010). The purpose o f this investigation is to determine how advances in neuroimaging technologies might augment extant models of embodied cognition and their relations to working memory models. We situate our investigation in organic chemistry education where students present visual-spatial, verbal algorithmic, and motor strategies f or spatial problem solving (Stieff et al., 2016). This approach allows us to illuminate how di fferent embodied learning experiences result in stu dents tapping motor or other neurocognitive systems during problem solving. Problem solving as embodied cognition Classic working memory models deemphasized the motor system to highlight the dichotomy between two distinct memory systems: the phonological loop that stores verbal information and the visual-spatial sketchpad that stores imagistic information (Baddely, 1992). The central executive system  integrates verbal and visual modes and controls cognition without storing any information.  The episodic buffer, however, is thought to serve as a temporary storehouse that can contrast information between the sketchpad and representations stored in  other memory systems. What some call “over-load” occurs when the information in a stimulus or the mental operations upon it exceed the demands of storage or buffer systems. Not all problem solving requires the body to buffer  information load. If a task requires movement (e.g., lifting an object), then some theorists reject this as evidence  of embodied cognition (Wilson & Golonka, 2013). Furthermore, people can perform many tasks without moving their body.  During problem solving, however, people sometimes display embodied cognition when they deploy the arms, hands, and fingers as a strategy critical to their success. For example, when solving spatial  problems people extend their index finger and thum b and simulate rotating an object represented by a two-dimensional diagram (Chu & Kita, 2011). People will spontaneously use the body to solve these problems even when not required. Such results provide eviden ce in favor of the motor  system operating as part of working memory because gesture delivers a benefit to problem solving accuracy on spatial transformation problems.

## Organic chemistry as a disciplinary context for embodied cognition

The domain of organic chemistry poses spatial trans formation tasks to students. These tasks are known to challenge students’ spatial reasoning. This challenge therefore makes embodied cognition via gesture production a useful strategy for protecting studen ts against failure on such problems. We have demonstrated previously that students deploy co-thought gestures during problem solving. Moreover, teaching students how to gesture protects them against decrements in performance that occur w hen students are taught with molecular plastic models that are then removed during problem solving (Stieff et al., 2016). What remains unclear is how gesture protects chemistry students. S ome students gesture frequently but perform poorly while others do not gesture and perform well. Gesture may therefore be epiphenomenal i n that unseen cognitive processes operate during gesture-based learning. We aim to illuminate these processes. One challenge in determining the mechanism responsible for gesture benefits is that gesture production att enuates across problem solving trials (Chu & Kita, 2011). M oreover, some students may never overtly produce ge stures despite relying upon motoric representations to solve problems. We there fore seek to determine the mechanisms responsible for gestures’ protective role in spatial problem solving even in the absence of overt gestures. fNIRS offers a tool for assessing embodiment during authentic problem solving Some studies have utilized fMRI to explore how gest ure production enhances learning by engaging the mo tor system (Wakefield, 2019). However, fMRI is highly influenced by motion artifacts, preventing gesturing during problem-solving. We leverage an innovative neuroimaging system called Near Inf rared Spectroscopy (fNIRS). fNIRS is less influenced by motion artifacts than fMRI thus allo wing gesturing, delivers better spatial resolution than EEG, has better temporal resolution and lower cost than fMRI, and can be used in naturalistic environments (Boas et al., 2014). fNIRS is often used in studies of motor movement  and cognitive processing (Boas et al., 2014). Cognitive neuroscience demonstrates that the motor planning occurs in frontal lobe in the absence of overt action and that internal motor traces reinforce inf ormation stored in working memory —disrupting these brain regions leads to poorer performance (Marvel et al.,  2019). Examining neurocognitive processes therefor e identifies neurocognitive systems that support problem solving, especially in cases of equifinality, where different factors lead to similar performance. Indeed, the variability in the neurocognitive basis of problem solving predicts subsequent behavior, and forecasts response to instruction, even when behavioral measures fall short (Supekar et al., 2013). To our knowledge, however, the neurocognitive basis of gestures in ST EM spatial problem solving remains unknown. This leads us to the following question: What are the neural correlates responsible for successful spatial problem solving in organic chemi stry? Does gesture aid problem solving by drawing u pon neurocognitive resources for working memory demands or upon resources that are involved in motor planning?

## Methods

Undergraduate students (N=27; 17 female, 10 male) b etween the ages of 18 and 25 from a large Mid-western university were recruited by university mass email.  Informed consent was obtained, and participants we re randomly assigned to  receive either Gesture or  Model based instruction. The gesture group was taug ht to use specific gestures, while the model group used a tra ditional ball-and-stick model. Both methods aimed to help students map and translate spatial relationships of 3D molecular st ructures into 2D diagrams (Stieff et al., 2016). Participants were then sat at a computer monitor di splaying molecular diagrams. Using a block design, accuracy and response times were collected via the software E-Prime. Participants were first shown a source image (see Figure 1) for 3500 ms, then a fixation point was displayed for 2500 ms to a llow participants time to imagine the solution, and finally, they were shown two target images (see Figure 1) for 1500 ms followed by another fixation point for 2000 ms. They were asked  to touch the corresponding side of the touch screen  that correctly translated the spatial relations from the source to target image.

## Results and discussion

To determine whether gesture and model-based instruction produce parity on accuracy, we as sessed students’ spatial problem-solving using an organic chemistry diagrammatic tra nslation task. Participants were evaluated based upon their percent accuracy across all trials by instructional condition and problem type (See Figure 2). Accuracy. A repeated-measures ANOVA was performed to evaluate the effect of instructional condition (Gesture vs. Model) and problem type—Fischer to Dash-Wedge (F-D) and vice versa (D-F) on accuracy. Overall, there was a significant interaction between problem type and instructional condition on accuracy (F(1, 23) = 5.10, p =.034, η²p =.181). Results showed that the Gesture group was m ore accurate on the F-D problem type: M =.87 (SD =.12, Range =.56 -.94) compared to participants in the Model group: M =.73 (SD =.27, Range =.28–.94). This indicates that gesture protects against decrements in spatial problem solving specifically when problems pose opaque spatial information first. Thus, gestur e-based instruction is beneficial for solving problem s that require first decoding the 3D spatial information represented in a 2D source diagram. The disparity found between these instructional conditions could be the result of gestures ability to assist in visualizing and ke eping track of spatial information that is not immediately apparent.

## Implications and future directions

Some embodiment perspectives maintain that cognitio n “offloads” working memory to the body. These resu lts fail to support this view. Rather thandecreasing n eurocognitive load, students who learned with an em bodied gesture-based approach demonstrated more effort as evidence by increased frontal activation. This was especially the case for the most successful students. We see a minimum  of two possible theoretical implications. First, gestures may not “lighten” load. If true, students who learned with gesture experienced desirable difficulty during problem solving. They encoded resolute motor repres entations that allowed them to engage in effortful,  but productive spatial reasoning. One way to assess this claim is vi a think-aloud protocols and this approach is underway by our team. A second explanation preserves extant working memory models but with the motor system as an integral component (Marvel et al., 2019). Thi s view highlights gesture’s role in identifying mis matches between a student’s internal representation and the  external diagrams. Research shows that gesture and  speech work together to resolve discrepant meanings. If this mismatch view is true, then this suggests that gesture’s utility extends beyond speech, acting as a neurocognitive a nd embodied tool for aligning internal and external representations. This latter view suggests broader educational potential for gesture as a learning tool.
