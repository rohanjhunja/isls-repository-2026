# Mobilizing Youth STEM Learning Trajectories on Bicycles

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines the STEM learning trajectories of two Black male youths, Chicke and Brandon, who participated in a STEM cycling program. A video recording by Chicke on one of the group bicycle rides is examined to understand how the program served as a juncture through which the two boys engaged in spatial literacy practices, application of science knowledge, and active participation in norms and social practices of group bicycle riding for safety and efficiency. Mediated discourse analysis is used to render findings from the video clip in relationship to the larger data corpus of the three-year project. The study has implications for how youth are positioned in and invited to STEM learning opportunities.

## Study context

This paper examine s video data from a multi-year study of a STEM cycling program offered to ris ing 9th and 10th graders. The program involved a two-week summer camp and continuity bicycle rides the f ollowing spring and fall. Youth of color were recruited from a northeastern, rustbelt city and first-ring suburbs in the USA  to participate in the program. Past participants were invited to return the following year as mentors. Here, we report from the third year of the program, which included 25 youth of color. The summer camp is designed to introduce youth to STEM concepts through the medium of the bicycle and related technologies (e.g., helmets). Youth each received their own used bicycle that they learn ed to tune and repair as separate constituent parts and as an engineering system. STEM concepts were reinforced both during the working on the bicycles and during specific modules from a STEM bicycle curriculum. Example mod ules included: helmet design and testing, tire traction measurement, mechanical advantage of bicycle gears, among others. During the second week of the camp, youth participated in bicycle rides led by partnering community cycling organizations.  The routes were designed for youth to experience a range of historical and cultu ral assets of the city and surrounding areas. The b icycle rides presented the participating youth with opportunities to trave rse areas of the city with which they may  have been unfamiliar. The bicycle rides were also planned for youth to learn about science, hist ory, and culture situated in specific places in the city.

Youth mobility and learning In this paper, we conceptualize how youth learn wit hin and across contexts based on three foundational motivations. We name these foundational motivations  in order to situate our work within specific field s as well as communicate our purpose and commitments for doing this work. The first foundational motivation is to create lear ning opportunities that purposefully connect multip le places of learning. Our goal is to disrupt the notion that learning is limited to the four walls of a classroom. From our perspective, youth are alread y engaged in complex mobilities and trajectories th at establish relationships among diverse physical places, as well as virtual s paces. We draw on Leander and his colleagues (2010)  who define mobility in the context of learning as involving: physical mobility, the embodied movement from place to place (in this case aided by the bicycle); virtual mobility, the use of networked technology to build and maintain social connections across time and space (in this c ase the mobile phones owned by our youth and othe r GPSenabled tracking devices); and educational mobility, the distribution of learning opportunities across people, tools, and learning environments. This last characteristic of mobility served as central design criteria for the planning of the summer cam p and continuity rides. We intentionally planned le arning opportunities for youth to engage in STEM concepts and related social, cultural, and historical issues in particular places of significance; in community with particular people with STEM and communi ty-based expertise; along curated routes that traverse d diverse urban commercial, industrial, residential, and recr eational environments; and within an educational in stitution’s campus and buildings.

Our second foundational motivation acknowledges that youth learning is both life-long and “life-wide,” involving complex learning trajectories that are bo th historical and that traverse any number of forma l and informal social settings and relationships. This learning lives  (Erstad, 2012) perspective allows for a broader conceptualization of learning based on socio-cultural learning theory and social practice theory. Socio-cultural theory accounts for the relationships and mental fu nctions of people and the social, cultural,  and historical situations in which they navigate throughout their lives. Social practice theory accounts for how acti vities, emotions, experiences, relationships, identities, e tc. aggregate over time and develop durability for individuals and groups of people.

Our third foundational motivation takes into consid eration that learning need not be framed with an assimilationist ideology that positions youth in a deficit perspective and measures learning by degree  of fidelity with a pre-established epistemological and ontological ideal. Rather, we view youth learning from an asset-based perspective that positions youth as knowers and doe rs who already possess genius that should be cultiv ated (Muhammad, 2019; 2020) and cultural richness that m ust be sustained (Brown, 2019; Paris & Alim, 2014).  We are less interested in how our youth adopt STEM-related concepts and practices, and we are more int erested in how they adapt them to be commensurate with who they are in the mo ment and who they wish to become in the future. We extend this perspective to the adults and elders whom we recruited to be part of the summer camp and continuity rides. Everyone involved is considered t o be a teacher and a learner, already possessing sign ificant cultural knowledge and experience to share with eac h other (Moll et al., 1992; Yosso, 2005). As the re search team, we positioned ourselves as learners who are t aught by our youth, our community partners, and our  invited experts, while also sharing our knowledge and experience.

## Methods

Focal youth The video data presented in this paper was recorded by one of the participating Black male youths, Chicke, using a GoPro camera attached to his helmet. Chicke was one of six youth who volunteered to wear a GoPro camera on his helmet during the bicycle rides of the summer c amp. We encouraged the youth to verbalize what they  were experiencing during the ride, in addition to their usual social interaction with each other, so we cou ld better understand how they were making sense of the experi ence in relationship to the skills and concepts the y learned during the first week of camp. Chicke was one of the more vocal students during the rides, which made for richer video data to explore for our study.  Featured predominately in Chicke’s video recording is his riding partner, Brandon. Chicke and Brandon have a shared history going back to the ir childhoods and were rarely seen apart from each other during the two-week summer camp. The youth were instructed to ride  two abreast while on the open streets and single  file when on sidewalks or bike paths.  As riding partners, Chicke and Brandon rode alongside each other, which afforded socialization and sharing of what they were experiencing. Focal bicycle ride For the ride selected for this paper, the youth were transported by bus (and their bicycles by box truck) to the fourmile route start, which was a community mural that was being i nstalled on the side of a building. The youth had an opportunity to hear from the artists who were he lping to create and install the mural. The youth le arned about the science involved with both the adhesive used to attach the mural panels on the side of building, as well as the weather proofing involved in preparing the mural to  withstand the city’s brutal (and recently deadly) winter conditions. The route then led the youth through th e center of the city and adjacent residential neigh borhoods toward a bicycle path that led to a park. At the pa rk, the youth enjoyed a cookout provided by a partn ering community cycling organization. The youth learned a bout th e historical significance of the park as a pivotal international port for the Underground Railroad.

## Data analysis

For this paper, we selected a five-minute segment of Chicke’s video recording for analysis.  We selected this segment as exemplary in our data corpus because Chicke and Brandon were explicitly discussing what they were experiencing during the bike ride. In short, this s elected segment, and others like it, allowed us to explore our three foundational motivations among the experiences of our youth. The selected segment is from the last mile of the four-mile bike ride to the park. The part of the route f eatured in the video clip includes a one-way residential street with multiple intersections and a bike path that runs along a major state route but is separate d from the roadway by a concrete sidewalk. One important methodological consideration here is that our research team was also on this ride with the youth. We were riding ou r bicycles alongside the youth  guided by our community partners. Based on our post-ride debrief and collaborative data analysis sessions, we acknowledged experiencing comparable sensations, reacting to same features and phenomena in the built environment, feeling the same wind, smelling the same smells, and attend ing to comparable sights and sounds. This shared experi ence allowed us to consider the video recording with greater understanding and render Chicke’s experience in greater fullness through our own experience on the ride. For example, another youth, Abshir, appears sporadically in Chicke’s video recording. Sometimes he is present and other times he is absent in the video. If we were only co nsidering Chicke’s video without any other knowledge of the event, we would be unable to account for how and why Abshir appears and disappears from the front of the group where Chicke was riding. However, we were on the ride and know that Abshir had a habit of leaving his partner  and riding up to Chicke and Brandon to engage them in conversation, only to be sent back to his partner by the adults  helping to guide the ride. We would be at a disadvantage when making inductions from the video data without this shared experience of riding alongside the youth and gently guiding Abshir to adopt group ride norms. We used mediated discourse analysis (MDA) (Scollon, 2001) as the method for analyzing Chicke’s video recording. MDA is a microethnographic methodology t hat considers how action and discourse are mutually constitutive, focusing on the “concrete, real-time social actions to see these social actions as fundamentally discursive” (Scollon, 2001, pp. 8-9). Using MDA, we strive to privilege neither disco urse nor social action, but rather to consider discourse as one among many avai lable tools through which people take action. MDA g ives analytic primacy to mediated action, which informs how the transcription scheme is organized with the description of the social action in a column left of the transc ribed speech. This allows for the social actions to  be considered first and only afterward to consider the extent to which the recorded speech was a significant mediational means within that action (Ochs, 1999). The reason for this emphasis is that the learning our youth engaged in during our program was not only mediated by discourse but also  by embodied actions. Youth were ofte n asked to feel a phenomenon that was only later explained with STEM concepts and terms. For example, youth felt for friction in overtightened bearings and felt for resistance in t he pedals when selecting a gear for the terrain. Th erefore, attending to the se social actions first in our analysis is of parti cular importance.  Additionally, much of the communication on the bicycle rides was both verbal and gestural (with particular expected hand signals). T he MDA transcript for the analysis of Chicke’s recorded video is available upon request at the following URL: http://bit.ly/SC-ISLS25. The first column includes the time of day and the  navigational description of the route segment. The second column includes video sti lls of mediated actions taken and descriptions of t hose actions. The third column is the transcribed speech captured in the video. In some instances, another youth’s video recording of the same moment was used to hear speec h that was inaudible in Chi cke’s recording. The fourth column is the interpretation of the mediated actions taken and their significance within our broader known history of the youth based on our social interactions during the program and specific interviews we conducted.

## Findings

The findings for this paper are focused narrowly on  this single video recording. However, as  the full transcript reveals, the interpretation of the mediated actions  taken in the video recording are informed by emerg ent trends and patterns across our data corpus. For example, the MDA transcript includes an interpretation of Chicke’s hand signals and verbal reminders to other riders. Looki ng across the data corpus, we are able to determine  that these social actions fit a pattern of emergent social pra ctices Chicke was engaging in across the summer cam p. Below, we detail select findings that help explain the meaning and significance of the mediated actions taken in this single video recording.

Feeling the built environment Chicke and Brandon were commenting on and reacting to characteristics of the built environment in comparative ways to places with which they are familiar. They were in a sense “reading” the built environment along the route and making value statements about what they were se nsing. During the ride, the group turned on to a on e-way residential neighborhood street on which they were following a garbage truck. One of the ride leaders announced, “It’s garbage day!” Chicke and Brandon then had a c onversation about what day of week it was, because Chicke knew that it was not garbage day where he lived. They also made note of the speed bumps in the residential road and compared them to the speed bumps in their own n eighborhood. Later the boys made note of and called  out broken glass in the road as a hazard. They also joined the other youth in making gagging sounds and commenting about the smell of the neighborhood. Rather than attributing the broken glass and the garbage smells to the garbage truck down the road, Chicke asking jokingly, “What’s wrong with this neighborhood?” Brandon goes on to ask if they are on the west side of the city, but Chicke respon ds that they are downtown, presumably because they had ridden earlier on the route through the center of the city. Brandon states, “This feels like next to Quincy’s house,” which leads Chicke and Brandon to discuss the west side of the city in relationship to where they live. Brandon concludes, “Actually, it’s not that bad.” Of signif icance is how the built environment “feels” to Chic ke and Brandon in comparison to where they  live based on what  aspects they are attending to in the buil t environment and how. As Chicke and Brandon “feel” more parts of  the city by bicycle, they develop a more nuanced a nd informed sense of place.

Understanding the science of road hazards During the first week of the summer camp, the youth learned about bicycle tires and tubes. They each were given a bicycle wheel to remove the tire and the tube. They each learned how to patch a punctured tube with glue and a patch kit. They also learned ho w underinflated tires can experience a “pinch flat”  that looks like a snake bite in the tube when hitting a hole in the road or a curb.  Lastly, they learned how objects in the road can p otentially puncture and flatten a tire. Chicke and Brandon used this gained knowledge to un derstand why it is important to point out hazards in the road to other riders, a group bicycle ride norm reinforced in the training the youth received from the community ride leaders. Throughout the video, Chicke and Brandon engaged in the chorus of the group calling out and pointing to road hazards, “Hole!” “Glass!” These exclamations were interjecte d in the middle of conversations and took priority over  anything else the youth were attending to and/or di scussing. Chicke and Brandon understood that it was insufficient to avoi d the obstacle yourself and that it is important to  the whole group to point out the hazard to everyone, sending the message back down through the group. On a previous ride, Chicke and Brandon had experienced how one of the group cyclists got a flat, and the whole  group had to stop and wait for the flat to be repaired. Their experie nce repairing a flat tire and waiting for one to be  repaired for someone else provided them with a sense of urgency to both avoid and call out road hazards to keep the  group ride rolling.

Marshaling the group ride The norms and social practices for cycling in a group are predicated on safety. There are specific riding formations, habits of bicycle riding, and verbal and gestural forms of communication for fellow riders and drivers of vehicles that are all explicitly taught and reinforced on group rides. The youth were taught these norms and social practices during the second week of summer camp. They had an opportunity to practice them on our university camp us sidewalks and roadways with limited traffic before enacting them on our organized group rides on open roads and bike trails. Across our organized group rides led b y community ride leaders, the youth not only improv ed in enacting these norms and social practices but also began helping reinforce them among each other. The first couple riders, the community ride leaders, and the research team were primarily cuing the yout h to adhere to the norms and social practices. In subsequent rides, the yout h began cuing each other, in particular reminding e ach other to hold a specific riding formation. Chicke was one of these youth who was repeatedly reminding others to ride next to their partner, two abreast, and not to overlap wheels. Chicke repeatedly reminded Lawrence, who was behind him, to stop overlapping his wheel and stay back wi th his partner. Lawrence’s partner, Satish, was also rolling ahead of Chicke. Chicke reminded him, “Bro, wait! You got Lawrence. I got Brandon.” Chicke was participating in the marshalling of the group ride for safety and efficiency (less acceleration and decelerations that splinter and spread out the group).

## Discussion

The youth in this study were intentionally position ed as knowers and doers of science. The research te am and community partners regularly referred to the youth as “young engineers,” “original geniuses,” “young scientists,” “bike mechanics,” and “STEM cyclists.” They came to  the summer camp with knowledge and prior experienc e that they were invited to share and build upon. The  bicycles provided them with increased mobility to “feel” different built environments and compare them with what was familiar to them.  Taylor and Hall (2013) refer to this placemaking as spatial literacy practices that are mediated by sensory experience, affect, and discourse around what constitutes a “good” or “bad” or “not so bad” neighborhood. Chicke and Brandon engaged in these s patial literacy practices, though in this video recording, they had yet to account sufficiently for the smells  and broken glass in relationship to the activity of garbage co llection. The youth’s scientific knowledge of bicyc le wheels, tires, and tubes helped them understand the importa nce of altering each other to road hazards. They kn ew firsthand the labor involved in repairing a tube and rei nflating a tire on the side of the road. Likewise, the youth developed an experiential understanding of the importance of particular norms and social practices involved with riding safely as a group. They took on an active role in helping to marshal the group ride and more efficiently and safely arrive to lunch on time. These tentative fin dings based on a five-minute video segment demonstrate the potential to understand the significance of the STEM-related learning trajectories, social practices, and identities of our youth to their present and future lives.
