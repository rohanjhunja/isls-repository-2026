# Playing to Understand: Teacher Moves and Student Thinking in Nondigital Math Games

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how fourth-grade students engage in mathematical thinking while playing non-digital fraction games, with and without teacher facilitation. Conducted within a four-year Research-Practice Partnership, the Games Time project embeds biweekly 30minute game sessions in 3rd-and 4th-grade classrooms to promote equitable, engaging math learning without pull-out intervention. Using video analysis from a design research framework, we compared student discourse and manipulative use across six game sessions. Findings reveal that students engage in meaningful mathematical reasoning in both conditions, though teacher presence extends structured verbal engagement and mathematical discourse, while unsupervised play encourages exploratory intera ction with manipulatives. This work contributes to growing research on non-digital math games, offering low-cost, inclusive strategies for addressing unfinished learning and fostering student agency in mathematics classrooms.

A perennial problem for math educators is the range of student mathematics skills in classrooms, necessitating intervention at different mathematical concepts and/or procedures while simultaneously teaching grade level content. Historically, intervention h as relied on strategies such as rote learning that decrease student and teacher agency and engagement (Tan et al., 2022). To imagine new futures for mathematics, we need new ways to solve the problem of unfinished math learning.

This project is a Research Practice Partnership between our university research team and a local school district. In the 2021-2022 school year, the school district asked our team to design a math intervention that would

1) reach all students in grades 3 and 4 without pull-out, and 2) use non-digital math games. We co-designed Games

Time, a bi-weekly 30 min. session in grades 3 and 4 in which students play non-digital math games. This empirical contribution investigates the difference between supervised and unsupervised game play for fourth grade students playing fraction games. In collaboration with the teacher who participated in this study, we formulated the following research questions: How do students engage in mathematics while playing games? Do they en gage differently when the teacher facilitates the game compared to when students facilitate on their own? Theoretical approach Educational research has focused far more heavily on the study of digital games compared to non-digital games, despite the widespread cultural use of non-digital games. Nondigital math games can have a positive impact on early childhood math skills, like verbal counting, identifying numbers, and comparing quantities (Hendrix et al., 2018), with more impact for students with prior lower achievement (DePascale and Ramani, 2025). Math games can impact learning through specific mathematical representations such as a number line (Ramani et al., 2012) or subitizing with dice (Gasteiger & Moeller, 2021), suggesting visual models may be particularly important for game success. Despite a large body of research on teacher questioning in math in general (Roberts, 2021; McCarthy et al., 2016), there is little research on teacher questioning in the specific context of a game (DePascale & Ramani, 2025).

Methodological approach This paper emerged from teacher inquiry about how students may engage differently in games in the presence of a teacher. We focus our data collection and analysis on Math Games as Enacted, or how the math games were taken up by students and teachers in the  class. Once we decided to study the difference in engagement between math games with and without a teacher, we chose a classroom in which the teacher regularly both sat down with students to play games and allowed students to play without an adult, Ms. K.  We met with Mrs. K to co-plan the study and develop the research questions. Ms. K chose three fraction games to play: Numbers Wild, Fraction Fortress, and Fraction Formula, all of which are commercially produced and frequently used in her Games Time. 2704 The data sources for this study consist of six videos of students playing three different math games. For each game, there is a video of students playing the game with a teacher and without a teacher. There are four or five students in each video, and the videos are between 11 and 21 minutes in length (the video length reflects how long it takes to play one game from start to finish). This difference in length of videos reflects that games played with the teacher consistently took longer than those without.  The first author and an undergraduate research assistant captured the videos over a four-month period in a single 4th-grade classroom.  Each video was transcribed using the AI and then finalized by a research assistant. The first author reviewed each video in its entirety five times, developing a codebook through an iterative, inductive process. ## Implications

While games can enrich and extend the work of an effective teacher, they cannot replace the expertise needed for meaningful instruction.
