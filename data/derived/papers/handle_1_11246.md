# From Learners to Teachers: Mastering Machine Learning While Creating Engaging Environments for Young Learners

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
There is a need for Artificial Intelligence (AI) curricula that are developmentally appropriate and appeal to students’ interests (Lee and Perret, 2022). Embracing a Community of Learners theoretical perspective (Rogoff, 1994) and employing a Project-based Learning framework (Krajcik and Shin, 2014) we designed a unit on Machine Learning (ML) using Google Teachable Machine for early adolescents that incorporates interest-driven practices with the help of two Learner-Teachers. We implemented this unit in a summer camp with 9-14yo campers and documented the learning progress of our primary participants (high school students that assumed hybrid roles as Learner-Teachers) and the campers. The findings suggest that the curriculum produces quality learning outcomes including improved knowledge about ML, the process of training ML models, and a greater appreciation of the role of student interest in our Learner-Teachers in designing and implementing activities.

## Background and objective

One of the  biggest challenges in STEM education research is to  recognize technological advances early and to produce innovative educational research and practic es to prepare the workforce of the future. Artificial Intelligence (AI), including its subset Machine Lea rning (ML), uses algorithms and statistical methods  to computationally harness very large data sets, to simulate human intelligence across varying levels of complexity. The citizenry of the future needs to be competent w ith respect to using digital devices a nd able to symbiotically harness the power of AI tools in their professional  work. In recent years, as AI technologies have become mor e accessible and mainstream, there have been several conceptual frameworks proposed for AI Literacy (Touretzky et al., 2019; Wong et al., 2020; Long and Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021). Long and Magerko (2020) defined AI Literacy for K-12 as a “set of competencies that enables individua ls to critically evaluate AI technologies; communicate and collaborate effectively with AI; and use AI as a tool online, at home, and in the workplace.” (p. 2). Wong et al. (2020) proposed three goals for AI Literacy:

• Prepare young people to live with AI and to prepare them for the labor needs of the future.

• Enable students to understand and appreciate how th e most common AI applications work while

stimulating their interest in the domain.

• Train future professionals in non-AI fields on how they can integrate AI into their f ields ethically and

safely.

Focusing on the second goal, our objective is to in troduce middle school students to the basics of ML (e.g., what is ML and what goes into creating a goo d, supervised ML model) and stimulate their interes t in this area. This is important as most applications classi fied as AI that students encounter in their daily l ives fall under the ML umbrella.

## Significance and rationale

There is a need for AI curricula that is developmen tally appropriate and appeals to students’ interest s (Lee and Perret, 2022). Systematic reviews on the application of AI technologies in STEM Education across K-16 contexts (e.g., Ng et al., 2023; Xu & Ouyang, 2022; Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019) have not found studies emphasizing curricula that sought to integrate AI Literacy with  content areas and connect to student s’ personal interests. A recent systematic review of AI teaching and learning from 2000-2020 (Ng et al., 2023) recommends the use of age-appropriate AI/ML tools in primary and secondary sc hool settings, in contexts that appeal to students’ interests. Highlighting a significant research gap, they found just one study (Toivonen et al., 2020) that employed a Project-based Learning framework to improve students’ under standing of ML principles  using Google Teachable Machine (GTM). The AI education design community needs to consider structuring units that integrate use of AI/ML tools with diverse extracurricular activities. This integration helps develop an understanding of the wide scale of AI/ML applications across domains, which is essential to prepare learners for the workplace of the future, one where such applications are expected to be widely used.

## Theoretical and conceptual framework

We take our  design inspiration from the Community of Learners theoretical perspective (Rogoff, 1994), which considers learning to be an active process that occurs when learners are given autonomy and responsibility as they engage in collaborative activities. Learning is hypothesized as  primarily a participatory process instead of  a byproduct of receiving transmitted knowledge from experts or independent acquisition of knowledge by learners (Slotta et al., 2018). The role of the teacher is akin to a mentor that guides the learners as they exercise agency in forming lines of inquiry, critiquing peers, evaluating, refining ideas and artifacts produced by the community (Bielaczyc et al., 2013). Within this broader persp ective we reimagine and create new roles  for our primary participants, those of “Learner-Teachers” that straddle the space between Teachers and Learners.  Embracing the affordances and constraints of co-designing this study (Jurow et al., 2016), we hypothesize and conceptualize that this hybrid role allows these learner-teachers to deploy unique learning processes as they synergistically learn from the community by taking responsibility for their own learning and guiding the younger learners through the practices (see Figure 1).

Students build a deeper understanding of content when they apply the ideas in r eal-world situations that are relevant to them (Chen & Yang, 2019). ## Research design and methods

We employ a design-based research methodology for this study (Barab, 2014; diSessa & Cobb,  2004). Our curriculum incorporates extracurricular activities of student interest into a ML modeling project that exposes them to the practices of STEM experts (NGSS Leads States, 2013). To build ML models, students use Google Teachable Machine (GTM), a free online ML model building tool that can be trained to recognize images, sounds, and body poses. The model output is the probability that the testing data belongs to one of the pre-defined classes. The key design conjecture is that as students collaborate to build ML models with training data generated by them engaging in activities of their interest, they would engage with the content and hence improve their learning (e.g., improved knowledge about ML and understanding of the process of training ML models). Learners use ML tools and collaboratively engage with the various aspects of the ML process, helping them collectively generate knowledge of ML and the model training process. We first implemented the unit with three adolescents on a weekend  in a pilot study (Sharma & Shen, 2025). This study reports the second iteration we implemented in a summer camp. We implemented this study during one of the  “STEM Days” (every Friday 9:30am to 4:00pm) of a sixweek summer camp (led by Dr. Perry) that emphasizes nutrition and exercise science and took place at a university in Southeast U.S. The summer camp participants (campers) ranged in age from 9 to 14 years old and about 12-18 attended the STEM Days. We recruited two high school students, whose parents are acquaintances of the first two authors, as learner-teachers. Riley (pseudonyms are used in this paper) was a 14-year-old ninth grader and attended public high school. Patrick was a 16-year-old eleventh grader and attended private school. As learners, they went through most of the unit via Zoom prior to the implementation with the campers. They also created their own ML models independently at home. Prior to implementation with the campers, we met to discuss their experience and learnings. As unpaid teaching assistants  (TAs), they were tasked with designing ML modeling activiti es and implementing them with the campers, while retaining  flexibility to adapt and modify base d on the campers’ interests. Pre and post tests were administered to the campers  on the day of implementation. Two weeks after implementation, we conducted semi-structured interviews with the TAs. We also did a follow-up interview with Figure 1 Community of Learners Patrick two months after the first interview. We so ught to discover, describe and answer the following research questions:

• How did the campers’ understanding of ML and the ML  modeling process improve in the pre-post

assessments?

• The learning processes the Learner-Teachers employed as they built ML models on their own.

• How and to what extent did the task of designing and implementing activities for the campers improve

the Learner-Teachers’ own understanding of ML?

• How and to what extent did the designing task chang e the Learner-Teachers’ understanding of the role

of interest in higher student engagement and learning?

A total of 12 campers attended the STEM Day when th is study was implemented. The timeline of the unit is shown in Figure 2.

Figure 2 Timeline of the Unit The 12 campers were  placed in four groups for the modeling activity. Th e groups were formed to be heterogenous in age. This was based on feedback from previous STEM days that the campers preferred working in groups with similar aged children. The groups were first guided through certain modeling objectives using different data inputs (images, sounds, poses). The instruction team elicited views from the campers on variations of these modeling targets that the participants wan ted to focus on, e.g., different types of body poses (squats vs lunges etc.). The GTM models generated by the campers were stored. The  pre and posttest were the same and consisted of 10 multiple choice items and were delivered using Google Forms. The test sought to elicit their understanding of relevant terms (AL, ML, and algorithms), and of elements of Data Science (quality and quantity of training data for ML models). The maximum score was 10 (1 per item). The interviews (lasted for about 30 minutes) were video recorded and transcribed. The interview prompts were broadly organized along two themes: The experience of Riley and Patrick as learners as they engaged with the unit, and their experience as teachers as they designed, implemented and modified these activities during the camp with the campers. For the first theme, we were interested in learning about the challenges they faced during the  modeling process, how they overcame those, their understanding of data and how it evolv ed during the modeling process. We also asked them to share their view of AI and ML overall, and if it changed after building ML models. Finally, we asked them to share their future interest in engaging with GTM on their  own and in teaching it to peers and family members. For the second theme, we were interested in how the experience of teaching affected their own understanding of ML. We also asked them about the challenges they faced in choosing activities to model for much younger learners, what were some of the considerations behind the design p rocess, and the flexibility they needed to adopt du ring the implementation if any. The interview  transcripts from Zoom were uploaded into Atlas (a c oding software) and thematic analysis methods were applied (Braun & Clarke, 2021).

## Findings

The four groups of campers created 15 GTM models (6 image, 5 pose, 4 sound). Some examples were: face recognition image models, squat vs lunge pose model, and a sound model to identify songs.  The posttest scores were significantly better than the pretest for the campers (Figure 3). The average test score for the 12 campers increased from 6.50 (SD = 1.3 1, Range: 4-8) to 8.75 (SD = 1.14, Range: 6-10). A Wilcoxon signed-rank test indicated that campers' change in test scores  was significant (W = 0, p < 0.05). We acknowledge a high student to teacher ratio (4:1) may have facilitated learning in our campers. The TAs experimented with building ML models independently and developed a robust conceptual and practical understanding of ML and the ML modeling process  as they built, evaluated, and refined the models. They built models alone, which they found straightforward and manageable but noted that  it would have been more interactive and engaging with others. Specifically, they realized:

• The importance of training data: They emphasized the importance of providing ample and ba lanced

training data, e.g., Patrick used around 200 images per class to ensure fair com parison and accurate differentiation, a concept he termed “fair trial”. After experiencing some failures during model testi ng, Riley understood that fewer samples might compromise the model's performance and saw the significance of balanced high-quality datasets.

## Conclusions and implications

This design was an attempt to introduce young learners and to tea ch them about the complex technology of ML in a project-based, interest-driven learning environment. The dual roles assumed by our Learner-Teachers resulted in a deep understanding of our learning objectives. The process of designing and implementing the unit for other students led them to reflect on their own praxis and use those learnings in their design and implementation. New challenges that arose in their role as TAs provided more unique opportunities to understand the ML proce ss. During implementation, they developed an appreciati on of the role of student interest, and the importance of collaboration in a project-based learning environment. There was a high level of visible engagement and interest from the campers during the implementation. Future iterations of suc h design should include different types of extracurricular activities, other ML tools, and be implemented with a larger sample of participants.  Harnessing the synergies that result when students take on dua l roles as Learner-Teachers in the community is an important area that needs to be explored further in future studies.
