# Distributed Creativity Mediated by Social and Material Interactions in a Collaborative Pop-Up Makerspace in India

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Collaborative making involves the coming together of makers, sharing resources in the situated environment, influencing each other’s ideas and design processes. The article aims to contribute to the understanding of social and material interaction in making, which can inform the design of socio-technical spaces that promotes creative outcomes. In this study, we investigate how makers engage with each other and with materials while working in ill structured problem-solving making context. We specifically look at data from a medical device innovation camp which is a pop-up makerspace setting, to assert the distributive nature of creative making. Our analysis suggests that social and material conversations are significant in shaping the making process, engendering distributed form of creativity.

## Introduction

Places like hackerspaces, FabLabs, schools, museums, libraries, homes, online communities, and temporary ‘PopUp’ makerspaces have been explored as part of the growing Maker movement to promote maker engagement with technology, afford knowledge sharing, and collabora tive creative production (Halverson & Sheridan, 201 4). Making often involves events of problem solving, where makers work in groups and utilise the available resources to arrive at feasible solutions. In this context, group creativity has become increasingly important with emphasis on understanding how creativity emerges from sites of collaborations. At the group level, creativity i s not an individualistic mental process but it is embedded a nd distributed in social groups and collaborative n etworks (Sawyer & Dezutter, 2009). As per this perspective, creative thinking is distributed via interaction between people, dialog between people and objects, or interactions over time (Glăveanu, 2014; Sawyer & Dezutter, 2009). The idea of design as a conversation with the situation  (Schön, 1983) opens the path to understand the int erplay of social and material aspects in making, where makers  converse with fellow makers and materials, resulti ng in creative design possibilities. Often the maker-mate rial interaction leads to  being, doing and becoming material conversations in finding creative solutions to desi gn problems (Gravel & Svihla, 2021).  Being  material conversations considers the materials as well-known and not improvable, whereas doing material conversations involve exploring known material affordances that can be utilised to make progress in solving a design problem. A becoming material conversation is characterised by transfor med purpose, reconfigurations and modifications of materials (Gravel & Svihla, 2021).

Drawing from all these prior literatures, in this paper, we investigate how maker and material interactions involving being, doing, and becoming conversations contribute towards the distributed form of creativity in a popup makerspace. The temporary makerspace for this st udy consists of a five-day medical device innovatio n hackathon attended by medical, design, marketing, and engineering domain experts. Drawing on the perspective of design as an inherent practice within the making process (Halverson & Sheridan, 2014; Dougherty, 2012), we consider the domain experts as makers, the process they engage in as the making process, and the medic al innovation camp setting as the pop-up makerspace. W e position the creative aspects of the making proce ss as distributed phenomena (Hutchins,1995; Glăveanu, 2014; Sawyer & Dezutter, 2009). Analysis Data for this study was collected as part of a larg er five-day medical device innovation camp organize d by the Biomedical Engineering & Technology Incubation Cent re at a leading engineering institute in India. The organizers facilitated team formation by enabling participants to form clusters around a predefined set of twenty medical problems generated by multiple doctors. Teams were provided with prototyping kits consisting of: basic hand tools (knives, screwdrivers), power tools (hand drill, pillar drill), marking pens, pencils, rulers, drawing chart papers, plastic sheets, tapes, glue, sticky notes, basic electronic components (e.g., breadboard, LEDs, jump wires, battery). The audio and video data along with field notes and observation logs for day 1 and day 2, of two teams-Team A and Team B-formed the data set for our analysis, as the rest of the data was not available due to hard-disk storage failure. We follow the case study methodology (Merriam, 2007) to investigate the role of social and material elements on distributed creativity in maki ng. We used the content logs to conduct interaction  analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) focusing on talk and use  of artifacts and technologies, to unpack the momen t-byICLS2022 Proceedings 1301 © ISLS moment emergence of distributed creativity during group work. We identified ‘significant moments’ for analysis by looking for discussions on design decisions, participants talking with and through materials, ideational dialogic exchanges between participants, and shared agency (Gravel & Svihla, 2021) in our dataset. Further we u sed the approach of being, doing, and becoming material conversations to analyze these episodes (Gravel & Svihla, 2021).

## Findings

We found that makers in the pop-up makerspace demonstrated episodes of collaborative idea transfer, negotiation, building shared knowledge, and taking part in being, doing, and becoming material conversations. We present these episodes in the form of two cases to show the  kinds of social and material conversations and the interplay of human and non-human factors that shape the distributed creative making processes. Case 1: Blow through unit The particular episode discussed in this case is fr om Team A’s first design session (Day 2 of the medi cal innovation camp). Team A worked on medical needs arising out of obstructive lung diseases to extract the sputum that gets deposited in the patients’ respiratory sy stem. Team A consisted of a doctor-Subject Matter  Expert (SME) (male), designer (male), mechanical engineer (male), and an electrical & electronics engineer (f emale). The team has at their disposal a nebuliser mask, an  intermediate PVC prototype previously made by the SME, plastic balls, steel balls and marbles, in addition  to the prototyping kit. The following excerpt illu strates how maker and material conversations together contribute to the creative making process of addressing the problem of air leakage.

Designer: [looks around] what if I have this mask [takes the nebuliser mask from the work table and inspects the profile].. and attach i t to the pipe... not with the holes [points to the holes in the mask].

SME [connects the PVC prototype with the mask by pressing the pipe entry to the mask hole] You want to have it like this, right angles? Designer:  We need to seal the holes to make it work.. we can tape it. Designer: It should be taped from inside, otherwise. [tapes the holes in the mask] SME: Let me try it now. [wears the mask and PVC assembly, then blows] SME:  I can feel it now.. the air, but it is reduced.

Here, Team A members are sitting around the assigned work table, investigating the intermediate PVC prototype (see Figure 1(a)) and subjecting it to tests and manipulations. Even though the nebuliser mask was present at the table for a long time, the designer is the one having a doing material conversation with the material, by inspecting the profile and suggests using it along with the pi pe. The SME acknowledges the idea, initiates materi al negotiation by connecting the PVC prototype and nebulizer mask in angular configurations (see Figure 1(b)). The makers move towards  becoming material conversations through manipulating the joi nt affordances of PVC prototype and the nebulizer mask, integrating into a single unit and covering the holes with tapes, thus giving the unit a transformed purpose for ease-of-use and minimized air leakages. Team A continued to work on designs which were based on the heuristic of air column vibrations. In the above episode, the mentor joins the team at the work table and questions the team members about  the principles used. The mentor tests the prototype with different kinds of loads (e.g., plastic ball, steel ball, marbles) to understand the solution approach and show the po ssible prototype responses to Team A, by utilizing the properties of the size and weight of different types of balls available. Thus he engages in doing material conversion with the balls, prototype, and enters into conversation with the team about the various outcomes. The prototype thus becomes an anchor point for further interaction thereby leveraging the social and material conversations for co-construction of knowledge required to improve their design. ## Discussion and Conclusion

We have presented two cases of making in a pop-up makerspace having the theme of medical device innovation, where domain expert makers and materials involved i n being, doing, and becoming material dialogues through which, they traverse through the problem and soluti on space. As the makers and materials get into such conversations, we find makers trying to explore the  available materials and related affordances, to wh ich the materials respond, effectively entailing creative formulations. The makers are found to approach mater ials with preconceived material properties, which are either embedded in the making as such, or transformed into  other qualities so as to address the problem scenarios. I n Case 1, we have seen the translation of doing to becoming material conversion in a sequence of interactions involving the designer, SME, PVC model, and nebuliser mask while negotiating the problem of air leakage. Similarly, in Case 2, we have shown instances where SME involved in becoming material interactions leading to the creation of a  proof-of-concept with a combination of mobile phone and plastic sheets. These sociomaterial conve rsations show materials are more than just mediator s, but taking up the roles as actors, similar to that of m akers (Latour, 1996), which reiterates the material  aspect of creative actions (Pickering, 1993). The findings po int towards the distributed creative process, where  team members and material resources were actively contri buting to tackle uncommon problems (Glăveanu, 2014; Sawyer & Dezutter, 2009). In both cases, the mentor s played a crucial role in problematizing and refin ing the guiding heuristics, which enabled the teams to make progress towards a useful solution. The findings from the study suggest that makers, ma terials, and mentors collectively contribute to the making actions thereby upholding our argument that creative making processes are distributed across the social and material elements of the situated environment. Analyzing distributed creativity in makerspaces usi ng the social and material conversations lens, may help us  reframe our outlook towards the natural emergence of creativity in making and calls for more attention t owards human and non-human factors in situated lear ning contexts. Further studies are required to expand ou r understanding related to the sociomaterial entang lement in creative making contexts.
