# The Relationship between Teacher-Student Interaction and Student Well-Being in South Korea

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Interactions between a teacher and students are vital not only for acquiring knowledge but also for student well-being. Although student well-being is an important goal of K-12 schools, teachers are less likely to focus on it when compared to academic achievements particularly in an exam-oriented society. This study investigates how elementary school students interact with their teacher and how teacher-student interactions are related to student well-being in South Korea. Nine video recordings of a class were collected to analyze teacher-student interaction frequencies, durations, and types. This study found that student well-being was significantly correlated with the frequency and duration of teacher-student interactions. The autonomy-supportive interactions were not significantly correlated with student well-being, which might be influenced by the school culture in South Korea. This study underscores the role of teachers in supporting student well-being.

## Introduction

Teacher-student interactions are crucial in education, serving as a key channel for imparting knowledge and values. These interactions greatly influence students' phys ical and mental health, happiness, life satisfactio n, and future skills (OECD, 2017). Interactions, especially marked by mutual respect, significantly affects students' engagement, socio-emotional development, school satisfaction, and ove rall well-being (Poulou, 2020). Therefore, understanding interaction dynamics is essential for effective educational out comes and student development. Interaction Analysis can offer insight into analytical techniques that a re needed to complement current methods for studying teachers (Walkoe & Luna, 2020).

In the past, education had emphasized the acquisiti on of skills and knowledge for individual success. However, there has been a recent shift towards enhancing the quality of life students, specifically focusing on student well-being. Despite this shift, the PISA student well-being report indicates that the well-being of students in South Korea is lower than the OECD average. Furthermore, Korean students report a higher percentage of dissatisfaction with their lives compared to the OE CD average (OECD, 2017).  Given the authoritative and competitive educational environment, it is imperati ve to focus on enhancing student well-being in South Korea. Also, it can suggest a need for a well-being-oriented educational suggestion in classrooms because the class time is an essential period shared by both teachers and students. A comprehensive understanding of these interactions  with quantitative and qualitative data, lays the foundation for in-depth suggestions on how teachers interact with stu dents and how they can enhance student well-being. This study aims to identify the frequency and patterns of classroom interactions between teachers and students and to understand their impact on student well-being. The specific research questions are as theses: First, how do elementary school students interact with their teachers? Second, what is the relationship between the teacher-student interactions and student well-being? Literature reviews Student well-being A number of studies on well-being have traditionally been based on the frameworks like Psychological Wellbeing and Subjective Well-being, which primarily focused on adults  rather than children or adolescents. T he PERMAH well-being framework, rooted in positive psychology, has gained widespread application in school and adolescent contexts. The PERMAH framework includes subcomponents of well-being as positive emotions, engagement, relationships, meaning of life, accomplishment, and health (Butler & Kern, 2016). It can be applied to schools by emphasizing that students experience a holistic well-being that goes beyond mere satisfaction. Teacher-student interaction and student well-being The interaction between teachers and students in cl assroom uniquely differ from other social environments. A common process of classroom interaction involves a cycle of teacher initiation,  student response, and teacher feedback or evaluation, known as the IRF pattern (v an Lier, 1984). This pattern represents a combinati on of two adjacent pairs: the first being 'initiation-response,' which forms the first part of the second adjacent pair, followed as the 'feedback' after the 'initiation-response.' Students also have autonomy in the IRF pattern, whether the student takes the initiative to make the opening utterance, or responding on their own without the teacher's choice (Waring, 2009). Active participation and autonomy of students are crucial not only for learning but also for students' wellbeing. For example, interactions that increase student aut onomy enhance intrinsic motivation and social and emotional well-being (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Conversely, students perceiving their teachers as strict and frequently admonishing reported a decrease in their well-being (Van Petegem et al., 2007).

## Participants

For the current study, video data were collected from a fourth-grade class in an elementary school in South Korea. A total of 22 students participated in this study, and f ive students, who had different levels of well-being, participated in interviews.

## Data collection

To collect data on student well-being, surveys were conducted among students who consented  to participate in the research. The survey used to collect was adapted from Butler a nd Kern's (2016) original questionnaire and modified to fit the Korean student context (number of items = 24, Cronbac h's α =.92).  To collect data on interaction between a teacher and students, 9 video recordings of classes were gathered. One video contains about 40 minutes of recorded lessons, which is one lesson time of an elementary school in South Korea.

## Data analysis

The collected classroom video recordings were repeat edly viewed to transcribe all teacher-student interactions. Unilateral teacher statements without student respo nses were not defined as interactions due to the ab sence of explicit student responses, which are main requirements  in the IRF pattern. Based on these transcriptions, interaction frequencies for each student and duration mean per one interaction were analyzed. Interactions were categorized into teacher-initiated and student-initiated based on the initial discourse's origin depending on the IRF pattern. In addition, teacher-student interactions were categorized into autonomysupportive and controlling ones. The autonomy-supportive interactions emphasized the integration of students' interests and intrinsic motivations into classroom activities (Reeve et al., 2014), whereas the controlling interactions relied on extrinsic and ps ychological control through rewards and punishments (Vansteenkiste et al., 2005).

Correlation analyses were carried out to examine the relationship between teacher-student interaction variables and student well-being. The  correlation analysis results were compared with qua litative interview findings to derive more valid conclusions.

## Results

Throughout nine lessons, 233 interactions were occurred between a teacher and 22 students. On average, each student interacted with the teacher 17 times during  the nine lessons (i.e., 1.89 times per lesson). In addition, the duration of an interaction was in average 30 seconds, which varied across students from 18 seconds to 41 seconds. The percentages of student-initiated interactions (M = 27, SD = 18) and autonomy-supportive interactions (M = 23, SD = 12) were low, which indicated teacher-directed instruction (see Table 2). ## Discussion

This study showed that teacher-student interactions  played an important role in student well-being. Student engagement is considered an essential component of learning, and it is necessary to elicit responses from students to increase the frequency of teacher-student interactions. Furthermore, based on the evidence that student-initiated interactions also related to student well-being, teachers need to create a positive classroom  environment where students are encouraged to ask questions or voice their opinions to the teacher first. The autonomy supportive interaction with students d id not make a difference in student well-being. According to the TALIS report published by the OECD  (2019), lack of time to be with students is one of the factors that teachers consider to be a constraint t o provide quality instruction. Teacher-student interaction is the most fundamental way to build a relationship betwee n teachers and students, so it is essential that th ey have frequent contact and time to talk together to build a strong relationship. In circumstances with limited interaction time, students are likely more affected by the freq uency and length of interactions with teachers than  by the teachers' interaction style. Furthermore, in Confucian culture valuing respect for elders, being reprimanded is not anticipated to adversely affect student well-being. The influence of controlling interactions on student well-being may vary depending on cultural contexts.

This study highlights the importance of monitoring and understanding the teacher-student interaction and focusing on students’ well-being in educational settings. This study has limit ations because data were collected from only one class in an elementary school. Future research is necessa ry to explore the relationships between teacher-student interactions and student well-being in diverse school contexts.
