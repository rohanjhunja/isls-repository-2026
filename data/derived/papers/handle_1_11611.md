# Preservice Teachers’ Engagement with Systems Dynamic, Block-Based, and Paper-Pencil Models

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This pilot study explores how preservice teachers engage with paper-pencil, systems dynamic, and block-based computational models during a two-week science methods course. Despite recognizing the benefits of computational modeling, participants preferred paper-pencil models for future teaching. Data from screencasts and questionnaires revealed challenges in using computational tools to represent scientific concepts and suggested that static paper-pencil models may limit scaffolding for systems modeling. Findings highlight the need for scalable professional development in diverse modeling practices.

## Introduction and theoretical framework

Modeling has long been recognized as a fundamental practice in science education (Council, 2012; NGSS, 2013). Despite its recognized importance, many science tea chers focus on teaching canonical scientific models  (e.g., DNA, the atomic model, cell structures) rather than  actively engaging students in building their own m odels (Böschl et al., 2023; Gouvea & Passmore, 2017). This highlights the need to prepare preservice teachers to support their students in creating and constructing models.  Most existing research on supporting preservice sc ience teachers in modeling has primarily focused on paper-pencil models  (Chang, 2022; Quillin & Thomas, 2015; Schwarz et al., 2009). While these traditional approaches are valuable, th ey do not fully address the emerging trend of the past two decades toward integrating co mputational models (Bielik et al., 2020; Grotzer et al., 2013). Computational models have demonstrated significant potential in enhancing students' computational and systems thinking, enabling them to understand complex pheno mena and solve real-world problems (Authors, 2023). We argue that scaling up the integration of computatio nal models into science classrooms requires strateg ies that embed these tools into the existing framework of sc ience education methods courses. This paper present s a pilot study with preliminary results from what should be a year-long study involving three secondary science preservice teachers, aiming to address the following research questions: What are the attitudes teachers have towards integrating modeling in the science classroom? What was the teachers’ experience while constructing models using each modeling tool, and to what extent have they applied each modeling practice (construct, evaluate, revise)? What modeling tools are pre-service teachers likely to use in their teaching residency, and why?

## Context and methods

The study took place at a rural university in the western U.S. as part of a secondary science methods course aimed at preparing students for careers in secondary scie nce teaching. The seven-credit course met on Tuesdays and Thursdays, with each day including a 75-minute and a 90-minute session, separated by a 65-minute lunch break. The modeling intervention spanned two weeks, totali ng 660 minutes (11 hours) of instruction. The first  author taught three 21-year-old students with life science backgrounds: two stu dents who identified as White males (Students A and B), and one student who identified as a Black female (Student C). All students had rur al backgrounds and resided in rural communities. Prior to the modeling intervention, students were familiarized with the K-12 Framework, and the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS)  and received materials introducing them to modeling. The intervention began with an overview of the modeling practice (Schwarz et al., 2017; Shin et al., 2022; Windschitl et al., 2020), followed by construction of three types of models (see figure 1) aiming to explain the spread of a pandemic. Students were positioned as evaluators of the modeling tool (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2007) revising their models according to peer input and instructor support. Initially, students were tasked with drawing a paper-pencil model, followed by a gallery walk and peer feedback. Fifteen minutes were given  for model revision, followed by oral presentation. Then, students constructed the phenomenon using  SageModeler, an open-source systems dynamic software (Damelin et al., 2017). As with the previous exercise, students presented peers with their initial models, received feedback, revised their models, and presented their final version. The same instructional sequence was applied when students used  MoDa, a domain specific-block-based and agent-based modeling tool (Fuhrmann et al., 2024) integrat ing computational models using domain-specific blocks (Wilkerson et al., 2015)  with the Bifocal Modeling framework. To conclude, students designed and implemented a modeling lesson, with peers and the instructor role-playing as high school freshmen. We utilized and validated a series of open-ended pre-, mid -, and post-intervention questionnaires. The pre-questionnaire assessed students' prior experience a ttitudes, perceived challenges and benefits regardi ng the modeling practice in science education. The post-questionnaire focused on students' overall experiences with the intervention and future intentions to integrate modeling into their teaching. Additionally, mid-intervention questionnaires were administered after experiencing each modeling tool, to explore students’ reflections. To capture students’ modeling process and strategies, we recorded screen captures  of their computational models. The questionnaires’ analysis was based on each question category. Screen captures were analyzed using the Evaluation Tool for Computational Models (Bowers & Eidin, 2024), allowing a more nuanced understanding of students' preferences and strategies when engaging with different modeling tools.

## Results

Students’ attitudes to modeling and prior experience-All three participants expressed positive attitudes  toward the modeling, highlighting both its cognitive and a ffective benefits. They emphasized modeling as a ve hicle for engaging students in systems thinking —a cross-cutting concept they had previously studied —by fostering students’ agency and autonomy. They valued modeling as a tool to help  students make sense of scientific phenomena by making abstract concepts and mechanisms tangible and accessible. Students experience of each of the modeling tools-Students found paper-pencil models easy to create and revise but noted their static and messy nature;  Student B viewed the artistic demands as a drawbac k. In contrast, SageModeler was praised for its intuitive  manipulation of variables and clear representa tion of relationships. However, students emphasized they need more training to in systems dynamic models. Screencasts revealed early struggles will setting “collectors” relationship, such as Student B inco rrectly linking virus spread to susceptible populations (see figure 1b)—later corrected after instructor feedback. Students appreciated MoDa’s user-friendly interface, plain-English, and intuitive model manipulation (Students A and C). However, bugs and confusing code logic, l ead to long, difficult-to-debug scripts. The instructor’s strategy—removing blocks one by one —helped at times, but when ineffective, frustration grew. Students ran simulations more often in MoDa, while SageModeler re quired instructor prompting. In both platforms, stu dents struggled to model immunity and mutation. In SageMo deler, they approached the  virus spread with an agent-based lens rather than a population-level view, leading to simplified or inaccurate rep resentations. In MoDa, students couldn’t program the behavior of  three population types, nor they could simulate reinfection after virus mutation. Peer review in SageModeler and MoDa was more collaborative, while paper-pencil feedback remained individual and conceptual.

Future plans of modelling implementation-All students intend to use modeling in the future. Students A and C preferred paper-pencil models due to familiarity, noting that computational tools like SageModeler and MoDa required further training. All agreed paper-pencil modeling should serve as scaffold for digital tools.

## Discussion

Students’ positive attitudes toward modeling stem f rom their understanding of models’ role in science,  likely shaped by their NGSS training and concurrent life science courses. As seen in prior studies, revision was the most time-consuming practice, while defining variables was the most successful. Peer review differed across modeling types: collaborative in computational tools, individual in paper-pencil models. Students highlighted paper-pencil models as scaffolds that support revision and dynamic thinking, though agent-based features in their static models posed challenges when transitioning to systems dynamic modeling. Despite 11 hours of instruction, students remained hesitant to  use computational tools, preferring paper-pencil models. This underscores the need for dedicated mod eling courses for pre-service science teachers. While our small sample limited generalizability, the study sh ows that even personalized support may not fully pr epare preservice teachers to implement computational modeling independently.
