# Students’ Collaborative Roles and Perceived Learning During Making Processes as Part of a Programming and Robotics Tournament

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examined the experiences of students participating in collaborative making processes as part of preparing for a national programming and robotics tournament in Finland. Data were collected in three tournaments (2021–2023) through student interviews (n=40) and a self-report survey (n=58). Preliminary results suggest that students adapted their roles during making processes. The collaborative roles were coder, fabricator, designer, performer, and team leader. Perceived learning outcomes were classified into technological, social, and thinking skills.

Today’s youth grow up in a society where global challenges threaten the survival of our planet and humanity. Escalating climate change, overconsumption in the Global North, the ongoing exploitation in the Global South, along with rapid and under-regulated digitalization and artificial intelligence make the future unpredictable. In response to these challenges, it is crucial to prepare youth for an uncertain future by providing opportunities to acquire a broad range of skills and find joy in learning. Maker education and digital game-making provide such opportunities through open-ended projects. In these projects, youth face unpredictable innovation challenges they collaboratively address using digital fabrication tools (e.g., Blikstein, 2013; Halverson & Sheridan, 2014; HughesRoberts et al., 2020; Kafai & Burke, 2015; Korhonen et al., 2022). In the current study, we examined comprehensive school students’ experiences in collaborative making processes as they prepared for a national programming and robotics tournament organized by Innokas Network in Finland. The Innokas tournament features five events: Freestyle, Dance/theater, XSumo, Rescue, and GameDev. Given the limited research on tournament contexts, we aimed to prioritize the students’ personal experiences of the process. Specifically, this study seeks to answer: 1) What collaborative roles did the students adopt during the making processes? 2) What were the students’ perceptions of their learning outcomes?

## Participants and data

Data were collected in three tournaments: a) 40 interviews in 2021 and 2022 with students from the Dance/theater, Freestyle, and GameDev events, and b) 58 self-report survey responses in 2023 from students across all five tournament events. Participants were students from comprehensive schools in Finland, aged 10–15 (grades 3–9). We conducted the semi-structured interviews remotely, adapting the stimulated recall method (Lyle, 2003). The interviewer shared the students’ project diaries or parts of their inventions on the screen during the interview. The interview themes included students’ opinions on the making process, student collaboration and roles, and perceived learning. Based on the initial analysis of the interview data, we developed a self-report survey including both restricted and open-ended questions related to the interview themes. We performed qualitative content analysis using ATLAS.ti software to the interview data and responses to the open-ended questions. Student answers were mostly concise, which facilitated a straightforward analysis process for forming categories. Students could describe multiple parallel roles or learning outcomes, so the categories are not mutually exclusive. For the survey data, we conducted statistical analyses using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.

## Preliminary results

Roles Our initial analyses suggest that how students collaborate and distribute their roles varies across events, teams, and phases of the making process. Many students told working on multiple tasks and adapting their roles depending on the phase. Some reported working together without clearly defined roles, while others explained that their teams assigned specific roles, which remained consistent throughout the making process. Furthermore, most students mentioned helping each other with one another’s roles. We classified the roles described by the students into five categories: 1) coder, 2) fabricator, 3) designer,

4) performer, and 5) team leader. Each role category involves slightly different tasks depending on the event. The

coder role was the most common, with each team having at least one member responsible for programming. Some students also reported assisting their team’s coder by providing emotional support and encouragement. The fabricator role includes building robots, sewing costumes, and crafting props. The designer role was the most versatile including ideator, graphic designer, level designer, choreographer, screenwriter, photographer, videographer, and sound designer, some of which were event-specific roles. The performer role was exclusive to participants in the Dance/theater event, involving acting, voice-acting, and dancing. Finally, the team leader role includes tasks such as reporting on the project, presenting the project at school, and encouraging team members. Perceived learning Students were asked to describe their perceived learning, and their responses were classified into three categories:

1) technological skills, 2) social skills, 3) thinking skills, each encompassing several skill areas (see Figure 1).

This category includes skill areas such as programming, building robots, making graphics and other visuals (e.g., editing videos), and using various tools like sewing machines, drills, and saws to fabricate props and costumes. Moreover, we examined the level of the students’ self-assessed technological skills on a three-dimensional measure in the survey: academic, artistic, and computational digital competences were evaluated on a 5-point fluency scale (see Korhonen et al., 2024; Laakso et al., 2021). The means for each dimension ranged between 3 and 4 (academic: M=3.63, SD=0.952; artistic: M=3.11, SD=1.075; computational M=3.38, SD=1.051) indicating that the students evaluated their digital competences to be rather fluent. Furthermore, the students emphasized that collaboration was an essential aspect of the making process, and they reported learning various social skills, including teamwork, expressing their own ideas and opinions, negotiating choices and decisions, and asking for help when needed. The students also mentioned learning various thinking skills, such as problem-solving, creativity and ideation, patience, and persistence. The students often perceived their learning of technological skills as being accompanied by the development of social or thinking skills. A 7th grader reported “I’m still like learning about more coding, like making games taught me that, and like a bit patience”, and a 3rd grader described learning “coding, imagination and, like social situations, how to like act in them”.

## Concluding remarks

This study explored collaborative roles and perceived learning outcomes of students participating in making processes as they took part in a national programming and robotics tournament. The student interviews and selfreport survey data imply that students adapt their collaborative roles flexibly throughout making processes. Five role categories were identified: coder, fabricator, designer, performer, and team leader. Perceived learning outcomes were classified into three categories, with technological skills being the most frequently mentioned, often accompanied by social or thinking skills. The participating students assessed their artistic and computational digital competences as more fluent than those observed in a representative sample of students from Helsinki, Finland (Korhonen et al., 2024). Furthermore, previous studies have shown that teaching advanced and creative technological skills has been insufficient for students (Fagerlund et al., 2024; Korhonen et al., 2024). Our results suggest that collaborative making processes can foster learning these skills, as well as bring about other valuable learning outcomes for participating youth. Additionally, our findings provide pedagogical insights to help teachers incorporate making processes into their teaching.
