# Trajectories of Practice: Characterizing Students’ Decision Making Agency in Science Investigations

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
An aim of science education is for students to be able to participate in science by making adaptive local decisions that interconnect and integrate scientific practices. We build towards this goal by articulating a trajectory of practice -- a series of moves students make to pursue the scientific aims of their investigations, and showing how this description can be used to investigate the role of curriculum in supporting students exercising decision making agency.

## Motivation for Trajectories of Practice

Describing science as a “practice” is an alternative to depictions of science as an ordered list or cycle of activities— e.g., “the scientific method” (Ford, 2015). While s cientific practice is composed of component practic es, expertise entails more than just an ability to successfully p erform these components in isolation. Practice fore grounds the decision making processes required to “adopt'' and “adapt” component practices to the needs of specifi c contexts (Manz, 2015), a kind of knowledge that Ford described as a “grasp of practice” (Ford, 2008). In this view of scientific practice, agency is central. In part this is because of the inherent uncertainty in the activity of science, which is often captured in spa tial metaphors that describe practice as unfolding in a “field” or “space” of possibilities (Pickering, 1995; Gooding, 1990). Navigating through this space requires scientists to engage in reasoning about where they are, where it makes s ense to gonext, and how such choices will be viewe d by the scientific community. In this way, the path through this space can be considered a unique trajectory t hat emerges as scientists encounter and respond to the particular difficulties and opportunities that they encounter as they attempt to satisfy their goals.

Pickering (year) described these choices as “free moves”, expressions of human agency made by scientists to pursue the aims of their investigation. These mo ves, or accommodations, are shaped in response to r esistances to achieving scientific aims. We propose the idea of a  trajectory as a way to characterize students’ scie ntific decision making as enacted in context, over time.

A trajectory of practice is guided by an orienting aim that describes students’ intentions for how to carry out their scientific investigations. Descriptively, ori enting aims are useful for cohering accounts of stu dents’ decision making in science investigations. They also situate resistances as resistances to aims, which is aligned with Pickering’s

(1995) description of the Mangle, and is implicit i n the account Manz (2015) gives of student activity  with the wild

backyard. For example, when Manz described “the cha nging nature of plants” as a resistance it is as a resistance to “students attempt(ing) to draw conclusions about them.” (Manz, 2015, p. 104) The trajectory further describes a series of accommodations to resistances, comprising local aims, generative moves, and evaluations (see Figure 1). Each of thes e components foregrounds student decision making. Local aims describe students’ bids to overcome a resistance, g enerative moves represent the construction of ideas  or practices adapted to meet the local aim, and evaluation repre sents students’ assessment of how well the move ser ved to accomplish the local aim and accommodate to the resistance. This work provides a way to identify and characterize student agency in scientific investigations, a necessary precursor to understanding the instructional and curricular features that support it. ## Study Context

This research takes place within an introductory-le vel undergraduate biology laboratory course. The ce ntral design emphasis is on students conducting their own investigations, in groups of three to four, by designing experiments with organisms, and conducting simulation trials within an agent-based computer model (Gouvea et al., in press).

Using Trajectories of Practice to Unpack Student Activity: Two Case Studies In this poster we illustrate two episodes of student groups’ scientific activity using trajectories of practice. In the first episode, Group A works to design an experiment. We describe how their moves, such as turning to the simulation to validate their ideas when stuck, sustains their design work in the face of theoretical and practical c hallenges to their initial experimental proposal. In the second episode, Group B works to make sense of unexpected experimental data by replicating their experiment on a computer simulation. We describe how their moves, such as carefully calibrating the simulation inputs to match their wet-lab design, leads to them expanding their conceptualization of the study system, through reasoning across and within these t wo data production modalities (experimental design and computational model).

We then show how this description of decision makin g activity can be used to identify features of the curricular implementation which contributed to sustaining Group A’s engagement in conducting the desig n of their experiment, and to expanding Group B’s conceptualization of the system.

## Discussion

This work is an initial attempt to expand the research on students’ decision making agency in science investigations. Our focus is on identifying evidence of students ma king local, adaptive decisions that contribute to a  sustained engagement in making progress towards their own research aims. We see this characterization of student-driven traj ectories as an important refinement of resistance a nd accommodation as a dynamic articulation of emergent, locally adapted scientific practice for two reasons. By making orienting aims an explicit construct we can conside r how resistances to aims  intersect with student framing of lab spaces. For instance, if a student frames the lab a s about demonstrating a fact about how plants grow,  and sets their orienting aim around doing so, then resistances cau sed by unexpected patterns in plant growth might pr ompt an accommodation of searching for experimental error, rather thandeveloping more productive sensemaking practices (Hayes, 2021). Furthermore, expanding accommodation s to include student decision making around local a ims, generative moves, and evaluation provides researche rs with a concrete way to identify evidence that students are engaging in a dialectic of construction and critiqu e that comprises adaptive scientific practice (Ford, 2008; Manz, 2015). With these markers identified, researchers can investigate the role of curricular enactment in supporting such student activity.
