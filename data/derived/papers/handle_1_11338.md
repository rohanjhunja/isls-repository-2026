# Sustainability in Doctoral Education? A Student-Experience Perspective

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The sustainability claim increases the need to integrate more interdisciplinary, collaborative research activities into doctoral education in engineering. This introduces new challenges and opportunities for educators and students. In this paper, we examined 12 doctoral students' experiences during their entire journey of gaining engineering doctorates. Using a novel journey mapping method, we collected the participants’ documentation about their academic paths, reflecting on activities, milestones, and emotions. The individual observations (n=416) were clustered into nine main categories, emphasizing the self-management challenges (30% positive), and non-PhD life factors (53% positive), in addition to the research process (88% positive). Positive experiences were linked to teamwork, social support, and interdisciplinary collaboration, while challenges included self-management and personal well-being. Corpus analysis further highlighted linguistic differences between the groups, reflecting variations in their academic cultures. Our findings emphasize the need for sustainable doctoral practices, balancing autonomy and social support to foster students’ well-being and research breakthroughs.

Sustainable doctoral education calls for practices and structures Doctoral education is a rising topic in education r esearch, as the number of students is growing, the practices are evolving, and the career tracks are becoming increasingly d iverse (cf. Bastalich, 2017). These, together with resource constraints, call for practices and culture to enhance sustainability in doctoral education, both in the learning process and its outcomes. Special attentio n should be paid to  social sustainability, such as academic wellbeing (Schmidt and Hansson, 2018). Doctoral students’ well-being is controlled by the paucity of supervision quantity and practices  (Cornér et al., 2017), while challenged by a high level of uncertainty and the academic culture that favors individualistic competition ove r collaboration with concurrent expectations of independence (Schmidt and Hansson, 2018).

Sustainable doctoral education targets for interdisciplinary sustainability breakthroughs in doctoral research. Doctoral students are studying sustainability topics with increasing interdisciplinarity and complexity, where interdisciplinary activities as well as research collaboration are critical for creating new research ideas and knowledge (Carr et al., 2018; Piccardo et al., 2022). This collaboration with fellow researchers and external partners beyond their own discipline can result in not only learning  and learning-to-learn (Carr et al., 2018), but also confusion, while students’ existing beliefs and identities are challenged. Nevertheless, Feng et al.  (2024) indicate that among master's students, these types of complexities in problem-solving and collaborative approaches are common experiences.

Less is known about doctoral students’ experiences and challenges in their learning journeys, as they are usually studied through survey data  (cf. Castelló et al., 2017; Jairam & Kahl, 2012). However, a  deeper understanding of the students' experiences and their temporal variation is crucial  for designing supportive and sustainable doctoral education practices. Further, we  lack knowledge about the practices that would enhan ce interdisciplinary and sustainability in doctoral ed ucation in engineering, and how these practices wou ld impact the social sustainability of these research communities. Here, we analyze experiences of doctoral students working in interdisciplinary and actively collaborati ng research groups  in engineering. We apply a journey mapping method, which has been rarely used in higher education research, but allows us to examine students’ experiences with a longitudinal and holistic approach. Thus far only Turner (2015) has applied journey plots  in a doctoral education context, studying four early-career supervisors and identifying the main experienced challenges.

In this paper, we make a comprehensive synthesis of  the key factors contributing to graduate students’ experiences in their individual doctoral journeys, and further build understanding of sustainable doctoral education. Systematic identification of student experiences contributes to the future development of sustainable supervision and peer support practices.  Two research questions guided our study: 1) What ki nd of experiences do individuals’ doctoral journeys entail, and 2) wh at similarities and differen ces are observed between the two student groups working in different kinds of collaborative research environments?

## Context and participants

We studied 12 recent doctoral graduates  (average graduation time 5.3 years)  in engineering at Aalto University, Finland. The Finnish doctoral education follows the Bologna process with no tuition fees, and students are usually employed or financed by personal grants. In engineering, theses include 3-5 peer-reviewed journal articles firstauthored by the doctoral student. Engineering provi des an interesting context of diversity of doctoral  students in terms of their backgrounds, interdisciplinary resea rch, and career goals; more than 75% of the g raduates are employed outside universities (Education Statistics Finland, 2025). The participa nts were studied in two groups within the same doctoral program, but under different academic cultures. Selecting groups with varying levels of collaborative and interdisciplinary practices (Carr et al., 2018) enabled exploration of academic cultures influence doctoral students’ experience over time. Group 1 emphasized belonging, subsidiarity, and peer learning, with each student having a diverse advisory team. The focus w as on strengthening doctoral education and interdis ciplinary practices. Group 2 included six students conducting  more independent research with traditional supervision, including weekly meetings and annual seminars. The first author coordinated the project for Group 1 but didn't collect data from them. The second and third author s worked with the first author but didn't partake i n data collection; the third author was in a different university unit.

## Data collection and analysis

To study doctoral students’ experiences in their st udy paths, we employ ed the journey mapping method (Marx, 2023; Robinette, 2024). The empirical data consists of doctoral students’ longitudinal visualizations of their doctoral thesis process. Participants reflected on their thesis journey in a workshop, utilizing e.g., old notes. Each participant created their own journey map  by document ing activities, milestones, resources, and other observations of their journey using sticky notes ar ranged chronologically. Next, they graded the individual observations from –3 (strongest negative impact) to +3 (strongest positive impact; Nilsson et al., 2016). We analyzed the data with mixed  qualitative, quantitative, and linguistic methods. First, our inductive qualitative content analysis (Elo and Kyngäs, 2008)  resulted in nine categories  of experiences, which were quantitatively studied in RStudio. Third, corpus linguistic analys is compared data with similar corpora  (MICASE, Simpson et al., 2002) for the triangulation of expressions and emotional content.

## Results

Doctoral journey experiences of individuals The 12 studied doctoral journeys illustrate how ind ividual the experiences are even within on e research community (Figure 1). Our inductive analysis formed nine main categories of experiences (Figure 2), each having 2–8 subcategories. The highest emphasis on experiences was on the research process (mean +0.70, range-3–+3; 88% positive), which was described by all 12 participants. In the research process category, eight different focuses were identified, including data processing and analysis, writing, getting published, research as well as publication planning, review process, synthesis writing and  pre-examination, co-writing, and submitting. The second most common category was self-management challenges and solutions (mean-0.7, 30% positive), with 41 self-management or personal wellbeing challenges. This shows that while most of the experiences of th e research process were positive, the self-management issues substantially hindered the doctoral journey. These observations highlighted personal wellbeing challenges, such as worry about not meeting the expectations, lack of motivation, and need for ps ychological help. Self-management challenges emphasized how the students understood they lacked work and project management skills, resulting in scattered work and spurious working hours. Participants also reported 15 coping strategies that emphasized the importance of, for example, physical exercises and mindset change, such as “learning to take critical comments positively”  and “…it continues to remind me that there is a "low" moment in research & study”. The example journey maps (Figure 1) illustrate how each participant experienced numerous simultaneous events and happenings, and how, for example, academic community and self-management challenges are emphasized throughout the journey. The third largest group of experiences was related to non-PhD life (mean +0.3, 53% positive), highlighting the significance of other aspects of l ife that still contribute to the doctoral journey. We cannot approach these doctoral journeys without acknowledging the impact of COVID-19 and its impacts on individuals’ lives and well-being. Another important exogenous factor was famil y; people were losing family members and having babies, but they also received critical supp ort and encouragement from their families. Further,  holidays and moving to a new country were emphasized, the latter causing mixed feelings. Overall, when studying all 30 subcategories with their highest rankings, the importance of teamwork was undisputed; external research collaboration (+2.5) and co-writing (+1.7) were ranked higher than getting a journal article published (+0.96), which grant s the student a personal incentive scholarship. Towards the end of the journey, thesis synthesis writing (+1.8) highlighted both the small  group support and the confidence resulting from experience-based learning, and the progress in research. The l owest-ranked categories all focused on personal well-being; (the lack of) university’s well-being services (-2.0) and personal well-being challenges (1.6). Students reported various challenges with bel ongingness (-1.4). Personal well-being challenges were more evident at the beginning of the process, whereas se lf-management challenges became more evident after the midway point of their studies.

Comparison of the student groups Overall, Group 1, with the actively developed supervision, had more positive experiences compared to Group 2 with more conventional supervision. Self-management issues were emphasized in the later stag es of the process and especially among  the students in Group 2. Both groups had similar ex periences and self-management challenges, but also about the importance of the academic community and social support in positive experiences. Interestingly, journal review processes were highly negative exper iences in Group 1 (-1.50) compared to the positive experiences in Group 2 (+0.55).

With the corpus analysis, we noticed more differences between the two groups. While both share similar discourse community characteristics (Swales, 1990), namely common public goals, methods  of communicating among members, participatory communication methods,  genres that define the group, lexis, and a standar d of knowledge needed for membership. Despite this, a basic analysis of lexis and comparing them indicates a notable difference in these groups.   For example, the word uncertainty in Group 1 is the 221 st word, while in Group 2 it is the 353rd word. Frequency lists, naturally, list the number of occurrences of the word in question. In this limited wordlist, when looking at word work, despite the rank in the groups being different (Group 1 n= 2, Group 2 n=19), it appears this is used simi larly in both groups. When looking at the word time and many others, the notable feature  is that they appear in Group 1, but do not appear i n Group 2. These words include context-specific baby, which allows us to assume this is something current and also something that influences this researcher’s life quite a bit. Word useful can be identified as a positive word while teaching and funding are quite neutral, which makes it perplexing not to see them in Group 2 words. The word struggling can be viewed as negative, as well as emotional, but is only seen used by Group 2.

## Discussion and conclusion

With a novel journey mapping and mixed method approach, we were able to identify the depth of doctoral students’ experiences as well as  the importance of research collaboration and social support in their doctoral journey and learning. Doctoral studies feel insecure and uncomfortable  due to, for example, an all-consuming endeavor to learn and develop their academic identity, or conforming to institutional expectations and unwritten rules (Appel and Dahlgren, 2003; Pretorius and Macaulay, 2021). Interestingly, t he collaborative experiences, especially external rese arch collaboration and co-writing were ranked as more important experiences than for example  getting an article published. These may indicate how the motivation for the doctoral studies is a pe rsonal journey of making a difference, rather thandoing a job or obtaining a qualification (Stubb et al., 2014). Concerning these personal ly meaningful  experiences, the socialization processes are known to foster doctoral students’ critical research skills and enhance their professional development (Khalid et al., 2023). Nevertheless, many doctoral students still suffer from academic loneliness and isolation, calling for more engaging and active efforts for collaborative research already during onboarding, and knowledge dissemination of these topics for everyone involved in the doctoral education process (Gardner, 2008). We also uncovered the significance of non-PhD life in the doctoral experience. The importance of family as a source of social support has been identified in previous survey studies (cf. Appel & and Dahlgren, 2003) and we align these observations well. We suggest that s upervision should acknowledge the various life laye rs of a doctoral student and emphasize their importance in the entity. Finally, COVID-19 strongly contributed to the experiences, workability, and social sustainability of their studies. The numerous observations about self-management challenges point out the unavoidable cha llenging experiences in doctoral studies, but also an urgent need to prepare students to be resilient to these. Students usually develop these work-life management skills by experience, whether purpo sely managing themselves, finding support, or making tradeoffs (Martinez et al., 2013). Doctoral programs hold vast potential in providing training and knowledge to support students in balancing acad emic responsibilities, high demands, and the resulting wellbeing issues. Investing in practices for self-regulation, to strengthen motivation, and setting realistic goals already during the onboarding process could significantly improve the doctoral journey experiences (Stubb et al., 2014). The identified differences between the groups suggest the academic community as a major source of support for doctoral studies. Teamwork’s role in meaningful experiences and research progress  was evident. Overall, our data highlights the uncertainty and isolation of academia, where the most positive experiences were linked to active engagement in learning and contrib ution-making (Sarikaya et al., 2017). For doctoral programs and research groups, we recommend more active collaboration and social support for doctoral stude nts through culture and practices. While the drawbacks and uncertainty are inherent in groundbreaking research, the research communities in engineering hold vast potential to provide resilience and recovery. Special attention should be paid to prevent students from overreliance on the support and to strengthen their ownership of the degree wor k; however this balance between a directive supervision approach and giving freedom is among the key challenges reported by doctoral supervisors, too (Lee, 2008). To validate these findings, and to capture their temporal trends and causalities, we will next expand our analysis and examine the experiences with a larger data set.
