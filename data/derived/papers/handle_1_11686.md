# Beyond Boundaries: Rethinking Family, Nature, and Learning with More-Than-Human ‘Wild’ Others

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As society faces large-scale environmental challenges, promoting positive relationships between people and nature is extremely important. To better understand how education can foster lasting relationships between children and nature, we engage with Ecoportraiture, drawing on narratives, go-along interviews, and creative reflections with families whose teenagers have been recognized for their environmental contributions. Preliminary findings from one case highlight the importance of adopting a family assemblage perspective to appreciate and understand how more-than-human wild others mediate practices of care and forge connections to broader ecological systems. The everyday interactions with more-than-human wild others serve as a boundary object, fostering empathy and intergenerational practices of care and respect for life.

Nature connectedness pathway to sustainable living People and more-than-human others are confronted with environmental and climate change challenges, making it clear that everyone faces significant threats to their survival (Shukla et al., 2019). Promoting positive connections between people and nature is an important pathway t o creating a sustainable society capable of facing and mitigating these threats (Barragan-Jason et al., 2021). Strong positive relationships between nature and humans are important predictors of people’s willingness to engage in pro-environmental behaviors, such as adopting sustainable household practices, participating in l ocal actions to mitigate environmental issues, and volunteering for conservation efforts (e.g., Galway et al., 2010). Designing learning that succe ssfully promotes a strong and lasting connection to nature requires bridging out-of-school and family learning practices with school-related activities. To do this, we must understand how positive and lasting relationships a re successfully promoted in children’s everyday lives, through out-of-school family interactions. Preliminary data are used to explore the potential of a family assemblage approach and Ecoportraiture to understand  how to promote lasting relationships between people  and nature. The overarching aim is to understand, document, and celebrate family practice s and experiences  contributing to sustained relationships between teenagers and nature. The study has significance in providing insights about formal and informal approaches to stronger human-nature connections.

## Conceptual framework

Nature connectedness is a complex and contested con struct encompassing emotional, cognitive, and behav ioral dimensions of how humans relate to the living world. While some theories emphasize experiential, cogni tive, or identity-based bonds (e.g. Schultz, 2002), critics argue this reinforces duali sms (Fletcher, 2017). Alternative frameworks, such as Naturecultures (Haraway, 2003),  highlight entanglement and co-existence. This research takes a relational epistemological approach, recogn izing that hu mans, more-than-human beings, and inanimate entities exist in mutually reciprocal, dynamic, and  always-becoming relationships (Bang  & Marin, 2015).  The environmental learningscapes framework (Ardoin & He imlich, 2021) extends this by positioning families as boundary objects (Star, 2010), shaping multispecies  learning. By combining these frameworks, we  explore coproductive, dynamic relationships within interconnected social-ecological systems.

## Methods

Portraiture (Lawrence-Lightfoot & Davis, 1997) blends aesthetics and empi ricism to create evocative narratives that engage both academic and broader audiences. Ec oportraiture (Piersol, 2022) extends this by integr ating posthumanism and new materialism, highlighting huma n, more-than-human, and non-living entanglements, fostering hope, resilience, and coexistence.  By focusing on everyday practices of hope, resilien ce, and wisdom, Ecoportraiture offers a counter-narrative to prevailing discourses of environmental and societal despair. This paper draws on preliminary data gathered from one family: Urso is 18 years old and has been recognized in Canada for his conservation and outreach volunteering projects in the local community. Using a mix of interviews, family photo reflections, and creative activities, this research iteratively explores themes and practices central to familynature connections.

## Snippet of findings

This ongoing research highlights the potential of a family assemblage approach in understanding how more-thanhuman wild others shape family-nature relationships. One emerging theme is attentiveness to everyday encounters. Sitting at the kitchen table, Urso and his mother watch crows outside, imagining their liv es and reflecting on human impact. Later, Urso gently resc ues a spider, recalling his grandfather’s quiet,  lifelong reverence for small creatures. His reflections extend to wild animals: “Seeing bears, I wonder if they have enough food… We separate ourselves, but we’re all just try ing to get through the day.” Through stories, memor ies, and visuals, Urso illustrates deep care for often-overlooked wild beings, fostering intergenerational  environmental awareness. In Urso’s family, the everyday presence of and one-off encounters with more-than-human wild others — whether crows in the backyard, spiders on the wall, or geese —fosters intergenerational bonds and shared practices of care. The familial ethic of respect for life is transmitted through subtle, embodied practices rather than explicit instruction. The family interactions highlighted are part of a relational framework that views human s as interconnected with more-than-human others, emphasizing shared existence.  These practices extend beyond the traditional nuclear family unit to include wild more-than-human others.

## Discussion

The initial findings  demonstrate how more-than-human others mediate human-human relationships within families and connect individuals to the broader nat ural world. Individual  wild more-than-human others  create opportunities for connection and reflection on the impacts of human actions on the natural world, fost ering empathy across both species and generational boundaries. These entities serve as boundary objects through which families extend their sense of relationality to inc lude the wider natural world. Their wildness contra sts sharply with that of companion animals, which, as some argue, reinfor ce the human-animal boundary by existing within human-constructed environments and being placed in unequa l power relations as family dependents (Charles, 2016).

Adopting a family assemblage perspective recognizes  and celebrates the significance of more-thanhuman wild others in family life. This perspective enables a richer understanding of how relationships with nature are promoted and maintained. The family assemblage encompasses anything that contributes to the becomi ng of family life. However, this does not imply that fami ly is all-encompassing, but rather that it has the potential to include anything. Family should be viewed as a dynamic and evolving concept, defined by the activity of becoming with ever-changing members through everyday—or occasional—encounters. The concept of family assemblage allows for the exp loration of ‘family’ in terms of fluid, growing, or spontaneous relationships within a multispecies wor ld. Ecoportraiture provides the framework to delve further into the role of more-than-human others and non-living entities in the stories and environmental le arningscapes of participating families. By integrating these ins ights, we can better understand the mechanisms thro ugh which intergenerational practices of care and respect for  the natural world emerg e. This understanding contributes to a more sustainable and interconnected vision of human-nature relationships. Furthermore, this enriched perspective can help bridge out-of-school and family learning practices with school-related activities, effectively fostering strong and enduring relationships between humans and nature.
