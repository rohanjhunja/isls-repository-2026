## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
It is important in education for students to build skills. Underrepresented students often feel less welcomed and accepted and often fail to thrive in higher education. Mentoring in higher education can mitigate some of the challenges underrepresented students face, in turn helping them succeed. This poster describes a program designed and implemented to support student knowledge, confidence, community, emotional well-being, and career readiness.

## Introduction

In education, it is important to focus on assisting  students in building content knowledge and reachin g their academic and professional goals. It is also importa nt for students to learn how to learn (Barron & Dar lingHammond, 2008) and build skills related  to creativity (including problem-solving, critical thinking, design thinking), oral and written communication, and soci al and emotional well-being (Brown McNair et al, 2016; Cook-Sather, 2018; Duran et al., 2020). Mastering these skills are critical for s uccess in higher education, but importantly for the workplace (Deming, 2017). Student engagement, belonging, relationships, inclusive campus spaces, and instructional mindsets are also part of student success in higher education (Melguizo et al., 2021). Prior research reports underrepresented students (e.g., students of color, first-generation students) feel less welcome d and accepted and often fail to thrive in higher education institutions compared to their white, continuing-generation, and higher-income peers (Ostrove & Long, 2007). Having program s in place to support student success for underrepresented minoritized (URM) students and understanding which programs foster student success is vital for these students. Gainin g a better understanding of these markers of studen t success for URM students to thrive lifts them in school and in thei r future workplaces. Understanding and differentiat ing student success programs also provides equity-based learning environments for all students. Mentoring within higher education can mitigate some of the challenges  students face  and promotes student success (Collier, 2017) and skill development. Carefully prepared instructors, teaching assistants, and peer mentors can provide purposeful experiences to culti vate meaningful interactions and connectedness. Act ive, student-centered learn ing and purposefully designed, structured experienc es can provide extraordinary, memorable, meaningful, and transformational learnin g experiences (Duerden et al., 2018).  Peer mentoring relationships aim to help students navigate and sta y in school, but also to thrive and succeed in scho ol and life. This triad of focused mentoring from peers, teaching assistants, and instructors promotes problem-solving, critical thinking, and design thinking skill development, connectedness and the ability to thrive. With increases in student diversity and URM students, peer mentoring activities should focus on  cultural mindfulness and interactions and experiences because they can transform a student’s learning experience (Latham et al., 2020). Purpose Through a collaborative partnership between the University and a non-profit community partner, this work developed and implemented a holistic support program for URM students, targeting their knowledge, confidence, attitudes, emotional well-being, creativity skills, career readiness, and community building. We aimed to develop a model for student success for URM students and develop the capacity of the people associated with the program. Theoretical frameworks This research was guided by frameworks focused on the URM students and the people around them (e.g., faculty, peer mentors). For the students, this includes self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan, 2012) and social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1977) and for the other people a culturally responsive pedagogy called warm demanders (Ware, 2006). Self-determination theory focuses on promoting student i nterest in learning, valuing education activities, behavior regulation, motivation, and confidence in their own ability and capacity. Social cognitive theory suggests that learning occurs in a social context with dynamic interaction. The theory considers a person’s past experiences which factor in whether behaviors will occur, with the goal of understanding how people regulate their  behavior through control and reinforcement to achieve their goals. The warm demander pedagogy  provides an approach for educators to support students’ empowerment with their intellectual, social, and emotional knowledge (LadsonBillings, 1994). A warm demander educator is tough-minded, has high expectations for the students, has a nononsense and disciplined learning environment, and wants them to reach their potential (Irvine & Fraser, 1998).

## Research methods

The research included 12 students enrolled in a general education program for non-matriculated students with the aim to matriculate into a university. They were enrolled in the same courses each semester as a cohort, to provide cohesive support across instructors, peer mentors, and teaching assistants. The program include d multiple elements focused on student support; these wrap-around services included highly trained peer mentors modeling successful academic practices, activities to build relationships of trust, career development workshops, experience design activities, and more that were iteratively designed and impleme nted. E xploratory research data w ere collected from all students in the form of semi-structured interviews, observations, and a focus group. Data were analyzed to identify common themes using thematic analysis, with trustworthiness activities of member checking and peer debriefing.

## Results and conclusions

The wrap-around services in the program are being continuously refined to the needs of the students. The main themes from the research data include d: a) gratitude, b)  being organized, c) confidence change, d) universit y knowledge, and e) increased autonomy. The themes re port the students appreciated the experience and ga ined different skills. The wrap-around services did support them and their ability to thrive during this year. The hope is they can transfer these skills as matriculated s tudents in subsequent years. This has implications for URM students whoneed extra support early on, and that universities can target student needs in culturally relevant ways.
