# Comparing In-person Vs. Online Professional Development for Brazilian Science and Technology Teacher Trainers

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Pre-service and in-service teacher education in Brazil has increasingly moved online, with 6 out of 10 teachers graduating from exclusively online programs (Todos pela Educação, 2022). While there could be benefits to online learning in terms of access, evidence from the learning sciences and teacher accounts call for limitations of such approach, and more in-person learning experiences in the context of school systems in developing countries. Based on evidence from the design and implementation of a hybrid professional development constructionist learning program, we aim to shed light on the challenges and affordances of synchronous, asynchronous, and in-person modalities for professional development.

## Introduction and background

In the past decade, Brazil has attempted to improve  education by implementing new curricular standards  and recognizing the role of teachers in these reforms. In-service teacher professional development (PD) has become a central focus as research highlights its importance in enhancing student performance. Ongoing PD for teachers of all levels and subjects is essential to meet 21st-century educational demands and fulfill governmenta l and public expectations (Abrucio & Segatto, 2021). In 2022, 60 % of teachers in Brazil obtained their teaching degrees through 100% online programs (Todos pela Educação, 2022). With the Covid pandemic, online teacher education increased even further with PD for in-service teachers also moving online. In many states  and municipalities, it has remained online ever since. Yet a recent study found that 84% of teachers believe in-person PD better prepares them for teaching (IPEC, 2023). Based on a study co nducted as part of a non-voluntary PD program for teacher trainers (TT) in Brazil, this paper discusses the challenges and affordances  of online (synchronous and asynchronous) and in-person modalities for teacher PD.  In particular, the PD needs of teachers in developi ng countries might differ from those of more advanced educational systems, since the baseline of knowledge, access, and technological fluency might be very different, and public oversight over corporate universities less strict. Our program intended to foster the integration of t echnology and science in schools through a PD with TT where hands-on investigative activities, reflection and co-design were at the core. The initial expectation fo r the asynchronous online component was t hat it would scaffold the learning in the other mod alities. However, as the program evolved it was observed that the asynch ronous modality, although important, did not fit in to TT's agenda. Considering the relevance that these three modalities have on PD, this paper seeks to explore the following question: what are the affordances and challenges of online asynchronous, synchronous and in-person modalities for transformative PD, especially in school systems in developing countries?

## Methods: Program design, data collection and analysis

The program was designed and implemented by a group  of researcher-instructors. The program targeted fifteen science and nineteen technology TTs, comprising the entire cohort of such educators from all Regional Boards of Education (RBE) of a very large city in Brazil, wit h over 1 million enrolled students (SME/SP, 2023). It was a non-voluntary PD as all science and technology TTs of the municipality were required to attend by the Department of Education of the city. Organizing the agenda of all TTs for this program with several in-person and synchronous hours considered, was complex and therefore asynchronous work was also designed into the program, which was implemented during 13 months throughout 2022 and 20 23. To reach the program’s goals, one strategy was to support TTs to design and implement their own PD pr ograms for teachers to integrate science and techno logy in the city’s public schools, focusing on practice-based science learning (Schwarz. et al., 2017; Osborne, 2014; Duschl, 2008) scaffolded by a constructionist approach (Papert,  1986; Holbert et al, 20 20; Valente & Blikstein, 2019).

In this scenario, the program was designed to strengthen collaborations between science and technology TTs through the co-design of new PD programs for teachers. The program  was comprised of the following modalities: (i) asynchronous online, initially designed for approximately 6 hour s per month, offered resources and tasks for: (1) deepening their understanding of the theoretical foundations of constructionism, practice-based science education, backwards design and strategies for hete rogeneous group work, (Cohen & Lotan, 2017; Wiggins & McTighe, 2005), and (2) creating opportunities for metacognitive reflection on the learning happening during in-person workshops. The asynchronous tasks were held in an online platform, using multimodal approaches (texts, videos, podcasts); (ii) synchronous online meetings, 3 hours per month, were held in an online meeting platform, and encompassed case studies, analysis of activities (lessons plans) using rubrics, and co-design sessions. All the activities happened in small groups; and (iii) in-person meetings which happened monthly, had a duration of 6hs each and happened in the makerspace. Workshops commenced with a welcome over coffee and food. The agenda, goals, and expectations were presented,  followed by small group discussions sparked by a provocation in the form of an anchoring phenomenon.  This led to hands-on workshops where participants, with autonomy, engaged in investigation and creation usi ng activity and resource  cards (Cohen & Lotan, 2017). Sharing the process, products and reflections was w eaved into the workshop making sure TTs experienced  what was expected for teachers and students to experienc e. The co-design of PD for teachers was also an important element of these in-person workshops. It is important to make clear that while we intend to compare and illustrate the affordances and challenges of the different modalities, we acknowledge that the activities differed significantly in each format due to our learning goals and the nature and possibilities of each modality. We collected data from semi-structured interviews and written feedback forms fille d in by participants after every workshop and online meeting. Interviews were conducted with 14 out of 34 participants who chose to participate on a voluntary basis. Of the 1 4 interviewees, 5 were from science and 9 from tech nology TTs. Their personal characteristics, such as gender (86% ident ified themselves as women), educational background (43% have a master’s degree), and experience (64% have m ore than 10 years of work in the RBE), did not appe ar to significantly influence their responses. Interviews  were transcribed (18 hours) and analyzed together with feedback forms (N = 154) by open coding, breaking d own the text into small chunks based on the codes-synchronous, asynchronous and in-person-to categorize all instances where participants spok e about the three different modalities. Data was then analyzed to fin d evidence of the challenges or affordances related  to each modality. Two researchers cross-checked and validated each other’s code selections. ## Findings

In this section, we describe the challenges and affordances associated with the three modalities of the program. The main challenge faced by participants regarding the asynchronous modality was the time to conclude all readings and tasks. Our interviews showed that, particularly in the context of a large school system in a developing country, TTs are overworked and have several duties  and sudden unexpected demands-thus, finding ti me for asynchronous work was very difficult. Under pressure from other requests and under resourced city governments, the first thing they dropped was the online asynchr onous individual work, as expressed by the majority  of participants. While all participants in terviewed see the value of diving deeper into theoretical foundations and reflections, they all expressed the challenge of fi nding the time for the asynchronous activities. Rea ding papers was particularly challenging, especially “at the en d of the month things get more intense and we didn' t find time to read”. Figure 1 also shows a steep downward tren d in the tasks concluded on the online platform thr oughout the program. To tackle this challenge, we adjusted the required times for activities decreasing the volume from 6 to 2 hours a month, but we still saw a significant decrease in participation. We continued providing r eading and references, but as optional tasks. According to the  TTs, even not managing to do everything, having th e material there has been useful: 33% of participants interviewed said they were using texts, activities and prompts from the online platform in their own PD programs with teachers. As a TT mentioned: “I have been using everything from the course…I send my teachers all the texts”.

Balancing hands-on learning and reflection time in the in-person and synchronous modules was initially a challenge.  Research instructors planned learning experiences tailored to fit within the meeting duration, however, participant feedback indicated a need for more reflection and discussion time. In response, s ubsequent meetings were adjuste d to allow for more reflection time, addressing thi s challenge. The main challenge in the synchronous meetings was making sure all TTs could access the prompts, documents, and groups from any type of technology (laptops or phones). After a first se ssion where TTs had to use a collaborative cloud dr ive during the session, it was observed that it was necessary to have a clear map with links and prompts available prior to the session. Another challenge in the synchronous momen ts was the fact that many TTs were in their workspa ces in the RBEs where other demands came in constantly, di stracting them and resulting in less engagement in group activities. Close mediation by the instructors was necessary to engage participants and make sure all materials were available and clear. Connectivity, even in a l arge city in Brazil, was also a challenge: internet  failures disrupted many sessions.

The main affordances pointed out by participants regarding the asynchronous modality were related to how the format, the content and the activities help ed them deepen their theoretical foundations and re flect upon the learnings during moments that best suited them. As a participant recognized the value of a place where to find relevant materials for their own PDs, “the theoretica l foundations are so important, sometimes I do not have the time to read, but it is good to know they are there, that I can go back to them with time and focus”. For instructors, the online individual tasks were also an interesting way of checking on participants’ understanding. Synchronous and in-person workshops were highly valued by participants due to  the possibility of collaborative work and integration between teams of technology and science  TTs who seldom have the chance to interact. These modalities also allowed for an opportunity for collective reflections on case studies and the co-design of a PD for their own teachers. As highlighted by TTs, “Group work and co-planning worked very well” and "It was great to work together with our Technology partners”. A positive aspect of the synchronous modality was the possibility of TTs attending the PD without any commute time spent. The in-person experiences enriched the participants' understandin g of how science and technology integration could happen for the building of knowle dge in both areas and also inspired a culture of co operation and progress for new PD planning by the TTs. As rep orted by a TT, “The power of the in-person workshops lay in their ability to catalyze the fusion of science and technology didactic sequences, emphasizing that  true innovation emerges when disciplines converge, and collaboration becomes the heart of transformative education”. After nine in-person workshops (about 56 hours) it was clear that  TTs that had never collaborated before, even working side by side in their RBE, now felt part of  a community, open to active listening, exchanging ideas, and taking this experience to the co-planning of a PD for the teachers in their territor ies. The commitment of TTs in the in-person workshops was higher than in the synchronous  or asynchronous modalities, as seen in the small presence variation shown in Figure 1. As a TT mentioned, “so, I work in the same room as J., but we are usually doing different things, this has been a moment we c an think and work together.” Another TT complemente d by saying “the activities proposed  in the in-person workshops are favorin g integration (...) it is really mobilizing a series of things here (in the RBE) related to how we think and how we have to look and consider each other.”.

## Discussion and conclusions

Social, political, and technological shifts are dri ving transformations in education, calling for ongo ing teacher education to adapt. Public education complexities i n Brazil and in the Global South require enhanced s kills by teachers for equitable learning, emphasizing the ro le of teacher education and communities of practice. Our research analyzed teachers’ perceptions of synchronous, asynchronous, and in-person modalities in a blended PD program. Initial engagement spanned all modalities,  but online parti cipation waned significantly due to time constraints, while participation in in-person activities remained stable and was crucial for the program’s success.

Our findings suggest that, to guarantee asynchronou s work during a non-voluntary PD, time during wo rk hours should be allocated for these online tasks. Regarding the in-person modality, educators highly valued the hands-on experiences, peer collaboration, and co-design. Group work during in-person workshops not only led to meaningful learning for TTs, but also promoted enga gement and the building of a strong community that is now implementing PDs together and organizing an online platform to house a community of practice for techn ology and science TTs of the municipality. During a recent implementation of a PD designed by TTs during the program, a participant said: “This would have never happened  if we did not have this time and space together to  learn and co-design a PD”. Photopoulos et al. (2023) in a resear ch with 336 undergraduate students also reported mo re engagement and learning in in-person modality due to the possibility of face to f ace bonding and more effective group work. As we witness a growing trend for onlin e PD programs, the findings here question their eff icacy, suggesting that in-person experiences are essential for sustained engagement, learning, and community building. This issue is possibly not intrinsic to online lear ning everywhere: however, our work suggests that th e fast move toward online teacher PD in developing countries, given the context and labor conditions in those locations, could present significant risks for those teachers to eff ectively improve their practice. Online PD in those  under resourced contexts might seem like a “democratizing” force due to its low cost, but our data suggests that teachers simply do not have the right conditions to consume the material. Thus, and perhaps counterintuitively, the cost of face-to-face hours might be nominally higher, but in the lo ng run they might be more productive and crucially important for teachers in countries such as Brazil.
