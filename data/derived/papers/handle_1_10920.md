# Neuromyths About Learning and the Allure of Neuroscience

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research has shown that neuromyths — misconceptions about the brain, learning, and development — are prevalent among educators and the general public. The seductive allure of neuroscience explanations suggests that even irrelevant neuroscientific explanations enhance information appeal. The present study examined if neuromyths become more credible and shareable among the general public when paired with irrelevant neuroscientific explanations. Findings indicate no increase in credibility or shareability. Further research is needed to determine the origins of neuromyth prevalence.

## Introduction

Numerous neuromyths — false beliefs about the brain — persist among educators and the general public, and likely lead to ineffective educational practices (OECD, 2007; Pasquinelli, 2012; Torrijos-Muelas et al., 2021). For instance, misconceptions like "male and female brains function differently" or "the first three years of life define all child’s future learning" may influence program deve lopment and cause discrimination at school (Pasquinelli, 2012; Torrijos-Muelas et al., 2021). The reasons for the prevalence of these myths remain unexplored. However, neuromyths are often paired with neuroscientific ex planations and brain images while disseminated onli ne. For example, the following excerpt from a popular UK tabloid article claims men and women “are as different as alien races” by using distorted findings and the image from a study about brain wiring (see Figure 1): Figure 1 Image from Ingalhalikar et al., 2014 used in the press article (1) Men also have better connections between the front and back of the brain... This means they are better at things such as learning to swim or … parking a c ar. Women are better at, for example, remembering a face, which means making connections between different parts of the brain. The results are likely to be seen as supporting the theory behind best-selling pop psychology book … – that the sexes are as different as alien races (1).

Moreover, the series of studies on adding neuroscience explan ations to psychological findings showed the so-called seductive allure of neuroscience explanation s effect, where even irrelevant neuroscience explanations increased the credibility of information (Weisberg et al., 2008). This effect was extended to the effect of brain imaging on the credibility and quality of scientific reasoning (McCabe & Castel, 2 008). We believe that by combining the existing research on the effect of  seductive allure of neuroscience explanations and the studies on the prevalence of neuromyths, we could have an o pportunity to explain one of the reasons for the sp read of misbeliefs about the brain, learning and development.

Based on the literature review, the main research questions are the following:

• RQ1. Does a neuroscience explanation improve the reported credibility and shareability ratings of

neuromyths in a general public sample?

• RQ2. Does a neuroscience explanation accompanied by a brain image improve the reported credibility

and shareability ratings of neuromyths in a general public sample?

## Methods

All study materials, including the preregistration, are  available via: https://osf.io/gjhc4/. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before engaging in t he study. The sample consisted of 300 general publi c participants aged 18 to 76 (m = 40.04; sd =12.32) recruited in the UK through the Prolific platform. There were 4 (neuromyth) * 3 (a) control; b) neuroscience  text explanation; c) neuroscience text explanation and brain image) conditions. Each participant saw four neuromyths corresponding to one of the three conditions studied. The order of neuromyths, as well as which condition  participants saw, was pseudo-random. Each stimulus was introduced to a participant with the following text: “Imagine you are scrolling your  social media feed, and you see the following post”. This i ntroduction was made to put the context of seeing t his information in a real-life setting. After each neuromyth, participants we re asked how credible they found the information presented in the post on a 7-point Likert scale (1-Not credible at all; 7-Extremely credible) and how likely they would share that information when given an occasion (1-Not likely at all; 7-Extremely likely).

## Results

A one-way ANOVA was conducted on each neuromyth separatel y to check the difference in credibility and shareability ratings between neuromyths and conditi ons. This type of analysis was chosen to account fo r the random effect of participant since each participant saw all four neuromyths (within-subjects factor) in one of three conditions (between-subjects factor). The results of these analyses indicate no significant differences in credibility ratings between the conditions (Myth 1: Zero to Three (F(1, 298) = 0.131, p = 0.718, ηp2 = 0.004); Myth 2: Leftvs right-brained thinkers (F(1, 298) = 0.03, p = 0.863, ηp2 = 0.001); Myth 3: BrainG ym (F(1, 298) = 0.293, p = 0.589, ηp2 = 0.001); Myth 4: Male and female brains are different (F(1, 298) = 1.267, p = 0.261, η p2 = 0.004). The results also indicate no significant difference s in shareability ratings between the conditions fo r all neuromyths (Myth 1: (F(1, 298) = 1.353, p = 0.246, ηp2 = 0.005); Myth 2: (F(1, 298) = 0.244, p = 0.622, ηp2 = 0.008); Myth 3: (F(1, 298) = 0.28, p = 0.597, ηp2 = 0.009); Myth 4: (F(1, 298) = 0.317, p = 0.574, ηp2 = 0.001).

## Discussion and conclusion

The results highlight that neuroscience information doe s not necessarily improve the credibility and share ability of neuromyths about learning and development. One possible explanation might be the issue with the replicability of the original studies of the seductive allure of the neuroscience explanations. Some further research directions could be further analysing the data collected for the current study  and conducting a preregistered replication of the original seductive allure of the neuroscience explanatio ns study. Future research should also explore lay perceptions of the brain, addressing prevalent neur omyths while acknowledging the limitations of the c urrent study, such as the limited number of stimuli and po tential biases in framing stimuli.  Understanding the reasons for the prevalence of neuromyths among educators and the general public, including students’ parents, could help eliminate harmful educational practices and discrimination based on misconceptions about the brain and learning.
