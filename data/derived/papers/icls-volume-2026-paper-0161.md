# Learning Through the Arts: Reconstructing Disabled Identities in an Undergraduate Course

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Dominant medical-model narratives position disability as deficit. This descriptive qualitative case study examines how arts-based learning in an undergraduate course supported identity reconstruction for three self-identified disabled students at a U.S. re search university. Using students’ artifacts and post-course interviews, we traced “reconstruction episodes” marking meaningful stance changes and connections between course encounters and new self-understandings. Findings show students adopted social and cultural perspectives in how they talked about disability, used the arts to make sense of their own experiences and others’, and repositioned themselves as agentic participants in academic and personal communities.

Disability is largely viewed from the medical model, seeing disability a s deficit and something that needs to be cured (Baglieri et al., 2011; Goodley, 2017). This is one way to see disability, but not the whole story. There are other ways to understand disabilities (e.g., social and cultural models) that are less frequently introduced (Waldschmidt, 2017). We believe that each way has its own affordances and constraints. It is problematic to just tell one side of the story about disability; it is even more problematic when that side is deficit-oriented and disabled people internalize it and own this sense of self.

The arts offer different ways of seeing, knowing, and being, and they play an important part in learning and identity development (e.g., Bisht & Peppler, 2025; Halverson & Sheridan, 2014; Sullivan & McCarthy, 2007). Many have claimed that the arts have bee n marginalized in schools and called for prioritizing them in education (e.g., Bisht & Peppler, 2025; Eisner, 2002; Kokkos & Fleming, 2024). This paper presents a case stud y investigation of how disabled identities are reconstructed through participation in an arts-based course about disability and education, focusing on three college students who self-identified as disable d. W e use “reconstruction” here because participants indicated that they already held a disabled identity prior to our course, and our focus is on how that identity was reinterpreted and redeveloped through the course. We have always lived with the arts (Dewey, 1934; Dissanayake, 199 2). Arts and art-making serve as powerful resources to move beyond the ordinary and explore new perspectives, emotions, and ideas (Greene, 2009). Dewey

(1934) emphasizes that aesthetic experiences are not merely pathways to understanding academic subject matter

but are, in themselves, deeply engaging and meaningful. Learning scientists describe this kind of arts-based learning as learning through the arts (Halverson & Sawyer, 2022). It uses aesthetic processes and practices to develop ways of being such as “creative mind-sets, agency, and identities through participating in creative disciplines” (Seitamaa-Hakkarainen, 2022, p. 141). Self-exploration and cultural meaning-making are important parts of the arts-mediated learning process. As Halverson and Sheridan (2014) highlight, one of the unique aspects of arts learning is its explicit engagement with identity and culture. The arts offer “multiple solutions” in “learning about the self, the group, the community and diverse culture” (Peppler & Davis, 2010, p. 1003). Extending this sociocultural lens, artistic activity enables learners to create and share personal narratives that express who they are and the cultural resources they bring. In doing so, it supports learning and development that center their own experience (Halverson et al., 2023). DEA enacted arts-based learning in two ways: 1) curated materials about disabled artists and their work, which students perceive and reflect upon in ways that resonate with Maxine Greene’s articulation of the functions of arts as resources for expanding and deepening our understanding of “the other” (cf., Greene, 1995);  2) students’ own art-making used as inquiry, exploration, and presentation, which supports self-exploration, identity development, and cultural meaning-making (Halverson and Sheridan, 2014). We intentionally positioned the arts as a core resource for learning and identity development. Our conjecture was that the arts matter because they can  engage relational, affective, embodied, and imaginative ways of knowing that are often less available through discursive forms of teaching and reflection. Building with sociocultural theories of learning and identity (Wenger, 1998; Nasir & Hand, 2009), we see identity construction as developing the ability to participate in ways recognized as part of who we are, and 1287 who we are becoming (Nasir & Hand, 2009). In learning different ways of understanding disability thr ough the arts, students reposition themselves as they take up new discourses, tools, and practices (e.g., moving from a deficit, individual-impairment view toward a social or cultural view) and use resources (disabled personal narratives, artworks, artistic reflections) to make sense of their experiences, articulate new understandings about disability, and try different ways of participants the world. Research setting The DEA course structure begins with modules focused on disability and the social model, and gradually delving into the more complex, theoretical, and experiential intersections between disability, education, and the arts. Course objectives include defining a cultural perspective on disa bility, interpreting personal art experiences for deeper self and social understanding, and developing aesthetic responses that reflect individual perspectives or experiences. A fundamental aspect of our teaching methodology is the emphasis on the power of imagination (cf., Greene; bell hook)  through the arts. We encourage students to use the arts to explore new ways of seeing and understanding themselves, people around them and the world, particularly in the context of (dis)ability. More specifically, each week, students engage with four key components: Encounter, a section in each module where all students engage with the same material to create a shared intellectual context for discussion and learning  (i.e., slam poem, interviews, readings); Explore, a weekly activity encouraging students to delve into additional resources on disabilities, education, and the arts  of their own selection (i.e., guided art assignments, online art museum tour); Reflect, a weekly commentary on the weeks activities, known as “Documentation of Weekly Learning” (DWL), where students reflect and comment on their experiences in the course each week; and Connect, a weekly activity where students share their experiences in the course via an online discussion group. In weeks 4 and 7, we ask students to complete a retrospective “Arts-based Exploration of Learning” to provide opportunities for connecting course ideas to personal experience and for examining what these ideas mean in relation to students’ own lives, identities, and understandings of disability as they change over the quarter. This study adopts a descriptive qualitative case study methodology, which is appropriate for providing a rich, contextualized account of disabled students’ identity development in DEA and for describing “what happened” during the course (Yin, 2006, p. 112). We recruited three students Sam, Noah, and William (pseudonyms) who are enrolled in DEA for Winter 2024 and self-identified as having disabilities. Since we have read their work throughout the quarter, we purposefully selected three participants to explore disabled students’ disabled identity reconstruction in DEA (Patton, 2002). Two data types are collected: 1) students’ artifacts, which include seven documentations of weekly learning (DWL) written reflections, two arts-based exploration of learning (ABEL) responses, nine discussion posts, and one final project; 2) and individual interviews: approximately 45-60 minutes video-recorded semi-structured interviews with participants after the final grade was submitted.

## Data Analysis

Drawing on the nature of descriptive case study, the data analysis started with propositions that help direct attention to specific aspects of the phenomenon to be examined, clarify where to look for relevant evidence, and connect the study’s research ques tions to its theoretical perspectives. Our unit of analysis is the story line of disabled identity reconstruction, so we  started with tracing each participant’s identity development, using both inductive and deductive approaches (Yin, 2018). To trace the story line, we identified reconstruction episodes in the artifact data and then triangulated with interviews (see Table 1). Reconstruction episodes were flagged when a text 1) referenced a change in stance, 2) contrasted a “before/after,” and/or 3) connected a course experience to a new self-understanding. These episodes were “put it back together again more meaningfully” during synthesis (Stake, 2008, p. 75) and then compared cross-case to identify shared patterns and case-specific trajectories. Sam: Performing disability Sam was a PhD student in the drama department and identified with ADHD aiming to enter academia after graduating. In week 1, After viewing Carrie Sandahl’s performance art (Mitchell, 2013), the lab-coat imagery with diagnoses “written all over it” jolted h er: “In our culture we are taught to be ashamed of our medical histories… The image…was a truly impactful image.” She then connected to her K-12 memories of extended-time testing, shame, and a moment when her teacher quietly noted, “I will collect your test last.” The teacher wrote in a note, “I will never forget that feeling… she made me feel like I was part of the normal group.” Later, in the first Arts-Based Exploration of Learning assignment on week 4, where we asked students to make a collage, Sam was inspired by Carrie Sandahl’s piece and theatricalized her collage by pasting images on her body and integrating the character of Richard III. She taped printed images to her own limbs and recorded herself delivering the opening soliloquy, iterating three t imes. Mid-performance she experienced an “aha” moment, where she felt gratitude for “actually having a limb” and the recognition that insight emerged through doing— “I don’t think [it] would have clicked in the same way had I just read about something.” Watching and re-watching, she noticed “a little emotional occurrence” each time an image touched skin. The body became both canvas and instrument; representation was inseparable from sensation. Through this cycle (encounter artwork, personal connection, artistic experiment, re-narration), Sam shifted from experiencing accommodations as a personal flaw to recognizing how classroom routines make them visible as markers of difference, naming this “othering” as a cultural practice rather than a personal failing. At the end of the course, “looking back on this course, I really have learned a lot about myself as a neurodivergent person with ADHD and, also, as an educator…I think that this journey in this course has been so intensely personal…This [is] not only where I began, but how I hope to take this course with me as I head into other educational environments as both instructor and student.” Noah: Reclaiming “Non-Colors” Noah is a junior undergrad studying computer science aiming to be a teacher in the future. He is Malaysian and did his K-12 in Malaysian public schools. Throughout his schooling journey, although he was labeled as a “good student”, he navigated color blindness, emerging ADHD concerns, and mental health challenges. Those experiences shaped how he approached art and his disability. In schools where “important” subjects displaced the arts, he learned that “good art” often meant “correct color”, which is a criterion that never fit how he actually saw. Family comparisons deepened this stance: “My sister is an artistic person…which when compared to her, I don’t see myself as someone that candraw and paint…With color-blindness, it makes me believe that I am not going to do drawing and painting anymore.” For his final project, Noah decided to “paint” the way a color-blind person perceives, where he treated constraint as method. He chose layered grayscale to draw a sunflower, “paint[ing] it with layers as like an actual painting.” He expressed  the choice as a methodological commitment: if his eyes register the world through contrasts, then grayscale is the most honest and rigorous way to represent experience.  His artist statement verbalized the identity move: 1289 I am not a very artistic person in terms of painting and drawing…I see this as a challenge to myself to draw something that looks real, and ‘paint’ it with layers as like an actual painting. I was debating if I should try painting it and make it as a ‘normal’ painting art, and I decided to make it with different shades of gray.

Through this cycle (encounter artwork, personal connection, artistic experiment, re-narration), Noah repositioned himself from “not very artistic” to an agentic maker whose aesthetic rests on how he actually perceives. He framed the work as stepping out of the comfort zone, which is different from his earlier tries (collage, “random… small images,” music, dance) in class. This was a big move for him because he “pick[ed] up drawing… again after almost 5 years of avoiding making art,” and connected it to critiques of schooling that unsettled a previously unquestioned subject hierarchy. In his personal statement, he extended the stance to everyday noticing and articulated a future orientation: becoming an educator who attends to disability, empathy, and recognition, and, even if he does not teach, “someone who can care and empathize with others.” William: Imagining futures in color William, a Vietnamese American sophomore in Oceanography  and a member of the Reserve Officers' Training Corps (ROTC), identified disability through bilingual fluency struggles and perceived stuttering. Throughout the quarter he re-visited his past and current schooling experience and started questioning if the standardized measures speak about who he is and who he is becoming. He reflected: “I was classified as disabled… Certificates and rewards… only served to highlight my differences… It was the school that just wanted to represent that they are a diverse school.” In Week 4, he used visual language to process this history and to reconstruct his disabled identity. This collage “show[s] how someone [a disabled kid] that came from nothing becomes someone great” (Interview, 2024). He used a third-person tone in the collage itself and his artist statement. However, w hen I interviewed him, he shared that this was his dream, but it never happened.  In the interview, William walked me through this collage. He began with the middle section, where he used all black-and-white pictures to represent the "bad signs" and "bad past" (Interview, 2024), which was a reflection on his childhood. He shared that teachers and parents “were quick to judge based solely on my grades, unaware of my other talents.” As the kid became more successful in the collage, the images became brighter and more colorful. I interpreted this colorful part as a reflection of where he envisions his future, as he expressed that he is in the process of preparing himself for a leadership role in the ROCT.

In Week 7 (ABEL II), William extended this reconstructing process through a digital drawing of a “true warrior” who “prioritizes self-improvement and uplifting others,” with explicitly connecting disability to social readings of beauty and physicality and to institutional norms. The representational move (warrior/leader) was no longer speaking only about disablement but articulating how to lead within communities that sometimes produce disablement through their norms and designs.  He named the disabling logics as “societal norms,” and contrasted them with asset-based growth through practice (“emerged from a person at rock bottom… to a warrior committed to helping others”) to articulate an agentic, relational leadership stance he intends to carry into coursework, ROTC, and community life.

All three cases illustrate how disabled identities were reconstructed through participation in DEA, from a deficitoriented self-perception to a more asset-based view. Across Sam, Noah, and William, identity reconstruction unfolded through a recurring cycle, encounter artwork, personal connection, artistic experiment, re-narration, in which aesthetic experiences  became both the method and the medium of change. Students did not simply adopt new language about disability; they tried on new positions through artistic engagement. T hose artistic choices reorganized how they understood prior experience and future possibilities. In sociocultural perspective, the arts provided new resources to participate differently and be seen differently. Returning to the problem named in the introduction, our findings offer arts-based, sociocultural approaches that invite  different ways of seeing, knowing, and being, that  can meaningfully support disabled college students in reconstructing their identities.  Students changed how they spoke about disability, how they represented experience, and how they positioned themselves in communities they are part of. This is quite important because this reconstructed identity helps students name and reject deficit framings, reduce internalized stigma, and author different  narratives that better support  their lived realities. This also matters to us (educators and institutions). When students reframe disability to be more positive, we see and interact with them differently. We listen for strengths, communicate high expectations, and recognize potential rather than risk.  In this process, the arts in DEA worked as both epistemic and ontological support, which helped students know and become differently.
