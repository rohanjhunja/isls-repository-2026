## Abstract & Introduction

Exploring Art Synergies with Computing: From Artist to Computer Scientist Victoria Ouryvski, Toronto Metropolitan University, vouryvski@torontomu.ca Ali Naveed, Toronto Metropolitan University, ali.naveed@torontomu.ca Anjani Sirivella, Toronto Metropolitan University, asirivella@torontomu.ca Angelina Almanza, Toronto Metropolitan University, angelina.j.almanza@torontomu.ca Preeti Raman, Toronto Metropolitan University, praman@torontomu.ca Abstract: Visual artists reason, process, and create all the time. We examine this process of artmaking to reveal computational thinking (CT) practices and compare it with code-making, in hopes of better linking art and computer science (CS) together. Using a collaborative self-study method, four artist-researchers recorded and reflected on solving an art prompt and a coding question. We used interaction and thematic analysis approaches to analyze the recordings and reflections. CT practices were identified in both art-making and code-making, but art-making practices were not noticed as computational until collective reflection. This finding gives rise to a potential pathway for artists to see themselves in computer science by making CT visible in artistic practice.

Ever since Wing (2006) argued for computational thinking (CT) to be a “universally applicable attitude and skill set for everyone,” educators and researchers have been trying to define, operationalize, and broaden the construct of CT beyond programming to include diverse ways of reasoning, designing, and problem solving across disciplines. Over the years, CT has been reframed from a set of technical competencies to a more holistic form of sense-making and creative expression situated within varied social and cultural contexts (Grover & Pea, 2013; Kafai, 2016; Tissenbaum et al., 2019). However, despite this broadened conception, increasing diversity in computer science (CS) remains a challenge. Persistent underrepresentation of diverse identities in CS is oft en linked to perceptions of computing as highly technical, abstract, and detached from creativity and self-expression (Luo et al., 2021). Subsequently, those who see themselves as creative or artistic often worry that CS, or even STEM broadly, may not be a good fit for who they are or how they think. Such identity conflict has been explored in the learning sciences in recent years (Solomon et al., 2021), specifically in explorations around aesthetic and artistic appreciation within scientific inquiry through ArtScience (Hill et al., 2024) and in framing the arts as preparation for computing (Dahlem, 2023). Building on this body of work, we explore how artists’ creative practices reveal manifestations of CT and how recognizing these practices can open inclusive pathways for artists to see themselves within CS. If CT practices such as decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, and algorithmic thinking (Brennan & Resnick, 2012) are already present but unrecognized in artistic work, then the barrier to computing participation may not be capability, but  recognition. Understanding this distinction can help educators design inclusive pathways into CS. This inquiry is framed as a collaborative self-study (Samaras, 2011) in which four artist–researchers who identify as artists, but have varied relationships with technology, engaged in parallel tasks of code-making and art-making while capturing their processes through video, audio, and reflective conversations. This methodology provides us first-hand insight through the phenomenological experience of navigating artistic and computational identities, while the collaborative nature of the inquiry provides distance through mutual analysis. Specifically, we ask: (RQ1) How do we, as artist –researchers, experience and make sense of CT during our creative practices in art and code? (RQ2) How do our reflections, interactions, and artifacts generated through art-making and code-making reveal shifts in how we conceptualize creativity, computation, and our identities as computational thinkers? Through these questions, we aim to show both what CT looks like in artistic practice and how recognizing it might reshape who feels welcome in computing, with implications for extending these findings into actionable steps to foster a more diverse CS community. The purpose of this literature review is to position the visual arts as a context for exploring and building CT skills. We organize this literature review around three key themes that inform our explorations: the evolution of CT over the years, the relatio nship between identity and participation in CS, and recent work around the connections between the arts and computational practices.

2116 Computational thinking In 2006, Wing suggested that computational thinking was way more than Papert’s interpretation of it as "procedural thinking" used by computers to solve problems, changing how we view CT as a fundamental skill for everyone, not just for computer scientists.  Since then, learning sciences researchers and educators have contributed deeply to understanding what CT actually means and how these skills can be cultivated in classrooms. Brennan and Resnick (2012) provided a practical framework for CT consisting of four skills: decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, and algorithmic thinking. While this framework helped make CT practices visible during coding with Scratch (a block-based programming language) and is still widely used, it has been critiqued for not addressing learning that is context-specific. Tedre and Denning (2016) strongly argued that CT cannot be treated as context-free and showed that what counts as CT depends heavily on the situation, the tools available, and the community you're working in. Kafai's (2016) work has further reframed CT as something more social and cultural and showed how CT can support collaborative problem solving. Grover and Pea (2018) have pointed out that people can engage in computational thinking without realizing it. This matters because if you can't name what you're doing, you can't consciously use it in new situations. This gap between doing and recognizing informs the design of our study.

Identity and computing Computer science has a diversity problem (Agbo, 2024). The cultural story around who belongs in CS continues to be incredibly narrow. Myths abound around computer scientists being naturally gifted, awkward with people, and obsessed with machines (Cheryan et al., 2009). For anyone who identifies as being creative, caring about aesthetics, or enjoying working with people, computing is often perceived as “the wrong place to be”. Master et al. (2021) found these stereotypes actively push people away from CS, especially women and underrepresented minorities. In contrast, Harvard and Howard (2019) found that when an atypical CS course is available, there is an increase of enrollement from minority groups. Nasir and Hand (2006) show that learning is dependent on a sense of belonging in a discipline and that it's not just about what you know but also about who you are. STEAM, ArtScience and learning through art Many have argued for the arts’ place in STEM: Science, Technology, Engineering, and Math, evolving the acronym to STEAM (Allina, 2018). There have been many innovative ways of bringing art into STEM, like Peppler (2013). York et al (2022) have found that adding art into STEM can also increase motivation to learn, especially in minority groups like young girls. Similarly, Bares (2018), who found that the inclusion of art in a technical degree attracted a wider audience. When it comes to CS, the way Art is in volved is inherently different than that of other disciplines in STEM as it brings out self-identity through active engagement with art and code (Peppler & Kafai, 2006). Our research aims to extend this cohesion to provide a pathway to address underrepresentation in computing.

As four artist-researchers who had come together through a mutual curiosity around CS, we employed a self-study approach to this research. Self-study methodology can be understood as “a body of practices, procedures, and guidelines used by those who work in a discipline or engage in an inquiry” (Samaras & Freese, 2006). Self-study allowed us to critically reflect on our own creative practices, positioning ourselves simultaneously as subjects and researchers. Further, by engaging in collaborative inquiry and reflection, we were able to explore CT from an insider perspective, focusing on how it is experienced, interpreted, and expressed during the creative making of art and code. A collaborative self-study methodology proved conducive to shared analysis and reflexivity, making it possible for each of us to act as both a participant and a critical peer.

## Context and participants

All four researchers identify as visual artists with a range of skill sets, from an animator who worked in industry, a classically trained individual, and two hobbyists. All four had various degrees of knowledge, experience and comfort with computing and technology. Each of us participated in two creative problem solving sessions in art and coding, respectively. The art problem that the four had was a prompt in which they had to depict “Partnering,” and the coding question was at a difficulty level found in a typical introductory programming exam. All four participants were recorded as they solved the two problems. During the recording process, they were encouraged to think out loud and take notes; this allowed each of us to be a part of the thinking process. 2117

## Data analysis

Data collected during the self-study included video and audio recordings of each participant’s art-making and code-making session, reflective discussion notes and the artifacts generated from each of these activities.  Analysis of the recordings was guided by interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). We also conducted a thematic computation, and our own identities. To start the interac tion analysis, we reviewed the video data and noticed reasoning visible through think-alouds, gestures and interaction with the artifact, helping us make a coding rule sheet for the analysis (Table 1). This initial review and note-taking helped us code key moments thematically using five central CT practices consistently identified in the literature: abstraction, generalization, decomposition, algorithmic thinking, and debugging (Caeli & Yadav, 2020). Whenever an individual expressed one of the five themes in CT, it was noted, along with general gestures, thoughts, and any cues that pointed to the use of a CT method. Furthermore, Tolkamp et al(2025) guided the mapping of CT during art-making. For instance, during Nat’s art-making session, Nat said, “I feel like it's kind of like a yin and yang.” This was coded as implicit, since feeling is an intuitive sensation. Similarly, during Nat's code-making session, they commented, “Honestly I'll just do that instead, it will over complicate things if I do it the other way.” This moment was coded as debugging and we noticed that Nat started to alter their thinking. Both the art-making and code-making artifacts were unique to each of us varying in style, medium, complexity, creativity, and interpretation of the prompt (Figure 1). This led to videos of different lengths, which we had to account for in our analysis. For the thematic analysis, we analyzed our reflections and written notes, independently coding for segments that referenced creativity, computation, or identity. Methodological reflexivity and data triangulation were used to enhance reliability. Peer debriefing and dual member checking between the researchers provided trustworthiness to the research coding process.

Further, when solving the art problem, many felt there were no wrong answers on how to approach the prompt, allowing us to use a more implicit way of thinking. However, when solving the coding problem, we felt it was more structured; there was only one way of thinking that was correct, resulting in a more explicit way of thinking. For RQ2, we found limited identity shifts across participants. Thematic analysis of our reflections showed awareness of problem solving and creativity across both code-making and art-making, but little change in how we identified ourselves. Our artistic id entities persisted through code-making rather than shifting. As Jaz reflected, “I am more analytical than I want to be” and Nat said, “I don't like when the artist (identity) gets separated.” While we identified themes around lived experiences and awarenes s of problem-solving skills, the lack of themes relating to identity is itself significant: it suggests that maybe recognition, rather than transformation, may itself be a distinct form of identity work. This collaborative self-study examined CT processes during art-making and code-making. Our most significant finding is that similar CT processes were observed in both domains, with more instances during art-making thanduring code-making. The main difference was in the meta-cognitive awareness of participants. It was implicit in art-making, but explicit in code-making. This implicit-explicit distinction has important implications for the learning sciences and CS education. As Grover and Pea (2018) have pointed out, if one cannot name what you are doing, you cannot consciously deploy it in new situations. Our study extends this insight by showing where this implicit-explicit gap manifests. While artistic practice may cultivate CT skills without formal computa tional vocabulary, the lack of this awareness (and the naming of it) shows us the need for metacognitive scaffolding. We are excited that our analysis showed that artists' thinking inherently fosters CT. This provides us with a chance to bring more artistic input into the CS field. As one of us put it, art and coding are like ink and paper, respectively. Both creative.
