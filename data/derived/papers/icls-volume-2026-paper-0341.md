# Learning to Deconstruct AI/ML Systems: Scaffolds to Support Learners in Critically Evaluating

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Historically, the learning sciences have emphasized how computing construction activities can support learners in building relationships between old and new knowledge in interactions with others while creating artifacts of social relevance. With the ubiquity of socia l media and AI/ML, researchers have turned their attention to the social and ethical implications of computing, proposing that deconstruction—the process of critically examining and understanding how meaning and intentionality are encoded into computing systems and practices that impact people’s lives and the environment —is equally important. In this symposium we bring together researchers investigating how to design deconstruction learning activities in which young people critically evaluate AI/ML systems of social relevance. We present different perspectives on how to design activities and scaffold learners to examine the socio-ethical implications of AI/ML systems. We discuss the role of deconstruction in computing education and its relation to construction activities. Symposium overview The learning sciences have been concerned with how involving learners in the construction of socially relevant computing applications for and with other people can support knowledge construction (Kafai, 2006; Holbert et al., 2021). This interest in construction can be traced back to Papert’s work on constructionism (see Papert, 1991), which argued that knowledge structures are built through the creation of personal and socially relevant artifacts, centering on the connected nature of knowledge with its personal and social dimensions. Today, many tools and educational efforts have embraced constructionism, positioning young people as designers of applications, from commercial products such as Nintendo’s Game Builder Garage and Roblox to educational tools and curricula that have made designing applications a mainstay in most K-12 computing education initiatives (Kafai & Burke, 2014; Oleson et al., 2020; Waite & Sentance, 2021). 2203 Yet, with the ubiquity of social media and AI/ML and their ethical, environmental, and societal implications (Ko et al., 2020), coupled with the political or critical turn in the learning sciences (Politics of Learning Writing Collective, 2017), researchers have turned their attention to the equally important process of deconstruction. Iversen and colleagues (2018) define deconstruction as the process of “critically examining and understanding how meaning and intentionality are encoded into digital artifac ts and practices that impact upon our everyday lives.” Deconstruction involves describing, evaluating and reflecting on the values and intentions embedded in sociotechnical systems used in everyday life while considering their possible implications (Dindle r et al., 2020; Schaper et al., 2023). We argue that such practices can contribute to learners' development of both ethical and functional understandings of AI/ML systems. This involves assessing the properties of a system in terms of its inputs, outputs and materials (e.g., data); inquiring about the intended use and actual use of a system; foregrounding the values, worldviews, and assumptions embedded in the system; and investigating how the system impacts individuals, communities and the environment (Dindler et al., 2023). In this symposium, we bring together researchers taking different approaches to deconstruction to address the following research questions: How can we design activities that scaffold learners to deconstruct AI/ML systems? How does deconstruction support learners in developing their functional and ethical understanding of AI/ML systems? Contributions include presentations about tools and activities that foster deconstruction, participatory approaches that involve teachers in the design of deconstruction acti vities, and insights into learners' thinking processes when investigating socio-ethical issues. Furthermore, we discuss how deconstruction relates to construction and their role in computing education. This session is organized in three sections: (1) the chairs will introduce the topic and participants (~10 min); (2) participants will have seven minutes to share their work; (3) our discussants Jean Ryoo, a learning scientist and computing education researcher that has advocated for equity and inquiry-oriented computing, and Ole Sejer Iversen, a child-computer interaction and activity theory researcher that proposed the term deconstruction, will synthesize and reflect on findings (10 min), followed by a Q&A with the audience and presenters (~15 min). Presentation 1 — Deconstructing machine vision with breakable machine Olli Hilke, Nicolas Pope, Matti Tedre Many K–12 education initiatives use “Teachable Machine” -type tools to let students build simple models, but these activities face difficulties preparing students to interrogate or critique the complex commercial AI-driven systems they frequently use.  The AI-and data-driven systems from major companies can often produce biased or misleading results, but when they fail, it often happens in subtle and unpredictable ways —and because they are proprietary black boxes, it is nearly impossible to see why they failed.  Yet, understanding that AI systems are not neutral or infallible requires exposing their brittleness, hidden biases, and humandesign choices. Breakable Machine (Hilke et al., 2025) (spoof.gen-ai.fi) offers students an accessible entry point into scaffolded critical inquiry and analysis of construction, use, values, and consequences of AI systems.  It is a multiplayer, browser-based classroom game that lets students destabilize and probe an image classifier in real-time while competing with one another.  Using their personal mobile devices and a shared classroom display, learners are provided challenge labels (e.g., “astronaut”, “robot”, “lion”).  Their job is to stage photos that fool the classifier into confident but incorrect predictions. They experiment with props, clothing, lighting, or sparking curiosity, peer learning, and collaborative sensemaking.  By exposing and exploiting the model’s weaknesses, Breakable Machine transforms normally hidden vulnerabilities and failures into visible, manipulable phenomena.  This hands-on play in the classroom supports computational empowerment by enabling students to move from users to critical interrogators of AI.  It shifts the perception of AI systems from opaque oracles into fallible, human-made systems, and it turns the AI systems’ weaknesses into  space for inquiry, reflection, and ethical debate.

Presentation 2 – Computer Science Teachers as Designers of Deconstruction Activities Daniel J. Noh, Deborah A. Fields, Yasmin B. Kafai, Danaé Metaxa As the role of research-practice partnerships develops within the learning sciences, there is a growing need to expand participatory design (hereafter: PD) roles for teachers —beyond simply using, testing, or implementing lessons developed by researchers. Particularly, in the spirit of computational empowerment (Dindler et al., 2023), we have to engage teachers in roles of leading lesson design in innovative areas. In this work we share reflections from five experienced computer science (CS) teachers on thei r takeaways from leading the design and implementation of a novel set of lessons on AI auditing. AI auditing (or sometimes “algorithm auditing”) is a method that involves “repeatedly querying an algorithm and observing its output in order to draw conclusions about the algorithm's opaque inner workings and possible external impact” (Metaxa et al., 2021, p. 272). AI auditing is a way to deconstruct algorithmic systems to uncover harmful biases without access to proprietary code or inaccessible data.

In this study, five experienced CS teachers, alongside researchers, developed the first set of AI auditing lessons for high school CS classes. While researchers introduced the teachers to AI auditing through a combination of professional expert lectures, p articipation in pilot activities created for informal settings, and hearing advice from youth who had engaged with AI auditing, the teachers themselves led the outlining of topics, drafting of lessons, implementation of lessons in their own classrooms, and revisions. This work spanned one year, from Summer 2024 to Summer 2025, bookended by weeklong workshops (in-person and virtual). This research focuses on the teachers’ end-of-year reflections on the design process as a whole, asking: what did the teachers value from designing activities that scaffold learners to deconstruct AI/ML systems? 2205 Our research draws on iterative, thematic qualitative data collection and analysis applying grounded theory (Charmaz, 2000). Data include teachers’ individual post-implementation interviews (n=5) and, after initial included two additional rounds of open-coding and refinement. Overall, we identified four themes that the teachers drew from the PD process: professionalism, learning by designing, transparency and connection, and play. The teachers appreciated hearing from experts and valued the professional agency leading the design of and iterating on lessons themselves, in contrast to their usual role of receiving ready-made curriculum. Further, designing lessons gave teachers a deeper grasp of the pedagogical intention behind the deconstruction activities, enabling them to make purposeful adjustments in their own classrooms. They also emphasized the importance of transparency and connection, noting how rare and beneficial it was to share experiences with other CS educators and learn from youth who had engaged in similar activities. Lastly, playfulness and fun—through activities like experimenting with making physical computing motiondetection models with other teachers and even lighthearted icebreakers—helped build bonds and create space for honest dialogue amongst the teachers and researchers.

By examining what teachers found valuable in the participatory design of AI auditing lessons, this work aims to highlight the need for teacher integration in developing deconstruction activities, bringing focus to what may help enable these PD experiences to thrive. Such integration not only strengthens classroom practices, but also paves a path towards developing the professional agency of teachers who prepare youth to critically engage with and interrogate the technologies that are increasingly embedded in their lives. Presentation 4: Perspective taking through auditing Luis Morales-Navarro, Daniel J. Noh, Yasmin B. Kafai, Danaé Metaxa AI literacy efforts call for providing learners with opportunities to reflect on the ethical and societal implications of computing. Yet, these reflections are often based on only a single or a few observations of system behaviors. AI auditing has recently been proposed as a systematic approach for learners to conduct empirical investigations of AI systems (Solyst et al., 2025; Morales-Navarro et al., 2025). Auditing involves “repeatedly querying an algorithm and observing its output to draw conclusions about the algorithm's opaque inner workings and possible external impact” (Metaxa et al., 2021). Research with teens has shown that learners can lead their own audits to uncover potentially harmful system behaviors that have not been previously identified by experts (Morales-Navarro et al., 2025). However, how learners build their understanding of ethical and societal implications when auditing AI systems remains understudied.

In this presentation, we build on Ackermann’s (2012) conceptualization of perspective-taking as “people’s ability to experience and describe the presentation of an object or display from different vantage points,” (p. 28) to investigate how learners make sense of individual observations and draw conclusions of system behaviors. Ackermann, drawing from Kegan (1982), claims that deeper understanding is achieved by both “diving in,” actively engaging in addressing a problem, and “stepping out” to reflect and t ake perspective on the task at hand. This framework of perspective-taking has been widely used in computing education and learning sciences research to investigate youths’ understandings across various topics including program comprehension (Strawhacker and Bers, 2015), complex systems simulations (Wilensky and Reisman, 2006), and representations in data-science (Roberts and Lyons, 2022).

We conducted a week-long participatory auditing workshop with a group of 16 teens (ages 14– 15) in a summer program. Workshop activities were designed to support participants in systematically investigating potentially harmful algorithmic biases in ChatGPT outputs. Using screen recordings of one group of teens and the documentation of their audit, we analyzed how participants “dived in” making inferences about individual observations, and “stepped out” looking at patterns across tests. In our analysis we focused on one group that investigated if ChatGPT provided biased college application advice to high school students based on class/family income, race, or location. In this group, participants dived into writing systematic prompts and making observations of, for example, how ChatGPT recommended Black and Latinx students apply only for diversity scholarships and not merit ones. However, when they zoomed out, they noticed that race did not play a primary role in the colleges ChatGPT recommended the teens to apply to. Socioeconomic class and income, on the other hand, were the determining factors: the system responded to all prompts requesting advice for low-income students with recommendations to apply to community colleges, all prompts related to middle-income students resulted in recommendations to apply to public colleges and universities, and all prompts from high-income students resulted in recommendations to apply to Ivy League institutions. These biases became apparent only when zooming out and looking for patterns across their collective data. Our work shows how the systematic process of auditing fosters perspective taking by enabling learners to zoom out, notice patterns beyond a single or a few observations, and argue with evidence  about potential algorithmic biases.

Presentation 5: Computational empowerment of children by deconstructing LLMs 2207 Netta Iivari, Sumita Sharma, Pauli A. Klemettilä Artificial Intelligence (AI) has become enmeshed with all our life spheres and activities. Therefore, empowerment and agency of children in relation to AI are of utmost importance. Computational empowerment refers to how "children and youth, as individuals and groups, develop the skills, insights and reflexivity needed to understand digital technology and its effect on their lives and society at large, and their capacity to engage critically and curiously with the construction and deconstruction of technology " (Dindler et al., 2020). Transformative agency is an essential element of computational empowerment, referring to “children’s ability to transform the knowledge acquired (in schools or design) to action and change their own matters, their shared matters, or even societal matters for the better and more just society ” (Iivari et al., 2024a). We advocate collective transformative agency of children, i.e. how children collectively question and constructively break free from the unjust or oppressive status quo (Iversen et al., 2025, see also e.g. Sannino, 2022). In our work, we explore computational empowerment of children in relation to Large Language Models (LLMs). We have experimented with ways by which to foster children’s critical and curious engagement in deconstruction of LLMs, which refers to children’s cr itical inquiry into LLMs and their effects-their inner workings, intended purposes, embedded values and assumptions, and desirable and undesirable consequences (Dindler et al., 2023; Morales-Navarro & Kafai, 2023). Based on our empirical inquiries with 22 teenagers in June 2025 during a two-week AI Ambassadors program, we recommend the following methods for deconstructing LLMs with children and youth.

● (1) Algorithmic auditing of LLMs (Morales-Navarro et al., 2024). In our workshop, participants systematically explored and identified embedded biases and assumptions within responses generated by LLMs.

● (2) Exploration of manipulative design (Khan et al., 2025). In our implementation, participants used open-access textual datasets of their choosing to train character-level transformer models from scratch using Google Colab notebooks based on the NanoGPT repository (Karpathy, 2023).

● (4) Design futuring (Dunne & Raby, 2024) Working in groups, participants envisioned how AI could be used to address complex social issues, and considered what new issues it may cause. ● (5) Design activism (Iivari et al., 2024b). This was included in our workshop by designing broader activism campaigns to highlight the role of technology in mitigating or aggravating complex social challenges. This included provocative ad design, where par ticipants created videos to promote their solutions from design futuring, followed by designing contrasting 'anti-ads' to consider and highlight their potential issues (see Fig. 3).

Presentation 6: Critical construction to scaffold deconstruction Karl-Emil K. Bilstrup, Line Have Musaeus In this presentation, we will discuss how interactive tools and activities-through critical construction with data science practices and the design processes of implementing ML systems-can scaffold students' deconstruction of AI systems and products in their everyday lives.

We see deconstruction as a process of investigating how and why a system was constructed. Therefore, we cannot teach the deconstruction of digital systems without also teaching about their construction. Designing critical construction activities that promp t and scaffold reflection on digital technologies, however, is not trivial. They must not only demonstrate to students the types of problems a technology can address, but also its inherent limitations and the trade-offs involved in implementing the technol ogy in practice (Bilstrup et al., 2022a). Constructionism-based tools often operate in simplistic microworlds that are "undisturbed by extraneous questions" [p. 12] (Papert, 1980). Hence, they may not expose ethical issues or real-world complexities by intention. Other researchers have taken a design approach, using maker technologies such as 3D printers, laser cutters, and technologies for digital media creation, where students' mock-up' computational systems (Smith, 2015). While these tools and the activities around them put technology in students' hands, they do not necessarily expose critical design choices associated with the technologies. They often demonstrate the capabilities and power of the technologies, but sidestep the complexity and trade-offs i nherent in the design and implementation of computational systems (Bilstrup et al., 2022a). In our own research, we have investigated how critical construction tools and activities can scaffold reflection and imagination of possible futures shaped by AI technologies. Here, we have investigated how construction activities can be designed to deliberately provide students with hands-on experiences with unintentionally introducing biases into their systems (Bilstrup et al.,  2025) (Fig. 4A), making difficult decisions about data representativeness (Bilstrup et al., 2022b) (Fig. 4B), and experiencing how minor adjustments to ML parameters candramatically alter the output of their system (Kaspersen et al., 2022). These are all inherent challenges in developing ML systems that result in difficult trade-offs in the design process. Furthermore, we have investigated how students can get hands-on experiences with the ethical tensions (Whittlestone et al., 2019) of AI systems. For example, by prioritizing which personal data they are willing to collect to improve their ML system, while risking violating their users’ privacy. Presentation 7: Supporting teens’ everyday ethical thinking Rotem Landesman, Jayne Everson Adolescents are increasingly interacting with generative AI (GenAI) tools in their day to day activities (Madden et al., 2024). For example,  when working on a school project, teens may contemplate the meaning of academic integrity if they use AI to brainstorm. Alternatively, they may wonder about the credibility of the information they receive from a GenAI tool and their responsibility to fact check. As we work to equip young people with the tools to understand the impact of their interactions with GenAI and support them to make decisions that reflect their values and goals regarding these tools, recent efforts suggest engaging youth in processes of deconstruction (Iversen et al., 2018). A key component in deconstruction is considering the usage of GenAI through an ethical lens that reflects both a user’s individual values and broader societal perspectives. As part of the deconstruction process, therefo re, young people must engage in ethical thinking, and in particular– everyday ethical thinking. Everyday ethics refers to ethical aspects intermingled in our everyday lives (Van Mechelen et al. 2020). In this work, we build on a definition of ethical thinking as: the process of (1) identifying and describing ethical issues in a variety of contexts, (2) articulating the ethical considerations involved in different responses to those issues, and (3) providing a rationale for a position that addresses those co nsiderations (Lorié, 2023). Everyday ethical thinking, then, is the exhibition of those three dimensions in regards to the ethical aspects present in everyday life. Our work presents a novel pedagogical approach that elicits and supports teens’ everyday ethical thinking process in relation to GenAI tools. This approach is built on three principles: (1) teens reflecting on their everyday use of GenAI tools in a community of inquiry (CoI) of their peers, (2) using philosophical scaffolding tools, including ones from Philosophy for Children (P4C) (Lone 2012) to elicit nuanced discussions, and (3) reflecting on topics teens can relate to and care deeply about-in this case, using GenAI in school. We ran 6 workshops with 2 groups of teens (ages 13-17, n= 20) from 2 schools in the Pacific Northwest over the course of 2 months to see whether this approach elicited and supported teens’ everyday ethical thinking. The workshops were framed as working groups to think through and produce guidelines for using GenAI in school. Our results show that the workshops built with the three principles in mind fostered vulnerable and intentional spaces where teens recognized the ethical compl exities GenAI tools bring up in school, grappled with these complexities in community, and collectively reasoned through their thinking about how to resolve ethical complexities while exploring their values and goals. For example, participants exhibited everyday ethical thinking as they grappled with the complexity of using GenAI for ''busy work'', considered the values of learning, agency, and authenticity 2210 in their ethical decision making, and acknowledged the moral complexity when using GenAI on a regular basis (figure 5).

To our knowledge, this work is the first to directly explore an approach to support adolescents’ everyday ethical thinking skills about emerging technologies (i.e. GenAI) in a nuanced manner.
