# Advancing Dialogic Leadership through AI-Enabled Training: Insights from a Design-Based Research Project

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper reports insights from an ongoing design-based research project on developing an AI-enabled programme for dialogic leadership training. To ensure the effectiveness of the programme, one central challenge must be addressed: Can AI be designed to follow dialogic principles and provide trustworthy, supportive guidance that enables leaders to embed these principles into their practices? To address this, three design strategies were implemented: applying principles of dialogic intelligence and conversa tional heuristics to enhance AI’s capacity for generative dialogue; integrating open-source datasets and psychological frameworks to improve AI’s contextual awareness; and refining human-like interaction features to build AI’s credibility. Guided by these strategies, an initial prototype for leadership in performance reviews was developed and tested with 20 participants, yielding positive results. Looking forward, the project will improve scenario realism, introduce scaffolding mechanisms and develop evaluation mechanisms for a better learning experience.

Leadership plays a crucial role in shaping the value and culture of organisations (Ciulla, 2020). To be effective, leadership must be understood in relation to context and grounded in relational awareness (Cunliffe & Eriksen, 2011; Fletcher, 2012). Recent shifts in social contexts have created a pressing need for new forms of leadership. In particular, the contexts in which leadership operates have moved from tame with relatively simple and solvable problems, to wicked that characterised by heightened compl exity, uncertainty, and  full of  problems with no definitive solutions (Bocala & Yurkofsky, 2025; Majee et al., 2025). Leaders now face the challenge of reconfiguring workplaces, retaining talent, and responding to rapidly changing market demands (Majee et al., 2025). Against this backdrop, there is a growing call for dialogic forms of leadership that can navigate complexity, build resilience, and capitalise on emerging opportunities (Ahmed et al., 2023; Kith, 2024). The development of dialogic forms of leadership requires training programmes that are flexible, contextually grounded, and capable of embedding dialogic principles into the specific practical realities of leaders (Cunliffe & Eriksen, 2011; Kedian et al., 2016). Realising such training on a broad scale requires harnessing the potential of AI. Human trainers, mostly external to the organisation, can often face difficulties in designing training that is directly and consistently relevant to leaders’ day-to-day practice (Kedian et al., 2016); also, hierarchical dynamics and authority concerns candiscourage leaders from engaging openly with facilitators and training processes (Amestoy et al., 2014). AI, by contrast, is typically perceived as neutral and less judgmental (Borge et al., 2024), offering psychologically safe and adaptive environments that promote fuller participation. Besides, systems built on foundational models can interpret meaning in parallel, handle complexity, and adapt responsively to real-world scenarios (Alexander, 2024). Taken together, these capacities position AI not merely as a supplement but as a potentially significant enabler of leadership training. However, delivering AI-based dialogic leadership training is not straightforward. A central hindrance is that AI often exhibits social sycophancy, accommodating users’ preferences rather than constructively challenging them and supporting exploration of the unknown (Sharma et al., 2024). If unmitigated, this tendency risks reinforcing echo chambers and pushing leaders into monologic modes of leadership. To address this challenge, a design-based research project was undertaken by University of Cambridge  and Yeltic, seeking to explore design strategies to enable AI to follow dialogic principles and support leaders to embed  these principles into practice. Design concepts and process To ensure the design could be rapidly adapted as problems were encountered, the project followed agile principles, working in sprints to structure development (Banfield et al., 2015). In line with agile principles, prompt engineering was adopted as the central design method, as it enables rapid iteration, supports flexible adaptation to context, and allows continuous improvement through natural-language feedback (Bose et al., 2024; Kale et al., 2024). By leveraging the strengths of prompt engineering, academic and industry members of the team were able 304 to exchange ideas smoothly and respond effectively to emerging challenges, ensuring efficient collaboration and productive development processes that aligned with agile principles and harnessed the team’s full potential. Three groups of insights were adopted to guide the design of prompts intended to enable the AI to follow dialogic principles and provide trustworthy, supportive guidance for leaders in ways that are pedagogically effective and cost-efficient (i.e., minimising token usage):

(1) Central to the prompt design were dialogic intelligence principles (Wegerif, 2007; Wegerif, 2025) and

heuristics for dealing with workplace conversations (Brown, 2013; Stone et al., 2023). These informed the development of prompts that enabled the AI to assume the role of a dialogic challenger, to mitigate its tendency toward social sycophancy and to nudge leaders to adopt a dialogic mode of leadership. After all, adults typically operate within established frames of reference and may regard their understanding as sufficient; only when confronted with dissonance or conflicts do  they fully engage in the kind of learning that most effectively promotes growth (Mezirow, 1990). (2) Datasets about workplace conversations and the discourse of individuals with different personality traits,

systematically collected from open-source community platforms such as GitHub and Hugging Face. These datasets informed the development of prompts that assigned the AI contextually appropriate personas (e.g. distinct traits, conversation pattern, job seniority, and demographic backgrounds), to better mirroring the situations that leaders will typically encounter in their daily work.

(3) Insights from academic literature and professional training manuals, ensured that the prompts were

pedagogically sound, theoretically grounded, and aligned with best practices in leadership development. Guided by the design strategies outlined above, the first sprint of the AI-based platform was launched. Central to this sprint was a role-play session facilitated by an AI learning partner. The session focused on scenarios that frequently trouble leaders a nd demand sustained dialogic leadership, i.e. the performance reviews (Bradley & Campbell, 2016). Within  this role-play, the AI partner adopted the role of a dialogic challenger, simulating behaviours commonly encountered in such contexts and encouraging leaders to practise dialogic engagement. This first sprint was evaluated by the project team comprising education researchers, AI experts, business experts, and corporate managers, with the aim of identifying strengths and limitations in order to refine the version for prototype that could be tested with a more heterogeneous participant group. The evaluation showed that the AI partner effectively addressed issues of social sycophancy. In addition, the training scenario and the AI partner’s role were both judged highly relevant to managerial practice. Yet one central limitation was identified: th e AI lacked human-likeness. This matters because perceived human-likeness shapes individuals’ trust and engagement with AI (Ngo, 2025), which, in turn, can ultimately affect the effectiveness of training. To address this, strategies for enhancing human-likeness were introduced, including:

(1) The Big Five personality model and Plutchik’s wheel of emotion (Mondal & Gokhale, 2020) were drawn

on to enrich the prompts used to define the profile of the AI partner. The Big Five informed the design of new prompt blocks such as {behavioural_patterns}, which specified the AI partner’s reaction modes and response tendencies linked to its assigned personality traits (e.g., agreeableness prompting 305 cooperative language, neuroticism prompting heightened sensitivity). Plutchik’s wheel of emotion informed the creation of {emotional_states}, a prompt block assigning the AI partner dynamic, context-sensitive affective responses (e.g., anger, joy, anticipation) in alignment with its personality traits. Inclusion of these frameworks enabled the AI partner to be prompted to deliver emotionally attuned responses and present a more coherent personality.

(2) The refinement of UI/UX design (see Figure 2). More specifically, an avatar was added to the AI partner

to strengthen social presence. These changes provided a more concrete and human-like impression, enhancing users’ sense of interpersonal engagement. Also, the AI partner’s response speed was adjusted, and behavioural indicators like “ ···” were incorporated to signal that the AI was “thinking.” These modifications increased the perceived naturalness of the interaction.  Finally, overall UI was redesigned to better mimic commonly used communication platforms to restore a real-life liked context. The internal testing results indicated that the enhancements in human-likeness improved both authenticity and engagement. On this basis, the second sprint was integrated into the first prototype prepared for user testing.

## User testing and findings

User testing of the initial prototype was conducted from 18th June to 25th July 2025. Twenty participants took part in the testing. Participants were selected to ensure a relatively balanced representation of professional status (i.e. 13 professionals with managerial experience and 7 students with background in either AI, dialogic education, or leadership) and gender (i.e. 12 females and 8 males) to support the validity of the findings. User testing took place in three stages. First, participants were introduced to the scenario, their assigned role, the role of the AI partner, and the goals and ground rules of the role-play. Next, participants interacted with the AI partner freely until the role-play goals were achieved. The average duration of interaction was around 20 minutes per session, with all histories recorded. Finally, participants reflected on their experiences and evaluated the design through a semi-structured interview and a follow-up survey, with focus on: (1) the AI partner’s humanlikeness and its ability to overcome social sycophancy; (2) the realism of the designed scenarios; (3) the applicability of leadership skills and dialogic thinking in practice; and (4) suggestions to refine the design. Also, interactions with AI partner provided a context for all participants (n=20) to explore how to apply core dialogic thinking in leadership settings. This was evident in their answers to the question, “ What have you learned from the role-play session?” For this question, participants’ answers consistently referred to core aspects of dialogic thinking like the following:

• Moving beyond fixed positions: “appreciating difference” (P1); “be patient and open” (P4).

• Deepening mutual engagement: “need to invite others to share ideas” (P6); “practising some

strategies such as inviting others to think and talk, revoicing their words” (P7).

• Contingent reasoning: “a sentence can have different meaning that should figure out” (P12); “need

to explain consequence and reason of one’s actions” (P15). Overall, findings from this initial user test indicate that the design strategies are feasible and appear to be pedagogically valuable, with the potential to establish a foundation for scalable AI-enabled dialogic leadership training.

This ongoing project represents an initial exploratory step toward understanding how AI might act as a partner for dialogic leadership development. Initial user testing confirmed the effectiveness of three design strategies: (1) structuring prompts using dialogic intelligence principles and conversational heuristics to reduce the social sycophancy of AI to guide reflective human-AI interaction; (2) leveraging open-source datasets and psychological frameworks to define the AI’s personality and behavioral patterns to enhance the realism of human-AI interaction; and (3) integrating human-like UI/UX features such as conversational pacing and a human-like avatar to enhance naturalness, credibility, and sustained engagement.

The current design has several limitations. The most notable one is that the design  was tested with only a single scenario and a relatively small group of participants. Given this, while retrieval-based grounding improves contextual accuracy, the system may still not fully capture the complexity of real-life leadership contexts, where nonverbal cues, relational history, and organizational stakes play a significant role. Also, the AI partner operated in a reactive way, responding to participants input rather than intervening proactively at critical moments. As next steps and to address these limitations, the project team will broaden scenario coverage and conduct testing within organizations and engage larger, more diverse participant groups, aiming to acquire more user feedback, gain more insights about organizational  context and simulate more realistic scenarios. The team will also introduce scaffold fading mechanisms, whereby the AI ’s challenger intensity is progressively reduced as learners demonstrate greater dialogic competence, supporting gradual role internalization rather than sustained reliance on AI provocation. Finally, a log-based assessment approach involving turn-level coding of dialogic moves will be developed to evaluate the depth and development of dialogic thinking over time, with a  longerterm aim to explore how such training might contribute to preparing dialogically intelligent leaders who support sustainable and inclusive practices.
