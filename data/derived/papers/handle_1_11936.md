# Generative Failure: Learning Design in Programming with Generative AI

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study proposes the Generative Failure Method (GFM) to address challenges in applying Productive Failure to novice programming. Beginners often struggle with syntax errors, rarely compare diverse solutions, and lack tasks aligned with interests. Leveraging large language models (LLMs), GFM helps novices overcome initial barriers, adapt project goals, and incrementally cultivate open-ended exploration. Rather than imposing open-ended tasks, GFM expands problem contexts as learners progress. A pilot study found that LLM support enabled learners to produce functional applications, engage in iterative trial-and-error, and reframe “failure” as a constructive resource for learning.

Productive Failure (PF) has been shown to be effective in promoting a deep understanding of mathematical problem-solving (Kapur & Bielaczyc, 2012). The PF approach emphasizes fostering learners’ conceptual understanding: learners are encouraged to struggle with complex problems before receiving direct instruction. However, applying PF to programming poses unique challenges: (1) novices frequently encounter technical barriers, such as syntax errors and debugging difficulties, which often prevent from creating even one functional solution; (2) novices rarely have the opportunity to explore multiple approaches for each problem because programming exercises typically provide only a limited set of possible solutions; and (3) the exercises often lack personal relevance, preventing learners from integrating their own experiences and intuitive knowledge.

To address these, we propose the “Generative Failure” (GF) Method extending PF to programming using large language models (LLMs). GF is defined as the productive gap between learners' intentions and codes provided by LLM. Encountering GF compels learners to refine prompts, critique solutions, and iteratively develop their ideas, fostering deeper conceptual understanding. GFM leverages LLMs to (1) reduce technical barriers by generating executable code from learners' prompts, enabling meaningful trial-and-error; (2) provide diverse solutions for conceptual exploration and refinement on their codes; and (3) increase personal relevance by adapting examples to learners' experiences and intuitive knowledge to help them apply what they already know to the exercises. Thus, GFM maintains beneficial cognitive struggles inherent to PF, while leveraging LLMs to provide diverse solutions and personally relevant contexts, alleviating practical barriers for novice programmers.

## Method

Course design GFM adapted to “Software Design I.B.” which was a programming course using JavaScript (JS) at K University in 2024. The majority of the 35 enrolled students were first-time programmers. Figure 1 outlines the 15-session schedule: the course followed a project-based learning approach for open-ended task, with the students working on two application-development projects (orange). The students freely decided the theme of their applications. The students worked in teams of two or three, which were reorganized after the first project was completed. Peer reviews (green) were scheduled after each project to brush up on their work. Further, JS materials were introduced after the first project (purple) to enhance the students’ understanding of programming. The teacher’s explanations were kept to a minimum (blue). ## Overview of the 15-Session Schedule and Topics

The students could use a web application called "Classroom AI" during their projects. It was specifically developed for this course, featuring a user interface similar to ChatGPT. It provides the students with unrestricted, cost-free access to OpenAI's AI, equivalent to ChatGPT. 31 students consented to analyze their chat logs, providing data for this study. From these logs, 2,386 student utterances were analyzed using GPT-4o with custom-developed prompts (partially reflected in GF/non-GF categories mentioned below). Outputs were standardized via automated pattern matching (Ruby scripts) to address LLM variability, and then ultimately classified into GF/non-GF categories. GF utterances demonstrated the students’ trial-and-error behaviors, such as specific code modification requests, comparisons between different versions of codes, or requests for detailed explanations of codes. Non-GF utterances showed the students’ superficial acceptance, vague requests, or repetitive inputs. Representative examples are shown in Table 1. Post course reflections End‐of‐course survey responses consistently described activities such as “modified functionality,” “rewrote code,”‐ “adjusted parameters,” and “compared and evaluated functionality.” The students indicated that they gained insights by examining multiple code versions and clarifying points of failure with the LLM’s explanations. Efficacy of GFM The results highlighted that Generative Failure—intentionally engaging with failures by using LLMs as a partner—enhance the students in iteratively refine their ideas. This iterative approach aligns with constructionism (Papert, 1980), whereby learners produce tangible artifacts and learn through experimentation.

## Potential and limitations

GFM lowers barriers to programming for novices through encouraging iterative trial‐and‐error activities. The activities seem to enhance their creativity. In this exploratory study, the sample size is relatively small, and assessment of learning outcomes (e.g., coding proficiency) remains limited. Further research should measure skill acquisition and probe how learners generalize productive failure strategies beyond one course context. This study demonstrates how GFM leverages LLMs to support open‐ended programming tasks, reframing “failure” as a constructive resource for learning. Even novices without extensive technical backgrounds successfully developed and iterated software projects. Our findings suggest GFM’s potential to promote active participation and iterative refinement of programming exercises, reduce frustration, and deepen conceptual understanding.
