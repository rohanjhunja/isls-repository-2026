# A Lighter Shade of Black Boxes: Students’ Interpretations of the

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Scientific explanations rarely include all interactions and entities in a mechanism. Instead, some information is hidden in black boxes, keeping the focus on the explanation’s essence. Studies show that navigating between black-box explanations and detailed mechanistic explanations perplexes students as they misunderstand the reasons for including or omitting mechanistic information. Therefore, we examined students' preference towards information allowing for the formation of either a black-box explanation or a detailed mechanistic explanation. Fifty-two 9th grade students read three scientific problems in three contexts. Students stated and justified their preference of information. Analysis revealed students based choices on problems' context and explanation's productivity. Students preferred black-box information when recognizing the need to make a prediction and detailed information when recognizing a need to control phenomena. Results suggest clear explanatory goals assist students’ navigation between detailed and black-box explanations. This calls for a more explicit discussion around black boxes in the science classroom.

## Introduction

The new mechanistic philosophy, explained by Macham er, Craver and Darden (2000; MDC), identifies mechanistic reasoning as a tool scientists can use to explain, predict and control phenomena. A mechan istic explanation is constructed first and foremost from a set of entities, the cogs and bolts of the mechanism. Each of these entities has its own activity, i.e., the thing the entity does and by that affects other entities in the mechanism. Furthermore, each entity has its own properties, i.e., essential characteristics of the entity, allowing it to perform its activity. As one describes the entities and their features (e.g. activities, properties), along with a description of how they are organized together, it can be shown that when they are organized together just so, they produce the phenomenon one is trying to explain (Craver & Darden, 2013, p. 65). While mechanistic reasoning is an important part of the philosophy of science, it should not be considered a tool restricted to the use of scientists. Indeed,  in the context of cognition, mechanistic reasoning  has been described as a special case of causal reasoning, an  inference about the causal relationship between tw o events, made in a manner that is not solely dependent on co-variation information (Ahn & Kalish, 2000). Research in the fields of cognitive and developmental psychology has taught us that this form of causal thinking is so natural to us that 4-years-old children are able to comprehend simple forms of mechanistic explanations (Lombrozo et al., 2018), that both young children and adults display a bias towards experts sharing mechanistic informat ion (Lockhart et al., 2019) and that adults seek mechan istic explanations when trying to make sense of eve ryday events (Ahn et al., 1995).

While mechanistic reasoning seems to be performed a t all ages, Perkins and Grotzer (2005) provide a review concerning the development of causal thinking, suggesting that mechanistic reasoning is a dimen sion of causal thinking that advances gradually. For exampl e, while young students may provide surface level generalizations or token explanations when explaining a phenomenon (e.g. 'a trait is the result of a gene.'), more complex explanations, concerning underlying mechanisms are usually less common in such early ages. Following Russ et al.’s (2008) framework for analyzing classr oom discussion, multiple studies have also highligh ted the challenges students encounter while reasoning mechanistically in the classroom (e.g. Dickes et al., 2016; Duncan & Tseng, 2011; Haskel-Ittah & Yarden, 2017; van Mil et al., 2016). Furthermore, mechanistic reasoning has been included as one of the seven crosscutting concepts, listed as “Cause and effect: Mechanism and explanation”, in the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS) in the  United States (NGSS Lead States, 2013). This inclu sion contributed to the promotion of students’ mechanistic reasoning as one of the goals of science education. Although the ability to reason about mechanisms mig ht be related to a person's age or developmental stage, research in biology education has taught us that students’ ability to learn and reason about bi ological mechanisms also relies on disciplinary knowledge and specifically on students’ familiarity with the entities in a mechanism. Such studies have also shown that studen ts’ mechanistic reasoning can be advanced by exposi ng students to information about these entities. For example, data suggest that third graders improve in their ability ICLS2022 Proceedings 43 © ISLS to reason about a mechanism in the context of ecolo gy, as they iteratively conduct activities and are repeatedly exposed to the entities and entities’ activities in the mechanism underlying a phenomenon (Dickes et al., 2016). In a similar manner, in the context of genetics, 7th grade students have been found to use proteins, the key entities in the learned mechanism, mainly when they are fami liar with a protein’s relevant activity (Haskel‐Itt ah et al., 2020). Likewise, undergraduate students’ performanc e shows that they rely on their broader domain-spec ific knowledge of key entities to produce mechanistic explanations in novel contexts (Southard et al., 2017). As studies focus on students’ familiarity with entities in the mechanism, and as biological mechanisms tend to hold many entities, it becomes clear that the task of discussing all of these entities in the classroom becomes an immense one, to the teacher and students alike. Furthermore, considering the fact that some mechanisms can hold a vast number of entities and that every causal connection in a mechanism holds in itself a mechanism of its own, it can be argued that no mechanistic explanation is ever complete. Thus, we need to ask ourselves if we are paying as much attention as we need to the gaps in our mechanistic knowledge and mechanistic explanations. It is not surprising to find out that most people w ork with partial understandings of mechanisms, as no human can actually hold in his mind the complete details of a complex mechanism (Keil, 2019). This means most mechanistic explanations intrinsically hold in them some knowledge gaps, points in which a causal connection in the mechanism is left unexplained. While knowledge gaps in a mechanistic explanation might make us question the correctness or completeness of the explanation, we must remember that mechanistic discourse acknowledges the existence of knowledge gaps, and has even objectified them by naming and focusing on them under the term of “black boxes”. A black box, in the context of mechanistic reasoning, is a unit containing missing pieces, which we do not yet know how to fill in with entities and activities, in a way that it creates a gap between one step in the process to the next (Machamer et al., 2000). It should be emphasized that not every omitted or unknown detail in a phenomenon is a black box, just as black boxes are not simply forgotten details of a known mechanism. Black boxes are the parts of a mechanism for which we kno w the existence of a causal connection but are unab le or indisposed to explain the nature of this causal connection. In other words, it is knowing that A causes B without explaining how exactly A causes B.

While it would seem like black boxes are the nemesi s of any mechanistic philosophy, black boxes are regarded as a tool in scientific discourse. A good,  well-known example of black boxes in biology would  be the patterns and co-variation data produced by Gregory Mendel (1822-1884) in the 1860s, and rediscovered, during the beginning of 20th century, by Hugo de Vries (1848-1935), William Bateson (1861-1926) and others (Provine, 2001, p. 56). While Mendel was able to collect data in a precise, methodical manner, and use that data to detect inheritance patterns, he did not describe the under lying mechanisms of such patterns. The inherited fa ctors of Mendel, later to be known as ‘genes’, and gene variants, later to be known as ‘allele’, were never described in a mechanistic manner, but rather as part of a probabi listic model (Heim, 1991), meaning a model that all ows for prediction, without explaining the underlying mechanisms, hence a black box. This black box knowledge, at the beginning of the 20th century, was enough to articulate several laws, such as the law of dominance and  the law of segregation, allowing for the prediction of progeny population. Just as important, the acknowledgment of this probabilistic model as a black box (Morgan, 1909), which contains some unknown underlying mechanism th at needs to be discovered, allowed scientists to focus their research on the entities inside this black box. By the 1960’s the scientific community was able to describe the entities and the entities’ interaction s within it (i.e., unpack the black box), a joint eff ort that has led to the accepted model of today’s m odern times, describing the activities of several entities such as the DNA, RNA and ribosomes (Cobb, 2015). In a si milar manner, while the central dogma of molecular biology has been used a working model since the 1960s, th e full structure of the ribosome, for which Ada Yonath was awarded the Nobel Prize for, was described only in the year 2000 (Weizmann Institute of Science, 2009). These examples go to show that scientists use and apply mechanistic explanations containing black boxes, in a productiv e manner, all the time. Furthermore, while this cou ld be attributed to some kind of lack of choice (i.e., scientists have to make do with incomplete explanations), it should be made clear that even today, most mechanistic exp lanations concerning the central dogma would not be nefit from the knowledge concerning the precise structure of the ribosome. Likewise, today, a genetic consul tant can calculate, predict and explain the risk for the Tay-Sachs disease in one’s family (an explanation cont aining the Mendelian black box) by considering the alleles in a family, without unpacking any details about the c entral dogma (i.e., the underlying mechanism). In summary,  in scientific discourse, unpacking a black box whe n formulating a mechanistic explanation is dependent on the goal of the explanation, whether it is to ex plain the mechanism itself, predict the outcome of certain conditions or control a phenomenon by manipulating elements in its underlying mechanism (Machamer et al., 2000).

Although scientists accept and appreciate the exist ence of explanations containing black boxes, in the science classroom, science teachers usually hold the notion that while there might be different explanations using different amounts of detail and information, good e xplanations need to be as comprehensive as possible  by ICLS2022 Proceedings 44 © ISLS containing as many complex details as possible, in an effort to achieve a high a degree of similarity between the details in the explanation and the details in the phenomenon being explained (Justi & Gilbert, 2002). Furthermore, as demonstrated by a study in genetics education, s tudents find it difficult to rationalize the co-exi stence of explanations including black boxes, alongside with explanations including that same unpacked black box. This has been specifically demonstrated in the context o f genetics where black box explanations using infor mation about Mendelian inheritance patterns co-exist alongside unpacked mechanism explanations using central dogma information (Gericke et al., 2013). While science a ppreciates the existence and productiveness of blac k box explanations, the focus, in the classroom, on an ex planation’s ‘completeness’, and the lack of attenti on to the productiveness of an explanation, is probably one o f the reasons students often expect explanations to  be ‘complete’ without any regard to their usefulness (Passmore et al., 2014). Accordingly, in the traditional biology classroom it might be difficult to find examples of  students struggling with the idea of an explanatio n’s productiveness, simply because they are usually req uired to give the most ‘complete’ explanation inste ad of choosing the most useful explanation or reason about the productiveness of their explanation. While students’ and teachers’ tendency to search for the most ‘complete’ explanation can be attributed to a general bias towards detailed mechanistic expl anations, it has been demonstrated that providing c ontextual cues concerning the goals of the explanation dimini shes some of this bias. For example, Lockhart et al. (2019) demonstrated that while children and adults regard people providing mechanistic information about an object as more knowledgeable, they are more inclined to consu lt a non-mechanistic expert when they are informed that they need to sell an object. In a similar way, when  adults are asked to rate the ‘goodness’ of mechani stic or teleological explanation, they prefer explanations that highlight the kinds of relationships that they expect to be useful for the task at hand (Vasilyeva et al., 2017). To make matters more complicated, it has been demonstrated that students reason about their choice of an explanation using several non-etiological reasons, such as the level of interest they find in the explanation and explan ation complexity. For example, when students were a sked to choose between a causal explanation or a teleologic al explanation to a phenomenon, some preferred a ca usal explanation because they viewed it as more ‘interesting’, while others preferred a teleological explanation because they viewed it as less ‘complex’ (Trommler et al., 2018). Whereas the above-mentioned studies show that the c hoice between different explanations can be affected by contextual cues and that in some cases reasons for choosing an explanation can be non-etiological, all of them compare between epistemologically distinct explanations (e.g., mechanistic vs. teleological; mechanistic vs. functional). However, as previously discussed i n this article, in some cases the difference betwee n two mechanistic explanations can be the degree with which certain black boxes have been unpacked. While scientists determine whether to unpack a black box by evaluating the goal of the explanation, and specifically whether their aim is to explain, predict or control the phenomenon, it is not clear whether students can appreciate the existence of explanations containing black boxes when exposed to such goals. In light of the above, it became of interest to exa mine whether students are able to appreciate the co-existence of explanations containing black boxes, a longside with explanations containing these same un packed black boxes, when these explanations are presented in a context of different explanatory goals. To shed light on this problem, we strived to answer the following research question: How do 9th grade students distinguish between the explanatory powers of black box explanations when faced with different contexts, containing different explanatory goals?

## Research context and population

Ninth grade students in Israel are required to lear n genetics. The mandatory curriculum includes chapt ers concerning the mechanism of meiosis, the mechanism of the central dogma and Mendel’s principles. While Mendel’s principles are essentially a black box whi ch allows the prediction of traits in a progeny, th e central dogma is the underlying mechanism which explains how exactly genes affect traits. Thus, while the appearance of traits can be predicted or explained by taking i nto account the existing alleles and using Mendel’s principles, the unpacked central dogma mechanistic explanation allows detailing how entities, such as the DNA and RNA molecules, interact to produce such traits.

Fifty-two 9th grade students from two classes in the same school participated in this study. Both classes were taught by the same teacher, a science teacher with a M.Sc. in biology and on her 1st year of teaching. Students were taught about phenomenon and mechanisms in gene tics, specifically the mechanism of meiosis, the mechanism of the central dogma of molecular biology and principles derived from the Mendelian black bo x, in that order. No explicit reference was made, in the classroom, to the term ‘black box’. Furthermore, the teacher did ICLS2022 Proceedings 45 © ISLS not pay any attention to the merits or constraints of using the Mendelian black box explanation vs. the unpacked central dogma mechanism.

## Research design

To examine students’ preference of explanation in d ifferent contexts, each student read three scenario s in three different genetic contexts: gene therapy, genetic c ounseling and genetic engineering. Each scenario in cluded a phenomenon in genetics lacking information about it s underlying mechanism. The question at the end of each problem presented the students with a preference ju dgment task in which they were asked to choose whic h information is needed, in their eyes, to solve the problem at hand: a. information concerning the discussed alleles’ Mendelian inheritance patterns or b. the mechanism by which the gene affects the trait. The three problems dealt with the issues of gene therapy, genetic concealing and genetic engineering as summarized in Figure 1. We strived to minimize any effect derived from students’ ability to use or come up with any explanations. Therefore, we asked students to choose which information they view as needed to solve the problem but did not ask them to solve it or even judge a written soluti on. We consider students’ choice of option ‘a’ as a preference towards black box information and students’ choice of option ‘b’ as a preference towards unpacked mechanism information. Thus, we use the term ‘black box infor mation’ and ‘unpacked mechanism information’ to den ote students’ preference of either option ‘a’ or ‘b’, accordingly. This is not to suggest that students were aware of the type of information their answer is related to, but to classify students’ choice of needed information to the type of explanation that can be constructed from that information. As each of the students provided three jus tifications, one to each problem, a total of 156 ju stifications were coded by two coders. Students’ justifications were coded using an iterative bottom-up procedure, to reveal three categories: explain, predict and control. Justifications were coded as ‘explain’ when students wrote that the information they deemed as needed to solve the problem can be used to describe the phenomenon better or gain knowledge about it, without mentioning any other im plications of this knowledge. For example, in the g ene therapy problem, a student preferred to gain knowle dge about the mechanism by which the allele leads t o the discussed trait: “Because I am interested in knowing how the defect in [the allele] CCR5 causes immunity.” The ‘predict’ code was used when students justified their choice by writing that the information achieved enables them to foresee the outcome of the conditions described. For example, in the genetic counseling problem, a student preferred to gain knowledge about the inheritance pattern of the discussed allele because: “This will help the woman understand if her offspring will be sick.” The code ‘control’ was used when students claimed that the information can assist them in manipulating the outcome of the conditions described. As six students did not choose any of the options in the third problem and as an analysis of their justi fications revealed they did not see any of the expl anations as relevant to solving the problem, these students were excluded from the Chi Square test of the third problem. ## Discussion

Scientists accept and appreciate the existence of explanations containing black boxes, as unpacking a black box when formulating a mechanistic explanation is dependent on the goal of the explanation: whether it is to explain the mechanism itself, predict the outcome of certai n conditions or control a phenomenon by manipulatin g elements in its underlying mechanism (Machamer et al., 2000). However, in the classroom, teachers and students focus on an explanation’s ‘completeness’ (Justi & Gilbert, 2002; Passmore et al., 2014). In the genetics classroom, students have access to both black box explanations and unpacked mechanism explanations. However, choosing to use the wrong type of explanation for the task a t hand can lead students to incorrect conclusions (Gericke & El-Hani, 2018). Studies suggest that students strug gle with understanding the differences between blac k box explanations and unpacked mechanism explanations (Gericke et al., 2013; Marbach-Ad & Stavy, 2000). Our data points to a clear preference towards infor mation needed for the construction of a detailed mechanistic explanation in the context of gene therapy and gene engineering. Furthermore, data points to a clear preference towards information needed for the const ruction of a black box explanation in the context o f gene counselling. As students chose the type of information needed to solve the problem but were not asked to explicitly name each of the options, or even compare between them, it is impossible for us to determine whether they view each of the explanations as a black box explanation or a mechanistic explanation. However, our data does suggest that students were able to recognize some differenc es between the two explanations as they correlated each of them with different problems. Furthermore, students' justifications for choosing each type of information appear to point to their use of contextual cues embedded in the problems, a practice described in previous studies (e.g., Vasilyeva et al., 2017). Students referred to information concerning the underlying central-dogma-mechanism as one that allows control of the phenomenon, while re ferring to information concerning the Mendelian black box as one that allows for prediction. Therefore, our data hints at students' ability to classify each type of the offered information as different types of explanations, with different affordances towards their use in different problems. In that sense, it would seem that while previous research has demonstrated students' inability to rationalize the coexistence of central dogma explanations and Mendeli an explanations (Gericke et al., 2013), providing s tudents with contextual cues about the different goals achi eved by using such explanations allows students to produce justified decisions in the context of this co-existence. Although in this study students chose the content of information needed to solve a problem, they did not explicitly refer to the type of information (black box vs unpacked). However, we suggest that this is an important starting point to understand how students classify the information needed to formulate each type of explanation. According to our analysis, it would seem that students in this study perceived the provided black box explanations in a very distinct and consistent manner. When stud ents faced a problem in the context of genetic coun seling, almost all students in this study expressed a need for information about allele inheritance pattern, thereby taking a black box explanation approach. It should be noted, that the genetic counseling problem contained cues calling for the prediction of traits in the offspring population. The use of a black box explanation offers exactly this power. Therefore, students’ preferences seem to reflect a very strong connection, made by the students, between the black box explanation and its application in explaining and predicting the distribution of phenotypes in a population of progeny. Supporting this claim are the students’ justifications for their choices, as almost all students in this study expressed a wish to predict the outcome of the spec ified setup conditions. The above data suggest that  students view black box explanations as explanations that are strongly connected to the ability to predict traits distribution in a progeny, and to predict the effect of an allel e on a trait. While students acknowledged the black  box explanation’s power to offer prediction, it also became apparent that students perceived the unpacked mechanism explanation as one that allows manipulation of the scientific situation, in an exclusive manner (i.e., they did not view the black box explanation as one that offers a manipulation or explanation of traits). Our analysis of students' justification to the use of each type of information leads to the conclusion that students judged the information presented to them in terms of productivity. Furthermore, while in a mi nority of ICLS2022 Proceedings 48 © ISLS cases students presented justifications based on the need to explain phenomena, in most cases, students expressed a need to predict or control the outcomes of phenom ena. We find this approach, towards the productivit y of an explanation, concurrent with the way scientists view and use different types of information, as well as black box explanations (Machamer et al., 2000). Building on these students’ intuitive understandings, in the classroom, may enhance students’ distinction between different types of explanations and prevent conflation between a Mendelian black box perspective and a molecular mechanistic perspective, a known concern in the field of genetics education (Gericke et al., 2013).

In light of the above, our study supports the argument for explicitly teaching about the idea of black box explanations in the science classroom. We submit the idea that while classroom discussions tend to focus on the 'completeness' of explanations (Justi & Gilbert, 20 02; Passmore et al., 2014), real life explanations are rarely complete. Rather than being complete, explanations can be productive and can be judged according to th eir productivity (Passmore et al., 2014). Our results s uggest that students have the intuitive basis for g aining an understanding of the complex nature of various explanations. Therefore, explicitly pointing at different types of explanations and especially the presence of black boxes in explanations might be a way to help them establish a better understanding of the nature of different exp lanations. Namely, students understand that the nee d for unpacking black boxes is dependent on the contextual features of the problem. Thus, explicitly referring to black boxes in mechanistic explanations may help them und erstand what they can (and cannot) do with a given mechanistic explanation.

Lastly, this identified students’ sensitivity to th e relations between contextual features of the prob lem and the level of unpacking for a good-enough explanation calls for putting emphasis on the framing of questions and problems. It has been previously demonstrated that students who are unaware of the existence of a black box (e.g., the causal connection between genes and traits) find it harder to use new mechanistic information concerning the contents of that black box to construct a mechanistic understanding of the phenomena (Haskel-Ittah & Yarden, 2018). We submit that any mechanistic information discussed in the classroom should include not only an explicit discussion of the existence of the black box, but also a discussion of the reasons behind its unpacking. We view this framing as one that should include contextual hints on whether the problem requires an explanatio n, a prediction or a control of a phenomenon. As long as  problems presented to students hold little or no c ontextual hints, discussion about the goodness of an explanation cannot be broader than the rote learning of details. However, once an explanation becomes a solution to a context ualized problem, other features of the explanation can be evaluated and a more authentic discussion can follow.
