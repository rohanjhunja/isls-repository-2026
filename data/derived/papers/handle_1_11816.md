# Podcasts as Public Pedagogy: Diagnostic and Prognostic Framing as Processes of Ideological Articulation in the Politics of Women’s Sports

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Podcasts have taken a prominent role in the construction of sociopolitical reality. In this paper, we use critical discourse analysis with a focus on discourses of victimhood to analyze two podcast episodes about women’s sports, “The Fundamental Rule of the W: No Place for-isms” from the podcast A Touch More and “A Stolen Dream” from The Joyful Warriors Podcast. We combine recent learning sciences theorizations of ideology as interactional sensemaking with conceptualizations of diagnostic and prognostic framing from social movement studies to show how podcast participants construct divergent visions of social problems in women’s sports and their solutions, modeling sociopolitical sensemaking as a form of public pedagogy. Broader social concerns about race and gender both become problems for women’s sports and echo out into a more encompassing sociopolitical landscape, with implications for collective sensemaking that will shape who belongs in society.

## Introduction: Politicized learning in podcasts

How do people learn to interpret socially shared ev ents in terms of their sociopolitical import and me aning? In this paper, we examine two podcast episodes with very different perspectives from cisgender white women about women’s sports. In the first episode, “The Fundamental Rule of the W: No Place for-isms,” Sue Bird and Megan Rapinoe of the podcast A Touch More unpack race and gender biases they see in the WNBA. In the second episode, “A Stolen Dream,” Tiffany Justice, host of the Moms  for Liberty-affiliated Joyful Warriors Podcast talks with former collegiate swimmer and conservative activist  Riley Gaines about trans competitors in women’s swimming.

Podcasts have taken a prominent role in the constru ction of sociopolitical reality. In the 2024 US presidential election, major party candidates repeatedly appeared on podcasts–part of an ongoing shift in the media ecology. As Brady Brickner-Wood (2024) argued in The New Yorker, conversation podcasts engender a unique sense of intimacy, allowing politicians to “begin to sound like pals bantering at a pre-game, with the listener as a silent confidant” (para. 3). Furthermore, podcasts provided candidates with access to specific de mographic and interest-based audiences, who may resonate with particular m essaging. These demographic silos of “silent confidants,” we argue, are important sites where people construct ideology and becom e politicized (Curnow et al., 2018). In this paper we focus on how podcast hosts build political logics through claims to victimhood, pain, and/or justice (Chouliaraki, 2024). According to Young (2020), this “didactic, emotion-filled speech… cloaked in moral certainty and purporting to present an une quivocal truth” (p. 210) is part of an emerging and powerful learning landscape for listeners. Podcasts are part of distinct social spheres where diverging  discourses have different audiences that may not talk to one another (Young, 2020, p. 9). We understand podcasts as a site where practices of  sensemaking are modeled and shared with these publics. In this unique form of public pedagogy, po dcasters present patterns of sensemaking that may then be taken up by others. This sensemaking is political. While we referred above to partisan campaigning as an example of how podcasting has risen to prominence, we are m ore interested here in “politics” in the sense of h ow social goods are classified and allocated (Vea, 2020; Armstrong & Bernstein, 2008; Gee, 2005). In both “The Fundamental Rule of the W” and “A Stolen Dream,” podcast participants identify social problems and frame them for their audiences. We ask: How do participants in  podcasts about women’s sports collaboratively cons truct the framing of social problems as a kind of public pedagogy? Theoretical resources We build on literature in the critical learning sci ences that has investigated the construction of ide ology as a learning process. Drawing on the work of Stuart Hal l, Philip et al. (2017) define ideology as sensemak ing “that stabilizes, challenges, and/or transforms the distribution of material and symboli c resources in society” (p. 184). Ideology, in this view, operates through representa tional systems, including concepts and images that are “articulated,” or connected, in particular ways. Ph ilip et al. sh ow how chains of meaningful associations in the representational field can become shifted or “reart iculated” over the course of social interaction, le ading to potential expansions and convergences in ideologica l sensemaking. Building on this work, Daniel et al.  (2023) showed how participants in a n elementary science professional development program  drew on substrate (shared interactional resources; Goodwin, 2018) across mult iple timescales to rearticulate ideological sensema king. In their analysis, substrates in-the-moment, in recent community time, and in societal time shaped the kinds of ideological rearticulation that were possible (pp. 583-584).

In this paper, we aim to extend the recent work on ideology in learning through the lens of framing, drawing on the influence of social movement studies. The social world is perspectival and full of ambi guity, and people often engage in interpretive debat es over the significance of events. Framing is a so cial process through which people negotiate the meaning of ambiguous phenomena. Framing processes in discourse help social actors understand the meaning of the world around them and what sort of actions are required of them (Goffman, 1974). Learning sciences scholarship has explored framing in educational contexts. For example, learning scientists have investigated the use of epistemic and positional fr ames (Kaur & Dasgupta, 2024), where epistemic frame s are interpretations of knowledge and knowledge sources (Scherr & Hammer, 2009) and positional frames entai l interpretations of roles and relationships (Vande Sande & Greeno, 2012). Engle (2006) also investigat ed the impact of how educators frame the contexts of activ ity such that certain framings make connections bet ween classroom and other contexts clearer, supporting transfer. However, the increase in attention within the field on politicized learning contexts provides opportunities for the field to learn from social movement scholar s who have explored the impact of framing on how pe ople organize collective futures. Social movem ent scholar David Snow (2004) helps us understand t he link between framing and ideology, arguing that a key function o f framing is articulating, or drawing together disp arate elements of social reality into a coherent meaning system. Framing activity in mo vements can build public consensus, move people to action, and demobilize the opposition (Snow et al., 2018, p. 395). Within the ideological framing of social problems, we focus on two key framing processes: diagnostic framing and prognostic framing. Diagnostic frames i nclude “a diagnosis of some event or aspect of soci al life… as problematic and in need of repair or change; and the attribution of blame or responsibility for the problematized state of affairs” (Snow et al., 2018, p. 396). They typically involve the construal of a situation as a form of injustice (Benford & Snow, 2000; Gamson, 1992). Then, prognos tic frames propose a solution to the identified pro blem (Snow et al., 2018, p. 396). They articulate, given  the problem, what the proper course of action shou ld be to ameliorate it. We argue that podcasts offer moments of ideological sensemaking that may impact broader publics. In Talk and Social Interaction, Erickson (2004) suggests that social interactions are not separate from institutional life. Instead, he writes, “ It is the institutional principles for the conduct o f affairs and its standard operating procedures which frame the situation within which i nteraction takes place” (p. 180). In the case of po dcasts discussing women’s sports, for example, the princip les of i nstitutions like the WNBA and the NCAA lay the groundwork for discussions of fairness, justice, and boundary creation in women’s sports.

## Methods

We examine two podcasts from white women who analyze the role of women in sports. We use critical discourse analysis to understand how the podcast hosts were sensemaking together as a form of public pedagogy that worked to frame boundaries within the ever more popular ve nue of women’s sports. The two podcasts present ver y different worldviews and framings of similar terrai n. Within the selected podcast episodes, we examined the stories that are retold as a form of interactional substrate (Daniel et al., 2023) in the interest of modeling public sensemaking of critical moments in women’s sports. Once the podcasts and corresponding episodes were selected, we created transcripts of speech based on automated transcription published with the episodes. We then conducted individual open coding of both transcripts, with reference back to the original YouTube videos of gestures where relevant. After open coding separately, we came together and looked across codes for emerging themes within the text to discern patterns of argument ation and ideological modeling throughout the podcast. Af ter identifying themes, we constructed categories that began to ans wer our empirical questions (Saldaña, 2011) with th e discourse. This was not a neutral gathering process, but rather one that involved understanding claims  to power, pain, and privilege in the effort to shape sociopolitical narratives within women’s sports. We took inspiration from Chouliaraki (2024) as she used discourse analysis to analyze victimhood in public discourse. She used popular discourses to un derstand how claims of being “wronged” have become tools of the far right to legitimize social gain. She shared her methodological approach as follows: I approach these various texts, linguistic and visu al, as discourses–that is meanings produced in and through power struggles over who owns the la nguage of pain, who deserves to be protected as a victim, and who should be punished a s a perpetrator –that more often than not use victimhood to protect privilege, silence the st ructurally vulnerable, and legitimize the existing social order. (p. 12) Our study also understands discourses have the power to legitimize perspectives through the assemblage of claims to expertise, safety, emotion, and fairness to shap e ideology. In what follows we share our analysis o f these incidences of public sensemaking.

## Findings

In each podcast, white women involved in women’s ba sketball and women’s swimming, respectively, discus s “viral moments” in order to develop social and poli tical sensemaking about injustice, fairness, and id entities within women’s sports. Through these moments of analysis, white women athletes model sensemaking about the boundaries of sporting, adversity, and identity. Th rough these conversations the athletes 1) build up their credibility as serious athletes and therefore exper ts, 2) set up boundaries about acceptability in moments of controversy, and 3) offer visions of opposing polit ical ideologies within women’s sports. Their commen taries model sociopolitical teaching and learning that build on factions of the women’s sporting world that have become ever more popular and politically contentious. The first case offers a vision of women’s sports where a multiracial collective led by Black women pushes back on racist, sexist, and transphobic narratives coming from members of the media and fans. The second case offers a vision of women’s sports built on fear, violence, and dehumanization of trans people in order to protect white cisgender women from the “harm” caused by trans women competing in their sport. The following cases offer insight into how podcasts contribute to socio-political teaching and learning through public sensemaking.

Case 1: “The fundamental rule of the W” In the first podcast, Sue Bird and Megan Rapinoe offered their commentary on a viral moment in the WNBA. To begin, they shared the viral clip of a press conference between white sports reporter Christine Brennan from USA Today and DiJonai Carrington, a Black woman basketball player from the Phoenix Sun who had been playing in the league for three years. In the clip, Brennan asked Carrington about an incident in the first-round playoff game that took place on October 2, 2024 where DiJonai Carrington and Caitlin Clark, a white woman who was a rookie and an emerging star in the WNBA collided while Carrington was playing defense: Brennan:  DiJonai, did you, when you went and kind of swatted at Cai tlin, did you intend to hit her in the eye and if so could you just or if not either way, could you talk about what happened on that play?

Carrington:  I just don't even know why I would intend to hit an ybody in the eye. That doesn't even  make sense to me. But no, I didn ’t. I didn ’t know I hit her actually. I was trying to make a play on the ball a nd I guess I followed through and I hit her. So obviously, it ’s never intentional. That ’s not even like the type of player that I am.

Brennan: Did you and Marina kind of laugh about it afterward s? It looked like there was later on in the game–they caught you guys laughing about it. Carrington: No, I just told you I didn ’t even know I hit her. So I can ’t laugh about something I didn’t know happened.

We consider the audio clip of Brennan and Carringto n as a form of substrate for the sociopolitical ana lysis that follows. This viral moment allowed the podcasters t o set the terms of the social and political debate taking place in the press and on social media. Long temporal gaps can make prior events more difficult to draw on as substrate for ideological rearticulation (Daniel et al., 2023). The ability to play past audio within the presen t conversation helps podcasters overcome such temporal hurdles. Ju st after sharing the clip, Bird, who recently retired after playing 22 years in the WNBA as a 4-time WNBA Champion and 5-time Olympic Gold medalist, reminded the audience of her long and storied career. She also began as a rookie who, like Clark, was test ed when she first joined the professional league. Setting up her credibility as a star player who understands the rigors of both college and professional basketball, she analyzed the incident from the perspective of an elite white female athlete. Diagnostic framing: Racist and sexist social narratives The clip went viral because, according to Bird and Rapinoe, the reporter asked a “loaded question,” wh ich they defined as “a complex question that contains a cont roversial assumption or falsehood and is often used  to manipulate the respondent. The goal of a loaded question is to limit the respondent to a specific answer that serves the questioner’s agenda” (Episode 9, 20:18-20:32). In providing this definition, Bird and Rapi noe offered their first social analysis –helping listeners to identify a kind of question me mbers of the press use to manipulate the respondents. In this case, Rapinoe argued, “The whole thing is disingenuous because the whole premise relies on the belief that DiJonai, and this is something that’s been happening all year specifically with DiJonai and Caitlin, that DiJonai is targeting Caitlin or going extra ha rd at her or whatever.” Alongside the clip of Brenn an and Carrington, the definition of a “loaded question” p rovided additional substrate for the rest of the di agnostic framing to follow.

Bird and Rapinoe analyzed the clip to unpack the layered racist and sexist tropes that play out in the mass media and on social media about the world of the WN BA. Bird called out the sexist narratives circulati ng on social media and sports journalism, which accused t he WNBA players of being “petty and jealous” in the ir treatment of the new star rookie Caitlyn Clark. To Bird and Rapinoe, the assumption that Carrington ha d “targeted” Clark was articulated in connection with  the sexist trope that women are “pe tty and jealous” of other successful women. Bird then offered a historical account of sexism in the WNBA, suggesting that these narratives about pettiness and jealousy were false. Instead, she argued, it should be expected that professional athletes would guard good players aggressively.

Along with the gendered trope of “petty and jealous ” women, Bird and Rapinoe conducted an analysis of implicit racism. What was at issue was the way t hat narratives of white and Black players were bein g used to mark certain aspects of play as being either a legitimate part of women’s sports or as an inappropriate aberration. Bird and Rapinoe made several moves in their intera ction to analyze the use of narrative in this insta nce of play. First, Rapinoe says, “shout out [to Carrington], first team, all defense, only guard to be on defense. DiJonai is one of the best defenders in the WNBA.” In the WNBA, pl ayers receive votes from head coaches in the league  to be designated as “first team” or “second team” for bot h defense and offense. Carrington was voted Firs t Team AllDefense, an honor representing the very best defensive players from across the league in a given year. The players receive votes irrespective of their position, but b ecause guards tend to be shorter relative to other positions they often do not get voted “all-defense.” It was therefore notable that Carrington was the only guard to make the “first team,” indicating that she is exceptional at the very kind of defense that Brennan had questioned Carrington about in the post-game interview. Carrington was among the very best defenders, and we should expect that Carrington is “going to be on you,” Rapinoe continued. “Like she’s going to be in there.” The implication was that Carrington was just doing her job, and she was doing it exceptionally well. Next, Rapinoe advanced an argument about how unlikely it would be that a player even could in an y practical sense target another player’s eye. “First of all, the square footage of her [Clark’s] eyeball is pretty small,” Rapinoe said. “It is so hard to poke someone  in the eye.” Given the practical difficulty of hitting another p layer’s eye intentionally, it was ther efore very unlikely that Carrington would have intended to do so and succeed ed. Ultimately, the problem was problematic social narratives. “[T]he way that DiJonai gets narrated a nd other Black players in this league get narrated is they are targeting her. They are violently going after her,”  Rapinoe said. “The instinct is to protect the narr ation of the white players and to go after the narration of Blac k players. And that is a problem.” Whereas Brennan’ s “loaded question” assumed problematic targeting by Carrington, Bird and Rapinoe saw this instance as an example of one of the league’s best players doing exactly what was expected of her. Prognostic framing: Players, journalists, and league leaders must push back The diagnostic framing that Bird and Rapinoe offered lent itself to multiple forms of prognostic framing as ways of imagining a response that could solve the proble ms. Woven throughout their conversation, Bird and R apinoe modeled how their reasoning about the problems in t he WNBA led to particular solutions –all of which had to recognize and address the context of sexism, racism, and homophobia that the WNBA existed within. The solutions included the responsibilities of players, institutions like the WNBA and media companies, and everyone participating in public discourse around women’s sp orts. Some of these responses to the problems were framed as ongoing.

This was the case with the responses of players, who had repeatedly tried to dispel the notion that anyone was personally targeting Clark. Part of the frustra tion was that the narrative of targeting was underm ining the recognition of Carrington’s excellent basketball playing. Bird articulated how players transformed how  they saw themselves and their responsibilities as a result: I would have loved to have been spoken about just f or my play… But nobody ever let us do that. So what happened? We started to build a backb one, a little bit of an identity. We understood that in order to push our league forward, we were going to have to c ombat these things. And then you know what else we learned? A, it feels good to stand up for yourself. It feels even better to stand up for others. We starte d to learn that people were learning from us and be able to follow us in a different way. And who are we following? Black women. Sexism and racism were nothing new to women’s baske tball players. In fact, Bird pointed to her 22-year career to argue that in order to be taken seriously, women  athletes had to learn to defend themselves against  sexist narratives. They learned to build a “backbone” and an “identity” in order to fight back. Moreover, people were watching and learning through their example. Bird recounted that they learned to push back. In the final line, Bird recounted that this learning did not materialize ou t of thin air,  but that white women learned this by following Black women. This, she argued, “push[ed] our league  forward” and set up a space where players had to l ead in asking more from the media and fans. Hammering the point home, Rapinoe added, “It is necessary to speak plainly and be explicitly clear about what your values are,  what is not okay, racism is never okay. Sexism is never okay, whether online and in real life, misogyny, homophobia–all the things are never okay.” Bird and Rapinoe further argued that this could not  only be the responsibility of the players. Journal ists and the media had an important form of responsibility to fulfill as well: Rapinoe:  And back to your point about the greater responsibi lity that the media has in their line of questioning, in their understanding o f the long history of the WNBA, in their understanding and appreciation and w eaving in of the

Bird: Well, I’m just like, where ’s the article that deep-dives how the targeting is not taking place?

Rapinoe:  Right.

Bird:  Does that exist?

Rapinoe:  Nobody’s doing that. Some journalists like Brennan had followed the WNBA for many years and were quite familiar, according to Bird and Rapinoe, with the history of bias within and surrounding the league (“their understanding of the long history… and weaving in of the cultural context that the WNBA lives in”). As a result, they  argued, it was a matter of “journalistic integrity” that journalists had not provided a critical analysis of the “targeting” discourse. A further issue, implicating both players and media, that needed to be addressed was the lack of specificity in identifying where the problematic di scourses were coming from. Bird called for a “reset” in how everyone spoke about fans as a monolith: “Fever fans,” “Caitlin fans,” “new fans.” That actu ally has to go. That has to  go. That is  a large group of people… And what we’re talking about as we get into the meat of this, we’re talking about the faction of that group that is pushing racist agendas and is pushing hate and creating, yo u know, divisiveness online, acting as fans, acting as Fever fans, acting as Caitlin fans.

Here, Bird acknowledged that referring to “fans” as  a group was convenient shorthand in the context of  an interview, but it lacked sufficient precision. To her it was clear that rather than all fans of Caitlin Clark, or of her team, the Indiana Fever, push ing the problematic narratives of “targeting,” it w as rather a small “faction” doing so. Broadening the source of the narrative to “fans ” more generally might give the narrative more soci al power by making it seem like a point of broad consensus among all fans. Further, here Bird made the distinction that the problematic faction was merely “acting as fans,” marking them as outside the world of legitimate WNBA fans. Finally, Bird and Rapinoe engaged in prognostic fra ming about the responsibility of institutional acto rs involved in women’s basketball. Early in the podcas t episode, Rapinoe applauded the response of Women’ s National Basketball Players Association Executive D irector Terri Jackson to the Brennan-Carrington exchange, saying that Jackson had “rightly and very strongly called out” the interview as “an attempt to bait DiJonai.” Later in the episode, Rapinoe contrasted this institutional response with that of WNBA Commissioner Cathy Engelbert, who had suggested that players respond to racist and sexist narratives by taking breaks from social media: From the WNBA's perspective, A, the WNBA and the le ague office and Cathy’s role specifically is basically to protect and shepherd t he league both from a business perspective, from a player welfare perspective, from a health and safety perspective, and from a growth and investment perspective… And it needed to be plainly  stated,  probably multiple times throughout the year, that this is who we are.

Whereas Jackson had defended Carrington and problem atized Brennan’s interview questions, Engelbert had dismissed the concerns of players as being a social media problem. The right thing to do, which Rapinoe connected to Engelbert’s institutional role, was to state pub licly that “this is who we are” –people who stand against sexism and racism. Throughout this case, Bird and Rapinoe push ed back on who gets framed as a victim within the WNBA, suggesting that a white cisgender star rookie, Cailin Clark, was not the victim of bullying. Rat her the lack of protection of Black players who were subjec t to a “racist agenda” were of much more concern. These moments of analysis are an articulation of ideology that connects moments when institutional actors take seriously and act upon the concerns of women of color when they express a need for safety from racist and sexist threats. Case 2: “A stolen dream” In the second case, we examine an episode of The Joyful Warrior  Podcast from Moms for Liberty, hosted by Tiffany Justice, a white cisgender woman and right-wing activist. In this episode, the guest was Riley  Gaines, a white cisgender former college swimmer from Univers ity of Kentucky. We attend to the substrate provide d by Gaines’s retelling of  the moment in which Lia Thomas, a white transgender  woman competed in the NCAA Championships in the 2021-2022 season. Thomas won the 500 freestyle, and placed fifth and eighth, respectively, in the 200 and 100 freestyle. Gaines tied Thomas for fifth place in the 200 freestyle race during that championship meet, which became a flashpoint in political discou rse and in the courts. According to ESPN, after thi s event, “Thomas’ name was invoked in statehouses across the  country as legislators introduced bills d esigned to restrict transgender athletes’ ability to compete in sports, sometimes impacting athletes starting in elementary school. The bills, they said, were needed to protect the sancti ty of women's sports” (Barnes, 2022). Vocal opposit ion to Thomas’s participation within women’s swimming was led by Gaines, who wrote a book on her experience of the NCAA Championship debate.

Diagnostic framing: Victimization of vulnerable girls Early in the episode, Justice asked Gaines to describe her work ethic in college.  Gaines’s response worked to establish herself as someone who dedicated herself fully to swimming and sacrificed other aspects of college life to compete as a Division I athlete. She recounted h aving multiple practices in a day, icing muscles, g oing to classes, doing homework, eating dinner, and sleepin g, with n o time for anything else. Then Gaines said, “you woke up and did it all again… it was hard work and [to say that] sacrifices were made would be an understatement.” After seeking to establish credibility as an athlet e and a hard worker, Gaines decried the unfairness of competing with transgender women in swimming. Gaines modeled for listeners how this narrative of working hard could combine with victimhood to create boundaries around who should have access to the material and symbolic resources related to women’s swimming. To create these boundaries, Gaines used em otional appeals, claims to innocence, misgendering,  and use of a deadname (1) to bolster her own narrative while deh umanizing Lia Thomas  and therefore foreclosing access to material and symbolic goods associated with competi ng in the sport that she loved. Gaines’s diagnostic framing constructed the problem of innocent, hardworking, and vulnerable “girls” being victimized by a “towering” “man” who didn’t even have to try. Gaines described the flashpoint incident as follows: The swimmer who was leading the nation by body leng ths was a swimmer that none of us had heard of before… We really continued to stay in the  dark until an article finally came out disclosing that Lia Thomas is actually [deadname] a nd swam three years on the men’s team at UPenn before deciding to switch to the women ’s team. When I heard this, naturally I was stunned. I was baffled. I was shocked. We all were,  but really upon reading it, I felt this sense of relief like, “Oh duh. It’s a man. That’s why this person is excelling in the way that they are, is because they aren’t a woman.” I thought that’s how the NCAA would see it, especially given the fact that he was less than average as a man com peting against the men but lo and behold the NCAA did not… The NCAA saw nothing wrong with [Thomas competing], and so about three weeks before our national championships in Ma rch of 2022 they announced that Thomas’s participation in the women ’s category was a non-negotiable, meaning there was nothing that we could do as female athletes… I don’t think we understood the harm that would be had until we actually were on that pool deck wit h this man towering over the rest of us, beating us without even trying. That ’s when… those feelings of curiosity shifted entirel y to heartbreak and they shifted to demoralization… It f elt like I had been punched in the gut watching Thomas that first day swim prelims.

In this excerpt, Gaines began by painting Thomas as an outsider who “none of us had heard of before.” She used Thomas’s deadname to imply that she was “actually” a man who just “decided to switch to the women’s te am.” In this opening framing, Gaines modeled for the lis tener a kind of argumentation that denied Thomas he r gender identity and suggested that Thomas primarily decide d to switch teams to gain a sporting advantage rath er than offering the fuller picture of a person who had exp erienced “gender dysphoria and stress on her mental health, which led her to medically transition” (Barnes, 2022). Throughout this excerpt, Gaines repeatedly misgendered Thomas, finally suggesting that “this man” was “towering ov er the rest of us.” In doing so, Gaines created an image of cisgender female victimhood framing “us” c isgender women versus “this towering man.” At anoth er point, Gaines used similar imagery, stating that “this 6’ 4” man –a Goliath of a man –[was] towering over every other girl in that locker room.” This biblical reference depicted Thomas as a huge monster, and an adult, competing against small “girls,” who were victims. These moves to dehumanize trans athletes set up a frame in which Gaines and all other cisgender women were helpless girls a t the mercy of a governing body (the NCAA) who woul d not protect them.  “[I]t was traumatizing for me to know just how easy  it was for those people who created and enforced these politics to totally dismiss our rights to privacy without even a second thought,” Gaines said. Another notable aspect of Gaines’s argument were he r emotional descriptions of shock akin to the physical pain of being “punched in the gut.” She recounted feeling “stunned,” “baffled,” and “shocked,” reactions which she then generalized to the rest of the  team (“We all were.”). In this framing, Gaines set up an ex clusive “we” of other cisgender women in the competition who were wronged by Thomas and the larger institution of the NCAA. She went on to discuss “the harm” that “we” experienced by “being on the pool deck” and “in the locker room” with a transgender woman. Only through dehumanizing Thomas was Gaines able to discuss the harm done to “all” of the athletes (except Thomas) merely by sharing space. This modeling used gender tropes used by white women for centuries that appealed to innocence, vic timhood, and unspecified harm to limit who has acce ss to material and symbolic resources within society (Chouliaraki, 2024). Gaines and her interviewer, Justice, modeled a kind of outrage built from emotional distress, victimhood, and othering to protect white cisgender women from “harm.” This public sensemaking was a form of pedag ogy using “fairness” in sports to protect a certain kind of moral consideration reserved for cisgender women and girls. Prognostic framing: Exclusion for “safety” and “fairness” What did Gaines think should be done about this sit uation? She claimed that while “we’re accused all t he time” of wanting to ban trans people from sports, that is not what she was calling for. Rather, “you just compete where number one is safe and number two is fair. We canno t prioritize inclusion over safety and fairness, and you do that by playing in the category that matches your biological sex.” For the NCAA and other institutions regulating women’s sports, Gaines argued that “biological sex” as a sorting mechanism should override social identification (2). If vulnerable “girls” had been made victims by  the NCAA and Thomas, “safety” and “fairness” in fo rm of exclusion of trans women from women’s competition was the primary required resolution. At the same time, Gaines’s prognostic framing also included actions to be taken by individuals. In particular, Gaines spoke about modeling a particular form of Christian faith. “[B]efore standing for even objective truth,” she said, “I want to stand for biblical truth, living a Christ-like life. But all of that is why I’m able to go at the speed that I do with a smile on my face really and an incredibly light heart.” This statement signals a recognition of multiple forms of truth that may not always align in their implications. The prioritization of “biblical truth” was articulated with efficacy as a swimmer and activist–and with an affective orientation of joyfulness. Toward the end of the podcast episode, Gaines articulated a link between trans women’s participation in women’s sports and campus unrest about the killing of civilians in Palestine. Both were “adverse situations” that required people to stand up in opposition: [T]owards the end of this past semester in May [2024]... we saw this almost counterrevolution, which was so awesome for me to see because again I know more people who think like me than what is led on, but we saw people at Ole Miss and L SU, a lot of these Southern schools–so let me just say, send your kids to the SEC –we saw a lot of these Southern schools, frats and different things start to push back… but that gave me a lot of hope. It reinforced to me that we are here. I think day by day as unfortunately more and more adverse situations and circumstances happen that push us to those limits, people are becoming bolder, louder, more brave and courageous, and stating, I mean, the most  basic of truths, advocating for the bare minimum, at least in this case, of privacy, safety, and equal opportunity. This passage worked by invoking the existence of a large swath of society aligned with Gaines’s own po litical positions (“I know more people who think like me than what is led on”). She painted a picture of more and more people being “push[ed]... to those limits” and push ing back. The responsibility on such people who mig ht be listening was framed as both “the bare minimum” and socially significant. As an aside, she joked that conservative parents should send their children to universities in the SEC, the S outheastern Conference of US collegiate athletics, which comprised 16 universities. The imp lication was that students who attend those schools  would be surrounded in greater numbers by people they aligned with politically. Somewhat contradictorily, Gaines also argued for greater advocacy and outreach to people who may not already agree with her: We need to reach that middle audience –people who are moderates, people who have young daughters who know this is–instinctually they know this is wrong, but they’re hung up on being compassionate or inclusive or empathetic or those words that they use all the time on the other side to really pull at your heartstrings. Getting t o them and saying, “Well what about –what about us, as young girls? Who’s been compassionate to us? Is it really inclusion to ask us to smile and step aside and allow these men onto our podiums?” In this context, “we” can be read both as the inter locutors in the podcast and as including the broade r listening audience encouraged to speak up for their beliefs. She framed ideals of  compassion, inclusivity, and empathy as “those words they use all the time,” suggesting tha t their value or deeper meaning beyond slogans was dubious. In alignment with her diagnostic framing about the victimization of vulnerable girlhood, she asked, “w hat about us, as young girls?” Who would have the courage to stand up in defense of the real victims in this situation?

## Discussion and conclusion

In this paper, we have suggested that podcasts are a venue for public sensemaking with ascendant influence in US political discourse, discourse about what is good and who gets it. Within a social context where agreement about reality is fracturing and some political positions are becoming more extreme and violent, it matters how podcasts model sociopolitical sensemaking for broader public s. In “The Fundamental Rule of the W” and “A Stolen Dream,” we found that podcast participants revisite d past events as s ubstrate and used discourse in ways that rearticulated ideological connections between diver se elements of representation, modeling for public audiences particular ways of sensemaking about the boundaries  of women’s sports. We demonstrated how concepts of diagnostic and prognostic framing from social movem ent studies can provide focus for analyses of ideol ogical rearticulation. These framing processes also provid e linkages between the focal interaction (between p odcast participants) and broader horizons of collective se nsemaking and learning, providing guidance for the future action of listeners and other social actors. While we focus on the former, future research should use methods that attend to audience sensemaking to understand how th e ideological sensemaking presented in framing may be repeated by listeners or motivate new forms of part icipation in social life. For the learning sciences, this study also suggests the importance of attending to discou rses of victimhood in ways that acknowledge express ions of pain but situate them within an understanding of st ructural circumstances (Chouliaraki, 2024, p. 11). Not all expressions of pain are equal in credibility nor in their consequences for social hierarchy and exclusion.
