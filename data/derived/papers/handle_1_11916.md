# Students’ Participatory and Transformative Agency in Higher Education with the Progressive Design Method: A Case Study

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Progressive Design Method (PDM) is a method that can be used in higher education to engage students in project development. This case study investigates whether PDM fosters participatory and transformative agency in university students and examines any potential relationship between these two forms of agency. Participatory agency measured using degree centrality each of PDM. Furthermore, thematic analysis of student activity reflections used to assess transformative agency. Results indicated that student participatory agency increased during PDM activities. Additionally, students demonstrated transformative agency by identifying the strengths, weaknesses, and areas for improvement the PDM. A correlation between participatory and transformative agency also found. The implications of the findings for the further development of PDM are discussed.

Cultivating student agency is a critical goal within higher education (Klemenčič, 2017). In the literature on participatory agency, scholars emphasize that the active involvement of agentic learners in social practices and the co-construction of knowledge is essential (e.g., Jääskelä et al., 2021). Recently, some studies have turned their attention toward “transformative agency”, defined as the development of participants’ joint activity in explaining and predicting new possibilities (Haapasaari et al., 2016), which requires recognizing problematic situations and genuine engagement in changing the context (Lund &Vestøl, 2020). To foster students’ agency in higher education, the Progressive Design Method (PDM)- informed by the Knowledge Building (KB) model (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2010, and integrating Project Based Learning (PBL) with peer feedback-has been developed (Cacciamani, 2017). As highlighted in the literature, KB, PBL, and peer feedback are particularly effective to promote students’ agency (Du et al., 2022; Ketonen et al., 2023; Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2010). The present study aims to analyze students’ agency during PDM activities, by distinguishing between “participatory agency,” demonstrated through active participation in activities proposed by the PDM, and “transformative agency” exhibited through the critical analysis and evaluation of the PDM. The research questions (RQ) of the present study are, then: RQ1: Is PDM effective in promoting students’ participatory agency? RQ2: Is PDM effective in promoting a transformative agency? RQ3: Is there any relationship between the students’ participatory and transformative agency?

## Participants and context

Eighteen students (17 F) with an average age of 21.39 years (SD = 0.61) attended a Guided Practice Exercise (GPE) during their second year of study in Psychological Sciences and Techniques at the University of Valle d’Aosta (2021-2022 a.y.).

The GPE was supported by Knowledge Forum (KF), an online environment developed to foster sustained creative work with ideas through knowledge building activities. KF enables the creation of dedicated spaces, called “views”, for discursive interactions, wherein participants insert notes (written contributions) and build-ons (contributions connected to other contributions). The GPE was organized in eight hybrid meetings, including Design activity and Reflections on the experience. Design activity was structured into four Design Activity Steps (DAS). In DAS 0, students were asked to write a short bio note on a KF view individually, getting involved in reciprocal questioning through build-ons. The next three DASs involved planning an intervention project by students working in teams. In DAS 1, each team initiated its project by identifying the KB principles underpinning their work, and defining the objectives, context, and the participants to be involved. In DAS 2, each team defined the work phases, timing, tools, and human resources required for the project. In DAS 3, each team was engaged in identifying methods of evaluation of the project. For each DAS, a specific part of the project prepared by each team was published on a view in KF. In each view, each student could individually analyze other teams' projects and provide feedback. During the final meeting, students were engaged in the reflection phase to individually respond to some key questions posted on KF, in regards to the strengths and weaknesses of the PDM, and were asked to provide suggestions for enhancing its effectiveness.

## Procedure and data analysis

The participatory agency (RQ1) was operationalized following Jääskelä et al. (2017), who conceptualize agency as dynamic, contextually situated, and relationally constructed. For each student, degree centrality (Tabassum et al., 2018) was calculated by calculating the total number of build-ons provided (indicators of social influence) and build-ons received (indicators of social popularity) during the DAS. Differences in students’ degree centralities between each subsequent pair of DAS were analyzed using the Student t statistic. The transformative agency (RQ2) was operationalized by examining students’ reflection messages in three KF threads: Strengths, Weaknesses, and Improvement, based on the conceptual framework of vision of transformative agency proposed by Lund n Vestøl (2020). Overall, 13 messages were analyzed in Strengths, 13 in Weaknesses, and 9 in Improvement thread. Two independent judges (JA and JB) segmented the messages into Syntactic Units (SUs) identified by punctuation (see Cacciamani et al., 2018). The resulting Index of Agreement (IA) was 0.91. To identify the categories in the resulting 62 SUs, a thematic analysis (Braune & Clarke, 2006) was used. JA developed a coding scheme to categorize the content of each thread. Eleven categories were identified in Strengths, 7 in Weaknesses and 6 in Improvement thread. The coding schemes were then applied by JB to identify the categories in each SU. The agreement between the two judges was detected with respect to the agreement in identifying the same categories within the same SU. The resulting overall IA was 0.85. The frequency of each C category was then calculated for each thread. Correlations between the frequencies of categories and the degree centrality of students (RQ3) were analyzed using Spearman's rho. For RQ1, results show a statistically significant increase in degree centrality from DAS 0 to DAS 1 (t(17) = -3.89, p<.01), while it remains at the same level in DAS 1 and DAS 2 (t(17) = -.060, p>.05), and decreases from DAS 2 to DAS 3 (t(17) = 2.62, p<.01). For RQ 2, the most frequent categories are Technology (N = 9), Collaboration (N = 6) and Feedback (N = 5) in Strengths thread; Technology (N = 5), Group Size (N = 2), Time (N = 2) and Project Directions (N = 2) in Weaknesses thread; Time (N = 4), Groups Organization (N = 3), and Directions to Provide (N = 2) in Improvement thread. For RQ3, significant correlations have been found between degree centrality and the frequencies of the categories contained in the Strengths thread (Rho =.470, p <.05) and Weaknesses thread (Rho =.593, p <.01).

Concerning RQ1, we can hypothesize that students’ participatory agency might have been activated in DAS 1 by the project development and the peer feedback activity, and that managing these activities in groups might have favored its maintenance for DAS 2. This interpretation is consistent with Ketonen et al. (2023) who highlight that working in a group can allow struggling students to receive support when giving and receiving feedback, as they were able to echo other students. From DAS 2 to DAS 3, the observed decrease in students’ participatory agency was likely due to the increased complexity of the topic and the tasks involved in this phase. Regarding RQ2, by exercising their transformative agency, students' critical reflection appeared to enable them to identify factors related to skills deemed crucial in the contemporary cultural context. In the list of 21st-century skills outlined by P21 (2008), the learning skills category includes competencies such as collaboration, critical thinking and digital technologies skills. The students’ transformative agency is therefore reflected in their clear and consistent identification of the PDM’s strength, along with their ability to connect its weaknesses to concrete proposals for improvement. In response to RQ3, the correlation between degree centrality and the strengths and weaknesses thread can indicate that the more students have participatory agency in the activity, the more they can exercise transformative agency. These results are consistent with Klemenčič (2017) perspective, which underscores that that agency specifically centers on students' capacity to influence their learning environments. Therefore, we can conclude that PDM fosters a comprehensive experience of agency for students, encompassing both the participatory and transformative dimensions of agency. The current study can provide some interesting insights and practical implications, such as the possibility of designing university activities that can promote different dimensions of students’ agency. However, it is constrained by its relatively small participant pool and single-institution context. Future research could focus on a more detailed qualitative analysis of participatory agency by examining the content of the feedback students and the introduction of scaffolds to support students’ transformative agency..Braun, V., & Clarke, V. Computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) processes have been widely studied for several decades. Social-cognitive aspects, such as transactive quality of the discourse (Weinberger & Fischer, 2006), have been regarded as critical for deepening the shared understanding among learners. Besides, shared metacognitive processes, namely socially shared regulation of learning (SSRL), also play a key role in CSCL by enabling learners to coordinate cognitive tasks and maintain a positive relational climate to strive for a shared learning goal (Järvelä & Hadwin, 2013; Molenaar & Chiu, 2014). Both cognitive and metacognitive aspects of collaboration relate to the quality of social interactions and altogether contribute to a group’s socio-emotional climate (Kirschner et al., 2015; Sinha et al., 2015). However, the relationship between these elements and socio-emotional aspects in collaborative learning remains underexplored. This study addresses this gap by exploring how transactivity unfolds across different SSRL phases in CSCL and how it relates to the group's socio-emotional climate.

## Participants, data, and case group selection

Participants were international master’s degree students (N = 38; 11 men, 27 women) who enrolled in a CSCL course conducted as a collaboration between universities in Finland, Germany and the Netherlands in 2021. They worked in small groups of five for six weeks to collaboratively write a scientific article. Group work was organized (a)synchronously using Microsoft Teams, and students’ meetings were video recorded. Additionally, after each online meeting, students completed questionnaires evaluating their collaboration process. To observe varying socio-emotional moments, two case groups were selected with the following criteria. First, questionnaire responses (rated on a four-point Likert scale) were analyzed to assess students' subjective evaluations of their learning. Second, final course grades (ranging from 0 to 5) provided an objective measure of learning outcomes. Based on these criteria, two groups were selected: Group A (questionnaire M = 3.66, SD = 0.47; grades M = 5, SD = 0) and Group B (questionnaire M = 2.22, SD = 0.41; grades M = 2.2, SD = 0.97). Online meetings (6,4h and 6,52h each) were coded at utterance level (5,360 turns) using three coding schemes: Transactivity, SSRL phase and Socio-emotional climate. In this study, we focused on different modes of transactivity, namely, consensus building: quick consensus building (QC), integration-oriented consensus building (INT), and conflict-oriented consensus building (CON) based on the framework of Weinberger & Fischer (2006). SSRL phase coding refers to Zimmerman’s cyclical model of self-regulated learning (2008), including forethought (FORE), performance (PERF), and reflection (REF) phases. Other task-related interactions without regulation (No-Reg) and off-task interactions were additionally coded. Socio-emotional climate (positive, negative and mixed) was identified from students’ verbal and non-verbal cues (see Järvenoja et al., 2018). A Chi-square test of independence was computed in SPSS (version 26) to examine the distribution of transactivity modes across SSRL phases. Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA; Shaffer et al., 2016) was conducted using the ENA Web Tool (version 1.7.0) to capture the association of transactivity and SSRL with socio-emotional climates. The window stanza was set to three considering the nature of interaction among the members as QC can quickly interrupt interactions. This study explored how groups with varying performance levels interacted during online CSCL, with a focus on transactivity in groups’ regulatory phases and its association with socio-emotional climate. While integration-and conflict-oriented consensus building is regarded important in knowledge construction, quick consensus building supports SSRL. Conflict-oriented discussions during the forethought phase may trigger negative emotions, but they could enhance group performance if members engage in critical yet constructive dialogue that fosters joint coordination and mitigates negative emotions. The findings highlight the role of transactivity in regulation to shape positive socio-emotional climates. Further qualitative research is needed to fully explore these phenomena.
