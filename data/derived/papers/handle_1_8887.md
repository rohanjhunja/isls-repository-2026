# Analyzing the Influence of Student Problem Solving Approaches on Learning Through Teaching in Mathematics Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While previous research has examined what students learn as they agentically solve mathematics problems, less is known about what teachers learn through this process. We use this paper to address this oversight by analyzing interviews, lesson plans, and video observations of five novice elementary and middle school math teachers as they taught between six and eight lessons each. Findings reveal that teachers who rely on prescripted approaches more regularly developed knowledge related to classroom management and climate. Alternately, teachers who allowed students to agentically solve problems more routinely developed a deep understanding of students’ mathematical thinking. This study therefore contributes to extant learning through teaching literature by illustrating how pedagogical choices directly affect what teachers learn within these approaches.

## Introduction

As Nieminen et al. (2021) and others argue, allowing students to solve well-designed mathematics problems on their own (rather than teachers frontloading solution processes for students) leads to deep and meaningful student learning related to both mathematical content knowledge and socioemotional skills. However, less is known about what teachers learn when engaging students in this process. In response, we use this paper to address the following research question: what types of knowledge do teach ers develop when using prescripted or open-ended pedagogical approaches? To address this question, w e present empirical data from a study of early-care er elementary and middle school math teachers. Our emergent analysis of this data reveals that teachers who employ student-driven problem-solving approaches more freq uently develop knowledge related to the mathematica l thinking of students while teacher-driven approaches primarily result in knowledge development related to general pedagogical concerns. This paper therefore contribu tes to extant learning through teaching (LTT) liter ature by providing a nuanced understanding of learning that occurs through these divergent pedagogical approaches.

## Theoretical context

According to Sherin (2002), LTT exists as a critica l and at times intractable aspect of developing as a teacher. Previous studies have uncovered a wealth of value within LTT as teachers have developed both discipline specific content knowledge (CK) (Elmendorf, 2006; Leikin, 2006;) and pedagogical content knowledge (PCK) (Perkins et al., 2015; Zazkis & Mamolo, 2018) through this learning process. In terms of the mechanisms through which LTT occurs, Schön (1987) describes a simple three-p art framework that encompasses LTT: planning some s ort of lesson or learning experience, implementing that plan with students, and then reflecting on the res ults. Since its publication, multiple scholars have elaborated on these three steps and provided a greater sense of detail within each element. Simon (1997), for instance, describes  the planning stage as one in which teachers produc e prospective learning trajectories for students that  involve learning goals, proposed tasks, and hypoth eses as to how the learning process will unfold.

Focusing on mathematics education specifically, LTT  research has interrogated all phases of Schön’s process to better understand how math teachers deve lop both CK and PCK when teaching. In doing so, Lei kin

(2010) notes that not all pedagogical tasks embody the same potential for LTT. Instead, some math less ons,

especially those that create space for students to respond in new and unexpected ways, create significantly more opportunities for teachers to learn through the act  of teaching. Additionally, Leikin (2006) found tha t teachers often develop both CK and PCK by creating new lessons and anticipating students’ responses. These two practices within the planning process enable teachers to deeply engage with content in often unexpected ways. Yet despite these findings, what teachers learn when enacting l essons that rely on either student-driven problem s olving or whole-group instruction, and how that learning compares, remains under explored in the literature. In response, we turn towards new empirical findings related to LTT within both pedagogical modes.

## Methods

We collected the data used in this study for a larg er project that investigated how novice middle scho ol and elementary mathematics teachers’ PCK develops throu gh teaching (see Table 1 for demographic informatio n). This 2-day lesson plan  included teaching and assessment tasks, anticipated solution strategies from students, the underlying mathematical thinking for these strategies, and expected instructional strategies. By asking for these components, we aimed to capture teachers’ knowledge of students’ mathematical thinking and their mathematics teaching, two important elements of pedagogical content knowledge (e.g., Ball et al., 2008; Shulman, 1986). We then conducted a set of three structured interviews for each cycle of 2-day lesso n plans (one pre interview and another interview af ter each lesson) as a means of investigating teachers’ learn ing from their teaching. Additionally, we asked tea chers to videotape themselves teaching one of the planned le ssons. By including lesson plans, interviews, and v ideos of classroom practice, we were able to check the alignment between the planned lesson and the enacted lesson and ensure validity of our analysis through triangulation (Denzin, 2012). ## Findings and discussion

Through this analysis, we found evidence of teacher’s knowledge development related to all four criteria for high quality instruction defined by Fredericksen et al. (1998) (see Table 2 for examples). Additionally, we found that 2 of the teachers (Linda and Hanna) overtly relied on student-driven problem solving while the other 3 teachers (Xavier, Daniella, and Mike) primarily used whole-group instruction in each class. In comparing these two sets of codes, however, we also discovered that how teachers reported learning related to Frederiksen et al.’s (1998) four categories were not evenly distributed (see Figure 1). In particular, the teachers who structured their classes around student-driven problem solving reported learning about pedagogy and mathematical thinking during all 7 of the 2-lesson cycles they taught during this stud y (100%). By contrast, teachers who focused on whol e group instruction only reported learning about pedagogy during 3 of their 9 cycles (42.857%) and mathematical thinking during 5 of their 9 cycles (71.429%). In considering the other two categories, the relationship reversed. Teachers who focused on whole-group instruction reported learning about both management and climate during 6 of their 9 cycles (85.714%). The student-driven teachers onl y reported learning about management during 4 of th eir 7 cycles (57.143%) and climate during 2 of their 7 cycles (28.571%). Figure 1 Evidence of Self-reported Learning by Pedagogical Approach Based on these findings, we contend that a teacher’s choice to position either the student or the teacher as the problem solver within a classroom environment could hold significant implications for what teachers learn when teaching. This study then builds on Leiken’s (2010) assertion that teachers have greater opportunity to learn when students can respond to problems in new and unexpected ways. Specifically, our analysis provides a focus to previous research by unearthing the kinds of knowledge that teachers construct (and recognize as learning) in ICLS2022 Proceedings 895 © ISLS these interactions: by allowing students to complete tasks on their own (and guiding students through that process), teachers more frequently develop PCK and learn how students think about mathematics. Conversely, the teachers who relied on whole group instruction more prominen tly recognized the learning they did related to gen eral pedagogical concerns (management and climate). One possible cause of this diversion may be that teachers who merely present solution processes to students do not engage in interactions related to mathematical CK (or do so with lesser frequency) and instead focus on general pedagogical processes unrelated to content. Taken together, these findings extend the work of Schön (1987) and Simon (1997) by considering how the act of planning not only engages teachers in LTT but shapes future contexts where LTT occurs. In choosing between a teacher-driven and student-driven approach to problem solving, teachers also decide between what they can expect to learn. In future iterations of this project, we will continue to refine our analysis by further specifying the types of knowledge teachers gain in different teaching contexts while simultaneously unearthing learning mechanisms that allow for this development. Future research should also continue to develop a nuanced understanding of the relationship between pedagogical models and LTT.
