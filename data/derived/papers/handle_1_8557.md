# Decomposing Practice: Developing Reliable Analyses of Complex Classroom Discussion

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
There is relatively scant conversation about the details of processes through which research teams arrive at desirable thresholds of statistical measures for inter-rater reliability (IRR) in qualitative research. This paper used autoethnography to describe how a team of five researchers achieved a Cohen’s Kappa of.7 between each pair of researchers over a period of two years while iteratively developing a codebook to be applied to science classroom discussion transcripts. Detailed meeting notes kept by the researchers were used as data. The findings describe factors that contributed to the complexity of the transcripts, strategies that worked for the team, and the role of the purpose of the analysis in shaping the process of the analysis. Some implications for the field of teacher learning are discussed based on these findings.

## Introduction

Establishing rigor and reliability in the analyses is an important aspect of doing credible qualitativ e research. However, the process of establishing inter-rater reliability (IRR) could be a fraught one given the tension between the superimposition of an objectivity-oriented desired statistical value of consistency between raters (e.g. Cohen’s Kappa) over a constructivist orientation of qualitative research. In addition, consistency is further perplexed when the analysis in question seeks to describe, through codification, features of data that require high inferences. Thus a methodological question is, what conventions can be used to achieve very good agreement between raters, and yet still preserve some of the rich description of data that qualitative analysis lends itself to. The field is guided by conventions such as good thresholds for agreement (e.g., O’Connor & Joffe, 2020; Banerjee, et al 1999), but less is reported about the social processes and met hodologies used to develop shared ways of seeing da ta are captured in measures of interrater consistency. Few studies report on how to achieve interrater agreement in the context of dialogic and dialectical processes of te am-based research, where shared ways of seeing are informed and enriched by histories, cultures, experiences, a nd expertise (Hennessey et al, 2016). We see these often underreported processes as critical for theory and methodology building. This paper focuses on the pro cess of iterative development of a codebook which a team of five researchers utilized and achieved a.7 Cohen’s Kappa. The primary challenge of the process was harmonizin g the researchers’ individual lenses toward the dat a developed through their unique professional and personal histories into a collective lens that retained the richness afforded by the individual lenses while also yielding a desirable statistical agreement. Through autoethnography, we seek to surface tensions in this process of codifying a large corpus of rich and complex discourse data within the context of a longitudinal team-based research project and illuminate decision points that led to n ew ways of structuring the coding process, i.e., iterating on a codebook and developing shared ways of seeing the  data and categories to achieve very good IRR.

ClassInSight is a study of teacher professional lea rning around classroom dialogue. It is a 5-year research-practice partnership with two school districts in north and south county Sandiego, which serve a diverse (ethnic and linguistic) population of students. Sci ence classroom discussions of study participants we re audiorecorded, transcribed, and verified. These lesson t ranscripts were then used for analyses to create vi sual representations in a web-based app to facilitate te acher reflection and learning. An important dimensi on of this work was to develop a Natural Language Processing (NLP) model to automate the process of coding the transcripts and creating the said visual representations.

The specific analysis task for us (the research te am) was to decompose the transcript into codes that captured the dialogic function of the utterances in the transcript. For example, if an utterance was an invitation to elaborate on an idea then it would be coded as (I). The purpose of this analysis in part was to generate data to be used by a collaborating team to develop natural lan guage processing models for automating the analysis  and second, to represent codes in the form of analytics (visualizations) for teachers. The first iteration of the codebook started in Fall 2019. As we worked through the challenges of achieving IRR the codebook went through two more iterations and the desired Cohen’s Kappa on the third iteration of the codebook was achieved in Spring 2021. This ICLS2022 Proceedings 1629 © ISLS paper describes the iterative process and character istics of refinements as a collaborative team devel oped a codebook and training of multiple raters on complex conversational data. The question we intend to answer is how do we develop reliable shared ways of applying a set of codes to complex classroom discussion data?

## Methods

We used autoethnography to reflect on and distill f indings to be reported here from the two-year proce ss of achieving IRR in the context of a longitudinal team-based research project. Our primary data sources f or this autoethnography are detailed meeting notes recorded  during our IRR-oriented meetings between Fall 2019  and Spring 2021. Two researchers conducted open coding of the meeting notes and identified preliminary cod es related to excerpts that would provide insights int o the ‘how’ of our RQ. These thematic codes were di scussed and validated by the entire team of researchers that worked on the analyses. An autoethnographic codebook was developed based on the agreements reached during th e coding process. For example, “decision point” or “corrective measures/strategies” are examples of thematic codes that were included in the codebook that sought to identify and characterize critical moments, roadblocks, and decisions in our process. The same two researchers then coded the remaining 106 pages of meeting notes  using a qualitative data analysis software. A subs equent cross-code analysis of the excerpts resulted in the themes that are reported below.

## Complexity of the classroom discussion data

Our data (meeting notes) is rife with explicit and implicit acknowledgments of the complexity of the classroom discussion data. While various factors contributed to this complexity, we describe below two major contributors to this complexity-context and transcript.

An enduring question in decomposing classroom discussion data for us was to define the limits of context we may use while applying the codebook to a transcript. For instance, it mattered if a researcher was or was not present in the classroom to conduct the observation which resulted in the transcript at hand. If the researcher was present then they had more context to the data than a researcher who was not present. If the researchers were to learn to code reliably, then accessing such information during the coding process, while valuable to the analysis, was not desirable. Similarly, information about the  context of the school/teacher/student demographics, type of school-MAGNET, etc., and consequent linguistic practices often had a bearing on how we approached the data. This issue of context made it challenging to arrive  at precise definitions of codes whose application relied on a multitude of factors. We resolved this in part through the strategies described in the next section, but this is a work in progress.

Secondly, there were limitations to the recording and transcribing of classroom discussions. In that, we had to wrestle with the question of whether we were coding the discussion or the discussion transcript. The latter eliminated several features of the actual discussion-modulations, pauses, crosstalk, unrecorded utterances, etc. If the teacher posed a question and the next utterance in the transcript was the teacher responding to the question themselves, then did it mean that there was no resp onse from the students or that there was a response  and the teacher then elaborated on that response? In the li ght of the considerations described in the previous  paragraph (and some about machine learning to automate the coding process that will be described later), it was decided to code the transcript and not the discussion.

Strategies that helped in achieving  greater inter-rater reliability Segmentation The work of Wells and Arauz (2006) and Hennesey et al. (2015) provided a solid foundation for the thir d and successful iteration of our codebook. These codeboo ks provided us with a framework for the mechanics o f classroom dialogue in terms of episodes, sequences, and exchanges before trying to classify individual utterances, which focused on cognitive implicature of teacher elicitation and student responses. It was immensely helpful to break down the entire transcript into smaller chunk s which helped us understand the relationship betwe en each utterance (part) and the classroom discussion (whol e). Then, Hennesey et al. (2015) provided us with a  list of predetermined codes which we could then adapt to th e requirements of our data. The codes made for rich deliberation about how they may apply to our data w hich allowed for a far deeper and more meaningful engagement with the complexity of our data than would have been possible if we were not rooted in thes e prior works.

Historicity as Part of the Coding Process ICLS2022 Proceedings 1630 © ISLS Each researcher came to this work with their peculiar history with the processes of teaching and learning which shaped their approach to the classroom discussion data for this project. This afforded several possibilities for the analysis in that the definitions of the codes were made richer through diverse contributions. However, this also made ‘sticking to the codebook’ a challenging endeavor. The team referred to this as the peril and the possibility of coding in a diverse team. After the first few fa iled attempts of independently and reliably applyin g the selfdeveloped first iteration of the codebook to the da ta, we decided that we needed to undergo additional  shared experiences, beyond initial codebook training, that  would offer us some common ground to work with whi le applying the codes.

Three things were done by the team to achieve this  common ground. First, the team engaged in several rounds of collective coding where we individually c oded 5-10 lines at a time and then immediately shar ed the codes we had applied with our reasoning for applying those codes. This allowed us to gain a better understanding of each others’ heuristics in the coding process and, therefore, make changes to ours if necessary. Se cond, whenever it was difficult to reach an understanding over which code would be applicable in a particular instance or how a particular code would be applied, there wa s an emphasis on collective decision-making. The me eting notes repeatedly mentioned the verb ‘discuss’ at the close of meetings for disagreements that were left unresolved. Lastly, this approach of developing reliable analys es while also valuing the diversity of thought resu lted in the codebook being a dynamic document that reflected the evolution of our thinking around the application of each of our codes through the course of gaining these shared experiences. While three researchers achieved a Cohen’s Kappa of 0.7 in the current iteration of the codebook, there are several proposals for adding new codes or changing existing ones that are in deliberations for its next iteration. Reflecting on the Process of Coding The shared experiences were significant in moving us toward a better reliability statistic. However, they were not enough. At one meeting, as we uncovered the steps that each of us followed to code a particular lesson transcript, we found important differences in our approaches. Some read and coded the transcript simultaneously while others skimmed through the whole lesson to get an overview  and then coded in the second, deeper reading of th e transcript. Subsequently, all of us decided to follow the two-step coding process. Systematic differences were also observed in the application of certain codes among researchers. When a question was repeated by the teacher, some researchers coded both utterances of the question as a question while others coded only one. Developing rules for such recurring but systematic differences was helpful. However, on some occasions, it was difficult to decide on the application of a code to a particular utterance. For instance, the distinction between E and I was often difficult to make. E refers to the invitation for a ‘new’ idea and I refers to the invitation to say more about an idea that is al ready on the table. Sometimes it was challenging to  precisely determine whether a new idea was being invited or a previous idea was built upon. On such occasions, it helped to take a step back from our pursuit of a combinati on of individual and collective lenses and focus on ly on the collective (e.g., how can we all learn to think similarly while making decisions like these? What series of questions must we answer to make such a decision?).

Initially, we formatted the transcripts for coding such that each sentence was assigned a code. We realized that multiple sentences together formed meaningful utterances and as such should be coded as a group a nd not individually. However, these observations about what constituted a meaningful cluster of utterances we re made only during the deep reading stage of the coding process. This led to the question of whether one member of the coding team would cluster utterances to be coded and share the segmented transcript with others, thus excluding the clustering aspect from the reliability calculations, or whether this process would be conducted by  each rater themselves and clustering included in the reliability calculations? In the light of further challenges with the latter approach, it was decided to code at the turn level. In that, all the utterances in one turn of the speaker were assigned with a code. In instances where there were multiple applicable codes in a turn, the one that was taken  up by the following turn was applied. For example, if one tur n had both, a reasoning (R) and an invitation for e laboration (I) move, but the next turn responded to the (I) move then the turn was coded as (I). This was a significant step in moving us along in the inter-rater reliability process. The Impact of the Purpose of Analysis on the Process of Analysis The project’s purpose was to generate representatio ns of classroom discussion data that teachers would  use as tools to reflect on their practice. These reflectio ns and consequent inquiry or modifications in practice would constitute a form of ongoing learning for them. Additionally, one important aim of the project was to use machine learning techniques to automate this process of analyzing classroom data and generate representations of it. This would greatly reduce the turnaround time between a classroom observation and the generation of the representations based on it.

ICLS2022 Proceedings 1631 © ISLS Concerning the purpose of facilitating teacher lear ning, the question of “what is meaningful for the teachers?” came up repeatedly in the meeting notes. This was an important consideration of the analysis process with which the team had been tacitly working with. To illustrate, Hennesey et al.'s (2015) coding scheme included a broad set of codes called cluster codes and a set of detailed sub-codes within each cluster. For example, invite elaboration or reasoning (I) was a cluster code within which there were sub-codes like invite possibility thinking or prediction (I5) and ask for elaboration or clarification (I6) (Hennesey et al., 2015). Early on while adapting the coding scheme to our purposes, we decided to stick to only cluster level codes because the sub-categorization of cluster codes did not appear to add significant value to the affordance of the representations in facilitating teacher reflection.

Another issue concerning the purpose of facilitating teacher learning was that codes like reasoning (R) or building on ideas (B) were not yet frequently occurring in the data. So the challenge was how to use what is in the practice of the teachers to help them reflect on what could be. Since this is an ongoing project, it is a question we continue to grapple with. A strong contender for  a solution is to modify the code definitions such that they may apply to utterances that are proximal to a code  but not quite satisfy the current definitions such  that the teachers may start to see the beginnings of it in their data. As discussed in the next paragraph, the implications of such a move for machine learning have been a primary hindrance in realizing this possibility. While we developed shared heuristics for making decisions about codebook applications to data we were also constantly mindful of their complexity vis-a-vis the possibilities of machine learning at a given  time while acknowledging the quick strides being made in the f ield. This meant that we could not, for instance, u se information from several hundred turns prior as context to code a particular turn in a transcript. Beyond constraints, machine learning also brought several affordances in our thinking about the analyses. When necessary, we were able to set aside our subjective individual orientations and focus on building a relatively objective collective one because that is what the machine needed to learn. W e were also able to plan for the possibility of the  machine applying some low-inference and already reliable co des before the researchers even looked at the trans cript for the first time allowing them to focus on the more complex codes.

## Discussion

Shared experiences, maintaining a dynamic codebook,  repeatedly rooting ourselves in literature, and wr estling with the balance between mechanics and content were only some of the things that went into the team achieving IRR. The researchers’ deep care for each other and the work were also important ingredients of the success. While we have a Kappa value of.7 between each pair of re searchers, we acknowledge that there is a lot more work to do and we don’t yet have our final iteration of the codebook or the final Kappa. These details are entirely masked by the reliance on a single statistic as a bar for rigor. An important question is how do we go beyond the Kappa in our sensemaking around objectivity and rigor in the IRR process? This experience has made us somewhat wary of very high Kappa values, especially in the context of complex data and coding categories. As a field, it may be worthwhile to adopt a healthy skepticism of only st atistical values and to seek and provide descriptio ns of the processes that lead to very high IRR values whenever they are reported. Lastly, the processes of these analyses described here were deeply intertwined with the purposes of the analyses, which were to facilitate teacher learning and to help develop machine learning models of the analyses. If the purpose were to solely theorize on the nature of dialogue in science classrooms, then many more nuanced codes would have been available for us to use, possibly making it relatively easier to deal with the complexity of the data. While we tacitly acknowledged these purposes throughout the process, becoming conscious of them has given us a new frame with which to approach decisions in the next iteration of our codebook. As such, it may be worthwhile for others engaged in similar work to be  explicit about the purpose of their analyses and a llow it to conspicuously shape the process.
