# Building Models and Relationships: Distribution of Authority in an Elementary Science Classroom

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper describes a design-based research study exploring how interactions during modeling activities in a fifth-grade science classroom position students with authority. I explore how authority is inequitably distributed through students’ access to the conversational floor and interactional space, as well as through evaluations of intellectual and social merit. Qualitative coding is used to analyze students’ participation bids during whole-class activities and pedagogical actions that disrupt or perpetuate the inequitable distribution of authority among students in this class. Findings suggest that while authority can be inequitably distributed in a classroom community, teachers can enact moves that disrupt this inequity in moment-to-moment interactions. This has implications for supporting learning environments that foster equitable engagement with science practice and collaborative knowledge construction.

The distribution of authority in classrooms I take up Langer-Osuna’s (2016) definition of authority as “the degree to which an individual is interactionally positioned with academic and/or social power that can affect their collaborative work ” (Langer-Osuna, 2016). Drawing on others’ work exploring classroom dynamics (e.g., Engle et al., 2014; Langer-Osuna, 2016), I explore how students are positioned with authority in classroom interactions and how these positionings might lead to an inequitable distribution of authority over time. I adapted existing frameworks to focus on the types of interactions that are most common for positioning individuals with authority in whole-group discussions: the degree of access a student has to the conversational floor, the degree of access a student has to the interact ional space, and how peers and teachers evaluate the intellectual and/or social merit of a student’s contribution (e.g., the ideas they say or the actions they take). Access to the conversational floor is the extent to which students have opportunities to make contributions to a discussion (Engle et al., 2014; Gee, 2014). Over the course of a classroom discussion, the conversational floor is a socially negotiated and ever-evolving space in which the teacher(s) and students are allowed to make bids for access and for shifting the ways that the floor can be accessed by part icipants (Engle et al., 2014).  Students also have varying degrees of access to the interactional space during classroom activities, which is the extent to which other participants physically attend to the speaker through gaze and body position (Engle et al., 2014).

When students and teachers interact, they often make evaluations of each other that can be explicitly or implicitly communicated. These may be evaluations of talk, actions, or of the person themselves.  In this study, I analyze how students and teachers make judgments related to both intellectual merit and social merit, drawing on analyses by Langer-Osuna (2016). These evaluations are socially constructed – for example, students may be interpreted as having more credibility or intellectual merit even if their ideas are not canonically correct (Engle et al., 2014; Engle & Conant, 2002). Different classroom communities may have different criteria for this, but students are typically judged as having intellectual merit if they use science vocabulary words, explain their ideas clearly, or are seen as having sufficient “background knowledge,” as suggested earlier by Ms. Lombardi. Teachers and students both make these judgments: A teacher may say that a student’s idea is correct or incorrect (or hint as such by saying “not exactly” or “look back at your notes”), and students may evaluate each other’s ideas by expressing agreement or disagreement during discussions. In addition to evaluations of intellectual merit, students and teachers also make evaluations of social merit. These can include evaluations of behavior being considered on or off-task, such as teachers indicating whether or not a student is acting in a way that is characteristic of a “good student” or students telling each other to behave in particular ways  (Langer-Osuna, 2016). In addition, I argue that students evaluate each other’s social merit positively with actions like laughing at a student’s joke, smiling at each other, or engaging in relational actions like high-fives. They can also evaluate each other negatively by indicating that an action or statement is not socially acceptable (such as laughing nervously, cringing, or looking towards a teacher for guidance) or by making statements that are harmful to the relationship among students (e.g., “that’s dumb”).

These interactions happen numerous times throughout a single lesson or school day, and they continue throughout the entire time that a group of students and their teacher work together (typically for one or more years). When interactions repeat in patterns, these repeated positionings lead to a distribution of authority across the class community. These interactions stabilize to create relationships in which some students are systematically positioned with higher authority and others are positioned with lower authority. As these interactions occur, relationships of power in the classroom can be reinforced, stabilized, or disrupted over time. This leads to the way that students are positioned with status. Status is a socially constructed hierarchy in which some individuals are positioned with a higher rank than others, allowing them to become more active and influential (Cohen et al., 2014; Cohen & Lotan, 1995). Other researchers have considered status as a meso-level construct (larger than moment-to-moment interactions but smaller than societal power structures) to recognize classrooms as social systems in which larger power dynamics operate and influence interaction (Baldinger, 2017; Shah & Lewis, 2019; Shim & Kim, 2018). These studies indicate that status is often distributed inequitably in classrooms, creating barriers for learning and for students’ development of a positive learning identity. In schools, students who are white, monolingual English speakers, and non-disabled are often afforded more opportunities to learn than their peers who are non-white, multilingual, or have a disability (Committee on Equity in PreK-12 STEM Education et al., 2024; Darling-Hammond, 2010; Louie, 2020). Therefore, the ways that students are positioned with authority in their science classroom relate to identities such as their gender, race, language, disability status, or 643 socioeconomic status. These identities influence the ways that students are perceived by their peers and their teachers, and in turn, influence who is positioned with access to the conversational floor and interactional space as well as how they are interp reted as having intellectual or social merit. For example, a white student who says a similar idea as a Black peer may be positioned as having more intellectual merit due to the systemic prioritization of whiteness in academic spaces.

Analyzing these larger power structures adequately is beyond the scope of this study due to the small sample size (15 consented students in one science classroom). However, further work exploring the role of authority in science classrooms must take a critical lens by attuning to the ways that students’ identities affect the ways they are perceived and positioned. In this study, I attend to students’ race, gender, home language, and participation in special education services to begin exploring how these identities play out in classroom interaction, and these can be further explored in future work.

## Context and methods

This study uses a design-based, case study approach to better understand the learning ecology (Cobb et al., 2003) of a fifth-grade classroom as students and teachers engaged in whole-class activities focused on scientific modeling. It takes place in a fifth-grade classroom at an urban public school in the northeastern United States. The class was composed of 16 students (8 male, 8 female) and one teacher, “Ms. Lombardi,” a white woman who had been teaching for seven years at the time of the study. One student did not consent to participation in the study and is excluded from the analysis as per IRB protocol. During the 2022-2023 school year, Ms. Lombardi  and I began working together in a professional learning workshop focused on classroom culture and science sensemaking. During this workshop, we worked together to understand the challenges and opportunities that arise as teachers seek to create learning environments that are caring and foster deep engagement with science practice. We began to wonder how to establish and maintain an equitable classroom culture throughout science units as modeling activities progressed from students showing tentative ideas in initial models at the beginning of the unit, towards collectively explaining a phenomenon with a class consensus model at the end of the unit. Ms. Lombardi felt confident about eliciting and valuing diverse ideas when students drew their models at the beginning of a unit of instruction, but she felt less sure about how to do this at the end of the unit, where she noticed tensions between eliciting ideas from all students and supporting a canonically correct explanation of ideas in the fifth-grade science standards. During the workshop, Ms. Lombardi and I developed tentative ideas about types of pedagogical actions to try out in her classroom, such framing of the purpose of modeling activity in particular ways (i.e. to develop a collective explanation of a phenomenon rather than to prove each other right or wrong), developing activity structures that to elicit and understand students’ conceptual resources, using specific “talk moves” to support participation (Michaels & O’Connor, 2012), and particular norms to use with students. This study focuses on a series of six lessons that Ms. Lombardi  taught over 11 class periods during the spring of 2023. Lessons were adapted from the NGSS Storylines unit “Why do dead things disappear over time?” (https://www.nextgenstorylines.org) and focused on different types of modeling activities: Students drew individual models, shared these models with their classmates, revised their models in light of new information, and constructed consensus models in small groups and as a whole class. I attended each lesson and recorded video from whole-group and small-group activities, collected artifacts (such as students’ models), took field notes, and wrote reflective memos. whole-group discussions about modeling activities, including  students presenting their models for feedback, sharing similarities and differences they noticed between student models, discussing questions and uncertainties they encountered when drawing models, and constructing a class “consensus model” to capture all  ideas in a lesson or unit. I reviewed videos of all class periods from this unit of instruction and narrowed the data to 10 episodes (4-18 minutes long) in which students discussed modeling activities as a whole class. To address the first research question (How does authority become inequitably distributed among students during modeling activities in this classroom?) After coding all participation bids in an episode, I counted the number of times that each student in Ms. Lombardi’s class was positioned with authority. This allowed me to see which students were consistently 644 positioned with authority across episodes, as well as episodes that seemed to show a more or less equitable distribution of authority. The coding scheme is not a definitive way to see how all students were positioned during all episodes of study: for example, every student was not visible in every frame of the video because the camera was positioned in a way to not capture students who did  not consent to video recording. Additionally, not every interaction explicitly positioned an individual student with authority. However, I found this strategy useful as an empirical way to keep track of interactional positionings and to see if there were patterns over t ime for which students were more typically positioned with authority or not. My analysis is also strengthened by corroborating video analysis with field notes (for example, I frequently wrote in my field notes which students raised their hands when Ms. Lombardi asked a question) and the depth of the data corpus (observations of 10 class periods totaling 7.5 hours, and focused analysis of 10 episodes totaling 80 minutes).

To address the second research question (Were  there moments during these activities where the inequitable distribution of authority was disrupted, and if so, what pedagogical actions  seemed to allow this to happen?), I returned to the data set and my prior analysis to look for moments where students who were typically positioned with lower authority in the classroom (based on my analysis for the first research question) were positioned with authority. I focused my analysis of these moments on Ms. Lombardi ’s pedagogical actions, seeking to understand how her actions disrupted typical authority patterns. To develop a tentative coding scheme of pedagogical actions, I used field notes, transcripts, and memos from the professional learning workshop to identify strategies that Ms. Lombardi  said she wanted to try. In addition, as I began analyzing the transcripts for these actions, I noticed that Ms. Lombardi also used many “talk moves” that education researchers have identified as strategies to shift agency to students and promote collaborative sensemaking, so I added talk moves described in Talk Science Primer (Michaels & O’Connor, 2012) and Ambitious Science Teaching (Windschitl et al., 2018) to the coding scheme. I coded each transcript, alongside the video of each episode, for Ms. Lombardi’s pedagogical actions related to supporting students’ engagement with modeling practice and responding to each other’s ideas. I then looked across the analyzed transcripts to see how particular moves allowed students to be positioned with authority. For example, when a student in Lesson 2 who was typically not positioned with authority was positioned with access to the conversational floor, I traced the pedagogical actions that Ms. Lombardi had made before that student’s bid to see how these may have led to the momentary disruption of the class distribution of authority. To provide counterexamples, I also looked at the pedagogical moves used in Lesson 6 that seemed to align with (rather thandisrupt) the inequitable distribution of authority in this classroom. Positioning students with authority through their degree of access to the conversational floor and interactional space Students in Ms. Lombardi’s class gained access to the conversational floor by raising their hand and being called on, self-nominating (speaking without raising their hand and being called on), or being brought into the conversation by the teacher or by another student.  Although the norm in this classroom was for students to raise their hands before speaking, some stude nts would self-nominate—that is, speak without raising their hand — during class discussions. Typically, these were students who were frequently positioned with authority in other instances and held a position of high authority in the class distribution. Students were also positioned with authority through access to the interactional space, which occurred when their peers looked at them, turned their bodies towards them, or made other gestures to indicate attention, such as leaning forward or nodding. Differences in access to the interactional space were most pronounced when Ms. Lombardi invited students to come to the board to present their work. For example, during Lesson 2, Ms. Lombardi chose two students, Alexis and Sabrina,  to present the drawings they had made  of the decomposition phenomenon using the document camera at the front of the room. When Alexis presented her work, nearly every student stopped writing on their own papers and turned their bodies towards the front of the room. As she spoke, the other students nodded and kept their gaze and body positioning towards Alexis. However, when Sabrina presented her work, students looked down at their desks, turned towards other students, or got out of their seat s to sharpen their pencils. This interaction suggested that Alexis was positioned with higher authority than Sabrina by other students in the class, a pattern that was reinforced during other classroom interactions across the data set. Positioning students with authority via judgments of intellectual and social merit Students were positioned with authority when their ideas and actions were evaluated as having intellectual or social merit by other students and by Ms. Lombardi. Students evaluated each other as having intellectual merit when they evaluated their ideas positively, such as by saying “I like that idea” or indicating agreement verbally or by using the “I agree” hand signal, which was a normative action in this class. Students also evaluated each other as having intellectual merit by bringing up what a peer had said during a small-group discussion in the whole-group discussion. For example, during the construction of the class consensus model of the fly life cycle in Lesson 4, when Duncan was called on to share his idea, he began by saying, “this is what Catherine said [during the partner talk].” In doing so, he evaluated Catherine as having intellectual merit by deeming her ideas worthy of being elevated to the rest of the class.

Ms. Lombardi often responded to students’ ideas without evaluating them, such as by repeating what they had said and asking other students to add on. Other times, she evaluated students’ ideas with intellectual merit by saying “right” or “yes” after students shared, or by writing their ideas on the board. She rarely evaluated ideas negatively, although there were a few instances of this happening, such as during this discussion in Lesson 1 of what to include on a scientific model:

1. Ms. Lombardi: Any other things to add or any questions about what we are doing?

Were there moments during these activities where the inequitable distribution of authority was disrupted, and if so, what pedagogical actions seemed to allow this to happen?

I analyzed Ms. Lombardi ’s actions in Lessons 2, 4, and 6 to see what pedagogical actions she took during the whole-class modeling discussions in the  lessons at the beginning, middle, and end of the unit, seeking to understand if and how her actions contributed to the disruption or stabilization of authority in this class. Lesson 2: Initial models This lesson was characterized by a fairly equitable distribution of authority because most  students (10 out of 14) were positioned with authority, and no one student was positioned with significantly more authority than their peers.  During this lesson, students drew initial models to show how the decomposition of an animal’s  body happens and what causes these changes to occur. They shared their model with 2-3 other students and discussed similarities and differences between their ideas. Then, they discussed their models as a whole class. Ms. Lombardi employed several pedagogical actions that seemed to more equitably distribute authority during Lesson 2.  One strategy she used was positioning the model as a tool for figuring out how something happened or what caused it to happen, rather than just a way to show what occurred. She started the discussion by first asking students, “What are some things you saw change?” However, within the first 30 seconds, she shifted to asking students to share ideas for what caused these changes to happen:

Since there was less pressure for ideas to be “right,” more students may have felt comfortable sharing their ideas. Students who typically did not participate in other discussions raised their hands and shared ideas during the whole-class discussions in Lesson 2. These stu dents included Malcolm, Salma, and Sabrina, who were typically positioned with low authority in the class distribution. Additionally, during the discussions in Lesson 2, Ms. Lombardi accepted all students’ contributions, often repeating what students had said without evaluating their ideas. Sometimes, she asked follow-up questions or revoiced a student’s idea to re-broadcast it more clearly to the class. This strategy was one she had suggested using during the professional learning workshop when she said that she wanted to highlight student ideas so that those students could more clearly be credited as authors of knowledge. Lesson 4: The consensus model of the fly life cycle In this lesson,  authority was somewhat equitably distributed among students in this classroom: a few students (notably, Toby and Duncan) were positioned with more authority than their peer s, but the discussions in this lesson were characterized by most students in the class participating and being positioned with authority. During this lesson, students constructed a class consensus model of one component of the phenomenon, the fly life cycle. 647 During this lesson, Ms. Lombardi evaluated students’ ideas more than she did in the discussion of initial models in Lesson 2. Instead of just repeating ideas, she frequently responded by saying “yes” or “right.”  In doing this, she positioned students with intellectual merit.  Additionally, as students said correct ideas, she wrote or drew them on the class model. In contrast to the whole-class discussions in Lesson 2, Ms. Lombardi did not seem to be trying to elicit a variety of ideas during this discussion in Lesson 4. Instead, she looked for students to say specific ideas that she could add to the class consensus model. However, similar to her pedagogical actions in Lesson 2, she continued to ask questions that encourage d students to continue participating, such as “and then what happens?” “What should we draw next?” and “Is our model finished yet?” Asking these questions gave multiple opportunities for students to gain access to the conversational floor and to be positioned with intellectual merit because they seemed to encourage students to continue raising their hands and sharing ideas. Lesson 6: Final class consensus model In the last lesson of the unit, students worked in small groups to create models that connected all the ideas in the unit. Then, Ms. Lombardi presented students with a partially completed model of the entire animal decomposition phenomenon and facilitated a class discussion to complete this model as a whole group. In this lesson, authority was less equitably distributed than in the lessons earlier in the unit because three students (Chase, Margaret, and Catherine) were positioned with far more authority than their peers, with Chase being positioned with authority 14 times, Margaret 12 times, and Catherine 8 times. In contrast, the students typically positioned with the least authority in this classroom (Lauren, Salma, Solange, and Malcolm) did not speak at all during the whole-class In this lesson, Ms. Lombardi used strategies to support students to engage with each other’s thinking, but only a few students typically responded to these invitations. These students were ones who frequently participated and were positioned with authority. Unlike the discussions in Lessons 2 and 4, the discussions in Lesson 6 typically centered on the ideas of a few students (namely, Chase, Margaret, and Catherine). A possible reason for this shift was Ms. Lombardi’s use of the term “we” to emphasize a goal of collective explanation. After students shared ideas, she often said, “Okay, what do we think about that idea?” or “Is everybody good with that? In asking these sorts of questions, Ms. Lombardi positioned students with the opportunity to position other students with higher or lower authority by making evaluations of the intellectual merit of these ideas.  However, it seemed that only a few students held the power to engage in this work, as indicated by the lack of participation from all but the students most frequently positioned with authority. As a result, these students were able to continue being positioned with authority, further deepening the divide between students like Chase and Margaret and students like Lauren and Salma.

Over the course of this six-lesson unit,  authority was inequitably distributed in the class community. Some students were repeatedly positioned with authority during whole-class activities, while others repeatedly did not access the conversational floor and interactional space and were not evaluated as having intellectual or social merit. This pattern was not static, though, with lessons earlier in the unit having a more equitable distribution of authority. During Lessons 2 and 4, Ms. Lombardi  employed various pedagogical actions that seemed to disrupt the inequitable patterns of authority in this classroom. Consistent with other literature focused on supporting students’ science talk, she asked pressing questions to better understand student thinking and to support students to clarify their ideas (Michaels & O’Connor, 2012). After a student shared their thinking, she invited “cross-talk” by asking students questions like, “Who else has a thought about that?” or “Are there other things to add to that?” These types of questions supported students to not just deepen their own understanding but to engage in collective knowledge construction, as suggested by the Ambitious Science Teaching literature (e.g. Windschitl et al., 2018). A type of questioning that seemed particularly fruitful for equitably positioning students with authority during whole-class discussions was asking questions that asked students to explain or predict a phenomenon rather than just describing it. Consistent with other literature focused on supporting students’ science talk, Ms. Lombardi asked clearly framed questions that could spark multiple opinions and moved beyond simple “idea sharing” (Berland & Hammer, 2012; Michaels & O’Connor, 2012). By asking these types of questions, Ms. Lombardi positioned models as an epistemic tool for knowledge development, not just as a way to represent phenomena (Gouvea & Passmore, 2017). In response to these pedagogical actions, it seemed that a wide variety of ideas were acceptable, with many students sharing their thinking. This may have been because there was only one “right” answer for what happened in the video of the phenomenon, but multiple ideas could be considered reasonable in terms of predictions or explanations, especially earlier in the unit. Ms. Lombardi’s decisions about when and how to evaluate students’ ideas in whole-class discussions also to support a more equitable distribution of authority in her classroom. Early in the unit, such as in Lesson 2, 648 Ms. Lombardi accepted most student ideas without evaluation. She would repeat students’ contributions verbatim or close to their own words. Even when these ideas were not fully consistent with canonical explanations, highlighting them to the rest of the class allowed  these ideas to become useful resources for sensemaking (Campbell et al., 2016).  This strategy also showed an attentiveness and receptivity to students’ varied meaningmaking repertoires and supported a variety of students to be brought into the discursive space (Suárez & Krist, 2023).  This is similar to others’ findings that limited evaluation by the teacher in class discussions can support students to explore ideas more fully and to engage in argumentation with each other, placing students as the authors of knowledge rather than the teacher (Colley & Windschitl, 2016; Gotwals et al., 2022; Sandoval, 2019). However, Ms. Lombardi  also engaged in evaluation in ways that did seem to more equitably position students with authority. During Lesson 4, Ms. Lombardi  evaluated students’ ideas, but mostly in positive ways. By evaluating students’ ideas as correct or important, she deemed students as having intellectual merit, thereby positioning them with authority. This decision about when evaluation is productive or supportive has been shown by others as a tension that teachers experience (Colley & Windschitl, 2016), and other research suggests that the evaluation involved in public idea sharing can create feelings of risk for students (Krist, 2020; Wright et al., 2018). An outstanding question outside of the scope of this study is what pedagogical reasoning supported Ms. Lombardi’s decisions about when to engage in evaluation of student ideas, and this may be a fruitful area for future research considering the role of teacher evaluation in distributing authority. However, some of Ms. Lombardi ’s pedagogical actions, particularly those used towards the end of the unit, did not support her goal of equitably distributing authority. Interestingly, some of these actions conflict with others’ findings in the science education literature. For example,  the use of partner talk or “turn and talks” has been suggested as a way to lower the stakes and support students to become more comfortable sharing ideas with peers (Michaels & O’Connor, 2012; Windschitl et al., 2018), the analysis of episodes in this study did not show partner talks supporting a significant difference in students’ comfort sharing with the entire class. Although this study did not focus on students’ interactions during the partner talks, others have found that authority can be inequitably distributed between students in partner and small-group activities, which may cause these participation structures to stabilize inequities (Langer-Osuna, 2016; Shah & Lewis, 2019). When Ms. Lombardi asked students to evaluate each other’s ideas, such as in the construction of the final class consensus model in Lesson 6, only a few students engaged in evaluation, and fewer students shared ideas. It is possible that students in this class felt risk in having their ideas evaluated by other students. Other studies have suggested that this is a risk that students perceive in whole-class discussions, with students expressing that they are concerned about participating in ways that marginalize other students, cause others to evaluate them as incompetent, or lead to being reprimanded by the teacher (Penuel et al., 2023; Wright et al., 2018). Additionally, the role of larger power structures likely influenced the ways that students were positioned with authority in this classroom. Other studies have suggested that students within the same classroom can have different experiences due to larger s tructures of power that allow certain identities (i.e., whiteness, high socioeconomic status, and English fluency) to be prioritized over others in classrooms  (Committee on Equity in PreK-12 STEM Education et al., 2024; Darling-Hammond, 2010; McKinney de Royston et al., 2021). While this study did not focus specifically on students’ identities, findings suggest that students who were Black, received special education services, and/or spoke a home language other than English were less likely to be repeatedly positioned with authority than their peers. Future work could explore this phenomenon further to understand how students’ identities impact the ways that authority is constructed in science classrooms. This study shows how interactions between students and teachers during whole-class modeling activities create a distribution of authority amongst students in the classroom.  These findings have implications for practitioners and researchers concerned about  equitable engagement in science practice. This study illuminates that whole-class modeling activities have important epistemic and relational ramifications for students. As students work to construct understandings of phenomena through the use of models, they also position themselves and each other as having more or less authority relative to their peers. This inequitable distribution of authority can create challenges for teachers to navigate, but this analysis of Ms. Lombardi’s classroom suggests pedagogical strategies that may be helpful.  An ongoing question is how teachers can use pedagogical actions to disrupt an inequitable distr ibution of authority during whole-class modeling activities at the end of a unit, such as final consensus models. Ultimately, this study suggests that while authority can be inequitably distributed in a classroom community, teachers can enact moves that disrupt this inequity in moment-to-moment interactions.
