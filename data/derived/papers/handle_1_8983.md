# “You Are Not Valued as They Said”: Re-Constructing a U.S. Higher Education Environment Negotiated by an International Student

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper responds to the call for examining and promoting a more diverse and equitable education environment by shedding light on an international student’s experience in U.S. higher education. Using narrative analysis, the author unfolds the participant’s proactive actions and resistant voices. The results also show the hidden “de facto” in the environment that the participant experienced with, further contributing to the education practices by unveiling the limitations of the current U.S. educational system.

## Introduction

In the past decade, the number of international students at universities in the United States has grown from 690,923 to 1,075,496, increasing from 3.4% to 5.5% of the t otal student body population (Institute of Internat ional Education [IIE], 2020). Many universities now have a more diverse student body and increasingly emphasize the importance of equitable and inclusive educational practices. Despite this, international students’ needs continue to be overlooked, with a growing body of literature  suggesting that they persistently experience discrimination, microaggressions, and/or a lack of support from the  educational system (Marginson et al., 2010). In ad dition, scholars argued that much of the research focused o n international students has taken up a deficit per spective. Page and Chahboun (2019) noted that much of the cur rent research on international students has been based on the assumption that international students simply serve to “internationalize the curriculum” (p. 873), resulting in little attention being given to the everyday experiences and perspectives of the international students themselves. Xu (2018) also argued that ample research used the acculturation model when studying international stu dents' overseas experiences. Such a model assumes that the goal is for international students to adapt to the host country's culture. Such deficit models serve to overgeneraliz e international students’ motivation for studying i n another country, simplify their overseas trajectories, ignore their agency, and minimize the challenges they face. Despite international education experience is a self-forming process, Tran and Vu (2018) revealed that there is a lack of study in understanding how international students negotiate their agency. They clarified that the achievement of agency is not merely students’ effort. It is an inter-relationship among the individual (micro-level), the resources and spaces given by the institutions (meso-level), and the larger structure (macro-level). This study will contribute to scholars’ call for unfolding how international students negotiate and express themselves within the U.S. educational system, and also to reveal the  hidden norms of the U.S. higher education. The res ults can also create dialogs for policy-makers, educational practitioners, or scholars who work on higher education study or with international students.

## Methods and Data

The data were from my larger project exploring international doctoral students’ academic trajectories in the U.S.

The study was conducted in a predominantly white university in the mid-west U.S. with 6 participants in total. In this specific study, I focus on Tabitha, a Chinese female international doctoral student’s data becaus e (1) the research activities were done in narrative forms; and (2) the research meetings have lasted for a year since August 2020 until now, therefore creating a rather complete trajectory of her academic experience; (3) a similar challenge of funding-searching was identified as a critical e vent (Mertova & Webster, 2012) that had been consis tently emphasized and later changed her personal beliefs a nd attitudes toward the education environment she l ocates within. The meetings were conducted in an online fo rmat because of pandemic. There were 16 meetings up-todate resulting in a total of 44 hour’s video recording. Tabitha also created monthly diaries with her reflections on her life. Negotiation of funding re-occurred and be came critical in the past year in Tabitha’s life. I  started the analysis with the segments directly related to these events. Data were the video recordings and the written journals on September 21st, 2020, March 1st, 22nd, and April 18th, 2021. Both the videos and her diaries were analyzed using qualitative data analysis software ATLAS.ti 9. Data were analyzed in its original language, Mandarin (the mother language of both the participant and myself), and later translated into English for reporting. As I watched the recording or reading through the journal, I marked the components in the narratives (e.g., characters, events, actions…etc.) and also analyzed the meaning behind the narrator’s interpretations, decisions, and reflections. For each segment, I summarized and generated the following: (1) critical moments: what are the events that are presented as influential in her narratives  in that specific period; (2) key persons: what are  the critical characters in these moments, what roles do they pla y; (3) emotion: how does the narrator feel about th ese ICLS2022 Proceedings 1858 © ISLS experiences/interactions; (4) narrator’s voice and explanation: how does the narrator perceive these moments and reproduce these moments through her narratives; (5) decision or action: what action does the narrator decide to perform in response to the experience; (6) de jure vs. de facto: what the narrator considers things/th e system should be vs. what actually happened; and (7) macro-meso-micro structure: through her narratives and the analysis of the above themes, re-construct the system that t he narrator negotiates herself within, the system t hat allows these experiences happen. Finally, I compared these  themes across the four segments/across these time spans, therefore generated a clearer picture of how an int ernational student posits and voices herself in the  fundingsearching experience under the U.S. higher education system.

## Summary of Results

With the seven analytical structures that I proposed in the method section, I was able to re-construct an U.S. higher education environment where Tabitha interacted with by the following findings: (1) individualism seems to be an underlying culture. In the environment Tabitha loca tes within, many staff and faculty members do not a ctively pay attention to students' external needs (e.g., resources, academic works) or internal needs (e.g., mental status or personal development). Even when Tabitha requested help, some replied with the most basic, "official" answers while some rejected to give a hand by shirking responsibility. Tabitha described the phenomena using “playboy” as a metaphor, that: “they did not deny my question ing yet they also gave no response; and they took n o responsibility” (041821). (2) When university people hiring graduate assistants, there seems to be a h idden preassumption and pre-requisition to international stu dents’ language ability before getting to know thei r other strengths. Such as when R (Tabitha’s advisor) queri ed around the funding opportunity at the institutio nal level, he received a response directly stating Tabitha, as  an international female student, is unlikely to ge t a position. The staff answered without asking for further evidence to support her ability. When Tabitha tried to apply to other positions, she sensed that native-speaker-level English proficiency seemed to be a baseline for many j obs, even the employers do not explicitly stated it on the jo b post. (3) Mentor/advisor plays a critical role in  international doctoral student's trajectory. This strongly influe nces how an international student perceives the hos t country's educational culture. Across Tabitha's trajectory, her advisor R's role switched from a powerful protector, a father figure that seems to be omnipotent, gradually to a faculty member that acknowledged and also carried out a certain degree of the norms established by the institution. Tabitha reflected that "In my first year, I thought our program was supportive. Now I started to sense the gap [the  problems of it]” (041821). Her perceptions toward the institution changed as time passed and as R started  to be bounded by the implicit rules of the rules s et by the institution.

On the other hand, Tabitha positioned herself as an  active character who fights against challenges. For example, knowing R became the scapegoat that the of fice blamed on, Tabitha insisted that she will not accept such a result and instead respond with “quitting”. Here, “quitting” is a weapon and a voice that Tabit ha took to speak out for the unfairness she saw and encountere d, which is different from what some literature pos its as a passive result and the ending of a student’s trajectory. Many reports also tend to position international students as consumers and customers of overseas education (Marginson, et al., 2010). Some universities and scholars assumed that quality education attracts international students. After admission, it is the international student's responsibility to accept and adapt to the host country's education al system. Tabitha's reflection provides another pe rspective, showing international students are not merely passive receivers: “From the decisions made by the department and the program [on how they decide to support us], we also, at the same time, evaluate if this is the place that worth staying. It is a fair position for both side” (030121).
