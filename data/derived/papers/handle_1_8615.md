# Implicit and Explicit Emotion Regulation for Conflict Resolution: Narrative and Self-Compassion as Anti-Bullying Training

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Bullying poses a risk for equity and justice in learning. It can compromise learning achievement and hinter task performance. Emotions are key to handling bullying, thus need to be understood for the development of anti-bullying technological supports. We investigate effects of combining implicit and explicit emotion regulation strategies on empathy and conflict resolution styles among adolescents in a school bullying context. Based on dual-process framework of emotion regulation (Gyurak et al., 2011), the experimental group engaged in selfcompassion induction as explicit regulation in combination with narrative construction as implicit regulation whereas the control group completed narrative construction only. The combination of implicit and explicit regulation was associated with significant increases in selfcompassion subscales of self-kindness and mindfulness. It also elicited indicators of prosocial conflict resolution style: namely other-inclusion behavior and a tendency to inclusive choice of beneficiary from the bystander perspective. Effects on empathy were not larger with additional explicit support.

## Theoretical background

Bullying can take  forms of direct physical or verbal aggression, indi rect manipulations of social relational structures (Björkqvist et al., 1992), or even recen tly emerging forms of aggression through digital me dia (H. J. Thomas et al., 2014). Recent findings support that bullying does not only concern those who are directly involved in an incident (e. g., Dodge & Price, 1994; Hodges et al., 1997), but is rather a socially grounded group process through which even seemingly less involved members of a peer group, so called bystanders, can contribu te to reinforce, moderate, or prevent incidents of bullyi ng (Peets et al., 2015; Salmivalli et al., 1996). B ystander behaviors affect adolescents’ perception of the vic timized (Gini et al., 2008) and such social context s candetermine how they behave in bullying incidents (Sa lmivalli et al, 1998). Targeting the group as a who le and enhancing general levels of empathy of its members are, thus, regarded as crucial aspects of systemic intervention/prevention measures against bullying (Kärnä et al., 2011). Bullying poses a risk for equity and justice in learning contexts as it compromises learning achievement, especially for the unsupported and repeatedly bullied students (Juvonen, Wang, & Espinoza, 2011) and diminishes performance due to attentional deficits related to processing negative emotions (Dolcos et al., 2020). Bullying is a source of socio-emotional conflict, which often leaves bystanders, especially, at an emotional limbo (Hilt et al., 2019). It may thus render them unable to take vital anti-bullying actions. Therefore, emotions in bullying situations and their regulation need to be understood to devel op anti-bullying technological supports. Empathy, as a commonly investigated emotional compound in the context of bullying, refers to one’s reactions to the observed experiences of another (Davis, 1983). It is widely accepted that empathy consists of cognitive understanding of mental states of others (perspective taking) and emotional responses to perceived emotional st ates of others (Davis, 1983). Other-oriented emotional responses (empathic concern) signify concern for others, altruistic motivations (Batson et al., 1987), and regulation o f one’s own negative emotions (e.g., personal distress) (Eisenberg & Fabes, 1995; Panfile & Laible, 2012). Previous research has shown positive associations between empathy and defending behaviors of bystanders in bullying incidents (see van Noorden et al., 2014 for a review), who may become catalysts in conflict resolution and whose situational behaviors may depend on the regu lation of  their own negative emotions.

Emotions arise when one recognizes personal relevance in soc ial contexts and assigns meaning to the experience. Thus emotions are malleable recursive p rocesses where an emotional response to a situation  can change the meaning assigned to the emotion-provokin g situation (Gross & Thompson, 2007). Through such iterative emotion generative processes, we may infl uence which emotions we experience, when and how we experience and express them (Gross, 1998b). Accordi ng to the dual-process model, emotional responses a re modulated by the interplay of two ways of emotion regulation (ER): explicit processes, which require conscious effort, some level of metacognitive monitoring, and  awareness, and implicit processes, which are evoked automatically by the stimuli and do not necessarily  involve metacognitive processes (Gyurak et al., 20 11). One might benefit from explicitly learning and using strategies (e.g., reappraisal or detachment) by assigning different meanings to emotion-provoking situations or maintai ning a distant observer perspective (Gross, 1998a, 2002; ICLS2022 Proceedings 187 © ISLS Kalisch et al., 2005). At the same time, engaging in such explicit regulation processes may routinize and trigger these strategies automatically, making them implici t in the long run (Gyurak et al., 2011). Hence, exp licit ER seems to interact with implicit ER. While conscious effortful explicit processes might not be possible or advisable at all times, e. g. when attention needs to be focu sed on the learning tasks, implicit processes are a ctivated automatically (Dolcos et al., 2020). However, helpful implicit processes might not be cultivated without support. Thus, the interaction of implicit and explicit ER needs to be examined and compared to only implicit processes.

Using narratives to implicitly induce reliable emot ions in realistic conditions has enhanced scientifi c understanding of human emotions (Jääskeläinen et al., 2021; Kanske et al., 2015). Numerous studies of bullying have also used narratives in the form of fictional characters or hypothetical episodes to elicit relevant responses from young people allowing for interpretations of t heir perspectives and attitudes (e.g., Bosacki et a l., 2006; Jeniffer et al., 2006). In addition, narrative construction is suggested as a strategy to down-regulate negati ve emotions as it allows for expression of emotions, coherent restructuring of the events (Pasupathi et al., 2017), and active meaning-making based on one’s experiences (P lowman et al., 1999), thereby attenuating intensity  of emotional impact. Therefore, it can be considered a s an implicit ER without the need of conscious effo rtful processes and yet reflecting automatic emotional responses to a situation that may become transparent to oneself. Self-compassion (SC) is conceptualized as being kind and understand ing to oneself rather than judgmental (self-kindness), interpreting one’s personal experiences as part of human experience (common humanity), and being aware of one’s painful thoughts and fe elings without over-identifying with them (mindfulness) (Neff, 2003). Dispositional SC is positively correlated with ER (Finlay-Jones et al., 2015),  empathy (Neff & Pommier, 2013) and prosocial behavior (Welp  & Brown, 2014). Explicit long-term training of SC increases levels of dispositional SC among adolesce nts (Bluth et al., 2016) and supporting SC through simple instructions mediates effects of situation-specific self-related negative emotions (Leary et al., 2007). Following the dual-process framework of emotion reg ulation, we explore the potential of narrative as implicit ER strategy that can reveal one’s own emotional responses, and self-compassion as explicit ER strategy that may shift attention to these emotional responses. The two together, may influence how one experiences the bullying situation and one’s own role in it, and in crease empathy and prosocial behavior. We investiga te the following research questions: Is conflict resolutio n promoted through SC induction and narrative const ruction? Do the positive effects of SC induction transfer to  bystanders in conflict situations like bullying? C an inducing SC in combination with narrative construction promo te empathy and prosocial behavior beyond what narra tive construction alone can promote? We hypothesize that  additional SC induction will have stronger effects  than narrative construction alone, when assuming the bystander role in bullying situations and will: H1: increase levels of self-compassion, H2: increase levels of empathy, and H3: increase prosocial conflict resolution: other-inclusion behaviors and inclusive choice of beneficiary.

## Participants

We recruited 30 female participants, aged 15-16 from two classes of a girls high school in Korea, to c ontrol for gender differences with females showing higher use of ER strategies (McRae et al., 2008), empathy and prosocial behavior (Barr & Higgins-D’Alessandro, 2007) than males. They were all first-year students, randomly assigned to each class upon admission to the school. They ha d not acquainted their new classmates and the schoo l staff when the experiment started since the school had been closed due to pandemic. 16 students were in experimental group and 14 students were in control group. To avoid confounding effects from class or teacher, each group was invited to Google classroom respectively two weeks before school opening and completed all the tasks online.

## Procedure

Dispositional self-compassion and empathy were first measured before the intervention. After two weeks, subjects all engaged in implicit ER task (narrative construc tion) to avoid prompting effects. The experimental group completed additional explicit ER task (SC induction) prior to implicit regulation, to set the scene fo r more empathic reactions during implicit ER task. Right a fter the intervention, dispositional self-compassio n and empathy were measured again. Self-constructed narratives of subjects were analyzed for behavioral responses. Explicit emotion regulation Instructed SC induction was adapted from Leary and colleagues (2007). It consisted of one trigger question and three self-compassion questions. We referred to the  Revised Cyber Bullying Inventory (Topcu & Erdur-Ba ker,

2010) to compose the trigger question to reflect th e predominance of online interactions as a means of

Subjects in experimental group were asked to recall their unpleasant online communication experiences with the details of the s ituation and their emotions and behaviors evoked by  the incident. After that, they were to answer three open questions implementing subcomponents of SC: a) Try to think of ways that other people would also experience similar events and feelings like what you experienced (common humanity), b) Imagine your friend who is always sup portive and understanding, who knows your strengths and weaknesses, wrote a message to you and that made you feel much better and comfortable. What would have the friend written? (self-kindness) c) Try to describe your feelings about the experience in an objective and unemotional way (mindfulness). Upon completion of this task, they proceeded to implicit ER task. Implicit emotion regulation Implicit ER task consisted of a video clip and subsequent narrative construction. We prepared an edited excerpt from the movie Bang Bang You’re Dead  directed by Guy Ferland (2002). A diagram introduc ing three main characters with their pre-existing relational conflicts was presented prior to watching the excerpt. The video starts with the protagonist(P) arriving at school, shows him getting verbally and physically teased by the antagonist(A) and his friends, practicing a play script with his friend(F), and ends where he almost physically gets into a fight when a rehearsal with the theater team was interrup ted by the antagonist. Narrative construction was operationalized as follows. Subjects were told to i magine as if they were the screenwriter and continu e to write the story by answering open questions about the cha racters and the conflict. Subjects were to describe  what decisions or behaviors each of the three characters  would make and the reasons behind them. Finally, s ubjects were to describe how the story should end, in other words, how the conflict should be resolved. Measurements Self-compassion The Self-Compassion Scale for Youth (Neff et al., 2020) was used to measure dispositional SC before and after intervention. It consists of 17 items, three subsca les for compassionate (self-kindness, common humani ty, mindfulness) and three for uncompassionate responding (self-judgement, isolation, over-identification). Subjects rated how they act towards themselves in difficult times on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (almost nev er) to 5 (almost always). The uncompassionate subscales were  reverse coded. The total scores for pre(! =.842) and post-measurement (! =.828) indicate high internal consistency. Empathy Interpersonal Reactivity Index (Davis, 1983) was us ed to measure empathy traits. Three subscales of IR I were included: perspective taking (PT), empathic concern (EC), and personal distress (PD). Subjects rated how well an item describes themselves on a 5-point scale ran ging from 1 (does not describe me well) to 5 (descr ibe very well). An item from EC had a negative value in item  discriminatory power at post-measurement, therefore was not included in the analysis. The item analysis ind icates marginal to acceptable internal consistency (pre: ! =.547~.781, post: ! =.638~.803).

## Results

Self-compassion and empathy Levels of SC showed no significant differences between the two groups b efore intervention. We performed independent t-test with posttest results to see if SC induction elici ted significant differences in SC. Significant differences were found in subscales of SC (t(28) = 2.293, p =.03, Cohen’s d = 0.839) and mindfulness (t(28) = 2.813, p =.009, Cohen’s d = 1.03), but not in the total score (t(28) = 1.242, p =.225, Cohen’s d = 0.454), partially confirming H1 (see Figure 1). SC induction greatly affected subscales of self-kindness and mindfulness, but not overall dispositional SC.

Levels of PT and EC increased in both groups (Figure 2). However, there was no significant difference between the two groups after intervention (PT: t(28) = 1.793, p =.084, Cohen’s d = 0.656; EC: t(28) = 0.32, p =.751, d = 0.117; PD: t(28) = -0.997, p =.328, Cohen’s d = -0.354). Before intervention, a significant difference was observed in the levels of PD between the two groups (t(28) = -2.476, p =.02, Cohen’s d = -0.906), but not after intervention. PD decreased with narrative construction only (t(13) = -1.97, p =.07, Cohen’s d = -0.49), but increased with additional SC induction (t(15) = 0.465, p =.649, Cohen’s d = 0.13). Other than expected, narrative only decreased the levels of PD but in combination with SC induction, increased it. H2 is rejected. ## Discussion

Combining SC induction, as explicit ER support, with  narrative construction, as implicit ER support, showed expected changes in adolescents’ self-compassion and prosocial behavior, although not all hypotheses w ere supported. The combination of explicit and implicit  regulation showed significant increases in SC subs cales of self-kindness and mindfulness, and more constructive conflict regulation. For conflict regulation, other-inclusion behavior from the bystander perspective was more frequent i n the constructed narratives with additional SC support. There was also a tendency for inclusive choice of beneficiary. The combination tended to report resolving the conflict of bullying in favor of the victimized, than avoiding the conflict situation. Differences  in empathy were not significant: perspective taking and empathic concern increased for both, whereas personal distress decreased for narrative construction, as expected, but surprisingly increased with additional SC support. Significant differences in the subscales of self-kindness and mindfulness align with previous work that suggests training in SC facilitates a transition towards a more compassionate balance of SC components (Bluth et al., 2016; Phillips, 2019). By combining explicit and implicit strategies, we found supportive evidence after only a short SC-based intervention. SC support increased PD, possibly because it enabled adolescents to attend to and become more aware of the suffering of others, havin g soothed themselves first. The role of awareness i n how explicit SC supports ER processes needs to be exami ned further, to provide a differential account of i ts effects, based on kinds and levels (implicit-explicit) of promoted awareness, and its reflection processes th at are triggered.

Lack of significant differences in PT and in EC be tween conditions can be attributed to the metacognitive elements of SC which entails acting with awareness (Solem et al., 2015). Explicit SC support may have increased both the understanding of the mental states of the protagonist and subjects’ self-oriented emotional r esponding alike, especially in combination with narrative-based support. This might have prohibited expected implicit otheroriented emotional responding. However, we also fou nd that prosocial behavior, important for the defen der bystander role, was induced without significant inc reases in  PT and EC. This aligns with previous res ults that would predict that SC results in active prosocial responses with less empathy (Welp & Brown, 2014), after having dealt with socio-cognitive conflict through self-soothing. SC support elicited a tendency to resolve t he bullying situation in favor of the victimized which may sugg est that SC can contribute to create a supportive s ocial environment for the victimized. This aligns with sy stemic approaches on bullying with an emphasis on a n ecological entity (Cowie & Jennifer, 2008). Thus pr oviding technological anti-bullying supports for bystanders may create a new social context and foster equity and justice at school. These results point to differential effects of impl icit narrative-based and explicit SC instruction. T hey thus contribute to the discussion of the interplay between socio-emotional and cognitive aspects, particularly with regard to implicit vs. explicit support and socio-cognitive conflict resolution. Both implicit and explicit strategies evoke PT and EC, but only additional explicit SC su pport increases self-oriented PD and prosocial behavior. A possible mediating effect of SC on PD through soothing needs to be tested. This study shows important first results on how self-compassion induction in addition to standard narrative can promote constructive conflict resolution and a transition to healthy compassionate mindsets among adolescents. Since narrative has been associated with the development of the self, it is worth testing it s long-term effects. These effects, however, should be tested in vivo in relation with other factors that can account for one’s behav ior in bullying contexts, such as sociometric status, needs, and expectancies of social network of a peer group (Salmivalli et al., 1998). Since this study tested a female population to control for gender effects, transfer to a mixed-gender population alsoneeds to be tested.
