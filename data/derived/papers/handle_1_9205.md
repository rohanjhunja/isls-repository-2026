# Networks, Artifacts and Agency: Examining Engineering Students’ Collaborative Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study views learning as a process of creating new knowledge through collaborative work on shared knowledge artifacts. In this context, agentic conduct is essential for productive collaboration. The study describes the interrelation between characteristics of students´ knowledge and social network and their shared epistemic agency, and unveils how these relate to the students´ collaborative artifact-oriented activities. The participants were twenty-three second-year media engineering international students from a university of applied sciences. Data were gathered using combined quantitative and qualitative methods. Social network analysis methods were employed to analyze the structure of their epistemic and social network. Qualitative content analysis methods were applied to identify indications of epistemic agency in the students’ collaborative artifact-oriented activities. Results show that, although the knowledge network of the participants’ group was not very dense, knowledge exchange and creation took place in some groups. Students indicated as central in the knowledge network also played an important role in the collaborative design of their group’s shared artifacts. Indications of shared epistemic agency were identified at the micro-level of the interaction, but no structured agentic patterns were identified in groups’ collaboration.

## Introduction

Computer engineering is a domain in which complex problems and product development activities are constantly being identified as sources for learning. Problem solving and product development invol ve generating ideas, alternatives, knowledge that can function as potential solutions. Such activities requir e the learners to engage in working with new notions and methods, engage in inquiry, or generate new ideas and knowledge, often impossible to conduct individually. Collaborative projects are used as activities in such se ttings, as they are considered to support both joint knowledge construction and the learning of collaboration competences so much valued. At the same time, collaborative projects also represent a setting in which participants can rely on each other’s knowledge, skills, competences, support, when engaging in the exploration of new ideas, strategies, and the production of new knowledge artifacts. In this study, we attempt an integrated approach, by using mixed m ethods to examine both the relational aspects of the collaborative process (through social network analysis) and the knowledge co-creation (through analysis of students talk and reflections).

The present design-based research examines the social structures that developed in collaborative groups in the context of collaborative design of a user interface of a re-use li brary component by media engineering students. It build on the premise that learning is a process that involves crea ting new knowledge through collaborative work on shared knowledge artifacts (Paavola & Hakkarainen, 2005). In this context, agentic conduct is essential for productive collaborative artifact development. The relational e lements are intertwined with the collaborative epistemic endeavour aimed at developing the shared knowledge artifacts. Collaborative creation of artifacts and relational agency Constructing shared knowledge artifacts involves more than only carrying out dialogic interaction. It requires the combination of individual and collective contributions, learners getting actively involved giving ideas a concrete shape; a complex endeavour that brings about groups’ agency.  Knowledge artifacts embody the type of activity they mediate, and perhaps, the most general include material artifacts (e.g., a pen), abstract or intangible artifacts (e.g., software or reports), and processes (e.g., manufacturing processes) (Ha kkarainen & Palonen, 2009). We regard shared epistemic agency  as the capacity to enable deliberate collaborative, knowledge-driven activities, with the aim of creating shared knowledge artifacts (Damsa et al., 2014). It implies that group members jointly act in creating and developing these artifacts, and as a result, we assume that it is not expressed in each individual member’s activities or pursuits, but in shared efforts at the group level. S hared epistemic agency suggests a specific type of practice, which is not automatically attained when groups of s tudents work on collaborative assignments. In this sense, shared epistemic agency is not a trait but an emerging, gradual process (Schwartz & Okita, 2004), simultaneously triggered and influenced by individual input, interaction with pee rs and shared artifact.

Discussing the concept of relational agency Edwards emphasizes that it allows individuals “to work with others in order to expand the object that one is working on” (Edwards, 2007, p. 7). The agentic essence lays in the fact that individuals not only recognizes that another person may be a resource, but that one also acts towards eliciting those resources and negotiate the use of those resources Hakkarainen and Palonen (2009) identified four CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 250 levels of epistemic agency, and the main criterion in this hierarchy was the degree of sharing of explanatory knowledge by the participants involved with the others. Students with a high level of epistemic agency participated in distributed regulation of inquiry and in knowledge sharing, and mediated knowledge exchange  within the community. Zhang et al. (2009) discussed awareness of each other’s contributio n, sharing new ideas and complementary contributions tonew ideas as the main characteristics of  high-level of collective cognitive responsibility, which we also associate with epistemic agency. The assumption is that agency is that collaboration on shared-knowledge artifacts involves a social element, enhanced during group work. Furthe rmore, agency is a capacity that is socially developed and influenced by persons’ and groups’ social ties. Aims This study addresses the socio-relational aspects of learning through joint knowledge and object development in small groups in a media engineering education context. First, this study aims to  shed light on the nature and the characteristics of the students’ overall network, and on the network of the individual students.  Second, we try to understand whether and how joint construction of knowledge takes place in the collaborative groups. Finally, the study attempts to identify whether and how the students’ network relates to the way they engage in and contribute actively to the co-construction of knowledge artifacts. To this end, the study is attempting to answer the following question: What are characteristics of the students’ co-creation process reflected through their network and collaborative group learning activities? To achieve this, qualitative analyses are applied, in which the characteristics of students’ network and the indication of active engagement and knowledge co-construction are combined with the activities observed and reported by students on the task. We expect that the personal and group (social) network characteristics will be related to the co-construction proces s and to the way the students engage and contribute to it.

## Methods

This study employed a design-based approach (Collin, Joseph & Bielaczyc, 2004) in which multiple cases (Yin,

2003) were embedded and followed-up. Twenty-three (23) second-year media engineering international students

from a university of applied sciences participated in this study. All students  were enrolled in an international degree program in Media Engineering. The Media Design and Integration course is a second-year undergraduate course in a study program, preparing students to become media engineers. The  general aim of the course is to support students to understand the design process of different digital products, by also taking into consideration the intertwined nature of the technical software design process with the user experience design process. ## Findings

The findings presented in this sub-section provide insight into the characteris tics of the overall epistemic and social network within the student community (i.e. class) in the context of artifact-oriented collaboration. Intensity of interactions. The density and centralization of the network were measured in the whole class. The measurements depict the networks according to seven networking dimensions, a s employed in the pre-and post-survey: collaboration with peers, seeking for advice, receiving new ideas, responsibility for the collaborative work, emotional support, informal relations inside and outside the class, and technologi cal mediation of interaction and collaboration. The overall network of the student community was not very dense. From the seven dimensions Collaboration, Informal network and Advice registered denser networks in the  pre-test (.27, respectively,.20 and.17). This degree of density inside these dimensions was  relatively steady and comparable also in the post-test for the dimension Collaboration (.26). For the dimensions Advic e and Informal network the density became lower in the post-test (.10, respectively.15). A low density value  in both pre-and post-test was registered for the dimension Epistemic input, i.e. giving and receiving idea s (.11, respectively.10). The density of the networks Responsibility and Emotional support are the thinnest of all, both in pre-and post-test. The meagre average density in the pre-test indicate that, maybe except collaboration and inf ormal network, the aspects investigated do not ‘live’ that much inside the community. The interactions that indicate exchange of ideas and discussions at epistemic level seem to be minimal. Also, although a reasonable network exists at informal level it seems that support of emotional nature is sought outside this community. General characteristics of the collaboration process At the general level of collaboration, findings reveal that collaborative process evolved unequally, and groups struggled to organize their work in the way that collaborative shared knowledge artifacts (e.g., drawings, common prototype, criteria lists) could take place. Coherent collaborative patterns of activity emerged only at the stage when the best individual paper prototypes were discussed. In four of the seven gr oups, the ideas from the individual paper prototype were merged and students selected the best features from each individual product based on discussion and negotiation. The students indicated by the social network analysis as  less central in the knowledge network, participated also more actively in this phase of the process. T he assessment of the final prototypes showed that the majority of the prototypes for the Re-use library m et the quality criteria established during a joint class discussion.

Actions indicating shared epistemic agency and its relational aspects In an illustration, Group 1 consisted of three students: Ellen, Tina and Andy. The pre-test centrality figures showed that Andy was rather isolated and was not involved in much collaboration and interac tion at epistemic or social level inside the class. In the answers to the open questions, he indicate d that, when necessary, he sought for expertise and new ideas outside the class community, from teachers and field experts. Ellen and Tina had more central positions in the network, with higher in and outdegree measures. They we re indicated as collaboration partners, and especially Ellen was indicated as part of the informal networ k by some, and as someone who provides emotional support. This group succeeds, under time pressure, to combine pr oductive actions, which result in the shared paper prototype and a balanced way of interacting at r elational level. The groups was very focused on the content of the task, on understanding what the task takes to be fulfilled, and the shared knowledge artifact comprises. Their discussion is organized around this artifact, whic h is first framed, before the group CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 252 members start generating ideas meant for its development. Both at epistemic and relational level this group shows indications of agency, which lead to productive interaction.

## Discussion

This study described relational aspects of agency in the context of the collaborative creation of shared knowledge artifacts by groups of engineering students, using social network analysis and analysis of interaction. The attempt to examine the relational aspects of artifact-oriented collaboration and of shared epistemic agency turned out to be a considerable challenge, conceptually and methodologically. Through the qualitative ana lyses of discursive data, together with the analysis of the artifacts versions, we aimed to pinpoint micro-level elements that indicate agentic conduct and actions typical to artifact-oriented collaboration. At this level, relational aspects were closely related to the process of design and the collaboration strategy adopted by groups. The rationale for using social network analysis was based on the idea that interactions at micro-level would be  reflected in the type and characteristics of the network. The assumption was that repeated actions identified at this micro-level could form patterns of actions that would be visible at network level, based on the reflective data collected from students. The rather low density of the overall and group networks impeded the attempt to establ ish a clear link between these two levels and aspects of interaction. Possibly, the level of reflection of the students did not allow a detailed report in the social network data on the activities and actions performed by students, identified through observations. This study attempted to shed light on the mechanisms of joint work for crea ting shared knowledge artifacts and agentic and relational aspects of productive collaboration. The results are partially in line with ideas indicated by studies on collaborative learning and knowledge building. Groups that collaborated at epistemic level seemed to be more productive in terms of artifact creation (Zhang et al., 2009). It is noticeable that groups that were more active at relational level did not perform well; this could be expl ained by the lesser space contentrelated interaction was given, but also by the fact that intentions expressed in an informal context are maybe more difficult to concretize. Another noticeable is the low network values related to responsibility for the shared product. Although, in practice, this responsibility was taken, as shown by the observationa l data, the majority of students did not estimate this very accurately when they report on it. This might be an indication of acting without clear intention, which assigns this type of behaviour a low degree of agency (Damsa et al. 2010). From an educational practice perspective, this study informs educators about the challenges encountered when attempting to support and elicit artifact-mediated collaborative learning and students’ active engagement in creating knowledge. As expected, features of productive collaboration, i.e. that type of in teractions between students that leads both to the development of new artifacts and to the  growth of the individual knowledge, can not be directly inferred from the social network. Characteristics of the knowledge and social network represent a good starting point when trying to understand mechanisms of interactions. However, when a ttempting to design and support productive collaboration, educators need to take into account didactic aspec ts, individual characteristics, and scaffolding possibilities.
