# Effects of a Co-Regulation Model for MR Teacher Training: HRV and Self-Compassion as Indicators of Emotion Regulation

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teachers' effective emotion regulation is essential for supporting students' emotional and cognitive development, and technology-based trainings need objective measures. This study examines the relationship between self-compassion and physiological stress (HRV) in a Mixed Reality (MR) teacher training simulation. HRV was measured during emotionally challenging classroom scenarios. Post-training interviews were analyzed for self-compassion behaviors. Results show that lower physiological stress correlates with higher self-compassion and perspective-taking. MR training and self-compassion may serve as effective tools to enhance teachers’ emotion regulation and well-being.

## Introduction

Teachers are increasingly expected to manage both c ognitive and emotional demands in classrooms, with emotional labor contributing significantly to stres s and burnout. In response, emotion regulation has been identified as a critical competence in teacher training. According to Gross (2015), emotion regulation involves the processes by which individuals influence which emot ions they experience, when, and how they experience  and express them. Teachers often face challenges when t hey inadvertently send mixed emotional signals, such as feeling anger internally while attempting to appear  calm, underscoring the critical need for developin g effective emotion regulation skills to understand others' per spectives (Hargreaves, 2000). Successful co-regulation in emotionally challenging scenarios requires moderate affec tive empathy, the ability to understand and validat e others' emotions effortlessly, particularly in high-arousal situations where explicit regulation is dif ficult (Järvelä et al., 2019). Recent findings sh ow that self-compassion enhances physiological stress regulation, as indexed by Heart Rate Variability (HRV), and serves as a prote ctive resource against teacher burnout (Maratos et al., 2024). Mixed Reality (MR) offers an innovative opportunity  to practice and develop emotion regulation skills through the use of interactive virtual agents, which simula te emotionally responsive scenarios (Holz et al., 2 011). In this study, we explore how a co-regulation model embedded in an MR training scenario influences physiological and psychological indicators of emotion regulation in pre-service teachers. Heart Rate Variability (HRV) is a widely used physi ological marker for assessing stress response and emotion regulation. Higher HRV reflects greater autonomic flexibility, supporting recovery from stress and linked to better emotional and physical health (Steffen et al., 2020). Conversely, lower HRV is ass ociated with stress, anxiety, and emotional dysregulation (Segerstrom & Nes, 2007). Given that HRV captures physiological aspects of stress and regulation, integrating self-compassion as an emotion regulation strategy provid es a more comprehensive understanding of these internal proce sses. Self-compassion involves nonjudgmental awareness and care toward one’s emotional experiences (Neff, 2003a). It fosters resilience in high-arousal contexts due to increased self-awareness and adaptive coping (Ferrari et al., 2019). Studies link self-compassion to reduced psychological stress and increased HRV, suggesting its dual role in emotional well-being. We hypothesize higher self-compassion is associated with increased HRV (H1), g reater perspective-taking (H2a), and fewer non-reflective behaviors (H2b).

## Methodology

Sixty pre-service teachers enrolled in teacher training progr ams participated in this study. Participants engage d with the MITHOS training system (Bhuvaneshwara et a l. 2024; Chehayeb et al., 2024), interacting with v irtual student avatars in emotionally challenging scenarios. Physiological stress was quantitatively measured using Polar band sensors that captured HRV data during these interactions. Following the simulation, participants participated in structured reflective interviews, where qualitative analysis explored their emotional responses and underly ing motives. Qualitative data was analyzed using the EL AN software (see Figure 1), guided by constructs fr om the Self-Compassion Scale (Neff, 2003a). Participant respons es were categorized into positive (s elf-kindness, common humanity, mindfulness) and negative (self-judgement, isolation, over-identification) self-compassion behaviors (see Table 1). Quantitative HRV data processing included calculating Root Mean Square of Successive Differences (RMSSD), Standard Deviation of Normal-to-Normal Interbeat Intervals (SDNN), and the LF/HF ratio, reflecting autonomic nervous system activity and stress regulation (Shaffer & Ginsberg, 2017). Correlation analyses were then conducted to explore relationshi ps between these HRV metrics, self-compassion behaviors, and perspective-taking.

## Results

52 participants, 14 male and 38 female, with a mean age of M = 23.9 years, SD = 4.97 were considered for analysis. Eight videos were excluded due to inferior quality or missing Polar Band data for respective participa nts. The correlation analysis between HRV metrics and positive self-compassion (hypothesis 1) suggest that while RMSSD (p-value =.332) and SDNN (p-value =.096) may not show strong associations with  positive self-compassion detected instances, the LF/HF ratio (p-value <.05) provides meaningful insights into the stress-compassion relationship, particularly highlighting  that individuals with higher stress levels exhibit lower levels of positive self-compassion.

The correlation analysis between positive self-compassion and behavioral indicators (hypothesis 2) showed a moderate positive relationship with perspective-taking (p <.001), suggesting that teacher trainees with higher self-compassion were more inclined toward perspective-taking behaviors. However, due to limited instances, the correlation between positive self-compassion and non-self-reflective behavior could not be analyzed effectively. This limitation was possibly influenced by the reflective interview design, which prioritized analytical cognitive rather than mindful self-reflection.

## Discussion and conclusion

This study employs a mixed-methods approach, combining qualitative interview a nalysis with quantitative HRV data to explore the complex relationship between se lf-compassion and stress regulation. Preliminary resul ts demonstrate a statistically significant inverse rel ationship between the LF/HF ratio and self-compassion, indicating that individuals with higher self-compassion exhibit lower sympathetic arousal (i.e. less stress). This aligns with prior research (Di Bello et al., 2021; Duarte et al., 2016). Additionally, a moderate positive correlation between self-compassion and perspective-taking supports existing findings (Gilbert, 2014; P ark & Tsovaltzi, 2022), suggesting that self-compassion enhances emotion regulation, particularl y during classroom confli cts. Teachers who practice self-compassion may engage more empathetically with students, thus improving classroom dynamics and reducing burnout.

Effectively addressing teacher stress through emoti on regulation is essential for sustaining the teach ing profession. MR simulations successfully replicate a uthentic classroom scenarios, enabling the practice  of crucial socio-emotional skills. The present findings emphasize the role of self-compassion in bolstering teachers' emotional resilience, job satisfaction, and overall  well-being. Future research employing real-time HRV data collection during reflective tasks could yield deep er insights into the imme diate physiological impacts of self-compassion practices and leverage them for teacher training.
