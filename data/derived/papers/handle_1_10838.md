# Learning Environments Designed For and With Learners with Disabilities

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In response to the necessity of inclusive learning environments and more active discussion involving learners with disabilities, this hybrid symposium aims to share five different research projects from varied settings with different learners with disabilities and facilitate discussions about learning environments for and with learners with disabilities. Projects shared in this symposium include a wide range of learner ages, various types of disabilities, and diverse technological supports in formal and informal educational settings. This symposium invites researchers, practitioners, and students of any ability from any location by using the hybrid format. This symposium can contribute to expanding scholarly discussion on various challenges and opportunities related to learners with disabilities and how learning environments could be designed for and with them

## Background and objective

People, regardless of their abilities or disabilities, learn throughout their lives.  Aligned with  the movement advocating diversity, equity, and inclusion and the increased awareness of various visible and invisible disabilities, learning of people with disabilities has been focused from various perspectives. In the learning sciences, scholars have investigated how people with disabilities lear n (e.g., Bell et al., 2012) and how to use technologi cal and instructional support to promote their learning (e.g., Lewis, 2017). Universal design for learning (UDL) provides various possibilities of how to design a learning e nvironment considering accessibility to include and enga ge learners with any abilities (Balta et al., 2020; Rose, 2000). However, there is a criticism that research often c onsiders learners with disabilities merely as resea rch participants or as ‘clients’ who receive aid and assistance (Allen et al., 2023). Therefore, there is a need for more active discussions considering learners with disabilities as agents who constantly interact with educators and shape technology-enhanced learning environments. Additionally, research tend s to be (and must be) very specific to certain contexts (e.g., computational intervention for autistic learners in astronomy learning), but such an approach makes them less easily applicable to other  learning environments and learner capabilities. The refore, hosting a symposium with research from various contexts conce rning learners with disabilities would help the fie ld of learning sciences facilitate discussions from a holistic perspective. This symposium aims to facilitate discourses about learner-centered learning environments for and with learners with disabilities by sharing research projects with different focuses conducted in diverse setting s. Particularly, across the five projects, we consi der how learners of a wide range of learner ages (from adolescents to adults) with various types of disabilities (neurodiverse/behavioral and physical)  could interact with other s (educators and peers) in technology-enhanced environments (from online/virtual to tangible/maker tools). Our discussant, Zac Alstad will also provide insights gained from research and practical applications of learning technologies to learners with disabilities. We acknowledge that there have been different termi nologies to refer to learners who have disabilities, such as learners/people with disabilities, disabled learners/people, learners/people with special needs, etc., and we consider various discourses and discussions around terminology choices. In this proposal, authors  from each project made a consensus to use  learners with disabilities while individual projects in this session use a spe cific term chosen and defined based on their research contexts. Session overview This hybrid symposium will include five research projects about varied learning environments for different types of disabilities (7 min each), following the co-chairs' welcome message and introduction of the ses sion (3 min). Then, the discussant will initiate the discussion by asking questions and commenting on the projects (7 min). The last 15 minutes will be used to talk about comments and questions from the audience, facilitated by the co-chairs. For the hybrid format, we propose providing various choices for the audience (e.g., low-sensory room, and website for asynchronous discussions in addition to the synchronous session).

Game-based task design to measure representational flexibility development of autistic adolescents in a virtual world Jewoong Moon, Fengfeng Ke, and Zlatko Sokolikj Aligned with neurodiversity principles, employing Naturalistic Developmental Behavioral Interventions (NDBIs) (Schuck et al., 2022), this study aimed to design and integrate various game-based tasks into a virtual reality (VR)based training program for autistic learners. We explored how game-based assessments (GBAs) within VR can effectively measure and foster representational fle xibility (RF) —the capacity to understand and manipulate various forms of representations. Addressing previo us feedback, this paper elucidates the connection  between GBAs and RF development and details the methodologi cal approach, focusing on qualitative analyses of engagement patterns as indicators of RF progression.

Autistic learners often exhibit unique patterns of interaction with their environments, diverging from typical-developing individuals (White et al., 2007). With the growing emphasis on inclusive education, federal guidelines stress supporting learners, including th ose with ASD. Emerging research highlights educatio nal interventions that aid autistic students in STEM le arning. A pivotal factor for successful STEM learning is representational flexibility (RF; Deliyianni et al., 2016). RF refers to an individual’s ability for pattern recognition, attention switching, and selecting alternative repr esentations. This ability is intrinsically connected to holistic thinking, transforming ideas across multiple repres entations, and creating adaptive solutions aligned with STEM tasks. VR with its immersive and interactive nature, has been  identified as a promising tool for promoting RF in autistic students. A challenge exists in tracking the process of autistic learners in VR settings. In this sense, gamebased assessments (GBAs) offer a p romising alternative (Tlili et al., 2022). GBAs pro vide an interactive and engaging evaluation technique tailored to assess autistic individuals’ developmental process. Conventional testing can induce anxiety (Oswald et al., 2016), especiall y in autistic individuals who may be sensitive to p ressure or unfamiliar situations. Game-based tasks have the potential to evaluate a range of skills that involves problem-solving, adaptability, and decision-making—all of which are closely linked to RF. Despite the promise of GBAs, research on their integration in VR-based training for autistic adolescents remains scant. Our research question is: How are game-based tasks designed and integrated to measure auti stic learners’ representational flexibility in virtual reality-based training?

This study included seven autistic adolescents aged  12-16 years, diagnosed according to the DSM-5 criteria for Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). Participants were recruited from local communities. Inclu sion criteria required participants to have a formal ASD  diagnosis (DSM-5), be in the age range of 12-16 years, and have guardian consent. We focused on capturing four  subset RF skills in the training: Attention-switching/cognitive shifting (ACS), alternative rep resentation (AR), pattern development (PD), and p attern contextualization (PC). Study data involved video recordings of VR training  sessions, capturing participant interactions and engagement with the GBAs. Performance data from each GBA, including task completion t ime, accuracy, and adaptability to changing rules, were automatically logged by the VR system. Post-session interviews and observations provided qualitative insights into participants' experiences and perceived challenges. Each study participant in  this study took part in one-hour VR training session s multiple times (6 –12 rounds). All study participants joined the virtual world using a  desktop-based VR browser supported by Opensimulator. This approach was to ensure accessibility for participants who might experience discomfort or  logistical issues with HMDs. The VR training was designed to enhance RF through a series of carefully crafted game-based tasks. Each session was methodically structured, beginning with  an introduction to  the day’s task, followed by a period of engagement with the VR environment and the specific activities planned for the session. The sessions concluded with a debriefing period, offering participants the  opportunity to reflect on their experiences, artic ulate any challenges faced, and discuss their achievements within the virtual setting. To facilitate a set of RF subset skills, we designe d and delivered game-based tasks as game-based assessment: (1) scavenger hunting game, (2) ball sh ooting game, (3) Gomoku game, and (4) online Wiscon sin card sorting test. First, a scavenger hunting game aims to assess autistic students’ ability to match a 3D item to its corresponding overlaid 2D map location that evaluat es visuospatial RF. They are asked to find their 3D  items (treasure) using a 2D map within a limited time. Second, the ball shooting task assesses a learner’s ability to adjust trajectories to hit a target, testing aspects of sp atial reasoning and prediction in RF. Students are guided to contemplate the angle, height, and force of a virtual ball launcher at varying distances. Third, Gomoku is a board game where players place their pieces (black or whi te) on a board to achieve five pieces of the same c olor in a row (horizontally, vertically, or diagonally). It aims to assess strategic thinking, planning, and pattern recognition. Last, a web-based Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST; Stoet, 2017) game was embedded to measure executive function, particularly adaptability and flexibility  in identifying and adapting to changing rules. Emb edded in VR as a heads-up display (HUD), students need to select a card ma tching the four displayed choices. Students need to understand the hidden rule guiding the card choice changes unpredictably during the task. Qualitative analysis focused on engagement patterns  observed during the VR training sessions. Using thematic analysis, the research team coded video recordings and interview transcripts to identify them es related to engagement, motivation, and challenges encounter ed. Special attention was paid to how engagement pa tterns differed among participants and how these patterns correlated with RF skill development. This analysis aimed to understand the nuanced ways in which autistic adole scents interacted with and benefited from the GBAs, providing insights into the potential of VR-based training to support diverse learning needs wi thin the neurodiversity framework.  We detailed the design and integration of game-based tasks within VR-based RF training for autistic adolescents. Our preliminary findings highlight the potential of these tasks in tracking RF development engagingly. Our design within the virtu al setting emphasized inclusivity, ensuring tasks r esonated with the unique learning needs of autistic adolescents while maintaining engagement. Playful and tactile computational thinking programs for blind and low-vision teens in makerspaces JooYoung Seo, Kyungwon Koh, Eugene Cox, and Si Chen Physical coding and electronics making tools (hereafter, CEMT) offer “object-to-think-with” to develop learners’ computational thinking (CT), which can promote thei r scientific reasoning (Papert, 1993). Unlike their  sighted counterparts, Blind and low-vision (BLV) teens lack accessible CEMTs and its pl ayful activities in both formal and informal education settings (Seo & Richard, 202 0). For example, widely adopted block-based CEM T frameworks (e.g. Scratch; Blockly; LittleBits) require visual interactions for drag-and-drop user gestures or colorschema adherence. Furthermore, only a few specialized educators and professionals are equipped with accessible teaching materials and know-hows on serving BLV patrons (Miele, 2017). This symposium panel shares how our mixed-visual-ability team at the University of Illinois at UrbanaChampaign (UIUC) has been addressing this accessibility issue. Funde d by the Institute of Museum and Library Services (IMLS #LG-252360-OLS-22), we have focused on playful CT curriculum development with accessible CEMTs for BLV learners and, along with inclusive professional development on serving BLV patrons for library maker professionals since August 2022.

We developed a prototype of our accessible CT curri culum in Spring 2023 at the Champaign-Urbana Community Fab Lab located at the UIUC.  To engage the FabLab staff and the UIUC iSchool stu dents in the playful and accessible CT curriculum development, t he two PIs (Seo & Koh) offered a series of independ ent studies on accessible library makerspaces at the iSchool in 2022 academic year, where a total of 10 undergrad and graduate students co-designed the curriculum with three Fab Lab instruct ors. The accessible CEMTs carefully chosen for our prototyped curriculum include 1) Cod e Jumper—a tactile block coding platform (for introducing basic computer science concepts), 2) Sn ap circuits —easy snap-together electronics (for teaching electrical engineering fundamentals), and 3) Snapino —Arduino extension based on the Snap Circuits (for h ardwaresoftware prototyping). While the Code Jumper (Figur e 1, left) was the readily accessibl e product made by the American Printing House for the Blind, Snap Circuit s (Figure  1, right) and Snapino required accessibility modifications, such as braille labeling for each el ectric part and replacement of the default Arduino IDE with screen-reader-friendly Visual Studio Code platform. We also consi dered multimodalities when designing our curriculum so that learners with varying degrees of visual impairments can enjoy the lessons with their preferred combination of any remaining senses, such as touchi ng, hearing, and even residual vision. We designed three lessons in the way that each concept can be transfe rred to the next learning activities. For instance,  Snap circuits and Code Jumper were introduced on the first day to  cover electronics and coding fun damentals respectively. Participants were engaged with the hardware-software prototyping with Snapino on the second day, building upon the prior concepts they have learned.

A two-day summer camp took place at the Chicago Lighthous e in early August 2023. Three Fab Lab instructors led the program, while two PIs and two research assistants, doctoral students at the iSchool, observed and offered assistance. The camp included thre e sessions: Electronics with Snap Circuit (3 hours), Coding with Code Jumper (3 hours), and Computer Engineering wit h Snapino (3 hours). Three participants (ages 15-20, 2 males and 1 female) attended our camp and completed surveys regarding their confidence level, sense of agency, and emotional reactions on CT activities before and  after our pilot curriculum intervention. We also c onducted focus-group interviews with the participants and the inst ructors to capture their nuanced experiences. All camp sessions were recorded for analysis.

The research team is currently analyzing collected data, including session recordings, focus group dat a with instructors and participants, as well as pre-and post-surveys. Instructors are revising the curriculum ba sed on feedback from the pilot program. The next project phase involves disseminating the program to various public library makerspaces in Illinois. In clusive research and design of interventions are fo unded in collaborative approaches, designing i nterventions and conducting research both for and w ith the communities being served (Bigby et al., 2014). In the case of t he neurodivergent community, a group that includes cognitive variations from expected societal norms like autism  and dyslexia, researc h has historically been conducted on these students rather than with them (Kapp, 2019). From a social view of disability, the external policies, practices, and environments neurodivergent students move throu gh on a daily basis are mutable factors that can cr eate unnecessary inequities and barriers to learning (de n Houting, 2019). We can leverage the Learning Scie nces to transform learning practices to plan for neurodiversity, a biological reality of neurological variance among every population.

Learning Sciences research conducted with neurodivergent researchers and students can allow t hose encountering barriers and discrimination to shape t he questions being asked and goals of interventions  (Kapp, 2019). But effective collaborative research require s better understanding of how to moderate student-researcher interactions and navigate power imbalances (Hawkins, 2015). Being neurodivergent can create further po wer imbalances since these students may st ruggle to effectively convey their ideas (Lewis et al., 2023). Neurodivergent-to-neurotypical communication can also result in misun derstandings and misinterpretations (Neff, 2022). Limited research has explored: a) The  challenges that occur when attempting to include neurodivergent students in higher education researc h and b) Effective measures for supporting their in clusion throughout the research process. This work expands on existing research by reflecting on a design-based research project collaborating with three neurodivergent undergraduate students over the course of a semester. In spring of 2023, students across all undergraduat e Computer Science courses and the accessibility resource office of the same North-Eastern United States university were invited to participate in the first phase of a design-based research study. Their ta sk was to collaboratively design training materials  for Computer Science instructors seeking to better address the needs of neurodivergent students in undergraduate Computer S cience classes. Three students who identified as neurodive rgent responded to the invitation and participated in research and design activities from January to May of 2023. Students were asked to participate in several joint  co-design meetings (approximately one monthly) and engage in weekly reflective activities, including: 1) a short  guidedreflection form to be filled out as needed about any challenges encountered during their program and 2) short (~15 minute) written tasks to guide reflection on specif ic course attributes (e.g., sensory processing in t heir learning environment). The goal of including them was to a) better understand the experiences and challenges th ey faced in Computer Science and b) identify the practices they would like to see instructors enact to improve their learning experience in higher education. Lessons learne d from this process represent a reflection on the d esign process, including meetings with other researchers, changes to the design process that occurred over the projec t, and discussions with the students.

The project transformed multiple times over the course of the Spring 2023 semester, due in large part to a combination of expressed and unexpressed needs on  the part of the three undergraduate Computer Scien ce students. Challenges arose and changes were made to account for them where possible, but in most cases suggest alternative approaches to apply to future studies. One of the biggest challenges was that students str uggled to produce data throughout the co-design process for all individual tasks, with in creasing difficulty as the semester continued. This was, in part, a parallel of the dif ficulty they were having completing coursework and managing their time and mental health in school. For most students in the design team, reflecting on and verbal izing their experiences as neurodivergent students was also a s ignificant challenge without real-time support. After discussing challenges with the students, reflection s were moved to in-person interviews. This helped to invite richer discussion and probe issues that were difficult for some students to express. Ultimately, having fewer time commitments and more executive function support for  the students involved may have helped to remediate  the majority of difficulties. These findings also sugge st that similar principles to inclusive learning frameworks like Universal Design for Learning (e.g., including mult iple modes of representation and engagement) can im prove inclusion for neurodivergent students collaborating in Learning Sciences design-based research. Yong Ju Jung, June Abbas, and Moses Munyao Following the criticism that maker education, in re ality, does not include “all” populations (Melo & Nichols, 2020), research on various underrepresented groups has been increasing. Youth with disabilities is one group, and studies pointed out accessibility issues and challe nges of makerspace and maker equipment. For  example, Seo

(2019) discussed that many maker toolkits were not fully accessible, their instructions were not docum ented

enough for learners with disabilities (for blind makers, in this case), and the materials lacked tangible designs and multi-sensory functionalities. Also, Allen et al. (2023) suggested different strategies to make a makerspace more accessible by creating adaptive technologies and bu ilding partnerships with disability communiti es. However, while many studies mainly examined the importance o f physical accessibility of tools and materials, th ere has been a lack of consideration of people, particularl y educators’ (i.e., facilitators’) perceptions, exp eriences, and practices, and how they interact with youth with disabilities in makerspaces. Therefore, this study addresses how library makerspace facilitators support youth with disabilities and how they perceive disabilities in making. This study, funded by the Institute of Museum and Library Servi ces (LG-252323-OLS-22), focused on makerspaces and maker programs in U.S. public libraries. We first collected survey data about the prevalence of makerspaces or maker programs for youth with disabilities in public libr aries nationwide  and then  conducted group and individual interviews with library staff (12 in total from 11 libraries in the U.S.) who were in charge of makerspaces or maker programs in their libraries and had provided programs or related services for you th with disabilities. Our semi-structured interviews were conducted via Zoom for a bout 1 to 1.5 hours each and fully transcribed. During the interviews, participants shared about th eir education settings as well as their experiences when they directly interacted with learners with di sabilities in their maker programs and/or makerspaces. Using the thematic analysis approach, interview transcrip ts were reviewed and open-coded first by three researchers, and repeated discussions to reach full consensus we re conducted. We note that this is an ongoing project, and at the time of this proposal submission, it is planned  to have on-site observations and interviews with youth with disabilities and their caregivers, and we hope to share some from this additional data set. Our preliminary findings from interview data show how facilitators’ approaches to youth with disabilities would be different in the preparation phase for makerspace and maker programs and the actual scaffolding phase during an educational program. When preparing, faci litators actively considered disabilities  by building partnerships with other organizations or schools to  reach the youth with disabilities population. Acce ssibility of equipment and tools was importantly considered in t he design of space, so any mobility aids (e.g., whe elchairs) could come in without any problems (although some m akerspaces could not have such spac e-related flexibility due to limited space). Some assistive tools, such a s noise-dampening headphones and adaptive scissors, were provided for youth with sensory sensitivities, and some library makerspaces ran a sensory hour when they turned off the lights, did not run any machines that would make noises for youth who experience sensory issues. However, once the accessibility of physical tools a nd materials themselves was well-considered and prepared, many facilitators tended to speak to youth’s (any) ability instead of disabilities as they actually interacted with youth with disabilities. As a participant said, “ They [youth with disabilities] engage in everything  with everybody else, it’s just accommodations,”  facilitators repeatedly expressed their belief in t he ability of youth with disabilities. They also considered various ran ges of youths’ i nterests to provide flexibility in their choice-making or make adjustments based on varied interest s; for example, when a participant noticed a child with a nonvisible disability who just wanted to watch Mickey Mouse cartoons during a  flower-drawing program, they talked to the child and said “you know we’re all painting whatever we were painting at the time, leaves or flowers or something I said, but if you want to paint Micke y Mouse, I drew one for you”, so the child was moti vated to engage in the program.  Such strategies would be aligned with the importance of openness and process-centered approaches of the maker mindset and any other inter est-driven learning for any abilities. Furthermore, in some cases, the facilitators co-tinkered with youth with disabilities, for example, by  helping a learner with a physical disability make a creative hand with a potato masher for sewing act ivities. Such examples show that youth with disabilities performed as active makers and tinkered with creative ideas based on their own unique situations. We acknowledge that we are only present ing preliminary findings and data collection and analys is processes are ongoing. Still, our study provides im plications on what and how educational practices oc curred in library makerspaces for youth with disabilities. Fa cilitators considered the importance o f physical support for accessibility when preparing a program, but their s caffolding and educational support during the progr am were more centered on abilities and interests. Based on these implications and further data collection and analysis, this project will contribute to developing design princi ples and guidelines for maker education programs fo r youth with disabilities and understanding how they learn in library makerspaces. Trends and issues in technology-enhanced teacher training programs for special educators Yunjeong Chang, Joo-Young Lee, and Mary DiCioccio There is a growing emphasis on establishing inclusi ve learning environments in preK-16 education, leveraging evidence-based practices to enhance academic success for students with disabili ties (Leifler, 2020). The use of technology-enhanced or online teacher training programs has se en a notable rise, offering flexibility for both general and special educators to engage in professional development tailored to their individual learning needs at any time and place (Hickie, 2022). Despite this inc reasing interest, the efficacy of these technology-enhanced or online teacher training programs in equipping educa tors with the knowledge and skills necessary for in clusive pedagogy remains uncertain. This systematic review aims to examine trends and issues in empirical rese arch focused on advancing inclusive pedagogy through teacher training programs within technology-enhanced learning environments. The goal is to provide insights into future research directions. The research questions guiding this review are: (a) What are the outcomes of online tea cher preparation and professional development progr ams designed to promote inclusive pedagogy? (b) What in structional components are integrated into online t eacher preparation and professional development programs to foster inclusive pedagogy? and (c) What content is covered by online teacher preparation and professional development programs to encourage inclusive pedagogy?

Using PsycINFO, ProQuest, and Web of Science, we systemat ically searched for peer-reviewed studies published from 2010 to 2022. Employing 21 search co des (e.g., inclusive*, online*, teacher*), we ident ified 12 studies from a pool of 12,031 articles based on inc lusion/exclusion criteria. The second and third aut hors coded data related to research questions, achieving a 92%  intercoder agreement. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with the first author. In addressing inc lusive pedagogy content, we referred to the Council  for Exceptional Children (CEC) Field and Clinical Experience Standards.

Our findings show that four dependent measures were  used across the studies: (a) pedagogical content knowledge and skills, (b) content knowledge related  to specific content areas, (c) satisfaction of qua lity of the programs, and (d) academic achievement of  the students with disabilities. When it comes to th e effectiveness of the study, eleven (out of 12 studies we reviewed) studies examined wh ether the PD programs positively impact on the enhancement of pedagogical knowledge and ski lls. Among them, only eig ht studies reported positive impacts of the PD programs on participants’ pedagogical knowledge and skills. Three of the twelve stud ies reported teachers’ satisfaction of the online teach er training programs and reported satisfaction with  the quality of the programs. Only two of the twelve studies examined t he impact of the online teacher training programs o n students with disabilities, all of which demonstrat ed positive outcomes. When it comes to instructiona l formats and components, six studies provided a full-online program, and the other studies selected a hy brid format (i.e., blended learning). Four studies trained pre-and in-service teachers synchronously, and the five studie s used an asynchronous approach. Eight studies incorporated c ase studies or pr acticum in school or clinical settings, but only two adopted teaching demonstration in the training context as practice-based training methods. Two studies did not include information related to the training  methods. Eleven studies covered content related to the seven CEC Field and Clinical Experience standards. One st udy did not include any information related to cont ent they taught during the program.

Based on the findings, we assert the need for improvements in current research on remote or technologyenhanced PD for teachers to create inclusive learni ng environments. We advocate for more comprehensive research methods, incorporating multiple data sourc es through technology-enhanced learning environments. A key limitation is the reliance on self-report data, hindering a comprehensive assessment o f educators' acquisition of inclusive pedagogy knowledge. To address this, w e propose using diverse data sour ces such as reflection journals, instructional materials, and video record ings in future research. Classroom observations and  student perceptions should also be integrated for a holistic evaluation approach (Liu & Phelps, 2020), providing valuable insights into the transformation of knowledge into instructi onal practices, especially in technology-enhanced teacher training programs. Additionally, empirical research reveals a deficiency in practice-based training for PD trainers, despite its potential in tech nology-enhanced learning environments. For special educati on teachers, particularly, the importance of teaching practice-based training, focusing on real-world challenges, is underscored (Hsiao et al., 2019). Special educators need in-depth knowledge of disability characteristics to use evidence-based practices like explicit instruction effectively. However, existing research often lacks comprehensive details on the content knowledge provided in these programs. Futur e studies should examine h ow integrated learning technologies can change beliefs and practices towards inclusive pedagogy, documenting both the learning process in training programs and the creation of inclusive environments. In our presentation, we will detail our systematic review method, including the PRISMA search, selection cr iteria, and reliability calculations, and discuss synthesized findings from twelve studies, complete with results and coding tables.
