# Researchers’ Roles and Context Complexity in Early Stages of Design Research

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This work explores complexities and considerations around early stages of design research. We describe our design of a storyline framework aimed at supporting minoritized youth engagement in critical technology education. We present preliminary data to highlight the contextual complexities, researchers' role in design, and challenges in adapting established design research frameworks. We close with three considerations focused on students anticipated interests, embracing nonlinearity, and being responsive to context needs.

In this work we discuss the initial stages of the co-design process, emphasizing the critical role of researchers' decisions and positionalities in setting the trajectory of design efforts. We particularly focus on the insights of two designers, who are also the researchers and authors of this paper, as they collaboratively design a storyline framework to foster critical technological engagement among Arab Palestinian youth in Israel. These design efforts are guided by design research principles from Bang & Vossoughi (2016) and Reiser et al. (2021). Y et, these design efforts are confronted by our understanding of the intricate realities that Arab Palestinian youth experience as members of a historically minoritized and marginalized community  and society. Our design approach is informed by our background in education work with Arab Palestinian youth and education research, leading to the development of  curricula that address critical technology education, such as examining biases in social media or surveillance and marginalization patterns that Arab Palestinian youth navigate daily (Sa’di, 2016). This illustrates the role of early design research  s tages in tackling the multifaceted complexities of context, resource allocation, and access to educational materials. Addressing these complexities is particularly significant when working with learners from nondominant communities,  creating possibilities for/with them to interrogate and critically examine technology as systems intersecting  with norms, cultures, histories, ideologies, practices, and policies (Mawasi et al., 2023).

Within the field of  Learning Sciences, a range of frameworks have been proposed to refine the designbased research process. Notable examples include the storyline framework (Reiser et al., 2021) and participatory design (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). These design frameworks, while differing in their methodological approaches and objectives, offer essential design principles that guide research work with learners and educators (Zuiker et al., 2017). The storyline framework primarily aims at developing instructional curricula, prioritizing  student perspectives and support ing their engagement in scien tific knowledge building. This is facilitated by enabling students to explore  phenomena, ask questions, and develop arguments and coherent explanations for their inquiries (Reiser et al., 2021). On the other hand, the participatory design framework serves as both a pedagogical and methodological  instrument, considering historical contexts, power dynamics, and the transformation of relationships in the learning process. This framework underscores the  significance of these elements in shaping educational experiences (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016).

Design-based research frameworks provide robust tools for conducting education research with learners and educators. However, a scholarly gap exists regarding the initial stages of the design process, especially when it involves nondominant communities that include historically minoritized groups. These phases are crucial to the for the formation of design principles, the provision of design research  environment, and assimilation of knowledge of diverse  team members to braid with kindness  their distributed expertise to support their local communities (e.g., Kimmerer, 2013). The early stages  of design are also pivotal for adapting, modifying, and resisting research tools and instruments which are developed in contexts different from those the researchers are engaged with. As researchers and designers adopt and adapt these methods, they must consider the complexity of these contexts to ensure the principles of design are not only theoretical, but also practical and oriented to real-life challenges experienced both individually and collectively. This poster invites a discussion on the significance of early design stages aimed at collaborative knowledge construction among researchers to contextualize design research and unravel  the educational possibilities it presents. It highlights the possible impact of researchers' positionalities and expertise on design process and addresses challenges encountered in adapting existing design frameworks to suit a different geographic context.

## Methodological approach: Context, data source, and initial analysis

2337 The pr imary objective of our project  is to develop a storyline framework that aims to engage Arab Palestinian youth with critical and ethical perspectives of emerging technologies, focusing on artificial intelligence and generative AI as a case. Leveraging our positions as  designers, researchers, and community educators, we have combined our field and academic expertise. In July 2023, we dedicated approximately 15 hours to design meetings that spanned until November 2023. These sessions, a blend of in-person and virtual meetings, were pivotal for our collaborative design process. They yielded robust des ign outcomes, including logistical notes, resources exchanges, and the foundational structure of the program’s storyline. In addition, we integrated auto-ethnographic reflections to document and reflect on our design thinking process and decisions. Throughout our design work, we  documented our process, capturing insights through meeting n otes, annotated shared resources, and the creation of collaborative artifacts such as brainstorming notes and diagrams. A critical aspect of our methodology was the exploration and critique of the storyline framework’s potential limitations and constrains at an early stage of design. To address the paper’s goals, we relied on three key artifacts: First, an affinity diagram through which we derived the primary themes of the storyline curricula design and their resonance with our study’s context  with Palestinian youth  (Hanington & Martin, 2019). Second, brainstormed associations that deepened our understanding of the community’s multifaceted identity and its interplay with technology (e.g., through examining multiple meanings of identity, land, housing, police, resilience, forced displacement, among others). Finally, an analysis of two resources-YouTube video on counter-mapping (Global Oneness Project, 2018) and a radio interview on surveillance technology in unrecognized Arab villages. These resources illustrated the multifunctionality of maps as tools for storytelling, surveillance mechanisms, and perhaps even erasure or concealment of histories, enriching our design of learning activities that probe the sociocultural and political aspects of technologies.

## Major findings

Our findings elucidate three d esign considerations: (1) leveraging  researchers/designers’ expertise to speculate students’ interests and shape trajectories for their learning, reflected in initiatives aimed to critically examine and recognize embedded power dynamics within technological design, (2) recognizing the  need for moving beyond strict design research frameworks  and embracing the inherent messiness and nonlinearity in designing and creating meaningful educational content. This rests on a dynamic  and iterative approach that resists the fixed structure and invites ongoing discussions, content development, and reassessment of preconceived notions about the community, and (3) designing while being aware of and responsive to local contexts and needs. This includes the ability to recognize the narratives and discourses community members use to articulate their stories and lived experiences. These considerations are reaffirmed by the ongoing war, which has posed a challenge to our work and reasserted the imperative to adapt our design strategy and timeline to  navigate the complexities of the sociopolitical environments we work in. These adaptations play a crucial role in ensuring our design principles are not merely theoretical but are applicable and attuned to real-world challenges, thereby fostering a conducive learning environment amidst changing social and political landscapes. Through this reflective process, we aim to contribute to the broader discourse on design-based research by highlighting the importance of flexibility, context responsiveness, and inclusion of local knowledge  in design procedures  and knowledge construction. This is especially significant when working in/with complex and diverse environments that educational initiatives have the potential to not only adapt to, but also thrive within.
