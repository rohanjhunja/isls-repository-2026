# Cosplay Connections: Exploring Women Science Communicators’ Comic con Artifacts, Practices, and Re-imagination

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Science communication is shifting towards inclusion, where science communicators’ identities, culture, and backgrounds are valued, not quashed. However, there is a dearth of research that explores how this shift affects science communicators' experiences in everyday spaces. We observed three women, cosplaying scientists who were communicating science at a comic con then interviewed them about their experiences. Using the conceptual framework of figured worlds, we explored how they relate to cultural artifacts, build scientific practices, and re-imagine science identities. We found that women scientists used cosplay characters and costumes to drive conversations with visitors. While experiencing being objectified and sexualized in their scientific practice, they are trying to resist these interactions and share their own experiences to others to construct new narratives on possible identities of scientists. Our findings add to understanding of science identity, how it is constructed, reified, and countered within public spaces.

## Introduction

Oftentimes scientific identities are created and im agined as individuals being a “certain kind of pers on” (Zuckerman & Lo, 2021, p. 2). However, researchers have indicated that the for mation of a science identity is socio-culturally constructed and interdependently shaped across various realms or intersections of people’s lives (Avraamidou, 2019). Moreover, scientific practices,  such as communication, may be construed as objecti ve and separate from the influences of sociocultural processes, but they are deeply interconnected with social and cultural norms, values, and actions (Canfield et al., 2020).  As people have conversations and learn about scien ce, they shape meaning and construct identities in relation to others and about themselves and who they are (Z uckerman & Lo, 2021). Stereotypes about who participates in the practices of science persist with assumptions of scientists needing to have masculine traits (Millet et al., 2018). In our research setting, we inquire into the experiences and perspectives of scientists who identify as women and are at a comic  convention (i.e., comic con) primarily to communicate about science. We use a sociocultural a pproach, figured worlds, to shape our methods of in quiry and connect the unique findings of our research to this conceptual framework. Figured worlds are a framework that describe how cu lture and identity are not static; individual actio ns within a figured world are what produce and change cultural practices, artifacts imbued with social me aning and individual identities in relation to others (Holland, 2001; Scission, 2020; Urrieta, 2007; Zuckerman & Lo, 2021). We explored how women scientists interact with publ ic visitors in a comic con setting, and the interco nnections of artifacts that make up values and meaning in thi s figured world. W e investigated how women scientists re-imagined their identity in a comic con setting bringing their unique history in a person from other figured worlds to each interaction (Holland, 2001). We analyzed the experiences and perspectives of three women scientists using a figured worlds conceptual framework which conside rs action as culture, artifacts as representation o f shared value and mediators of identity, and re-imagination as a central process to the creation and recreation of identities in a sociocultural context (Holland, 2001; Scisson, 2020; Urrieta, 2007; Zuckerman & Lo, 2021). We asked: how do women scientists at a comic con relate to cultur al artifacts, build practices at the intersection o f science communication and cosplay, and enable re-imagined “science identities” for themselves and others?

## Background

A helpful way we can explore perspectives of women scientists in a sociocultural context is by further  defining the context in which learning from/with others is t aking place. In this study, women scientists were s cience communicators at comic cons in the United States. S cience communication can be broadly construed as ei ther scientists (“experts”) transmitting information to the public or as a dialogue between multiple entiti es including decision-makers, experts, and the public regarding public understanding, appreciation, excitement, and knowledge of science (Kappel & Holmen, 2019). Some examples of science communication include science cafes (Childers et al., 2021), science blogging (Mahrt & Puschmann, 2014), communicating about science on social media (Lam et al., 2019), using peoples’ interests of collegiate sports to increase data literacy (Clegg et al., 2023), and scientists having science-based conversations in “everyday spaces” like laund romats and bars (Stofer et al., 2019). Additionally, scholars have examined the intersecti on(s) of identities that moderate scientists’ commu nication practices, finding that scientists’ backgrounds, in terests, and lived experiences can encourage richer, more meaningful connections (Rodrigues et  al., 2023). Notably, the perception over recent dec ades persists that scientific practices are associated with a masculin e persona as determined by the “Draw a Scientist Te st” given to many North American children (Millet et al., 201 8). Carlone and Johnson (2007) provide a nuanced lo ok at science identity for women scientists, focusing on recognition, including recognition of oneself as a “science person” as well as recognition by others of oneself  being a “science person” (p. 1208). Further explor ation of science identity has been conceptualized by Avraami dou (2019), who reifies recognition as a core featu re of science identity, and indicates that such recogniti on is a core part of a social context. Recognition of oneself and recognition of others as it relates to “science” id entity aligns to a figured world’s sociocultural un derstanding of identity as an active process where people develop or “figure” identities in relation to others contextually. Figured worlds are a lens to understand social cont exts that focus on how individual actions make up broader culture, and artifacts or symbols that represent values and beliefs (Holland, 2001). Conversely, a  figured world perspective would reject the notion that indi viduals are influenced by culture because individua l actions and relationships are what actively create and shape culture which does not exist outside of or separate from them (Sisson et al., 2022). In addition, a figured worlds perspective described how identities of individuals are mediated by or interdependent with artifacts (Zuckerman & Lo, 2021). Individuals all have a unique ‘history in person’ of experiences across different figured worlds which t hey bring to each context or figured world and may result in continuity or discontinuity of meaning and values i n different figured worlds (Zuckerman & Lo, 2021). For example, a study of middle school girls found that despite interests in science activities, racial dynamics for girls from minoritized backgrounds could constrict studen ts’ sense of science identity (Calabrese Barton et al, 2013). The concept of third space in a figured world lens has been suggested as a way for marginalized groups of learners to identify with science (Chafee & Gupta, 2017; Jackson & Seiler, 2018). The concept of third space suggests that a given context can be im bued with individuals’ experiences from other figur ed worlds, and was analyzed in learning settings such as online forums and SciCafes (Chafee & Gupta, 2017). In a SciCafe, the figured  world of science participation, such as a presentation and d iscussion on science, meets the figured world of in formal socializing, like a cocktail hour, as individuals a re allowed the opportunity to learn about science i n a relaxing, encouraging, and accessible way (Chafee & Gupta, 20 17). Figured worlds, in particular those that have the potential to increase accessibility and inclusion i n scientific practice, are highly dynamic sites of interaction and learning where participants bring in their own expe riences and share in the creation or re-creation of cultural artifacts, practices, and possibilities.

The concept of figured worlds is a sociocultural model that looks at the flow of activity within a specific social setting and how individual identities are im provised there. In our analysis of a comic con cont ext, where the scientists dressed in cosplay (i.e. dressing up as a character from a movie, book, or video game) and interacted with public visitors, the figured world involves th e creation of artifacts (i.e. cosplay) and (re)crea ting science communication practices and identities within this novel space. A figured world's conceptual framework provides a useful lens of research when women scientists are  attempting to create new possibilities about what activities are practices of science and who participates in a science learning space. Figured worlds is a conceptual framework to better understand how artifacts embody shared va lues interacting with identity, and how women scien tists reimagine their identities in this context as a way o f providing others with expanded opportunities to i magine themselves as scientists too.

## Context and participants

We partnered with an educational initiative, Cosplay for Science, that is dedicated to making science accessible through cosplay and attending comic cons (Stoneburg  et al., 2020).  The initiative is open to any scientist who wants to communicate their science by using cosplay  and attending comic cons. Scientists with varied i dentities (i.e. queer, underrepresented) with varied scientif ic expertise (i.e. astronomy, chemistry, herpeto logy, paleontology) are affiliated with the initiative. C osplaying scientists  usually spend two full days at pop-up museum booths at comic cons, where they are dressed  as characters from media and have materials that r elate to their cosplay and science (Figure 1). For example, when cosplaying as Star Wars characters, scientists  created a pop-up museum booth called the Galactic Archive, where they connected the planets, fauna, and flora featured in Star Wars to the real-world aspects that inspired them, such as how the tauntaun’s horns and adaptations to living in a cold climate were modeled off of bighorn sheep.

For this study, we explore the figured worlds of th ree women cosplaying scientists whose pseudonyms were Sabine, Leia, and Maz. Sabine was a psychologi st who identified as a straight, white woman betwee n the ages of 25-34. Sabine cosplayed as a naturalist from the planet Sorgan, a lush, tropical planet featured in the show The Mandalorian, wearing field clothing such as a field vest, pants, and carrying a notebook to record information about mosses and other flora from Sorgan. Leia was a chemist who identif ied as a straight, mixed-race woman between the ages of 25-34. Leia cosplayed as Leia Organa, a character featured heavily in a majority of Star Wars storylines as a Jedi, military leader, and politici an. Maz was an astronomer who identified as a bisex ual, mixed race woman between the ages of 18-24. Maz cosplayed as a Star Trek officer who was po rted into the Star Wars universe due to a misaligned transporter who then s ought to study the different planets within the Sta r Wars galaxy.

## Data collection and analysis

This study utilized data that were collected for a larger study. Data were collected in two phases: ob servations at comic cons and post-comic con semi-structured interviews. In the first phase, we obser ved cosplaying scientists at a pop-up museum booth at a three-day comic con in the western United States. In the second phase, we conducted one-on-one, ZOOM-based, semi-structured interviews no more than three weeks afte r the comic con. These interviews lasted approximately 45 minutes and contained questions that asked scientists to reflect on their experiences at the comic cons, and expanded on rese archers’ observations at the comic cons. Interviews  were recorded via Zoom, transcribed in Otter.ai software, and then cleaned by the authors listening to the recording while reading the transcript. Transcripts were uplo aded into MAXQDA for coding, and the larger study focused on narrative structuring and effective framing of s cientific information. However, during coding, we a lso coded utterances as “other,” meaning that the utterances were of interest but did not fit into any of the de fined coding category for narrative structure and effective fram es. It is within this "other" content that we inqui red into codes on artifacts, practices, and re-imagination.

We selected Sabine, Maz, and Leia from the larger p articipant pool to analyze how the participants’ experiences related to the figured worlds theoretic al frame. For initial coding, w e used descriptive codes  which assign basic labels or topics  (Saldaña & Omasta, 2016) to categorize the data that we originally interpreted as “other”. We conducted initial coding through line-by-line analysis of the transcripts. Coded segments ranged from a sentence to a paragraph. For example, at this stage the in-vivo code “character spurs conversation” was labelled directly from participant wording and used across subsequent transcripts as applicable. Lastly, we conducted axial coding which aims to sort, compare, and classify th e initial codes we compiled (Saldaña, 2015). During this focused stage of coding, we revised and reworded tentative codes constructed at the initial stage, and we categorized all codes into alignment with our conce ptual framework of artifacts, practices, and re-imagination. For example, the subcode “costume” constructed in i nitial stages was now categorized under artifacts. The researchers individually read each interview transc ript, coded it, and then the research team met  to discuss codes as agents of a figured world in the comic con setti ng. We focused on the interviews of Maz, Leia, and Sabine as they were participants with many instances of the “other” code category in the larger study. In addition, we chose these three  participants as they shared the common experience o f identifying as women who were science communicators. These three participants were partic ularly descriptive in their use of artifacts, practices, and reimagination, thus they were the focus of analyses.

## Findings

Cultural artifacts: “Character spurs conversation” Within the comic con space, people cosplay by dress ing in costumes and pretending to be in character a s they interact with fellow comic con attendees, listen to  panel discussions, make purchases at booths or att end interactive exhibits, such as a pop-up museum. Comic con cultural artifacts include costum es made from purchases or handmade elements, and renderings of p opular culture at booths for sale or display. In pa rticular, a comic con costume or cosplay is a cultural artifact as it represents the value of roleplay that attendees share in this space. This allows for people to spark interactions with each other as they give compliments, roleplay or converse in general. For the cosplaying women scientists, connections with the public were often spurred by the characters they were dressed as. Leia explained how she chose a cosplay: I personally like to put a lot of thought into, like, who I dress as and like…you know, how I do it, and I usually like to take inspiration from something I see. So it's going to be some character. But… there is a danger to that where people see jus t that,  and they don't really care about anything else. (Interview, December 27, 2022).

Leia chose characters from popular media for her cosplay which was a process that took her a long time to decide. While these characters were likely to be of most interest to others at the comic con, she expressed that could also be a challenge. She descri bed how others might not be interested to hear abou t her science communication information if they were too interested in the char acter she dressed as. In addition, Sabine, a psycho logist, described her perception of Leia’s costume, saying,  “just being dre ssed as a character spurs conversation, or at least like compliments. Like with Leia's for example…people were like, ‘oh, my gosh, that's a great Leia costume!’ Or, ‘can I take a picture with you?’  (Interview, December 18, 2022). For Sabine, she truly enjoys how cosplaying sparks interest and connections. Sabine described how “I feel like it was just, it just felt like those are my people. Like, those are the people I want to hang out with. ” (Sabine, Interview, December 18, 2022). Although Sabine was at the comic con communicate science, she really felt a part of the community. She explained further: Us being dressed as Jedi, dressed as fighter pilots, and stuff… it would make people like “oh, these are Star Wars people”, and they would recognize that quicker than they would recognize anything on the table, or like, see any of our signage or anything like that… having a cosplay… is helpful. (Interview, December 18, 2022) The costumes that the women scientists wore were th e most recognizable artifact at the pop-up museum booth. Although there was a variety of communication mater ial, such as booth signs, fossils, infographics, and decor, these artifacts were not as helpful to spark conversation as being dressed in cosplay for the women scientists. The women scientists took time to consider what charact er they constructed for their cosplay to relate to others’ interests and the artifacts on display at the pop-up museum booth. The cultural artifact of cosplay c ostumes is something that the women scientists used to display  their own interests in a particular character from  popular media, as well as an enabling influence to generate connections with the public who came by the pop-up museum booth.

Practices: “Are they really interested in science… Or are they just chatting you up because you’re a woman?” The comic con context has its own set of practices that include norms, behaviors and attitudes, such a s what is expected of people participating in cosplay and character. What is expected of some groups of people, especially women, includes playing with ch aracters that are oftentimes sexual or risqué in their costuming. Moreover, women cosplaying scientists experience the feeling of being sexualized or objectified regardless of th e style of cosplay they are dressed in. Sabine, who worked alo ngside Leia at the pop-up museum booth, noticed that some members of the public would say inappropriate things to the scientist. Sabine described that “people will say weird things to Leia and Leia didn't really like that…. I didn't hear anything super terrible, but I think she's experienced that in the past ” (Interview, December 18, 2022). Both in the past and at the present, Sabine witness ed Leia experience uncomfortable or even “terrible” interac tions. Sabine found it to be a negative experience when interacting with th e public. Similarly, Leia was aware of what she per ceived as the “inappropriate” motivations for people coming up to her in cosplay. Leia discussed how when doing science communication and outreach she would assess or gauge audiences’ background, unders tanding, and interests. In addition to this as a wo man in cosplay, she also considered the publics’ motives for conversing with her. Leia wondered: Are they really interested in science, or they just kind of want to like, argue, because, like you're a scientist? Do they get a kick out of arguing with the scientists,  or are they just chatting you up because you're a woman? And blah blah blah and “ No, I’m not wearing my wedding ring, because that's not part of the costume”, but it sho uldn't matter.... So it's just one more question that's in the back of your head as you're trying to interact with people… what's kind of funny is like I'm in a Leia costume. I'm in classic Leia, so I am so covered up. But you know, like even like that it's like you get like vibes of they're here to… just ke ep talking to you... And you're just like, “okay like, please leave.” (Interview, December 27, 2022) For Leia, beyond considering such things as the pub lic’s knowledge and interest in her science communi cation, she was often aware of other motives. She felt some  may have had hidden reasons for interacting with h er as a cisgender woman. The question of if someone interacted with her to actually learn science or simply for “chatting [her] up” is something she considered internally. W omen scientists wish to step out of conversations w here they are uncomfortable as sexualization or objectificati on of their cos playing affects what types of costumes they choose to wear.  Leia further described  as a woman in cosplay that there were “categories, and one really big category is the sexy cosplay… you're just like, ‘how do I want to navigate this? How do you do that at a con?’... sometimes people will just like, touch you and like  that's not cool ” (Interview, December 27, 2022). Here Leia explains her understanding of the cosplay norm of t he “sexy cosplay” category and some of the problems  she perceives arise from it, i n particular non-consensual touching. While Leia explained “you will  never find me in the sexy Leia, the slave costume, although you know, props to those who wear it” (Interview, December 27, 2022). She celebrated those who used sexy costuming, but f ound this practice misaligned with her work as a science communicator in the space. Leia positions herself i n opposition to this as “classic Leia, so [she is] covered up” (Interview, December 27, 2022) yet despite this dis tinction she too encountered situations she did not  feel comfortable with. The feeling of discomfort was sha red by Sabine who described “I would not feel comfo rtable dressing up as slave Leia to educate children… I mean it might get some adults to like, pay attention and stop for a longer period  of time.” (Interview, December 18, 2022). Both scie ntists were highly cognizant of how others might perceive the cosplay artifacts that they chose to wear in the comic con setting as they communicated science. The norms that lean towards sexy costuming are connected to associated behavior and attitudes; namely unwanted attention encountered by the scientists. Therefore,  they might remain aware of it throughout interacti ons as “just one more question that's like in the back of your head as you're like trying to interact with people” (Leia, Interview, December 27, 2022).  The women scientists experience d objectification or felt aware of the potential for inappropriate interactions as it relate d to their cultural artifact in the form of a cosplay  costume. There fore, the norms and practices at the comic con did not empower these women scientists to choose any cultural artifact for a costume. They remain ed highly aware of their overlapping positions as a wo man, as a scientist, and as a cosplayer while selecting their costumes.

Re-imagination: “Representing scientists and… who cando science” For the women in our study, the modern-day field of science is built upon a foundation of prac tices that often excludes women. While more and more women become present in the science community, a connection to this past remains. Sabine felt  deeply passionate about shifting common perceptions  about who is a scientist. She expressed the importance of representation in science, saying  that cosplaying helped “[show] off the variety of scientists and who cando science. That has always been a really big passion of mine… trying to show women in science are more underrepresented. We’re historical ly marginalized folks that cando science ” (Interview, December 18, 2022).  Sabine use d her presence in the cosplaying space and other comm unication contexts to represent a woman in science and other diverse iden tities that are in science, but have been historica lly marginalized. Despite being part of a historically marginalized group in the broader science community, she was driven by her passion to reverse the stereotypes su rrounding “who cando science”. Sabine wished to co nstruct new representations of scientists and to shift norm s of who participates in science. Similarly, Leia p ursued a reimagination of the identities and practices of a scientific community member to include cosplay. She described: I kind of doubt people look at us and immediately g o, “oh, yes, these are all professional scientists”… So yeah, I guess it's to just let peop le know “Actually, you know, we do both. We're all very interested in cosplay, and we're ver y interested in science, and most of us have some kind of specialty that we would love to talk t o you about.” (Interview, December 27, 2022) For Leia, the existing range of practices and perce ptions for a professional scientist did not include cosplay. By being at a comic con as a professional scientist in  cosplay costume, she hoped to let people know that  scientists can also be cosplayers. In addition, Maz, an astronomer, connected with f ellow women to share her own experiences, and, hopefully, inform them of future opportunities they might consider. For Maz, buildin g connections included her seeking out people from a similar demographic. In particular, she gravitate d towards fellow young women cosplayers. She described: Oftentimes, when I am trying to find someone who se ems approachable…  I am looking for someone who I feel like I share some sort of demographic with where I'm like, “okay, well, I'm gonna try this person since I know we have this thi ng in common”, and I fe el like a lot of the most successful interactions I feel like I did have  was with a lot of sort of other young women who either came to the booth or the panels, or you know kind of in that demographic area. (Interview, December 29, 2022) Approaching others who she perceived as young women with similar interests allowed Maz to build a foundation to interactions. She found her interactions with other young women were often more successful than other groups of people who came to the booth. Maz would even pro vide her contact information and continue on with t he interactions at comic cons through email conversation. She described her experiences talking with one particular visitor as follows: It was one young woman who was really interested in  talking to me… she was like, I think, perhaps either at community college or just starting out at like a 4 year institution, and she was really interested in just like learning more about like me, basically and kind of just like, how I got involved with the project and kind of you know what I worked on, and you know, like all it in that sort of like space of yeah, just sharing a little bit more about me. (Interview, December 29, 2022) For Maz it was a success to talk about science to the young woman wh o was interested in hearing about Maz’s personal story and experiences. By “just sharing a little bit more about [herself]” (Interview, December 29, 2022), Maz positioned herself as a positive entity that young women might be able to relate to in the prese nt or imagine possibilities for herself into the future. Through representation of diverse identities in science, pe ople from all backgrounds may be better able to connect with others, and imagine science as a future possibility for themselves if they had not previously considered it.

## Discussion

A figured worlds conceptual framework considers processes and symbols such as artifacts, action as culture, and re-imaging of figured worlds that integrate different ‘histories of a person’ (Holland, 2001; Sisson et a l., 2020). In our research context, the  women scientists in cosplay recreat ed practices in this figured world by sharing in conversations about science and resisting inappropriateness or harmful conversations they do not wish to be a part of. Moreover, their own lived experiences as women in figured worlds outside of the comic con realm allow them to construct stories of the identity of “who” is a scientist. We asked: how do women scientists at a c omic con relate to cultural artifacts, build practices at th e intersection of science communication  and cosplay, and enable re-imagined “science identities” for themselves and ot hers? A figured world perspective views the everyda y practices of community members as the guiding force  to both form and reform the community.  In other words, the women scientists in a comic con setting created pra ctices on communicating, or not, which were situate d within their “history of a person” from all other figured worlds they participate in. We found that women scientists connected to fellow cosplayers through artifacts li ke costuming and roleplay, and resisted interaction s that felt inappropriate to them due to the intent of others. Further, women scientists actively re-told their own lived experiences and sharing with others which served to provide a re-imaging of what is possible for others who wish to pursue scientific careers or endeavors.

Cosplay costume as cultural artifact For the women scientists, cosplay costumes as artif acts are a way for them to roleplay in the comic co n culture, present themselves as scientists, and, ideally, lim it instances of being perceived as too “sexy”. The meaning and expectations within and across figured worlds often  involved continuity and discontinuity, or shared v alues and competing values (Zuckerman & Lo, 2021). Therefore, the participants' sense of identity as scientists who cosplay is impacted by the comic con cultural value of sexy costumes. Presenting oneself as sexy is not cultural value and practice that is shared between the other figured w orlds the participants partake in outside of this s etting. Both Sabine and Leia discussed how the comic con setting  typically includes a large focus on sexy costuming  for women participants as they experienced in past events. Our participants understood that sexy costumes were part of comic cons norms and practices, but they positioned themselves as separate from the creation and di splay of these artifacts. From a figured worlds perspective,  an artifact is a representation of shared values a nd a mediator of identity (Holland, 2001; Scission, 2020; Urrieta, 2007; Zuckerman & Lo, 2021). For both Sabine and Leia, they rejected sexy costumes as a cultural artifact as it created in them a sense of discomfort and heightened their awareness of others’ unwanted attention. Instead, t hey chose to position themselves as women cosplayer s who did not create or wear sexy costuming or roleplay, but supported those who did. The practices of connection and disengagement By initiating connections between cosplayers, and, conversely, stepping out of particular conversation s, Maz, Sabine, and Leia created new norms of what it means to be a woman in science in a comic con space. Interestingly, the women scientists described how being in cosplay was essential to sparking conversation with others at comic cons. In particular, cosplaying was a way to spur a  conversation or compliments which then had the pot ential to lead to deeper discussion about the particular science communication topics connected to the booth. This supports the figured world’s notion that a person’s cultural capital, such as knowledge and experiences, from one figured world may overlap in a new figured world  (Zuckerman & Lo, 2021). Maz, Leia, and Sabine capit alized on their cultural knowledge of cosplay to configure their sc ience identity into the space in relation to visito rs and fellow scientists. For example, Maz chose to connect with visitors who  also had marginalized identities, such as those she perceived as sharing something in common with while Sabine fo und a deep connection in this space as she described fellow cosplayers as “her people”. Maz, Leia, and Sabine allowed this informal science education space, the comic con, to become a place where fellow w omen were encouraged to connect with them through t he practices of conversation, connection, and shared interests. Historically, marginalized groups, such as women, face barriers in science education in formal and nonformal settings (Avraamidou, 2020; Chaffee & Gupta, 2018; Jackson & Seiler, 2018; National Science Foundation, 2020; Stromholt and Bell, 2018). In our research context, the women scientists did not wish to relate or connect with booth visitors who they perceived as being “inappropriate”, “weird”, or “terrible”. Therefore, the women scientists disengaged or stepped out of conversations t hat felt this way. I n particular, Leia experienced various forms of objectification when interacting w ith the public at the comic con. To resist the occu rrence of these interactions, Leia considered such things as asking people to leave. Additionally, she chose to carefully consider the type of costume that she would wear in the comic con space by avoiding those which were too “sexy” and therefore might be perceived as an interaction not about science. Re-imagining science identities Our participants actively drew on their ‘histories of a person ’ (Holland, 2001), such as past experiences and academic knowledge to present and re-imagine themselves as cosplayers, women, and scient ists participating in the comic con with fellow scientists and members of  the public. They shared detailed accounts of th eir own experiences to others which served to construct new  narratives on possible identities of scientists in  this space. Importantly, participation in one figured world may lead to changes in how individuals interact with and identify in other figured worlds (Sisson, 2022; Zuckerman & Lo, 2021). Therefore, the participants' participation here can support their science identity in other figured worlds, and it is possible that their interactions and connections with members of the public could shift how those individuals behave or interact outside of the comic con in relation to science identity. The participants were particularl y passionate about shifting gendered assumptions ab out the identity of scientists. Due to both historical and current marginalization of women in science (Avraamidou, 2020), these women scientists work to include diverse iden tities of people in settings that intersect with sc ience and cosplay. Our participants are science communicators who prom oted learning for a diverse audience that is not typically associated with being a space for science  and scientists – at a comic con. By working to expand both who can be a scientist and where conversations can take place, our participants are strengthening their practice in equitable science communication.
