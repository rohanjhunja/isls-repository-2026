# Supporting Computational Problem-Solving and CT Learning Through Paper-based Problem-Solving Aids

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Educational Robotics (ERs) are widely used to teach Computational Thinking (CT) in elementary schools, yet they do not effectively support systematic engagement with computational problems. This study investigated whether integrating ER activities with familiar, relatable tasks could support young learners’ problem-solving and CT learning. It compared CT learning gains and the problem-solving strategies of two groups: one using ERs and tablets with teacher support, reflecting current classroom practices (control condition), and another integrating paper-based tasks with the same technology (treatment condition). Students (grades 4 and 5) worked in pairs to solve scenarios involving a robot navigating mazes. Results showed a significantly positive e ffect on CT learning for the treatment group and demonstrated that students in this condition approached the problem more systematically and appl ied different debugging strategies. The findings support the design and use of scaffolds grounded in specific models of computational problem-solving.

Educational Robotics (ERs) are widely used to teach Computational Thinking (CT) in elementary schools. These systems enable students to express themselves through computational concepts and support collaborative, situated learning (Eguchi, 2014). However, research suggests that ER activities should be optimized to yield greater learning benefits (Del Olmo-Muñoz et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2023). Since many ERs are integrated into problembased learning frameworks (Hsu et al., 2018), limitations may stem from ineffective problem-solving approaches (Norton et al., 2007; Atmatzidou et al., 2018; Chevalier et al., 2020). The technology does not lend itself well to engaging in systematic problem-solving as children tend to adopt a ‘doing’ rather than ‘thinking-and-doing’ approach (Lye & Koh, 2014), focusing primarily on programming instead of other problem-solving steps necessary to develop effective solutions (Weintrop et al., 2016; Shute et al., 2017). Instructional interventions to address this tendency include limiting access to the programming interface (Chevalier et al., 2020) and delaying computer-based feedback (Chevalier et al., 2022). These strategies are broadly designed to temporarily redirect children’s attention away from programming itself. Other instructional interventions include providing additional activities and tasks, including involving non-digital tools. While the impact of such tools has been examined at middle and high school (Loksa et al., 2016), their potential benefits in elementary schools remain largely unexplored. Younger students are more accustomed to hands-on activities, including drawing, which allow them to express themselves more naturally. Non-digital tools may therefore serve as a valuable way to help them leverage their skills and strategies more effectively. This study explores the effect of providing young students (grades 4 and 5) with paper-based problem-solving aids designed to prompt students to focus on one probl em-solving step at a time, and to guide the processes of problem understanding, idea generation and formalization – understood to be the key computational problem-solving processes (Romero et al., 2017). While non-digital activities are common in elementary Computational Thinking education (Huang & Looi, 2021), they typically target the teaching of CT concepts and programming skills rather than specifically supporting problem-solving strategies.

The study involved a two-step analysis process. Step 1 examined differences in CT learning between children who worked with an ER, tablet and the problem-solving aids, and children who worked in a learning setting that is characteristic of current educational practices, namely a setting that includes an ER and a tablet, and occasional teacher support. Step 2 involved the analysis of problem-solving strategies in the two settings that may explain differences in CT learning. As such, this study addressed the following two research questions: RQ1:  How does CT learning differ between students working with ERs and tablets and with occasional teacher support, and students working with ERs, tablets, and paper-based problem-solving aids? RQ2:  How do children’s problem-solving strategies differ between these two learning conditions, particularly in terms of transitions between problem-solving steps and debugging strategies? 121 Computational problem-solving Computational Thinking shares higher-level steps with other problem-solving approaches (Romero et al., 2017) but involves processes specific to computational problems. Loksa et al. (2022) defined computational problemsolving as the “process in which mental representations of problems and solutions are refined then translated and expressed as code” (pg. 18). This definition highlights the importance of developing abstract representations of the problem and translating solution ideas using the formalisms associated with computer science (Wing, 2010). The design of the aids used in this study w as broadly grounded in this definition, incorporating graphical representations and tasks designed to help children in developing abstract representations, proposing ideas, and translating and formalizing them to prepare for programming. Problem understanding and the code are closely intertwined, as the constraints and opportunities of the programming language influence how the problem is represented (encoded). Thus, children need to be supported in systematically carrying out key problem-solving steps to understand a problem and gradually translate that understanding into executable code. This presents a unique challenge for design and evaluation. General problem-solving processes such as problem understanding, solution proposing and evaluation can be supported by scaffolds, (Tikva & Tambouris, 2021; Wang et al., 2024) but in computational contexts these processes take on specific forms that require targeted support. A relevant formulation of these specific processes is the Creative Computational Problem Solving (CCPS) model (Chevalier et al., 2020), which guided the design of this study’s scaffolds. The model distinguishes five stages of computational problem-solving: (1) Problem Understanding (UND), (2) Idea Generation (IDEA), (3) Behavior Formulation (FORM), (4) Programming (PROG), and (5) Evaluation. During Problem Understanding, the problem-solver identifies – through decomposition and abstraction – the problem. During Idea Generation, envisioned robot behaviors are sketched through drawings or expressed in natural language grounded in problem understanding. Behavior Formulation involves concretely describing behavior, considering the physical constraints of the environment — in this study, mazes — and the capabilities of the robot (e.g., sensing color or distance). Envisioned behavior is expressed as an algorithm in pseudocode (simplified codes for algorithm design) or in natural language. At the Programming (implementation) stage, the program is created by translating the algorithm into executable code using the elements of the programming language. Lastly, at the Evaluation stage, the code is executed, and the robot’s behavior is observed and compared with expected behavior. The solution is either found appropriate or must be corrected or redefined. Details of the problem-solving aids designed for this study are presented below. The decision to use a paper format was intentional; they are low-cost, accessible, and easily integrated into existing classroom routines without requiring additional technological infrastructure. This format also encourages collaboration—its  large physical footprint allows all team members to simultaneously add notes, draw diagrams, and highlight key information in ways that are often constrained by the limited screen size of electronic devices. We were particularly interested in exploring how such a design treatment could be employed in large classroom settings where individual scaffolding is in constant demand and teachers have limited time to attend to all. Supporting problem-solving in CT learning contexts It is widely recognized that ERs do not support effective and systematic  problem-solving as they emphasize implementation (programming) and evaluation (observing robot behavior) over other critical steps such as problem understanding and idea generation (Shute et al., 2017; Chevalier et al., 2020, 2022). In classroom contexts, teachers can provide the necessary support (De Wever et al., 2021) but large class sizes limit opportunities for interactions  (Buss & Gamboa, 2017; Sadik et al., 2017). Digital or non-digital scaffolds are known to provide support by simplifying complex processes, and foster reflection and metacognition  (Azevedo & Hadwin, 2005).

Research on supporting computational problem-solving in middle and high schools has identified several forms of support, including question prompts (Chetty & vander Westhuizen, 2014), feedback (Beck et al., 2018), and graphical representations of the problem-solving process (Loksa et al., 2016). Loksa et al. (2016) used paperbased representations of problem-solving steps in a youth programming camp, enabling students to track and reflect on their problem-solving progress. Numerous studies have shown that scaffolds generally improve CT and programming learning outcomes (Tikva & Tambouris, 2021; Shin et al., 2025). However, only a few studies have focused on supporting younger learners’ computational problem-solving. Angeli and Valanides (2020) developed two scaffolding strategies for 5–6-year-olds, using cards to visualize and manipulate objects, and verbally articulating plans before programming. Both were found to be effective. Atmatzidou et al. (2018) examined two  levels of guidance (minimal vs. strong), finding that strong guidance led to more effective problem-solving strategies. 122 Scaffold designs often target specific problem-solving processes. For example, Kwon (2010) developed scaffolds to support problem understanding, recognizing that inadequate understanding can lead to coding that fail to align with task requirements (Denny et al., 2019). Other scaffolds  focus on translating mental representations into code. Loksa et al. (2016), for instance, integrated activities that prompted students to simulate potential code, sketch solutions, and prototype before implementation. Broadly, the literature highlights the benefits of prompting students to step back from programming to engage in reflection and planning. Non-digital learning aids can play a crucial role in this context by fostering self-directed learning and enabling learners to review earlier problem-solving steps (Belland, 2014). Children’s problem-solving strategies The prevalence of engaging in ineffective strategies in children’s computational problem-solving has been documented in prior research (Lin & Liu, 2012; Weintrop et al., 2016) but there is yet little work describing and evaluating their problem-solving strategies in detail (for notable work, see Chevalier et al., 2020, 2022). Norton et al. (2007) studied young learner’s dialogue and actions in an ER CT learning context, characterizing their problem-solving strategies as more or less “methodical” (Norton et al., 2007, pg. 272) depending on whether they identified and solved subproblems, and whether they engaged systematically in all necessary steps (problem understanding, proposing solutions, etc.) before programming. Other work has emphasized that effective problemsolving proceeds iteratively from problem understanding to implementation, but that it also benefits from cycling back to earlier stages (e.g., from programming to problem understanding), as this helps children to grasp the link between problem understanding and the code (Kopcha & Ocak, 2023). Broadly, similar to problem-solving in other domains, effective problem-solving strategies are characterized by the structured engagement in essential problem-solving steps, which typically proceed iteratively from one step to the next and include returns to earlier steps when an implementation does not solve the problem.

## Participants

Participants were 47 students from a rural Midwestern U.S. elementary school: 28 fourth graders and 19 fifth graders. A demographic questionnaire included questions about their experiences with robots and programming. Age ranged from 9 to 12 years (M = 10.15, SD =.78). Twenty students identified as female, 27 as male. Most students identified as White (n = 28), followed by Hispanic (n = 11), Asian (n = 3), Black (n = 3), and Other (n = 2). Results showed a few students had a toy robot at home, but none had programming experience.

## Procedure

The study was conducted in scheduled science classes spanning 7 days of activities, for 40 minutes per day. On Day 1 students completed a demographic questionnaire and were assessed on CT competencies using paper-based tests. This was followed by two days of training how to program the robot. Then, over three days, they worked on the problem-solving scenarios, one scenario per day. The study concluded with a post-test on the final day (Day 7).

Students were randomly assigned to teams of two. One team had 3 members. The 23 student teams were assigned randomly to one or the other condition and worked in different classrooms. Twelve teams were assigned to the Control Condition (henceforth CC) - students working mostly on their own with occasional teacher support; and 11 teams to the Treatment Condition (henceforth TC) – students working with the paper-based handouts. Assessing CT learning Assessment of learning CT focused on CT practices, the dimension most closely related to problem-solving (Brennan and Resnick, 2012). They include abstracting and modularizing, reusing and remixing, algorithmic thinking, and testing and debugging (Basu et al., 2021). For the present study, items from two validated CT tests were selected: from the Interactive Assessment of Computational Thinking (IACT) developed by TERC for grades 3 to 8 (Rowe et al., 2021) and from the CoolThink CT Practice Assessment developed by SRI for 4th to 6th graders (Basu et al., 2021). Scoring was the same as in the original test items. The maximum possible points w ere 28 for both pretest and posttest.

Collecting and coding problem-solving process data Data on problem-solving steps were collected via in-class observations and audio-video recordings of the learning sessions. Twelve of 23 student teams (6 per condition) were randomly selected and followed across three problemsolving days (Days 4–6). Three cameras recorded CC classrooms and four recorded TC classrooms. In a passive observer stance three researchers observed CC teams, and two observed TC teams, following the same team across all days. Does CT learning differ in the conditions?

To answer RQ1, a two-way ANCOV A was conducted. It included the pre-test scores as a covariate, and grade as a factor to ensure that observed effects were not confounded by initial differences between grade levels. The and CC groups, respectively. The difference is significant at the 0.01 level (F(1,42) = 9.86, p = 0.003), with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.92). Cohen’s d was calculated for the pairwise comparison as it involved a single degree of freedom. The main effect of condition was of moderate size (η2 = 0.099) representing the proportion of variance explained by a factor after accounting for the covariate. This measure is appropriate in ANCOV A models (Lakens, 2013).

The main effect of grade level was not significant (F(1,42) = 0.152,  p =.699) indicating no significant difference in post-test scores between grades 4 and 5. The covariate (pretest scores) was a significant predictor of posttest performance, F(1,42) = 46.78, p <.001, i.e., higher pretest scores were associated with higher posttest scores. All assumptions of ANCOVA were tested and met. The homogeneity of regression slopes assumption was satisfied, confirmed by the non-significant interaction between condition and pretest scores, F(1,42) = 2.64, p =.112.

How do problem-solving strategies differ in the conditions? Are there differences in the transition probabilities of action pairs in the two conditions? To identify potential reasons of the differences in CT learning, problem-solving strategies were identified in terms of the transition probabilities of action pairs. It was hypothesized that the paper-based aids would structure problem-solving because they provided external anchors for the key problem-solving steps, prompting reflection and revision (Belland, 2014). Means and standard deviation of transition probabilities of all action pairs by condition are shown in Table 2. Table 3 summarizes the action pairs that showed statistically significant differences between conditions, highlighting where problem-solving strategies diverged. The r esults largely confirm the expected impact of the aids. TC students transitioned from understanding to idea generation, to formalization, then to programming (implementation), while CC students tended to quickly move from problem understanding to programming. TC students transitioned at a significantly higher rate from UND to IDEA than CC students (CC: 0.08, TC: 0.27; p =.002; action pair 1 in Table 3). CC students frequently transitioned  directly to programming (UND – PROG; CC: 0.69, TC: 0.31; p =.016; action pair 2). At the IDEA step, TC students  were more likely to translate the envisioned robot path (often drawn on the paper sheet) into formalisms aligning with the programming language compared to CC students (IDEA – FORM; CC: 0.16, TC: 0.67, p = 0.002, action pair 3), who tended to moved directly from envisioning the robot’s path to programming (IDEA – PROG; CC: 0.77, TC: 0.16; p <.001, action pair 4). In the TC groups, this meant sketching the algorithm by hand; CC students had to accomplish formalization verbally or by browsing through the lists of available programming elements on the tablet. This likely prompted the students to directly move to programming with little reflection on how to structure the algorithm.

CC students more frequently tested their code immediately after writing it compared to TC students who during coding tended also to cycle back to checking, revising or refreshing their understanding (PROG – UND; action pair 5 in Table 3), and checking their written sketches of an algorithm (PROG – FORM, action pair 6). The difference between conditions in the PROG – ERR transition probability  (CC: 0.7, TC: 0.38) was statistically significant (p <.001; action pair 7). It appears that instead of engaging in frequent programming – evaluation cycles, TC students made an effort to understand how the problem, the sketched solution, and the code were related. By frequently engaging in these back-and-forth processes, they gradually translated developing abstract representations of the problems and solutions into code (Loksa et al., 2022).

127 How do debugging strategies compare across conditions?

As expected, in both conditions programming (PROG) and evaluation (ERR, PSu) were the most frequent actions, though their frequency differed (TC: 47.8%, CC: 74.9%, t(29.62) = -10.957, p <.001). Students tended to control the robot by producing code and observing its outcome. Without aids directing attention to other problem-solving steps, students focused on the tablet and the ER. Prior work shows that restricting access to the programming interface can reduce the programming–evaluation cycle (Chevalier et al., 2022). In this study, evaluation refers to observing the robot failing to meet a subgoal (ERR, e.g., turning right at a yellow box), and debugging is defined as the actions following an ERR. The analysis examines which actions students tend to engage in after an ERR. The problem-solving aids appeared to be effective in prompting a debugging strategy that seeks to identify the sources of an error, notably also errors outside the code. When the robot did not behave as expected TC students were more likely to transition back to checking and working on the algorithms handwritten on the ‘Programming Elements’ handout (ERR – FORM; CC: 0.11, TC: 0.41; p < 0.001; action pair 8 in Table 3). They are also more likely to return to check their problem understanding (ERR – UND; CC: 0.03, TC: 0.19; p = 0.012; action pair 9). CC Students were more likely to return to programming (ERR – PROG; CC: 0.73, TC: 0.36; p < 0.001; action pair 10) or amend or produce a new idea (ERR – IDEA; CC: 0.11, TC: 0.02; p < 0.001; action pair 11). Errors in code can be traced back to all problem-solving steps, including problem understanding, and – generally – proficiency in debugging is linked to inspecting the output of all prior problem-solving steps (Fitzgerald, et al., 2008). Novice programmers are known to focus primarily on the code during debugging, checking it line-by-line rather than returning to previous problem-solving stages (Kolikant & Mussai, 2008). Young learners tend to remove code, produce new code or carry out “quick fixes” which are considered unproductive trial-and-error strategies (Misirli & Komis, 2023). This tendency is inhibited when CT learning designs include activities with non-digital artifacts (Zhang et al., 2025). This study shows  that integrating paper-based activities with ERs significantly enhances the learning of computational thinking (CT) in elementary schools. Providing tangible, collaborative media for students to express CT processes through familiar forms such as writing and drawing encouraged a more systematic problemsolving. Notably, students using paper-based handouts more frequently revisited and revised earlier steps (e.g., problem understanding), an indicator of effective problem-solving (Kopcha & Ocak, 2023). They also exhibited fewer programming–testing–programming loops, often seen as an unproductive trial-and-error strategy (Shute et al., 2017). Unlike other scaffold s designed to support domain-independent problem-solving steps (Wang et al., 2024), this study relied on a CT-specific model (Romero et al., 2017; Chevalier et al., 2020). The process of abstraction was encouraged  through analysis, drawing, and writing, and as was the process of behavior formalization via programming language elements.  Broadly, the study results encourage  scaffold designs grounded in models of computational problem-solving.

The paper-based aids also allowed more opportunities for collaboration (Wei et al., 2021). On the large drawing and writing paper, all team members could contribute equally and simultaneously, while in the control condition access to the shared tool (the tablet) had to be negotiated. In addition, in this condition students had to maintain shared understandings by verbalizing their reasoning, while in the treatment condition, students could check their drawings and writings (Suthers & Hundhausen, 2003). In the control condition, code was often rewritten or deleted, removing opportunities to review the outcome of prior del iberations. Shared, inspectable representations may also have prompted more productive debugging strategies (Kapur, 2016).

The study has some limitations, including that it was conducted in one school. But a total of 1806 distinct actions were analyzed, and this may be sufficient for its purpose, i.e., to examine the impact of the problemsolving aids. To further verify their impact, a larger and more diverse student population would need to be considered. A limitation is also that the treatment combined an open drawing sheet and a structured handout, so the effects cannot be attributed to either component individually. Longer-term, this study is intended to inform the design of effective scaffolds in classrooms where a teacher is also present to provide guidance. Paper-based aids led themselves well to this purpose due to better visual and physical access (e.g., pointing). Future work will need to investigate how teachers would adapt the support they give when scaffolds are used as well, and whether teachers perceive them as adding value to their teaching.
