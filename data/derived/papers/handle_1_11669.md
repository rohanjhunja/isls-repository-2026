# Teacher Candidates Co-Constructing Anti-Deficit Narratives about Learners in Video Club

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We extend prior work to elucidate ideological sensemaking as an interactional process that supports teachers’ anti-deficit noticing in Video Clubs. Our framework positions noticing as a political act, shaped by the frames (Louie et al., 2021) teachers and facilitators use to contextualize learners’ activity. Through understanding framing in relation to ideological sensemaking, we show how individual stances contribute to teachers and facilitators’ co-development of anti-deficit narratives. We demonstrate this by analyzing a Video Club of undergraduate teacher candidates. We highlight patterns (i.e., attending to learners’ gesture, positioning learners to listen and learn with and from each other, recognizing the value of silly contributions) that resisted dominant ideologies and positioned learners’ activity within transformative visions of science teaching and learning. We contextualize these findings within prior professional learning sequences to inform future Video Club designs.

## Introduction

Video analysis provides opportunities to pause classroom action, allowing for observ ing, interpret ing, and planning responses with peers and facilitators (Grossman et al., 2009). These pauses for analysis can allow teachers to make connections to principles of anti-deficit work and engage in shared sensemaking to develop antideficit narratives about learners (Louie, 2020; Louie et al., 2021). By anti-deficit, we mean forms of engagement that “deliberately challenge deficit discourses, intentionally attending to and elevating the humanity, intelligence, and [disciplinary]…abilities of marginalized people” (Louie et al., 2021, p. 100). In this paper, we explore Video Club (Sherin & Han, 2004) as one form of video anal ysis, a space for teachers to engage in sensemaking  that actively works to resist dominant ideolog ies that shape common deficit narratives about all learners, but particularly those from marginalized communities. P rior work has illustrated through retrospective not icing interviews (Sherin et al. 2011) forms of noticing that might b e considered anti-deficit (Louie et al., 2021). In this paper, we set out to better understand the interactional processes through which anti-deficit noticing and narrative construction might occur (e.g., Daniel et al., 2023). We ask: what does anti-deficit noticing look like in interaction? Based on analysis of a teacher candidate (TC) video  club (VC), we claim ideological sensemaking describes the interactional processes of anti-deficit noticing of learners’ contributions and capabilities. Framework: Ideologies, ideological sensemaking, and framing We begin with the assumption that children make sen se. Moreover, children are brilliant in their creat ivity, their unique perspectives on the world, and the ways they engage in and transform cultural practice. For instance, Keifert & Stevens (2019) described the ways that yo ung children (ages 2-8 years) recruited others to engage in inquiry to examine phenomena they were curious abou t (e.g., bees, imagined biology, fog), and Keifert (2 021) documented how those same young children drew on and creatively innovated family practices such as imagining standing in a pool of water or eating a booger towa rds new goals like pranking a sibling with a preten d booger made of glue. Yet, d ominant ideologies about children stand in stark co ntrast to this assumption  that children make sense.

Ideologies are socially constructed ideas that help  people make sense of society and how society works (Hall, 1996). Ideologies are  neither good nor bad, but are tools for understanding the world  (Louie, 2020). They show up through narratives that humans use to  interpret their experiences. Ideologies that reproduce societal inequity we call dominant ideologies (Daniel et al., 2023), while those that disrupt inequity and assumed “commonsense” narratives towards more equitable futures we call transformative ideologies. Dominant ideologies are driven by systemic adherence to idea s of whiteness that deeply impact how we see all children, inflicting unique harm on children from marginalized communities. These ideological hierarchies are preserved through the retelling of deficit narratives.

The assumption that children are brilliant informs a transformative ideology that serves to disrupt dominant, harmful ideologies. Children’s brilliance is shaped by their experience  participating in and creatively transforming cultural practice within families and broader cultural communities of practice (Gutiérrez & Rogoff, 2003; Keifert, 2021; Rogoff, 2003).  Thus, spaces that cultivate children’s brilliance m ust take up heterogeneityseeking approaches that value multiple perspectives  of phenomena and multiple ways of knowing (e.g., P ierson et al., 2023; Warren et al., 2020). For instance, Warren et al. (2020) described Jonathan, an African American 7th grader, who shared his perspective on living and non-living entities and was shut down because he was perceived as problematic. Jonathan’s story emphasizes the potential benefit s of understanding the sun as both non-living (the classic Western Science classification of the sun as an independent entity) and part of living sy stems (a relational view that reco gnizes the sun as a part of most living systems on Earth). Both views have value and provide important perspectives on the ideas of livi ng/non-living entities. Cultivating heterogeneous ways of knowing and being requires that researchers and edu cators counter deficit norms that assume children a re lessthan, not-yet-human, incompetent social practitioners, not-yet-capable of science, and troublemakers so that researchers and educators (adults) can learn with a nd from children  like Jonathan. It is therefore critical for teachers to develop anti-deficit noticing practices.

The existing t eacher noticing literature provides an articulation  of noticing as attending to student thinking, interpreting that thinking, and planning/responding to student thinking in ways that value students’ ideas as productive for sensemaking. Noticing, like ideol ogies, is not good or bad in and of itself, but rat her is shaped by the assumptions that inform the practice. Louie et al., (2021) have called this  shaping of assumptions “framing”—an “interactive process of co-constructing a particular frame  and coordinating activities around it ” (Louie et al., 2021, p. 97). Louie et al. emphasize how framing is not a neutral act, but one  tied to ideologies; the act of noticing is always political, either aligned with dominant ideologies that reinscribe bi ases against children or working to disrupt dominant ideologies towards tran sformative futures.  This prior work was based on retrospective noticing interviews (Louie et al., 2021; see also Sherin et al., 2011). We recognize a need to see the work of anti-deficit noticing in action.

In the present manuscript, we focus on framing as a political act within noticing. We argue that framing (Louie et al., 2021)  is accomplished within ideological sensemaking  in professional learning sequences that include VCs. Ideological sensemaking is an interactional process  of co-developing narratives about experience and events through taking and building on stances o r verbalized utterances demonstrati ng sensemaking; coconstructed narratives can reify dominant and/or transformative ideologies in the interpretation of experience and events (Daniel et al., 2023; Philip et al., 2018; Philip, 2011). Ideological sensemaking, including reframing, is necessary within U.S. K-12 school contexts because of the systemic impact o f dominant ideologies and related endemic presence of deficit narratives. Even for th ose educators and researchers who embrace transform ative ideologies, the endemic nature of deficit narrative s demands a constant process of reflection and resi stance. We recognize this tension in how we talk about  the active processes of sensemaking. We write (re)framing to acknowledge that in the present analysis, we have n ot traced histories of participation to make claims about whether someone is framing (for the first time) or reframing prior ideas. We also write (re)framing to acknowledge that at some times participants can take up anti-deficit noticing with ease, and at other times participants engage in reflective process through ideological sensemaki ng to arrive at anti-deficit noticing. In this context, we  ask: What does anti-deficit noticing look like in Video Club interactions?

## Methods

The present manuscript draws on data from a semeste r-long science methods course. The Equitable Science Sensemaking (ESSe) project grew out of our prior wo rk with in-service elementary teachers (e.g., Pierson et al., 2021; Pierson et al., 2023; Pierson et al., 2024; Keifert et al., under review). Through ESSe, we wanted to explore how to support TCs to engage in ideological sensemaking and anti-deficit noticing of young learners’ capabilities and contributions. Data collection included VCs in the first year of the project. VCs lasted roughly one hour and included one or two TCs presenting video of their practice. Prior to VC gatherings, presenting TCs uploaded their video clips to a shared platform. Other members of the VC then viewed and commented on the clips. During VCs, facilitators supported TCs to play the “Stop Game” a practice of watching the clip all the way through, asking clarifying questions, and then watching through aga in with TCs and/or facilitators calling ‘stop’ to indicate they wanted to pause and discuss student thinking. The VC  drew on their shared history of professional learni ng together, including immersive learning experiences (e.g., Loucks-Horsley et al., 2009; Windschitl et al., 2019), shared readings to critically reflect on ideologies and narrative s of learner s, opportuni ties to  develop heterogeneity-seeking approaches to science teaching and learning  (Pierson et al., 2024), and collaborative planning (Ball & Cohen, 1999; Loucks-Horsley et al., 2009). Three TCs participated in the focal VC during which one, Boophis, shared a clip of her teaching a sample lesson. Data from assignments across the semester and inter views provided context for how each of these TCs self-identified. Boophis identified as White, Scout iden tified as Black, and Jamie as a Spanish/English bil ingual learner who grew up in a Latinoneighborhood. Facil itator Ashlyn, a white former teacher and current science teacher educator and researcher,  had prior experienc e facilitating both pre-and in-service teacher VC and had taught in the same district where these students wo uld be placed for student teaching. Ashlyn was a vi siting facilitator, not their science methods course instructor (Andrea, author 5, was their instructor). Teo, author 1, reviewed reflections provided by VC facilitators to look for instances of teachers’ first VC presentations in which they (re)framed learners’ ac tivity in ways that resisted dominant ideologies. Teo also looked for a video of TCs taking multiple turns in a row (rather than facilitators dominating conversation). Having selected a focal episode, Teo and Heather  followed Jordan & Henderson’s (1995) guide for inte raction analysis including developing preliminary transcripts, sharing clips with ESSe team and an Interaction Analysis Lab to support for collaborative sensemaking. Teo and Heather then elaborated the transcripts based on a modified form of Jefferson transcription (Jefferson, 2004). We examined sequences turn-by-turn to understand the stances taken by individuals and considered those stances in rela tion to each other and an understanding of ideologi es in interaction (Daniel et al., 2023). This work highlighted sequences of ideological sens emaking, individual turns and their framings, and the work to articulate anti-deficit narratives. We drew on the following ideas (Table 1). ## Discussion

Prior research has demonstrated how Video Clubs can make space for teacher noticing (Kang & van Es, 2018; Sherin & Han, 2004; Sherin & van Es, 2009; van Es, 2012) and has outlined principles towards equitable noticing (Chan et al., 2021; Tekkumru-Kisa et al., 2018; Rosebery et al., 2016; van Es et  al., 2017)). Louie et al. (2021) demonstrated that prior VC participation led to anti-deficit noticing through retrospective interview. We set out to understand what anti-deficit noticing looked like in interaction. We have shown VC s can support developing anti-deficit noticing through ideological sensemaking. We showed that TCs, together with facilitators engaged in ideological sensemaking that supported articulating the capabilities and contributions of learners in powerful, antideficit ways. In our discussion and implications, we summarize evidence in support of our claim, describe patterns of anti-deficit noticing, and articulate the relationship between (re)framing and ideological sensemaking. Co-constructing anti-deficit narratives We demonstrated that TCs  engaged in ideological sensemaking to (re)frame learners’ capabilities and contributions within anti-deficit narratives in ways that resist ed dominant ideologies when they were supported by explicit anti-deficit prompting from facilitators and extended sequences of professional learning. For instance, Ashlyn’s prompt to consider alternate experiences t o the guitar led Boophis to recognize the boom as not merely silly (as she originally viewed it in the moment of the lesson) but a s a potentially central resource to learners’ sensemaking about sound waves (for further work on faciliators’ roles, see Pierson et al., this volume and Pierson et al., under review). Ongoing ideological sensemaking  supported TCs to (re)frame learners within anti-deficit narratives that valued learners’ contributions, rather than assuming these ideas needed to be transformed to be of scientific value.

One important pattern of anti-deficit noticing was the TCs describing, enacting, and (re)framing learners’ gestures as valuable sensemaking resources. This is  a particular affordance of VCs as teachers can watch, pause, and rewatch video to examine and replicate ephemera l gestures. TCs noticed how students used multiple representations to make their thinking visible and coordinated these representations to deepen understanding and make connections (e.g., Pierson et al., 2021; Piers on et al., 2023). The VC discussion made space for TCs to reflect on these gestures and the multimodality of learners’ conversations as resources for classroom science learning. By naming and mimicking learners’ gestures, TCs were, as Jamie noted, drawing on Bang et al.’s (2017) first principle to “notice sensemaking repertoires”  and the second principle to “support sensemaking” by using children’s repertoires as tools for science practice (p. 39). Attending to the diversity of multimodal forms in which children make sense of the world is critical to rec ognize their sensemaking (Ochs, 1979). Too often children are positioned as not capable in part because researche rs and educator s are not prepared to attend to the sense that children are making (e.g., Keifert & Stevens, 2019) or learning designs do not center their resources (e.g., Keifert et al., 2020). Through VC, TCs had opportunities to examine the importance of reco gnizing and mimicking students’ contributions.

Another important pattern of anti-deficit noticing focused on articulating the dynamic relational interactions as students had opportunities to listen and learn from each other, rather than assuming only teachers have valuable ideas, experience, and sensemaking pr actice. Boophis’ (re)framing of the boom from silly to valuable implicitly recognized that students’ contr ibutions were valuable to each other  as an act of diffractive noticing (Jeong & Steele, 2023) that emphasized the value of multiple perspectiv es and experiences for collaborative scientific sensemaking about phenomen a (Pierson et al., 2023). Rather than assume that a student contribution was merely fodder for the teacher to rearticulate towards “correct” science explanations, Boophis emphasized that the boom, as a shared experience, would be “more relatable to students” to describe the ephemeral phenomenon of sound waves, rather than an imagined guitar with  which some students may not have had direct experience. Once again, student contributions were seen as valuable for other students  in the collective work of collaborative sensemaking in science.

This positioning of student contributions as valuab le for other students imagines science inquiry in classrooms as a collaborative affair. These framings of students’ inquiry actively  employ a transformative ideological understanding of children’s capabilitie s that challenge and resist traditional dominant ideologies that take up fixed representations of Western Science and scientific inquiry and position young children as not capable of scientific practice beyond observation, descript ion, and categorization. This transformative view s erved to highlight the continuous processes of becoming within educational settings, allowing for a multiplicity of viable pathways in learning and sensemaking. TCs affirmed learners’ full dignity (see Espinoza et al., 2020)  and acknowledged through a diffractive lens the value of ethical interactions in classroom lear ning (Jeong & Steele, 2023).

Video Clubs, (re)framing, and ideological sensemaking Through our analysis and discussion, we are articulating for ourselves and our readers  the ways that VCs served as a context for ideological sensemaking (Daniel et  al., 2023)—an interactive process of developing narratives about experience and events (Philip et al., 2018). As we engaged in line-by-line analysis of VC sensemaking and through our examination of an excerpt of the episode focused on stances and counter-stances, we illustrated the collaborative and public ways that Boophis, Jamie, Scout, and Ashlyn co-created narratives about what learners are capable of and the brilliant contributions students made to support their own and each other’s learning. VCs allowed TCs to pause and step outside of the flow of teaching t o reflect on their own practice and the contributions of the learners in their classroom similar to van Es et al.’s (2017) findings that pausing video to discuss moment-to-moment interactions supported equitable noticing. Moreover, as a social activity broadly, VCs afford space for ideological sensemaking in interaction. In our analysis, this social component meant that w hen TCs were doing the delicate work of (re)framing towards  anti-deficit noticing, they had the opportunity to collaboratively think with peers and facilitators who introduced and modeled anti-deficit stances during VC and the extended sequences of professional learning tha t preceded these VCs. Collaborative (re)framing  supported TCs to resist dominant ideologies and work towards transformative futures for themselves and their students. We wish to articulate explicitly that (re)framing occurred during ideological sensemaking, an active process that is possible when teachers have prior e xperience with anti-deficit narratives and images of science learning that draw on transformative ideologies. (Re)framing requires reflection on dominant and transformative ideologies and their associated narratives and assu mptions. For instance, Ashlyn responded to Boophis (re)framing a learners’ contribution from silly to central emphasizing the challenge of that work: “That’s so tough though in moment, taking everything that students’ say seriously even if it seems a little bit silly.”  Ashlyn recognized the assumption that silly means not seri ous and not  valuable, naming Boophis’ (re)framing as disruptive to that assumption. She then emphasized that in making this disruption, Boophis helped students know “that whatever they throw at you you’re gonna be li ke ‘Ok, tell me more’.hhh ‘Ah, like how are you co nnecting this?’…you’re both validating uhm, and asking them to make  connections that might be challenging for them.” Ashlyn’s feedback recognized the impact of Boophis’  disruption in saying how students will be positive ly impacted (making connections, taking what they see seriously, challenging them). This sequence demonstrat es how ideological sensemaking supported disruption of  dominant ideologies, (re)framing learners through antideficit narratives, and imagining transformative futures.

## Conclusion

Prior research demonstrated that Video Clubs make s pace for teacher noticing (Kang & van Es, 2018; She rin & Han, 2004; Sherin & van Es, 2009; van Es, 2012) and has outlined principles towards equitable noticing (Chan et al., 2021; Tekkumru-Kisa et al., 2018; Rosebery et al., 2016; van Es et al., 2017). Louie et al. (2021) demonstrated that prior VC participation led to anti-deficit noticing through a retrospective interview with a teacher. Extending this, we illustrate d interactional ways that Video Club support ed anti-deficit noticing through ideological sensemaking (Philip et al., 2018; Daniel et al., 20 23). We elucidated these interactional processes fo r the co-construction of anti-deficit narratives in teacher candidates’  learning. Teachers co-constructed anti-deficit narratives that resisted dominant, harmful ideologies about learners by naming and taking up children’s gestures, articulating the importance of children learning fr om each other, and (re)framing learners’ co ntributions as valuable even when they appear to be silly.
