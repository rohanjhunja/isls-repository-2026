# The Paradigm Pivot: The Role of Community Cultural Wealth in Shifting Pedagogical Practices

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores the adaptation of a Community Cultural Wealth (CCW) pedagogical framework from mathematics to English Language Arts (ELA) education, focusing on secondary schools in the U.S. Utilizing a qualitative case study, four high school ELA teachers participated in workshops to integrate CCW principles into their teaching. The study probes teacher self-reports to investigate the translation of mathematics-derived CCW pedagogical practices into ELA contexts and their impact on teachers' perceptions and methods. Findings indicate a pedagogical shift from teacher-centered to student and community-directed approaches, emphasizing collaborative learning and leveraging students' cultural capital. This shift suggests CCW's potential in fostering asset-rich learning environments across diverse academic disciplines. Opportunities for further empirical research on CCW pedagogies in K-12 are discussed.

## Introduction and background

Classrooms across the globe increasingly serve ling uistically and ethnically diverse student populatio ns, yet teacher demographics often remain composed of ethni c groups who retain political and economic power in  their respective countries (Alan et al., 2023). Research highlights that in classrooms where teachers are fr om the dominant culture, and students consist of those fro m minoritized backgrounds, teachers often hold defi cit views of their students (Thijs et al., 2012). Such defici t-based views lead to  lowered expectations, reduced academic achievement, a decrease in student motivation, and increased disciplinary referrals (Kolluri & Tichavakunda, 2023). Asset-based pedagogies are presented as a countermeasure to such deficit-based narratives (López, 2017). However, research highlights the challenges of impl ementing asset-based pedagogies within classrooms due to non-recognition of students’ strengths and lack of educ ator competency in translating theory to practice (Brown et al., 2022). Community Cultural Wealth (CCW) is one asset-based conceptual framework that critiques deficit thinking, and we assert that CCW can serve as a ped agogical tool to support the implementation of asse t-based pedagogies in instructional contexts across academic subject areas. Community cultural wealth as asset-based pedagogy Yosso (2005) posited that marginalized communities have knowledges and skills that are not widely reco gnized in research or utilized in practice called community cultural wealth. There are six forms of capital recognized within Yosso’s conceptual framework. Aspirational c apital is the ability to maintain high aspirations despite challenging circumstances. Navigational capital rep resents the skills necessary to navigate social ins titutions, particularly those that perpetually disadvantage mi norities. Social capital refers to the networks of people who can support and guide minority students. Resistant capital entails the ability of minorities to challe nge negative stereotypes through the ownership of counter identi ties. Familial capital includes knowledge acquired through kinship networks, including emotional and moral learning. Finally, linguistic capital encompasses the multilingual communication skills extant in communities of color. The framework advances that all Students of Color  have cultural wealth and  that poor student outcomes occur when educational s ystems fail to recognize and access student assets. Recognizing community cultural weal th in the context of ethnically and linguistically diverse schooling provides an empowering foundation in whic h to an alyze the academic experiences of minoritized students.

Community cultural wealth is an interdisciplinary framework that has mostly been analyzed within U.S. educational contexts that serve marginalized commun ities (for a recent review, see Denton et al., 2020). Within traditional schooling contexts, CCW has often been explored in the postsecondary reflections of Black and Latine students (Ortiz et al., 2019). Much of this analysi s on community cultural wealth of students of color  in higher education highlights the role of CCW in STEM access and persistence (Samuelson & Litzler, 2016). Compared to higher education contexts, community cu ltural wealth has been explored to a lesser extent in K-12 spaces. Studies that do analyze CCW in K-12 tend to focus either on non-instructional areas such as critical mentoring relationships (Liou et al.,  2016), theoretical areas such as the emancipatory o pportunities of CCW in STEM classrooms (Ojeda-Ramirez et al., 2023), or focus on single-class case studies (Jimenez, 2020). Although not directly observed, there have also been examinations of student reflections on their classroom spaces from Black, Indigenous, and People of Color high school student s from low-income communities; these studies identified aspirational, familial, and navigational capital as  being especially important to the academic persist ence and achievement of students from the student perspectiv e (Liou et al., 2016; Tolbert Smith et al., 2022). Due to the limited amount of empirical, systematic studies acr oss multiple classrooms in CCW in K-12 spaces, the authors conducted a study to identify a preliminary, comprehensive set of pedagogical practices that are representative of CCW in the classroom.

CCW pedagogical framework development The CCW pedagogical framework emerged from a larger  study examining the impact of instructional coachi ng on middle school mathematics teaching practices using video recordings of classroom instruction (Menzies et al., 2024). Two elementary mathematics classrooms serving ma jority black and brown students emerged as having shown consistent student enthusiasm and participati on in conceptually-rich mathematics. Further theoretical and thematic analysis highlighted how students embodied forms of community cultural wealth activation. Pedagogical practices related to four of the six ty pes of community cultural wealth were regularly observed in this dataset. At the most general level, t he character of the pedagogical practices for these  four types and their interrelationship can be summarized as: teachers cultivated a classroom community (social capital), and this social capital was foundational to their culti vating high expectations (aspirational capital), le veraging storytelling & dialogue (linguistic capital), and c ultivating persistence and leveraging connections (navigational capital). We use the term “leverage” when the pedagogical practices utilized the extant capital of students, and we use the term “cultivated” when pedagogical practices developed and strengthened that form of capital for students. Within this dataset, the majority of the observed C CW pedagogical practices focused on cultivating CCW. Collectively, there were twenty-three different pedagogical practices that were seen as cultivating and leveraging community cultural wealth in the students during the observed mathematics lessons of these two teachers. Within each kind of CCW, the specific pedagogical practice s could be clustered into pedagogical themes (betwe en two and four themes per CCW), with a different function al purpose to each theme. Each pedagogical theme had between one and four unique associated teacher acti ons (see Table 1). Table 1 represents a subset of our larger findings.

## Methods

A qualitative case study design was used in this study to investigate the potential effects of the community cultural wealth pedagogical framework on the pedagogical practices of secondary English Language Arts instructors.

## Participants

We recruited four current high school ELA teachers whose classrooms were at least 30% Black, Latine, Indigenous or English Language Learners. The recrui ted teachers had to be current classroom instructor s, and at least five weeks of regular instruction remaining in the academic year was required (i.e., not testing or other endof-year administrative or make-up work time). Each participant taught in a suburba n school near a major metropolitan area. ## Data collection

To assess the ways in which the CCW pedagogical framework may have more generally influenced the workshop attendees’ pedagogical practices, one-on-one semi-structured interviews were conducted with each teac her. The one-on-one interviews took place between  2-4 weeks after the workshop series ended to allow ti me for teachers to incorporate elements of the CCW pedagogical framework into their classroom practice.

## Data analysis

The first step in the coding process began with analysis of the one-on-one interview transcripts using open coding in Dedoose to develop preliminary, descriptive code s (Lieber et al., 2021; Saldaña, 2016). Next, patte rn coding identified emergent patterns among the descriptive codes, and then systematic ex amination of all teacher responses was conducted to identify potential varia nces in their self-reported pedagogical practices before and after participation in the CCW workshop series. Cod es from systemati c analysis was organized into thematic categories (Saldaña, 2016). Thus, themes represent a comprehensive analysis of pedagogical practices f or the teachers as a single cohort.

## Discussion

Analysis of teacher statements revealed epistemic s hifts in their pedagogical practice after exposure to the theoretical tenets of community cultural wealth and aligned practices ide ntified in the CCW pedagogical framework. Teacher self-reports highlight a transition from a fundamentally  deficit-focused knowledge decomposition and acquisition model that presumes students lack relevant knowledge (Kalinec-Craig et al., 2021) towards an asset-based model that presupposes students have cultural ly-informed knowledges, strengths, and skills that can be leveraged in the classroom (Yosso, 2005). Building upon the CCW pedagogical framework, this study advances the premise that CCW pedagogical pra ctices can provide a conceptual and practical instr uctional framework across mathematics and English Language Arts. This study can also support practitioners in translating theory to practice in relation  to assets-based pedagogy, a transition which research shows i s a critical barrier to implementation (Brown et al., 2022).

Although the sample size limits the generalizability of the findings, the self-reported shifts in pedagogical practice suggests that practical interventions desi gned around community cultural wealth can lead to m eaningful shifts in teacher perspectives and classroom actions. The teachers in this study suggested that implementing CCW pedagogical practices should be a whole-school effort to support systemic shifts that diver ge from predominant school cultures that exhibit discord among staff, p romote individualistic behaviors, and enact disprop ortionate discipline policies which research shows can erode student trust and achievement (Thapa et al., 2013).  Despite what may often feel like herculean systemic challen ges to overcome, these findings point to the possib ility that implementing asset-based pedagogical practices can shift the pedagogical fulcrum fr om deficit-based to asset-based with the introduction of community cultural wealth as a pedagogical model.
