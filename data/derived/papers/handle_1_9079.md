# Multimodal Data Fusion to Track Representational Flexibility of Adolescents With Autism Spectrum Disorder During Virtual Reality-Based Training

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We implemented multimodal data fusion to develop a prediction model tracking representational flexibility (RF) of adolescents with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) in virtual reality (VR)-based training. RF is a key competency related to individuals’ capability to select, coordinate, and create alternative representations inherent to STEM subjects. To promote ASD adolescents’ RF development, research has used VR-based 3D simulation design tasks. However, it remains a challenge to track ASD adolescents’ RF development during VR-based training. Using supervised machine learning, we implemented decision-level data fusion with RF evidence from multiple data sources. To train the model, we used a total of 178 audio-and screen-recorded VR training sessions of eight ASD adolescents. We compared model performance metrics of several candidate classifiers. Methodological details, findings, and implications of multimodal data fusion will be discussed. Keywords: Multimodal data fusion, virtual reality-based training, autism spectrum disorder, representational flexibility

## INTRODUCTION

Adolescents with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) are underrepresented individuals in STEM education. ASD features developmental delays that include cognitive and social skill challenges that cause difficulty in STEM education. ASD learners typically lack representational flexibility (RF), which is a competency related to individuals’ ability to choose and execute various representations inherent to STEM subjects. Researchers stated that RF leads to deeper understandings of multifaceted and complex tasks in STEM education. However, they have difficulty in developing RF. Recent research has introduced virtual reality (VR)-based training to foster ASD adolescents’ RF development. VR enables students to experience various 3D design tasks and these design tasks can foster students’ representation practices. However, it remains an open question regarding how to track ASD adolescent’s RF development during VR-based training. Given that ASD adolescents’ cognitive-affective states are dynamic, it is essential to track their needs to provide ASD adolescents with timely and adaptive learning supports during VR-based training. There is a lack of empirical research tracking ASD adolescents’ multimodal interactions during VR-based training. This study aims at developing a prediction model that tracks ASD adolescents’ RF development using a multimodal data fusion approach. The primary research question is: how does multimodal data fusion predict ASD adolescents’ RF development during VR-based training?

## Literature Review

Representational flexibility in integrated STEM education RF is an individual’s cognitive capability to switch attention and choose different modes of representations for knowledge comprehension (Herbert & Hayne, 2000). RF enables an individual to understand, reproduce, and apply knowledge to adjust problem-solving behaviors and solutions to surrounding circumstances. STEM practices typically require students to perform knowledge synthesis and application through multiple rounds of problem-solving. Enhanced RF enables students to better select and compare various representations aligned with design tasks. Researchers state that ASD adolescents’ low RF explains their difficulty in academic achievement in STEM education (Moon, Ke, & Sokolikj, 2020). It relates to their difficulty in identifying, executing, and evaluating design task features during problem solving. It may subsequently lead to disengagement, as evidenced by ASD individuals’ dynamic cognitive-affective states. Virtual reality-based training for learners with autism ICLS2022 Proceedings 889 © ISLS Virtual reality (VR) has been used to promote cognitive and social skills development of ASD individuals, by fostering ASD learners’ engagement and immersion (Parsons & Cobb, 2011). VR can act as a promising platform for promoting ASD learners’ RF development. VR enables ASD learners to choose and compare various representations as 3D stimuli at a high degree of learner control. Recently, Moon et al. (2020) implemented VR-based cognitive skills training that engaged ASD learners in a variety of virtual engineering design quests (i.e., designing elevation bridges to fulfill different environmental and social contexts). In another study, Ke and Lee (2016) provided ASD learners with VR-based design-problem solving tasks. They examined whether and how the tasks promoted design-oriented interpersonal discussions to develop ASD children’s perspective taking skills and RF. Despite the emerging uses of VR-based interventions for ASD youths, there is a lack of empirical research on VR-based learning assessment. Because VR-based learning interventions are dynamic, it is vital to track students’ real-time behaviors. Considering ASD adolescents’ developmental features, they are likely to experience dynamic cognitive-affective states during VR-based training. Automatic assessment will then help to drive adaptive learning support. Data fusion in education research Research has increasingly proposed the usage of data analytics to track students’ learning. Research demonstrated a variety of computational techniques for the automatic assessment to track behavioral evidence of learning. For example, Moon et al. (2020) developed a prediction model that detects ASD adolescents’ RF development using supervised machine learning. They collected ASD adolescents’ verbal utterances related to RF facets and trained a classification model. Several other studies used computer logs to build a prediction model. However, there is a limitation with the current prediction models tracking learner states. They relied on mostly a single data source. Considering that VR is designed to foster students’ multimodal interactions, predicting students’ learning states from a single data channel appears limited. Research reported the advantage of multimodal learning analytics (MMLA) to track students’ behaviors in highly-interactive digital learning environments (Worsley & Martinez-Maldonado, 2018). MMLA aims at leveraging and predicting student learning by merging and interpreting multiple data inputs with computational techniques (Worsley & MartinezMaldonado, 2018). Among many approaches in MMLA, multimodal data fusion is a method systematically integrating more than two different data sources. Henderson, Rowe, Paquette, Baker, and Lester (2020) conducted multimodal affect detection by collecting and fusing different types of student trace data in gamebased learning for medical training. However, little research explored the feasibility of multimodal data fusion design, integration, and implementation in VR-based training.

## Method

Data We used a total of 178 audio-and screen-recorded 1-1.5 hour VR training sessions of eight ASD adolescents. Study participants participated in VR-based training (Moon et al., 2020) that contains multiple design quests (i.e., elevation bridge design and non-player character design) aimed to promote ASD adolescents’ RF development. We sampled students’ behavior data from two VR-based training modules (Figure 1). In the first module the participants were requested to build a mechanical bridge that allows island residents to move their cargoes without ships. In the second module the participants developed interactive NPCs by editing block-based computer scripts. The participants joined multiple rounds of training sessions and their behaviors were automatically logged in the virtual environment. In each training session, students had to investigate the surrounding natural and social contexts in the virtual scene, decompose design problems, prototype 3D design solutions, and conduct iterative tests. We collected two types of data: (1) video recordings of students’ avatar behaviors during VR-based training, (2) computer logs that recorded students’ training actions. Data Wrangling We carried out multimodal data fusion by blending two different types or modalities of data when predicting the states of RF enactment. Specifically, we used a late fusion approach (Meng, Jing, Yan, & Pedrycz, 2020) that classifies each modality results before fusing the outcome results. A late fusion approach merges the average confidence values of sub-decisions on classification and then determines the final classification result. There are four data-wrangling steps for the multimodal data fusion. Figure 2 portrays the data fusion process implemented in this study. First, we collected two types of data: (1) speeches and (2) behavior logs collected from study participants. Student speech data was their verbal utterances in relation to RF development during VR-based 3D design activities. To collect students’ speech data, we audio-and screen-recorded every training session. Computer logs of students’ behaviors in VR were also automatically recorded. Second, we ICLS2022 Proceedings 890 © ISLS conducted expert labeling to create benchmarking classification results of students’ RF enactment prediction. There are four facets of RF enactment: (1) attention switching/cognitive shifting, (2) alternative representation,

(3) pattern development, and (4) pattern contextualization (Table 1). We used a coding scheme that was

iteratively tested and validated through multiple studies (Moon et al., 2020). The coders independently labeled RF facets, then compared and discussed their results until reaching 100% interrater agreement. Third, we converted students’ speech and behavior data to a dataset for the training of the prediction model. Using a speech-to-text converting application, we transformed students’ entire speeches to text data. For data conversion, we also used the LightSIDE that conducted text vectorization (Mayfield & Rosé, 2013). In terms of behavior logs, we converted students’ behavior data to the frequency of each behavior feature. Based on the expert-labeling results, we conducted separate machine-learning classifiers with the two data sources (i.e., speeches / behavior logs) to compute the estimated probability scores of RF facets. Throughout candidate classifier evaluation, we chose random forest because it showed the best average prediction performance than the other classifiers. Fourth, we arranged the metadata of the collected data sources by time interval during VR training. Students’ entire training session data was divided by the time interval, resulted in a total of 1,441 data instances for model development. We then implemented the classifiers using confidence-based fusion from respective data sources at sub-decision level. We then computed the final prediction results of RF by averaging the confidence scores from the sub-decision classifiers of two data sources. We pre-set a threshold condition as higher than 50%. Last, we evaluated the prediction results between the two respective data sources and the fused one to investigate whether multimodal data fusion improved the prediction performance. We evaluated each classifier’s performance using the metrics as follows: AUC, F1 score, and precision. AUC is a performance metric to check whether a model can accurately distinguish between positive and negative classes. F1 score is a weighted average value of the precision and recall. Precision is the ratio between the number of positive cases correctly classified and the total number of positive cases. We have also tested several methodological features to refine the classifier settings—data imputation and splitting when training and testing a model. In terms of data imputation, we use the algorithm k-nearest neighbor (kNN). The goal of data imputation was to replace missing values of the input data in the prediction models. We set the ratio of training (80%) and testing (20%) data for model training. During the data fusion process, we found a potential imbalanced data issue in the training data set. Data imbalance indicates that data distribution is skewed so it is necessary to deal with imbalanced features through a computational technique. We used a synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE) that performs resampling by creating new data points of minority class. ## Results

The results show that multimodal data fusion improved the overall performance results of the fused classifiers. Specifically, the fused classifier yielded the best performance scores (i.e., AUC, F1 Score, and Precision) in tracking all RF facets. The fused model performance in the NPC design module was generally better than that of the elevation bridge design module. A possible reason of this result is that the training data set in the NPC design module contains more student speech and behavior log data than that in the elevation bridge module. When examining the methodological features of the selected classifiers, we found the optimized number of trees in random forest classifiers (n = 20), pruning condition (confidence level = 30%), and oversampling parameters (the number of neighbors = 20) yielded the best prediction performance.

## Conclusion and Future Research

The result of this study suggests the usefulness of multimodal data fusion in tracking RF during VR-based training. This study finding should inform on the design and implementation of MMLA in immersive learning environments. There are multiple future research directions. First, we will examine the applicability of dimension reduction techniques in developing a prediction model. Although the current prediction model has integrated multimodal data fusion, we did not evaluate which data features are best predictive of different RF facets. Therefore, it is worthwhile to implement dimension reduction to avoid data noises to make a prediction model efficient. Second, the present prediction model of this study only focused on detecting the positive occurrence of RF facets. Future research will build a prediction model that tracks both positive and negative cases of RF in VR-based training.
