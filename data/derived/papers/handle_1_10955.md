# From Prompt to Paper: A Case Analysis of AI and Academic Writing

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper critically examines the role of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI), specifically ChatGPT and Grammarly, in supporting academic writing with a focus on the role of prompt engineering. The study centers on a case involving a college sophomore who employs these AI tools to complete an English composition assignment. We evaluate how AI chatbots potentially enhance knowledge construction and academic performance through the lens of the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), constructionist theory, and social cognitive theory. The paper also explores the ethical considerations regarding the use of AI in academic writing, including issues of attribution and intellectual property, which are of the most importance. The study underscores the urgent need to develop comprehensive rules and guidelines for AI use in educational settings, ensuring responsible integration that contributes positively to the educational process.

## Introduction

The evolution of academic writing, from a traditional emphasis on penmanship to modern consid erations of originality and formatting, has been shaped by tech nological advancements like typewriters and spellch eckers. Today, Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI), exemplified by large language mo dels (LLMs) such as OpenAI’s ChatGPT, introduces new dimensions to this discours e. While GenAI cannot autonomously create content, its integration raises questions about ori ginality, ethics, and pedagogy in academic writing.  This paper not only explores the impact of GenAI on academic writing but also provides practical insights into its integration into education while addressing ethical concerns. T he emergence of chatbots based on LLMs, such as Cha tGPT, Microsoft’s Copilot, and Google's Bard, signifies a  significant advancement in AI-human interaction, offering assistance across various tasks and aiding skill development in students (Owens, 2023; Shoufan, 2023).

## Literature review

Because ChatGPT was introduced less than one year before this article was written, few studies address the topics presented here. However, several studies suggest that university students use AI chatbots for various purposes in their academic and social lives. Elkhodr (2023) found that some students perceive AI chatbots as a helpful learning resource, leading to improved performance compared to traditional search engines. Shoufan (2023)  found that students find AI chatbots  interesting, motivating, and helpf ul for study and work. These articles indicate that students utilize AI chatbots as a valuable tool in their educational endeavors. The theoretical framework for this study is grounde d in the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), constructionist theory, and social cognitive theory. This composite framework aims to understand how students engage with and accept tech nology by focusing on their self-belief in accomplishing tasks and achieving set goals.

The research questions were: How does prompt engineering within GenAI platforms like ChatGPT influence the quality and integrity of academic writing, and what are the resulting ethical and pedagogical implications for students and educators in higher education?

## Method

Data were collected in the fall of 2023. The participant emailed me a ChatGPT log file and his final assignment submission for review. The participant was then interviewed in person. I guided the open-ended interview based on my knowledge of the AI subject area. Follow-up questions were made in person. Joshua (a pseudonym) is a 20-year-old college sophomore studying computer technologie s. He reports having dyslexia and is experienced in STEM subjects, Pytho n computer programming, and online gaming. This case study follows Joshua’s experiences with an AI chatbot and another AI tool for one assignment in an in-person English Composition course.

One day, while Joshua and I were casually discussing the u se of AI in higher education, he proclaimed that he had used ChatGPT for an English assignment from a previous semester. I was intrigued and asked him why he had done that. Joshua recalls asking himself rhetorically, “If I can get my friend to help me with my paper, why can’t I get ChatGPT to assist me?” I further prompted by asking directly why he did not write the paper, and he responded, “I’m not lazy. I just see it as another tool for someone like me whoneeds it.” The assignment, as provided by his instructor, was  “Comparative Short Fiction 1000 words, MLA. Compare a significant element(s) of fiction from two works of short fiction.” Joshua’s prompt was: “Comparative Short Fiction 1000 words, MLA. Compare a significan t element(s) of fiction from two works of short fic tion: "Story 1" and "Story 2".” The response from ChatGPT was a complete essay of 6 47 words in length. It provided a title and subheadings of Introduction, Symbolism of the Title, The Symbolism of the Se tting, Symbolism of Characters, and Conclusion. For his next prompt, Joshua wrote, “Add quotes to this essay.” However, he followed this prompt with the essay, not realizing that ChatGPT remembers all information in a single chat. In resp onse, ChatGPT added a one-sentence quote to three of the content paragraphs. Next, Joshua prom pted, “Increase the size of this essay by 1000 words,” and again copied the entire current version of the essay. However, to Joshua’s surprise, ChatGPT added one thousand words to the e xisting essay. Joshua went back to the previous version. He copied two content paragraphs into ChatGPT and prompted, “Expand these two paragraphs by 100 words each.”  H is next prompt followed this theme by cop ying the concluding paragraph and prompting, “expand this by 100 words.” In all, Joshua used eight iterations, or prompts, in ChatGPT while working on his essay.

When Joshua’s essay was complete to his satisfaction, he copied and pasted it into Grammarly Premium by Grammarly Inc. at https://grammarly.com to corre ct grammar usage. One of his parents had a subscrip tion, and he could use it. He says he accepted all the recommendations  from Grammarly in bulk without reviewing them specifically. Lastly, Joshua ran the plagiarism checker in Gramma rly against his essay. For each sentence flagged by the plagiarism checker, he made minor modifications. The essay was complete. The final submission was 942 words long with nine paragraphs, and Joshua reported receiving a grade of “A.” The thesis statement for the final submission was, “This comparative analysis will focus on the significant element of symbolism in both stories, examining how the authors convey their messages about conformity, power structures, and the consequences of blind obedience.” This sentence was one word less than the initial  response generated by ChatGPT. Joshua had manually removed the word “respective”  from the sentence because he did not think he would have written it in that context.  The concluding sentence of the final submission was, “Both stories compel us to reflect on the power dynamics and societal norms that shape our lives and compel us to conform.” The initial response from ChatGPT did not include t his sentence. It was generated in the last iteration when Joshua specifically prompted ChatGPT to expand the conclusion by 100 wo rds. This final sentence is an exact copy of ChatGPT’s generation.  However, Joshua did not include the last three sentences  that ChatGPT generated in his submission because he felt they went farther than he would have if he had written the paper.

## Discussion and conclusion

Joshua's exploration yielded four pivotal insights with significant impact. Firstly, prompt engineerin g fosters critical thinking and constructivism, enhancing the writing process by guiding ChatGPT to provide more relevant responses and sharpening Joshua's knowledge constru ction skills. Secondly, ethical considerations aros e regarding citing ChatGPT as a contributor, highlighting the evolving discourse on AI's role in academic work and intellectual property. Thirdly, aligning with UTAUT theory, Joshua found that ChatGPT's predictability in aiding writing stems from its perceived usefulness and eas e of use, alongside considerations of social influe nce and facilitating conditions. Lastly, Joshua's engagement with ChatGPT enriched his understanding of chat engineering and essay construction. It deepened  his knowledge of the comparative analysis topic  and showcased  how interacting with AI can expand academic experiences beyond initial intentions.
