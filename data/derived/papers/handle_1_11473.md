# Centering Community-Rooted Actions of Hope: Beyond Knowledge Monoculture

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Our symposium is a confluence of researchers/pedagogues/designers/practitioners who embrace porousness of these identity categories, toward collectively questioning and challenging knowledge and environmental monoculture (Shiva, 1993). We recognize the resurgence of endangered ways of knowing and ways of living as a necessary intervention to environmental destruction and coloniality. While facing the overwhelming devastation of environmental degradation and repercussions from globalization and colonialism, we refuse to cede with ontological negation (Bang, 2020). Instead, we together seek to ground ourselves in community-rooted praxis and pedagogy of hope (Freire, 1992; Gutiérrez, 2016). We collectively present locally and globally relevant socio-environmental issues that arose from communities beyond academic siloes. Together, we highlight community-based actions of hope representing diverse geographical localities and histories. Collectively, we gesture toward knowledge polyculture that is life affirming and orients us toward plural alternative visions of multi-species flourishing.

Cultivating hope: Reflections on community urban gardening experiences in India to reimagine human-nature relationships in cities Deborah Dutta, Preeti Patil, Samira Mukherjee Studies estimate nearly 60 percent of the populatio n will be living  in urban areas by 2050 (Cohen, 2006). The growth of cities is especially noticeable in the Gl obal South, with an aggressive expansion of urban a reas. The ‘development’ trajectories employed in  the creation of urban spaces largely borrow from the Gl obal North and capital-intensive, industrial narratives, thus relying on pipeline models of resource extract ion (Shiva, 1993). The design of cities has been critiqued by practitioner s and scholars for creating a “metabolic rift ” as part of its exploitative processes of sustaining itself at the expense of other ecosystems (Dutta, 2024). As myria d forms of socio-ecological crises proliferate, with cities being re sponsible for a significant amount of carbon emissi ons, waste production and material consumptio n), there is an urgent need to reimagine our relationsh ip, roles, and place in cities. The issue is of special significance to the field of learning sciences, given the increasing scholarship on embodied and emplaced dimensions of learning, which also form the basis for socio-political action (Takeuchi & Marin, 2022). Critical attention to the geographical and socio-ecological infrastructure of urban spaces thus has implications for ways in which the situated, embodi ed and cultural aspects of learning and living are envisioned. In our projects, we focused on urban food systems a s a domain of practice and reflection. The socio-economic arrangements and often opaque supply chains in cities are entangled with questions of food security and nutrition in fundamental ways. As Frazier (2018) explains, “Food is a productive site for exploring the intimate intersection of bodies and cities. This is in part due to the in extricable relationship between food, the body, and  place. As scholars have long identified, food is a symbolic and material force that transcends and delimits particular places and communities” (p.12). Linking the act of consumi ng food to the conditions under which food is grown  and brought to our plates requires an empathetic, systemic way of thinking about the human–nature relationship.

## The context and methodology

Our projects explored community-based stewardship or “civic ecology” practices (Krasny & Tidball, 2012) based on the principle of humans nested within larger socio-ecological systems and being able to impact the systems in positive ways through feedback and virtuous cycles of empowerment. Drawing on Social Design-Experiments approach (Gutiérrez, 2016), our efforts call for attention to the dynamic nature of cultural interactions with food, soil, and plants and how these mediate transformative trajectories for individuals and communities. The approach focuses on resilience stemming from emergent, syste mic changes within the community. Our collective experiences of volunteering at an urban community garden, facilitating a school-terrace garden and collaborating with community partners served to highlight the evo lving knowledge and perspectives that grew along wi th the spaces, as they transformed into alternate visions of cityscapes ranging from local campaigns to save native seeds, petition against GMO crops, and a renewed interest in traditional farming practices adapted for urban spaces.

Together, these initiatives illustrate that i) feed back and competency arising from sustained interact ion with artefacts in community-based practices motivate individual sphere of actio ns, which can then extend into the wider community, ii) ecological perspectives are em bedded in 'performative' substances such as compost, native seeds, manure etc as interacting with these entitie s allowed growers to understand the embedded views towards nature in an embodied (i.e. non-descriptive) way, while also recreating  in an urban setting agricultural practices that are rapidly disappearing from India’s villages.  iii) embodied encounters are central to developing care-based relationships with the environment, and iv) shared-practices support extending ecological actions to the wider community. This study contends that to disrupt thes e discourses, it is essential to interrogate the foundational logic of dominant epis temological and ontological (e.g., neutrality, obje ctivity, and universality) assumptions of disciplinary education. In the U.S. public schools, emergent bilingual/multilin gual youth tend to be entrenched with the deficit discourse that gets inscribed in tools of learning imbued with epistemic injustices (Harper & Kayumova, 2023). The overwhelming emphasis is placed on academic lan guage development, assuming that language is the primary mediator of learning, devoid of racial and colonial struggles (Takeuchi et al., 2022). ## The context and methodology

The data for this study comes from a longitudinal r esearch program aimed at understanding the intersec tion between language-based racialization and science identity developmen t among multilingual youth from nondominant communities. Over six years, youth pa rticipated in a 2-week all-day summer program engaging in STEM projects tackling sustainability issues in the ir community. The overall goal of the program was t o understand what it means to dismantle deficit-based discourses rooted in raciolinguistic ideologies by positioning multilingual youth in asset-based terms and subsequently equipping youth with tools to re-position themselves as cognitively advantaged. Within the program, we drew on theories of pluriverse and positioning to co-design assetbased learning spaces in which the students, teache rs, and researchers were positioned as co-authors, co-researchers, and co-creators of knowledge. Given the longitudinal nature of the work, over the years we witnessed how local sustainability and environmental justice issues as a framework in the context of STEM a llowed young people and teachers to explore the interconnectedne ss of inequities in disciplinary, social, and envir onmental spheres.

## Findings and contributions

Our findings show that when youth’s home and community ways of being and knowing were centered as focal in disciplinary learning, their marginalized identitie s, as discussed by hooks (1991) and Wynter (1989), became integral to the radical possibilities  for reimagining social, cultural, and environmental  futures based on care, relationality, and interdependence. In these spaces, as youth and educators engaged with issues like f ood justice, soil degradation, and environmental harms, their pr actice of relationality as an epistemic approach enabled them to “see, hear, and listen” to the living and more-than-living entities around them, grounding their discip linary sensemaking in responsive, interconnected ways. Further examining the interconnected nature of these inequities, educators and youth began to explore how scientists  and engineers are not merely documenting a world “ out there” or designing detached solutions; rather, the y are actively shaping reality through the models, definitions, and systems they cre ate. This awareness opened a space for reflecting o n how the dehumanization experienced by multilingual youth within the culture of their s chooling was connected to broader epistemic violenc e—where dominant assumptions of neutrality, objectivity, and unive rsality in disciplinary fields contribute to both t he marginalization of human identities and the devaluation of nature (Kayumova & Dou, 2023). Youth, teachers and researchers came to see how the same epistemological and ontological frameworks that render certain forms of knowledge and experien ce invisible or invalid in their own lives also und erpin a worldview that dehumanizes and exploits natural systems, framing both people and the environment as resources to be managed rather than as interconnected beings with intrinsic worth. Building on this critical len s, the youth developed innovative projects that embodied both di sciplinary ingenuity and h olistic worldviews. They created smart-sensing food conservation boxes and designed “livin g” smart homes that incorporated mushrooms as natural decomposers and filtration systems for wate r, all while exploring energy harvesting through fl uid dynamics. We argue that deficit views of multilingual youth not only harm them by implying they must fragment their identities to engage in STEM but also alienat e them “from the real socio-scientific challenges their communities face, failing to position them as essential contributors to urgently needed solutions” (Bang & Marin, 2015, p. 531). Through youth’s endeavors for environmental justices, they demonstrated how STEM practi ces could be reimagined to honor care, relationality and reciprocity reflecting a transformative ethic that not only challenged monoculture of dominant onto-epistemologies but also contributed to a collective  hope for socially-environmentally just and thriving visions of living and futures. We know that turbulent times are drastically uneven for Indigenous, Black, trans and queer, working class communities, and other people of color (Ladson-Billings, 2021). Indigenous peoples have historically been through multiple pandemics —including the theft of land, boarding schools, and massive social and ecological destruction and upheaval —and therefore have millennia-old strategies for navigating the changes of the f uture (Meixi & Nzinga, 2024). Focusing on Storywalks as community members in the Franklin Avenue neighborhood, this paper explores Storywalks as a practice of reading, walking, and storying lands across families and educators (Marin & Bang, 2018). Given that a community's collective continuance i s predicated on the moral relations and ethical commitments that members of a collective ha ve to each other, we focus this paper on how storyw alking revealed and renewed key relations embedded within Lands and the openings that these created for future-making together.

## The context and methodology

The Storywalks featured here are hosted by families  of Nawayee Center School, located in the historic Franklin Avenue neighborhood and American Indian Corridor in Minneapolis, Mní Sóta Makhóčhe (in Dakota, where the waters reflect the skies). We ask: How might center ing families, lands and stories, reveal, enact, and  renew community ethical expertise and relational commitme nts in Indigenous-led public school contexts? What might we learn from Indigenous educators and strategists who have been organizing and designing educational systems in spite of being occupied? Nawayee Center School is a contracted 7th-12th grade alternative school with a yearly enrollment of about 45 students. It is located in t he heart of a diverse urban community in Minneapoli s that is home to over 35,000 Native people), traditional and  contemporary Dakota homelands that is also home to  many Ojibwe and Ho-Chunk people. The only remaining “survival school” (Davis, 2013) in the cities, Nawayee was born of vibrant intergenerational histories of almo st 55 years of community-based activism and organizing since the late 1960s and was a particularly rich site for these walks together.

## Findings and contributions

In our analysis, we draw from video data with stude nts and community members as we moved around the la nds and waterways in Minneapolis from September 2023—October 2024. Activities during this time included various guardian circle events, art workshops, and an Indig enous waterways camp. These events were designed and implemented by Nawayee Center School educators, com munity members and University researchers.  We particularly attended to critical historicity and f uture building through engagement with elders, expl oring local Land and waterways, and learning Indigenous histories and practices (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016). Secondly, plants-and milkweed in specific, connected us in trans-Indigenous relations. Learning the specific roles and responsibilities of  milkweed supported understanding connections acros s geographies specifically across educators in Chiapa s, Mexico and Minneapolis. Storywalks as a relati onal practice took seriously the rich tapestry of intergenerational family life at school and positioned the  school as a place of cultivating community hope, a kind of design towards collective continuance in loved places.

## Designing hopeful futures with a utopian methodology: The role of

heterochrony Antti Rajala, Mike Cole, Moises Esteban Guitart The necessary shift from unsustainable, fossil-fuel-based practices to sustainable ones can approached as a learning process involving individuals, communities, and societies. This paper advances understanding of utopian methodologies – a form of design-based research that can guide the process of envisi oning, implementing, critically evaluating and sustaining alternative fo rms of activity systems that prefigure a particular  utopian goal of an equitable and humane education system.  The paper aims to elucida te how different developmental timescales intersect in a utopian methodological st udy. Existing research on utopian methodologies poi nt to methodological difficulties of examining – often non-linear and heterochronic – development of individuals and activities at multiple timescales (Gutiérrez et al., 2020; Rajala, Cole, & Esteban-Guitart, 2023). The following research question guides our inquiry: What is the r ole of heterochrony in utopian methodology? Drawing  on a cultural-historical activity theory, our analysis centers on  the cultural and institutional organisation of act ivities, which are examined in their institutional settings and wider socio-historical contexts. Furthermore, the development of individuals and activities are shape d by cultural mediation. We conceive of the cultura l medium as the transformations of the physical environment accumulated by a social group during its historical development. Our focus is on heterochrony, referring to how the development of individuals and activities occurs in contexts in a mutual interchange at intersecting timescales (Cole, 2016). We distinguish educational processes operating simultaneously at the cultural-historical time (“macrotime”), among the course of ongoing contexts of activity interactions (“mesotim e”) and moment-to-moment time (“microgenetic”). Each of these time-processes is characterised by unique scales of temporality.

## The context and methodology

We examine a long-term research-practice partnership with two Finnish upper seconda ry schools. The research participants implemented projects,  which sought to reimagine and change the existing f orms of activity in the school and the neighborhood. The projects drew on p edagogy of concrete utopias (Rajala, Jornet, & Acci oly, 2023). A concrete utopia leverages potentials for c hange in an ongoing, existing activity. According t o Wright

(2007) the possibilities of implementing utopian in stitutional design can be enhanced by designing smaller steps

to convince people of the viability of such transfo rmations and to empower social, educational, and co mmunity actors. This paper analyzes the application of idea s of concrete utopias in schools in rural and urban  settings in Finland. Our utopian methodological approach was gu ided by the method of social design experimentation (Gutiérrez et al., 2020). The ethnographic data, collected during 2020-2022, include fieldnotes, and student and teacher interviews, which are analyzed using an inductive qualitative analysis approa ch based on open coding of excerpts that form thematically coherent, continuous units of analysis.

## Findings and discussion

The findings illuminate a pattern of successes and failures in the long-term commitment of the teachers and students of the two schools to change the local pra ctices of the school and municipality. An illustrat ive vignette will be taken from a study of an urban high school in which teachers organized a course on building su stainable futures. The course had begun with a visit to the community vegetable garden located on a school balcony, which the students developed through several courses as p art of an earlier utopian project at the school. Th e discussion of this earlier work created solid foundations for the group to extend the scope of their imagination beyond the school and toward envisioning a more sustainable ci ty. In one exemplary lesson, the students and their  teacher watched a video presentation abo ut novel solutions to energy efficiency in local sh opping malls. The students soon started to critique the idea of building energy efficient malls, which would not solve the problem of excessive consumption. The discussion broadened to address a new project in the nearby city of Helsinki to create a mall using the principles of a circular economy. Through discussing the contrasting sustainability, the students reflected on changing consumption patterns and new kinds of activities to foster change. Overall, our analysis of the school projects shows how multiple developmental timescale s were implicated in the process of individual and communa l learning. On a macro time scale the timing of the  climate crisis provided the motive and legitimation for the  projects and energized cultural-historical (meso) change in various domains of study within the school. Over me so and micro-level time scales the teachers and students created means to overcome institutional and sociocu ltural obstacles to their activity and implemented modest changes within the school and the municipality, including question ing unsustainable farming practices. At the personal level, the students’ emerging expertises a nd associated identities as active citizens capable  of taking climate actions in the wider cultural-historical learning process of the municipality.  This study contributes to understanding of the key issue how to design for su stainable change by providing a nuanced analysis of heterochronia involved in research seeking to promo te and examine social transformation. Furthermore, our analysis advances empirical basis for understanding and critiquing the social forces at work that constrain us and our theorizing – essential goals in constructing theory and planning the next steps of inquiry. From polycultures of farming to ecologies of knowledges: Agroecology, learning and co-design for climate change resilience Michael Bakal, Kris Gutiérrez In San Felipe del Agua, milpa is more than the fiel ds and crops: it is a network of families, commerce, and practices, much of which is very old. The milpa of San Felipe comprises traditional knowledge, handmade tools, the zebu-crossed criollo cattle used for plowing, burros, dogs, backyard tortilla factories, kitchen tables, meals, and hard work —it is a way of life centered around the maize, and a testament to not only biodiversity, but cultural diversity (Shiva, 2016, p. 52).

This paper aims to elucidate practices that enable ecologies of knowledges to flourish within communityengaged co-design efforts. We focus on a social design-based experiment (Gutiérrez et al., 2020) which sought to promote the ecocultural resilience and food soverei gnty of a Maya community on the front lines of the climate crisis through the cultural practices of agroecolog y. Agroecology refers to farming practices that pro mote soil health, nutrient cycling, and farming diversificati on. Beyond mere techniques, agroecology movements e nvision a radically transformed global food system in which communities can produce and consume healthy foods integral to their cultural heritages. Fundamental to this vision is challenging the monoculture of Western knowledge which governs the industrial model of food production, replacing it with an ecology of knowledges in which Indigenous knowledge and relational ontologies plays a guiding  role (Val & Rosset, 2020). We introduce a methodol ogical approach to participatory design—Participatory Design for Buen Vivir (PDBV)—which sought to leverage Maya Achí repertoires of ancestral agroecological knowle dge and practices to respond to the immediate chall enges of food production amidst a changing climate, while si multaneously envisioning and enacting a utopian fut ure. We explore the ways our team’s turn to historicity as a design principle (Gutierrez, 2016)—in this case, an intentional decision to privilege ancestral knowledge in the struggle for climate change resilience—opened new perspectives for collaborative design.

## The context and methodology

This design-based study took place in a Maya-Achi community located in the Dry Corridor of Centr al America, one of the world’s most vulnerable regions to climate change. The Maya-Achí face a syndemic, as the legacies of colonization, genocidal violence dur ing Guatemala’s 36-year period of state-sponsored violence (1960-1996), today collude with climate change and industrial ap proaches to farming to undermine Maya -Achí efforts to maintain their culture and way of life. Central to Maya-Achí efforts to navigate these challenges is the regeneration of the milpa system of farming, an ancestral practice of intercr opping of corn, beans, squash and a range of wild herbs. A “polycultural solution” to the climate cri sis, the milpa system enhances resilience by regene rating soil health, providing important nutritional c omplementarities, and revitalizing a practice at th e heart of the Maya - Achí identity (Gutiérrez, 2016). Yet although milpa farming has sustained Maya communities since the wild grass teosinte was first cultivated by humans some 9,000 years ago, today milpa production is being undermined by an influx of “technological packages” of non-native seeds and agrochemicals being marketed to Ma ya farmers by development agencies and agribusinesses.

The PDBV methodology sought to respond to these cha llenges by eliciting, building on, and strengthening the Maya-Achi community’s existing resilience practices, as well as the deeper onto-epistemologies that undergird them. By specifying “ Buen Vivir” (Living Well) as the telos of design, we explicitly  sought to challenge a persistent tendency of community develo pment programs to privilege Western knowledge syste ms, economic productivity, and a view of nature as an inert object or resource (Bakal & Reyes, 2023). In our analysis, we aim to (1) describe the tools and practices that constituted the PDBV methodology, (2) critically reflect on the ways these tools, and practices challenged the persistent monocultures of development, and (3) unpack the design imaginaries that took hold and animated the efforts of our team of designers as we used this methodology.

## Findings and contribution

Our findings illustrate the important interlinkages  between design principles of historicity, prolepsi s, diversity, and resilience (Gutiérrez, 2016). Specifically, we found the PDBV methodology helped align designers’ commitments and visions with those of the community  members with whom they collaborate, giving rise to strategies which revitalized and creatively innovat ed long-invisibilized knowledge and practices of the Achí community to address the constraints of the social and ecological environment. Shared commitments that emerged through this process included: eliminating the use of agrochemicals; sustaining collaborative learning and cultural practices; respecting mother earth; respecting elde rs and reclaiming ancestral knowledge; and preservi ng native seed varieties. Importantly, these commitments were  not understood as a romantic desire to return to a  bygone era, but rather as strategies for confronting new c hallenges by interweaving ancestral and new techniq ues while grounding in Maya values of respect and reverence f or community and Mother Earth. Ultimately, the utop ian vision of Buen Vivir was not a blueprint, but a “mo ving horizon of possibility,” (Gutiérrez et al., 2020, p. 331) which gained material and symbolic force through its rootedness in ancestral knowledge and practice.
