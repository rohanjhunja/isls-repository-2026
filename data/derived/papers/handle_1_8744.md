# Science Learning Through Drawing

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Engaging students in visualization, especially constructing visual representations has been suggested as fruitful to support science learning. This study explored how elementary students learn astronomy, which is a challenging topic for both teaching and learning, through drawing and communicating with others about their drawings. Data were collected from multiple sources and analyzed qualitatively. Findings indicated that drawing as cognitive and epistemic resource facilitated students’ reasoning in both individual and social domains.

## Background

Visual representations such as drawing are integral  to scientific thinking and reasoning (Prain & Tytl er, 2012; Tytler et al., 2020). Scientists use visual representations (e.g., drawings, diagrams, sketches) to make discoveries, test ideas, explain findings, and elaborate knowledge (Gooding, 2006). Researchers in science education broadly agree that students need to learn how to interpret and construct visual representations of science con cepts and processes (Prain & Tytler, 2012). In particular, th ere is an increasing focus on student-generated dra wings as research suggests engaging students in the process of visual representation construction supports learning better than simply interpreting experts’ visualization (Yo on et al., 2021). Researchers suggest the role of v isual representation should be to support learning with r epresentation, instead of learning from representat ion. This study aimed to explore how elementary students lear n astronomy science with and through constructing v isual representations and communicating with audience about their visualizations. Seeing from socio-cultural perspectives, representa tional work as epistemic practices of science is embedded in and cannot be separated from its social and cultural contexts (Yoon et al., 2021). Drawing to reason is the use of cultural symbolic and semiotic tools to make sense of the phenomena and solve problems (Tytler et al., 2020), thus is mediated by the tools used and constrained by its context and various physical and conventional resources available (Gooding, 2006; Lundin & Jakobson, 2014; Prain & Tytler, 2012). Constructing and refining representations is a core epistemic activity within science and should therefore be a major emphasis in the science classroom. Rathe r than “tools for something more fundamental, such as conceptual knowledge,” students’ representational w ork such as drawing is “enacting science learning a nd reasoning” because it is consistent with how knowledge is developed and communicated in the science community (Prain & Tytler, 2012, p. 2757). In constructing and refining drawings, students need to consider the extent to which it is coherent both within component parts of the drawing and across related representations (Tytler et al., 2020). The consideration of coherence is a key feature of model-based reasoning central to the epistemic practices of science (Lehrer et al., 2008). In this way, drawing is constrained by the need to achieve specificity of detail and the requirement of unambiguous communication (Prain & Tytler, 2012).

## Curriculum Context

There are many reasons for astronomy to be a part o f the education system and culture. Astronomy is de eply rooted in the history of almost every society becau se of its practical applications and philosophical effects. Astronomy is mainly based on descriptive methodolog ies, which are different from other science domains, in which scientists conduct experiments to test their hypotheses. Thus, astronomy shows an alternative approach to the “scientific method” in school context (Plummer & Tanis Ozcelik, 2015). Further, engagement with astronomy topics which fosters curiosity and imagination is w idely suggested as a method of inspiring students t o further study of science. Nevertheless, research indicates that some astronomical phenomena are difficult for students to understand and challenging for teachers to teach (Plummer & Tanis Ozcelik, 2015). Throughout the four-month research period of this study, a classroom science teacher and her nineteen students from a Grade 5/6 science classroom in a public school in Canada worked on an astronomy science unit. Students were encouraged and supported to learn cha racteristics of other planets and their moons in th e solar system by drawing a design to further current space  exploration. Students’ drawings were to be based o n their experiences, knowledge learned in this unit, and information from online research on their topics. At the end of the unit, each students presented their ideas with drawings to their peers.

## Research Methods

This qualitative case study employed multiple methods for data collection, including observing students’ drawing processes, interviewing students to learn how they drew from their perspectives, and collecting students’ drawings. ## Findings

Findings from this study were organized into the following two themes:

(1) In the individual domains, drawing has cognitive and generative functions for interpretation and sense

making. Drawing as a cognitive resource can enact students’ reasoning within the inquiry process. As a form of representation construction and refining, drawing p rovided students with opportunities to coordinate, integrate, and synthesize information in a coherent way to int erpret the astronomical phenomena under investigati on. Students in this study examined the coherence across multiple representations, including their own drawings, texts in books and websites, and online audio and video resources, to form their plausible claims. While achieving the coherence, students negotiated, refined, and advanced their understandings of the astronomy topics. For example, in this study, a student, Nate (pseudonym), explore d characteristics of Triton (i.e., Neptune’s moon) through drawing his design which is a rover for sample collection. During the interview, he shared how and why he made changes to his rover’ wheels in his drawing.

They were like the wheels of car, then I changed … I know that to have the right wheels, I need to know what the surface [of Triton] looks like, so my rover can move … NASA says it is all ice … wheels have cleats will not fall on the icy surface … By exploring relevant online resources, Nate collected helpful information based on which he refined his drawing. Through drawing, students coordinated, integrated, and synthesized information to form interpretation.

(2) In the public realm, drawings have social and critical functions for communication. When students’

designs and claims were introduced to the classroom community, their drawings were crafted into and functioned as an epistemic resource that could provide pictori al evidence for their claims. The community as audi ence examined the clarity of the visual representation, as well as the coherence among elements of the representation, to determine whether the visual claim was plausible and acceptable. For the purpose of communication, students always included other representations, such as anno tation, images and gestures, together with their dr awing to explain their designs. For example, to help his peers to understand how his rover’s wheels looked like, Nate also created a 3D physical model with foam balls and too thpicks. In addition, trough social interactions, s tudents’ drawings were further refined, as well as their understandings about the astronomical topics.

## Conclusions and Discussions

Findings from this study indicated that drawing as cognitive and epistemic resource facilitated studen t science learning in both individual and social domains. With its cognitive and generative functions, drawing helps students make sense of the phenomena as it enacts student reasoning. In the social context, students’ drawings function as epistemic resources for the purpose of communication.

Despite attracting children’s interests and affect ing their everyday lives, many teachers have found astronomy challenging to teach (Plummer & Tanis Ozcelik, 2015). Finding from this study suggest that it could be fruitful engaging students in visual representation construction such as drawing to learn elementary astronomy.
