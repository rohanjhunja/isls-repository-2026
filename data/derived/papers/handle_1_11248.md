# Assessing Directionally-Motivated Reasoning: Students’ Evaluations of a Digital Post on a Relevant Topic

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Little attention has focused on the role directionally-motivated reasoning plays in students’ evaluations of online information and how lessons on evaluating digital content might address this. This study analyzed student reasoning expressed via a short, written task designed to assess students’ digital evaluations of an online article about a relevant topic. Students were prompted to explain their opinion on whether students should always be allowed to use phones in school and then judged the reliability of a Verizon article that argued for the positive impacts of phones in classrooms. Evidence of students’ directionally-motivated reasoning emerged: Students whose opinions were aligned with the article more commonly argued it was reliable, and students frequently judged the article by engaging with the argument it made. Such tasks could support lessons on evaluating online information that help students consider the role directionally-motivated reasoning plays in their judgments.

## Introduction and framing

Interventions to teach students to effectively evaluate online information have multiplied in recent years. Research suggests that these interventions, which include in teractive lessons led by teachers (e.g., Brodsky et  al., 2023; Wineburg et al., 2022) and asynchronous modules or educational video games (e.g, Barzilai et al., 2022; Breakstone et al., 2021), can support young people to develop effective strategies for identifying cre dible information while avoiding misinformation in digita l spaces. Howe ver, m ost interventions have not, to date, tackled a central feature of our online experiences: Digital environments that we depend on for inform ation, including social media, are structured to present relevant information that engages our emotions so we spend more time on the platform and generate advertising dolla rs (Kozyreva et al., 2020). In such environments, i t is harder to deploy effortful evaluation strategies. When we interact with information on relevant topics, we ar e likely to judge posts that align with our beliefs as more credible while rejecting information with which we disagree (i.e., directionally-motivated reasoning or directional motivation; Kunda, 1990). These judgments often happen quickly and unconsciously, which makes them especially impo rtant to tackle in school, where students can learn  to slow down, build metacognitive strategies for monitoring t heir reactions and evaluations, and discuss what th ey learn with others (Chinn et al., 2020; 2021).

Although evidence for the role directional reasonin g plays as people evaluate information is relativel y robust (Kahne & Bowyer, 2017; Redlawsk, 2002; Taber  & Lodge, 2006), we have limited  evidence of such reasoning occurring in classrooms as students evalu ate online content  (see Damico & Panos, 2018 for an exception). Further, existing assessments do not offer insigh t into how, if at all, the evaluative approaches on which students rely shift when directional motivati on is at work. For example, instead of evaluating t he source, are students more likely to focus on the content of a post or on weak but ineffective cues about the site’s credibility (e.g., its top-level domain)? In this study, we report on analyses of student reasoning in response to a task designed to assess students’ evaluations of an online source  on a relevant topic—or, in other words, a topic likely to elicit directional motivation.

Assessments have been developed to target students’ online eval uations or their directional motivation, but tasks that measure both in combination are needed. Assessments that tap students’ evaluations of onlin e content by placing students in online contexts and asking evaluative questions are now widely used in research and classroom contexts (e.g., Brodsky et al., 2023; McGrew et al., 2018). However, these assessments are not purposefully focused on issues relevant to students  and do not gauge students’ opinions on the topic a t hand to assess what role, if any, directional motivation may play in evaluations. On the other hand, existing tasks to assess students’ directional motivation take place in envi ronments that do not sufficiently mimic online cont exts. For example, Kahne and Bowyer (2017) investigated parti cipants’ directional motivation through a task incl uded in an online survey. Participants viewed a graph or political cartoon accompanied by a short argumentative statement about income inequality or taxes like, “The rich can afford to pay higher taxes! Their taxes should go up!” (p. 12). However, the posts did not include a source and were embedded in the survey instead of being in a more authentic online context that participants could navigate at will; further, participants rated the how “accurate” the post was on a four-point scale but were not prompted to explain their reasoning for that rating. The authors found that participants were significantly more likely to rate a post as “accurate” if its argument was ideologically aligned with the viewer’s opinions. This study provides imp ortant evidence for directional motivation at work,  but questions remain about how students might interact with posts in more realistic digital environments or what kinds of reasoning they engage to assess the accuracy or credibility of posts. We built on these assessments to design a task that  would engage students in evaluating actual online content and target their directional motivation. Based on analyses of data from piloting this task with high school students, this study asked, How do students approach evaluating a website on a relevant topic?

## Method

Task design At its heart, the task engaged students in evaluating an actual online article to gauge their responses and evaluative strategies, similar to prior assessment designs (McGrew et al., 2018). We identified a topic about which we expected most or all students to have a preexisting opinion: whether students should be allowed to use their phones in school.  We expected this topic to be relevant because in a recent survey, 97% of American teens studied reported using their cell phone during the school day (Radesky et al., 2023). Next, we located an article that made a clear argument about the topic—in this case, an article arguing for the benefits o f students accessing phones in school. The article, entitled “Best practices for s martphone use in K-12 classrooms,” was written by t he telecommunications company Verizon and included a section on “Why smartphones are good for students.”  A successful, non-directionally motivated evaluation of Verizon’s cre dibility on this topic would question the source, given the potenti al conflict of interest between the pro-phone argument in the article and Verizon’s motives to generate business by selling cell phones and data plans. The questions students responded to as part of the task included questions before the evaluation to gauge students’ opinion on the issue and questions during their evaluations of  the article. Questions that students answered before the evaluation were intended to cre ate conditions for directional motivation by asking  students to state and explain their pre-existing opinion. First, students responded “yes” or “no” to the question “Right now, what do you think: Should students always be allowe d to use their cell phones during class?” The question was phrased in this relatively extreme way —instead of something like “Should students be able to occasionally use their phones in school?”—in hopes that some students would agree and some wo uld disagree with the statement and create groups that we could compare. Next, to furth er prime students’ directional motivation beyond as king them to indicate a “yes” or “no” preference, they were asked to  “Briefly explain your opinion.” Then, students moved on to evaluate the web site. They were given the link to the Verizon artic le and asked, “Is this website a credible source to learn about cell phones in class rooms?” and “Explain your answer, citing evidence f rom any webpages you used.” The task, administered via a Google Form, included a link to the Verizon article so students could visit the website and any other external sites to aid their evaluations.

## Participants

The task was piloted as part of an intervention to teach high school students to evaluate online infor mation. Students completed the task before any lessons on e valuation strategies. Participating students were e nrolled in 9th, 10th, and 11 th grade social studies courses (U.S. history, U.S. go vernment, and Modern World History) at a large, comprehensive high school on the East Coast of the U.S. At the school, 54% of students in the school were eligible for free or reduced lunch. Fifty-four percent identified as Hispanic or Latinx, 26% as Black, 13% as white, 5% as Asian, and 3% as two or more races. A total of 356 students completed the task. Analysis To analyze student responses, a priori codes were developed  based on previous experience analyzing student responses to assessments of online evaluations (e.g., McGrew et al., 2018). Through initial passes of the data, we refined and condensed these into five codes that ca ptured the kinds of reasoning students displayed vi a their written responses. See Table 1 for the final coding  scheme. Some codes captured students’ reasoning ab out the credibility of the source: When they reasoned about its expertise, students showed that they were considering the match between the topic of the article and Verizon’s role as a cell phone company. Students also focused on how trustworthy Verizon was as a source, with some students arguing that it was a trustworthy company and others describing a bias or conflict of interest that hurt its credibility. Finally, some students’ reasoning focused on factors other than the source: Students evaluated or engaged with the content of the article or based their reasoning on other features not directly related to the source or contents, including the top-level domain of the website. We then used the codes to analyze patterns in students’ reasonin g and worked to understand if and how patterns emerged based on whether a student’s expressed opinion about phones in schools was aligned or unaligned with Verizon’s argument.

## Findings

Students were more likely to argue the website was reliable if it s argument aligned with their opinion on phones in school, and to say it was not reliable if it con trasted with their views on the issue. Overall, stu dents’ opinions on the question of whether phones should be allowed  without restriction in classrooms were divided, with 58% responding “yes” and 42% answering “no” to the ques tion. Within students who argued for unrestricted p hone use—an opinion aligned with the Verizon article’s argum ent—61% argued that the article was reliable. Meanwhile, students whose opinions were unaligned w ith the article judged it as reliable 40% of the ti me. Thus, students more commonly judged the article as reliab le if they  agreed with its argument, which suggests that students’ opinions on the issue influenced their decisions about reliability. This finding also indicates that the task design achieved the goal of surfacing groups of stu dents who expressed contrasting opinions on the issue and made opposing arguments about the reliability of the source, at least some of which were possibly attributable to directional motivation.

Examining students’ reasoning for their judgments p rovides more evidence of directional motivation at work. Because directional motivation operates by pressing a reader to evaluate information by engaging with the argument it makes, instead of in a more effortful evaluation of  the source, we would expect to see students focusing on the article’s contents if directional motivation were a factor in students ’ reasoning. Students used a range of reasoning (all categories in Table 1), but, as Figure 1 shows, they regularly used content to  judge the article as both reliable and unreliable. Further, more students analyzed the contents of the article to justify their decision that it was reliable if the article  aligned with their opinion than if it was unaligned (65% versus 56%). For example, a student  who supported phones in school  argued, “From personal experience all the things stated in the article are true. Cell phones can be used fo r educational purposes and are very helpful tools f or students.” At the same time, more students analyzed the contents of the article to justify their decision that it was not reliable if the article was unaligned with their opinion than if it was aligned (36% versus 19%). For example, a student who opposed phones in school  wrote, “This website is not a trustworthy source to learn a bout cell phones in classrooms because it’s encouraging students to use their phones more in class by saying the benefits of our phones outweigh the negatives.” These students did not focus on Verizon’s potential strengths or weaknesses as a source; they keyed in on the argument the source made and the evidence it provided. These students engaged directly with the content of the article, often expressing direct agreement or disagreement with the argument it made. Figure 1 Students’ Reasoning for Reliable and Unreliable Judgments of the Website As Figure 1 shows, students who argued that the sou rce was reliable were most likely to focus their reasoning on the article’s contents. Meanwhile, stu dents who argued the source was of questionable  reliability were more likely to focus on Verizon’s potential biases in presenting i nformation about phones in schools. Both aligned and unaligned students used this reasoning, which was the most accurate, at similar rates.

## Discussion

We posited that a t ask like the one analyzed here  would allow us to tap students’ directional motivation as they evaluated online info rmation. We constructed a task that elicited students’ opini ons about a relatively extreme position on a topic we expected would be relevant t o students. We then asked them to evaluate a post m aking an argument about that topic from well-known source that students could evaluate without needing to do additional research. Analyzing students’ reasoning on this task provid es initial evidence that directional motivation pla yed a role in students’ evaluations of the Verizon article. Unlike tasks that gauge students’ evaluative approaches but do not tap their opinions on the issues being evalu ated or how those might influence students (e.g., McGrew et al., 2018), the questions that were part of this task assessed students’ initial opinion on the issue and their evaluation of the article’s reliability. This allow ed us to compare evaluative approaches for students  whose opinions were aligned and unaligned with the articl e’s argument. Further, by placing students in an au thentic online environment and asking students to justify their reasoning for the evaluation, the task provided insight into the evaluative strategies on which students relied—and might rely in the future as they evaluate information both in and out of school contexts.

At the same time, evidence for directional motivati on was not overwhelming. Students still regularly made judgments of reliability that were not aligned  with their view on the issue. For example, the mos t common argument against the article’s reliability —made by students who agreed and disagreed with the article’s argument—was that Verizon was biased. Students expressed the belief that students should be able to use phones without restriction in school but still critiqued the reliability of the source based on its potential for bias. Likewise, substantive percentages of students who disagreed with the article’s argument still argued it was reliable based on its contents or Verizon’s expertise or status as a trustworthy company. There are many possible explanations for these find ings that should be explored through future studies. First, students were still completing the task in a  school context, and thus may have been more likely  to perform as they believe they are expected to i n school than to express their real opinions. Secon d, directional motivation operates quickly and unconsciously, and we slowed d own the evaluation process by requiring students to  write written answers to questions. These hurdles are dif ficult to clear wit h any school-administered task. Other modifications are more feasible. For example, since  most students appeared familiar with Verizon, succ essfully completing the task did not require a more effortful evaluation strategy like lateral reading, where students needed to leave the article and search for information about the source outside the site itself (Wineburg & McGrew, 2019). In a task that required lateral reading, students’ directional motivation might be even more apparent as students may be dissuaded from investing effort to verify an article about which they had a strong initial reaction.

## Conclusion

Directional motivation is a reality for humans as w e evaluate online information. Research should cont inue to explore the role that assessments like this can pla y in school contexts. Such assessments can be a pow erful tool for teachers, researchers, and po licymakers to understand how to support students to  learn to evaluate online information and recognize, reflect on, and reason through the role directional motivation plays in their evaluations.
