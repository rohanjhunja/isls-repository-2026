# Student Crowd Research Environment: A Design-Based Approach to Large-Scale Collaboration

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The current phase of the digital age is characterized by large-scale phenomena. They shape the realms of social media, pop culture, and socio-political participation but also education and academia. Accordingly, the importance of collective practices of collaborative knowledge creation has been noted within the CSCL community. However, mass collaboration as a future-oriented concept that evolves the understanding of learning and inquiry is far from exhausted. To further this discourse, we present insights from a design-based research process of progressively developing a mass collaboration environment for higher education. This paper conceptualizes large groups as unique social entities and outlines design principles that facilitate coordination and collaboration on complex epistemic artifacts. We draw from empirical findings to illustrate distinct features of large-group interaction and scalable artifact-based collaboration, in particular engagement dynamics and participation types. We discuss the specific qualities of mass collaboration in educational settings and thereby renegotiate CSCL concepts.

## Introduction

Reflecting back, the 2010s have been a decade of the masses, as collective movements have played a decisive role in raising social, economic, political, and environmental issues and allowed for unprec edented visibility, engagement, and discourse. Further, the emergence of new forms of distrib uted labor as well as the global collectivization of knowledge creation in digital spaces shape the societies of the 21st century. Thes e large-scale phenomena are closely tied tonetwork technologies and are thus fundamentally intertwined with designed digital spaces, as described by Orlikowski (2007) as being "constitutively entangled“. CSCL discourse continuously merges technological developments and educational innovati on. The notion of massiveness and collectivity has been recognized as a social phenom enon of epistemic value (Cress, 2013). Mass collaboration redefines interaction, knowledge artifacts, and social entitie s and thereby touches the entire scope of CSCL design and research. The community already put forwar d theoretical and methodological approaches as well as systematic descriptions of collective knowledge practic es in various digital spaces (Cress, Jeong & Moskaliuk, 2016). Beyond programmatic, conceptual, and analytical work CSCL research also has contributed to the envisioning of educational technology and learning scenarios. The challenge of expanding the understanding of learning and collaboration (Chen, Håklev & Rosé, 2021) to explore large-s cale and scalable knowledge practices, therefore, entails an informed design process. A design-based research (DBR) approach to artifact-based collaboration at scale contributes to both angles: theory buildin g and development. DBR specifies design challenges and principles, implements educational environments, generates em pirical findings, and leads to conceptual clarification. Within CSCL research, DBR shifts the object of investigation to emerging social practices within material environments (Ludvigsen, Lund & Oshima, 2021) and thereby widens solely cognitive perspectives on learning and education (Stahl, 2017).

In this paper, we follow the suggested structure for presenting design rese arch by Peffers et al. (2006, p.84): “problem identification and motivation, objectives for a solution, design and development, evaluation, and communication”. Building on the found ations of mass collaboration, we outline large groups as distinct social formations and explore them through design and analysis to raise unique challenges for educational contexts. We draw from our experiences in the DBR project SCoRe-Student Crowd Res earch to illustrate the development and evaluation of a large-scale collaboration environment. In the first step, we present the main design decisions. In the second step, we apply three perspectives-the process, the actors, and the artifact -, to illustrate, deepen and challenge the notion of large-group collaboration as a unique social dynamic concerning scalability, participation, and orchestration. Concluding this paper, we discuss the implication that large-scale collaboration raises for digital infrastructure, instructional design, organizational measures, and pedagogical stances  that fundamentally challenge current practices within institutional education and learning communities alike.

## State of the art – CSCL perspectives on collaboration at scale

CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 132 Collaboration and collaborative learning are inherently social processes, as they involve more than one individual. Therefore, the investigation of learning within social constellations determines CSCL discourse. This leads to two simple questions regarding the unit of analysis: First, what are the respective social formations, and second, what is the analytical stance that shapes the understanding of interaction and learning? The pivotal social formation in CSCL design and research is the small group. Hence, collaborative interaction is often facilitated and observed between a handful of learners. In this context, CSCL theories and models tend to focus on cognitive processes and learning outcomes of individual participants in these group environments (Ludvigsen, Lund & Oshima, 2021) or on the group itself as a cognitive agent (Stahl, 2017). Despite the attention on small-group scenarios, their broader localization wit hin the classroom, the community, or social and cultural structures is also taken into consideration (Sta hl & Hakkarainen, 2021). Collectivity and large-scale perspectives are considered by CSCL key concept s, for example by communities of practice, communities of Interest (Fischer, Rohder & Wulf, 2007), or the notio n of knowledge-building communities (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 1994). However, the analysis of colle ctive phenomena like Wikipedia (Halatchliyski, et al., 2014), massive open online courses, open software development (Cress, Jeong & Moskaliuk,

2016) or social media calls for new conceptual, practical, and methodical approa ches to understand and design

for collaborative learning at a larger scale (Chen, Håklev & Rosé, 2021). Here, the paradigm of mass collaboration opens up a new conversation (Cress, Jeong & Moskaliuk, 2016). It allows for a the oretical renegotiation of collaboration, coordination, and communication (Jeong et al., 2017), redefines the role of shared artifacts and digital spaces as a stigmergic anchor for large-scale collaboration (Elliott, 2016), and calls for a systematic qualitative description of large and massive groups as distinct social entities (R eichelt et al. 2019). This development aligns with the methodical turn towards the socio-material emer gence of learning practices within the recent CSCL discourse (Stahl & Hakkarainen, 2021). Additionally, big-data approaches seem fruitful to reconstruct complex collaboration between a larger number of participants (Holtz, Kimmerle & Cress, 2018). Still under-researched are the implications of large-scale collaboration for  the design of CSCL environment and scenarios. How can these diverse real-world phenomena and theoretical approaches inform the development of learning technology and the facilitation of collaborative learning? Here DBR can serve as a fruitful approach to gaining empirical experience, to further specify the vision of large-scale collaboration within situated educational contexts, and formulating theoretical and practical transformations. Conceptual frame – large groups as a unique social formation Stating a certain number of participants seems like an obvious but also shallow solution to distinguishing a large group from a small group. Instead of a quantitative definition, qualitative characteristics are needed to define large groups as a distinct social formation and collaboration at scale as a distinct form of interaction. The investigation of mass phenomena suggests a critical threshold for large groups  that inherently prevents a consistent and inclusive reciprocal engagement (Chen, Håklev & Rosé, 2021). They possess a dynamic momentum that emerges and changes around a shared concern. Collaboration at scale  tends to be structurally diverse and temporally open as well as performatively bound to its trajectory, a s participation involves solely an act of meaningful contribution within the joint action (Reichelt et al. 2019). This entails a notion of collective agency in epistemic processes that reach far beyond individual content creation or knowledge acquisition (Jeong et al., 2017). To further qualify the interaction necessary for a productive large-scale process Jeong et al. (2017) propose a successive and interwoven model of attendance, coordination, cooperation, and c ollaboration: passive presence in a shared space, aligned participation, and distributed as well a s synthesized contributions to achieve a collective goal and carry out a shared responsibility. As digital environments are constitutive to computer-supported collaboration, the ro le of artifacts is significant for understanding large-scale interaction. Here stigmergy is an a pproach to define shared artifacts as pivotal entities that mediate, anchor, and thereby progressively (re-)generate the interaction (Jeong et al., 2017). Stigmergic collaboration is not per se a mass phenomenon. Rather, the unique property of a substantially artifactbased collaboration, coordination, and communication is its scalability to large groups of learners that share a vision, contribute actively within a shared plan, and thereby add to a shared outcome (Elliott, 2016). Against this backdrop, educational contexts bring with them a quite specific socio-m aterial nexus of practices. Therefore, the question, of how scale can serve as an asset (Chen, Håkle &, Rosé, 2021) and also comply with institutional constraints and requirements as well as notions of teaching a nd learning can only be answered regarding situated collaboration scenarios and implemented environments. Only through design of educational technology and its realization in specific learning context, the phenomena in question emerge. Therefore, we have to theorize this social formation as an entity as well as a process. Student crowd research environment – design and implementation CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 133 The main objective of the SCoRe project was the progressive development of a collective learning and research environment for remote and fully digital higher education courses. Particular emphasis was put on characteristics of productive knowledge work in large and interdisciplinary groups. A second objective was to sharpen conceptions, formulate design principles and compose analytical frameworks to guide the design and evaluation of digital mass-collaboration tools. The project combined expertise in advanced video technologies, virtual collaboration in interdisciplinary and large-scale groups, research-based learni ng, and education for sustainable development. It went through an initial exploration phase followed by three iterations of development, implementation, and evaluation from October 2018 till March 2022. Focal questions Based on the conceptual framework, the following four questions shall focus the presentation of the main design decisions as well as later guide the analysis of the collaboration within the described scenario.

• Did the environment and the provided research scaffold serve a stigmergic function and facilitate

(scalable) artifact-based collaboration, communication, and coordination?

• Did scale become a valuable asset within the collaborative research scenario?

• Did the design provide meaningful ways of participation for a fluid group of learners?

• Did a self-organized process between the students emerge that did not call for a central orchestration of

an instructor?

Design propositions The specific development and implementation context was determined by the educa tional setting: a fully digital course, an interdisciplinary group of students from different universities all  across the country, no predefined course schedule or mutual start and endpoints, solely asynchronous participation, and a low instructor-to-student ratio.

To envision a digital environment that facilitates epistemic activities within these constraints the support of collaboration, communication and collaboration was a core concern (cf. Ellis, Gibbs & Rein, 1991). The key design propositions realize the values of stigmergic artifacts, the communication to explicit and implicit decisionmaking, and the coordination to awareness and agency. We extended the model to take in to account large-group qualities (see Figure 1). The aspect of formation emphasizes the performative nature of large groups and focuses on collectivity and identity within a fluid social formation. The interface is sectioned into three parts, a navigation bar for  quick orientation in complex project environments, a main working area of simultaneously but not concurrently editable sections that contain text, image and video content, and a communication and activity channel that lists all edits and chat messages. The main asset of the tool is the support of artifact-based scaffolding. To support students in the organization of their collaborative work processes and to provide them with an outline of the epistemic activities they are engaged in, the main working area of SCoRe-Docs allows for the  creation of structured canvases. Canvases can be created from arrangements of related sections, that can be edited separately. New sections and sub-sections can be arranged and created seamlessly if a collaborative proc ess calls for an expansion or reorganization of the shared scope. Towards this end, SCoRe-Docs provides a generative and visually organized overview of all the (interim) results that have to be collectively produced. At the same time, it serves as the collective workspace that allows for large and remote groups of participants to contribute alongside each other within one semantic and structural framework and hence fosters the collecti ve work around shared artifacts. By systematic segmentation of the collaborative working area into separately e ditable and rather small sections the dissociation of authorship from the size or amount of one’s contributions is supported. A feature to facilitate artifact-based coordination  is the synchronization and alignment of collective processes based on status assignment. Within the SCoRe-Docs, all sections  are complemented by a progress model, which divides the overall process into a series of successive s tages. These stages are marked by a status indicator (closed, in progress, checking, done) that defies the current state for every section and helps to identify the currently needed activity. Section status can be set and re-set by al l participants and thereby mediates the collective understanding of progress and the notion of completion. This functionality combi nes the required procedural flexibility of large-scale asynchronous collaboration with clear incentiv es for joint quality care. Additionally, controlled versioning is implemented to ensures the self-documentation of processes as well as the consistency between all contributions. To ensure transparency and consistency throughout the collective research environment, SCoRe-Docs not only technically versions all changes made in each section but also forces users to describe the changes they have made so that others could follow up. This mec hanism also ensures that not only final results but also interim outcomes can be unambiguously and consistently referenced. The chat tools were designed to facilitate artifact-based communication, as every SCoRe-Docs environment generates a project chat and every section generates a respec tive section chat. Thus, large can to focus their communication within complex collaborative artifacts and processes. An overall communication channel serves as an orientation for multilayered discussions. Additionally, the SCoRe-Docs provides various awareness functions, including the activity channel that tracks all updates and change notes, visual cues on the navigation and the shared workspace that mark new content in relation to the last visit, a personalized landing page as well as a daily-digest e-mail that informs participants about new contributions and messages.

Sustainable urban traffic – a large-group student research project The other part of the educational design was the collaborative learning scenario. The course tha t will serve as an anchor in this article was open for participation during the winter term of 2020/21. T he provider was the Virtual CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 135 Academy for Sustainability [VA] a distance-education provider affiliated with the University of Bremen, Germany. The VA accepts students from all academic fields and issues  credit points for general studies requirements. The course had no predefined schedule. To achieve an overall wor kload of either 30 or 90 hours the students were free to manage the timing of their contributions alo ng the project’s progression. Thematically the scenario revolved around sustainable public spaces. The pedagogic key concepts  were education for sustainable development (UNESCO, 2020) as well as research-based learning (Lambert, 2009). To harness the large group as a collective asset, a research scenario was developed with the potential to scale in regard to the group size. For that purpose, a qualitative research s cenario that revolved around a large corpus of case-based empirical data and a comparative analytical design was deemed most suitable. This way, the scenario generated the need for repetitive tasks (data collection and analysis) and also synthesizing contributions (comparison and interpretation), allowing for a scalable number of students to pa rticipate in a meaningful way and also guiding the collaborative focus on a shared research goal. The student-research project dealt with the question of how power relations and inequalities are reproduced between cars, bikes, and pedestrians in urban traffic situations. Three main research tasks were conducted by the students: the collection of video data, images, and maps of urban crossroads, the qualitative data analysis, and also the interpretation and discussion of findings. The shared working environment provided generative templates for all the require d research tasks, for standards and procedural instructions for data collection and analysis, initial example s as well as integrated minimal instructions for the overall research process. This scaffolding w as meant to establish criteria but not to prescribe the process or the outcome itself and thereby define a space to foster emergent epistemic decisions. The instructors were present as patrons of the project, as they were responsive to active questions regarding the project design but did not interfere to evaluate the students’ contributions. Large-scale collaboration – an empirical investigation Reconstructing the collaboration As outlined above, all student research activity took place within one shared e nvironment. Therefore, the whole collaborative process can be reconstructed from the interaction data. During the course period, 16 students from various German universities joined the project. They collected case data from 13 urban crossroads and worked on an intricate typology of micro-interactions within the recorded traffic sequence s. They formulated comparative interpretations, descriptions of all analyzed patterns as well as implications for urban planning. The collaboratively written research document contained an estimate of 45.000 words by the end of the course as well as extensive image, map, and video data. It proposed comprehensive answer s to all initial questions, met the requirements for academic writing on an undergraduate level, and was compl iant with the general quality standards of qualitative social research.

All student activities were consistently logged and stored in an external learning record store. Records include the location within the research environment, the time, the user, the  content, and the interaction type. Interaction types were represented by verbs that defined a specific behavi or: opened, viewed, played, ended, paused, left, started editing of, viewed history of, updated, seek, canceled editing of, c reated, deleted, uploaded, typed, sent. For the period between 11/01/2020 and 04/15/2021, the dataset contained 212.000 entrie s. To allow for a targeted analysis various subsets were created and translated into tabular or visual formats. Analytically we correlate three perspectives: the process, the actors, a nd the artifact. The process is reconstructed by the chronological progression of artefact-modifying interactions, na mely created, updated, and deleted events since they suggest a deliberate contribution to the shared artifacts. The actor s are participants that trigger events within the project environment. Building on a performative unders tanding of large-group collaboration participants were considered actors when they triggered artifact-altering events and thereby became part of the collective process. In this context, the artifacts are all ac tor-created or actor-edited sections. Additionally, categories vital to represent the research logic like the case or the research task manually tagged in the tabular representations of the logged data. To answer the above-outlined focal questions examples from these data sets are chosen to illustrate the findings and facilitate further discussion. The project dynamic (Figure 3) shows that new case data was added after the main synergizing tasks had already been started (b8-b13). This did not create a  disturbance for the students but facilitated an iterative research process that prompted a continuous integra tion of new data to the growing typology. However, in the last quarter of the course time, the students used the  notice board for an open appeal not to add new data but to concentrate on the research questions so that the proj ect could be finished by the end of the term. In this, scale on the one hand became an epistemic asset but on the other was perceived as a resource to be collectively managed by the students.

Orchestration and organization The students worked remotely and asynchronously within the research environment, meaning there were no simultaneous sessions or course elements besides the shared environment. Within this context a self-organized along the research-scaffold, the instructors did only intervene to answer students’ questions that came up during the research tasks. Only one critical moment occurred during the data colle ction (b3-5). One student made a mistake while structuring their case data. This was shortly afterward replicated by the next student that submitted data and used the previous entry as an example. Here the instructors choose to intervene, clarify the scaffold, and prompt the group with the task of revisioning the data structures. The mistake s were then corrected by other students from the group.

## Discussion

Regarding the four focal questions, the environment as well as the researc h scenario can be understood from a perspective of stigmergic collaboration and as a self-orchestrated process among a scalable group of students. It has to be noted though, that the educational design only proposed one specific solution f or collaboration at scale. Basing the student research scenario on tasks that were inherently scalable and lead to one structured condensation was a productive approach. Tasks that generate less str uctured and more creative and maybe contradicting contributions are yet to be investigated. Here the question arises if the implemented but rarely used communication and documentation tools are sufficient to support the negotiation of collective consensus. Further, the notion of scale has to be iterated. In this scenario16 students a ctively participated. A group of 16 participants is not a small group in the scope of CSCL design but it is a stretch to call 16 people a mass or a crowd. However, we want to propose that in order to further incorporate sti gmergic concepts into CSCL design and research collaboration at scale has to be conceptualized as scalable collaboration, meaning by unique qualities and not by a number. Scalability and stigmergic collaboration allocate a distinct r ole to shared artifacts. Here, integrated development of digital environments and tools alongside corresponding scenarios is needed. Finally, educational institutions, and educational practices have to be considered conc erning their capability to incorporate the variance, openness, and fluidity that emerge in large scale collaboration.

## Implications

Culminating in the pandemic years digital transformations greatly affected the realms of education. In light of these new computer-mediated practices of teaching and learning educational institutions, educational design and educational research cannot position themselves beside or even against those de velopments but have to create opportunities to evolve their concepts and theories. They have to establish them selves as an active, critical, and driving actor (and not a reactor) to socio-technical change. A theoretically rich discussion and methodically diverse approaches are nee ded within the CSCL community to accommodate the emergence of innovative knowledge practices. A focus on socio-technical design and situated implementations can inform educational institutions to create environments that take up the demand of the younger generations for collective involvement and accountability while definin g requirements and cultivating skills needed to responsibly shape an uncertain future.
