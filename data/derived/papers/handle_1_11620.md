# One Girl is Praised, While the Other is Angry: How Teacher Praise Fails to Emotionally Support Students

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster presents a single-case study where teacher praise failed to provide emotional support to the students. We found this failure occurred because the teacher’s recognition of the speaker’s identity misaligned with the students’ perception of her role. This poster shows students are particularly sensitive to peer identities, particularly “representative” ones. If teachers ignore this, it may result in failing to offer praise.

## Introduction

In one of the lessons in our database, the teacher praised a student (G1) by rephrasing her response, demonstrating her discursive practices to provide emotional suppo rt. However, diagonally opposite G1, another studen t (G2) crossed her arms, turned her head, glaring at G1 in anger, before G1 awkwardly sat down. Based on the reactions of G1 and G2, it appears that the instructor ’s attempt at emotional support failed. Then, this research began with a question: Why did the teacher’s praise fail to provide emotional support in this context?

## Theoretical framework

A substantial body of empirical research confirms t hat teacher emotional support positively influences  both classroom climate (e.g., Wang et al., 2020) and stu dent participation (e.g., Ruzek et al., 2016; Schen ke et al., 2018). Previous studies highlight that emotional su pport can mediate or impact students ’ academic and psychosocial outcomes through their daily interacti ons with the curriculum, teachers, peers, and class room organizational structures (Wang et al., 2020). Howe ver, Schenke et al. (2018) demo nstrated that even within the same classroom, perceptions of teacher emotional su pport may vary significantly among students. Buildi ng on Ahmed’s (2004) perspective that emotions are not purely i ndividual but rather fluid and socially constructed, we argue that teacher emotional support should be exam ined as a two-phase process: (1) teacher-initiated emotional support (the act of offering support)  and (2) student-received emotional support (the student ’s interpretation and experience of that support), with the latter being interpreted within the peer interaction context (Hartup, 2005). In this study, we operationalize teacher praise as a key form of teac her-initiated support, given its prevalence as a fundamental emotional support strategy in classroom discourse (Brophy & Good, 1970; Downs et al., 2019).

## Methods

The current research employs a single-case study approach. This case serves as a revelato ry case, addressing the research questions in its real-life context and offering an in-depth, contextualized understanding (Yin, 2017). This selected case is drawn from a larger database comprising 36 Chinese language arts lessons at H School, a public elementary school (grades 1-6) in Guangzhou, China. We focus on Ms. Tian, a Chinese language arts teacher with 6.5 years of experience, who actively implements student-centered practices by respecting and listening to students’ perspectives in her classroom. This research is informed by Ethnomethodological Conversation Analysis (EMCA), an approach that repositions language as on e of several modalities, requiring the capture of s ubtle movements and gestures (Flood et al., 2018). Our analysis specifically examines cases of mismatch between these phases.

The failure of emotional support: Misalignment in identity recognition From the reaction of G3, B3, and B2 directing their applause toward G2, we could tentatively offer an explanation for this failure: G1 ’s ideas seemed to have been derived from G2, causin g G2 to feel her contribution had been stolen. This interpretation was both logically evid ent and Ms. Tian's immediate response when asked ab out G2's anger. However, this explanation does not fully acc ount for why G2 did not express anger when G1 began speaking, but instead attempted to help G1 articulate her thoughts more clearly. Upon re-examining this segment and closely analyzing the ca talyst of G2’s anger, we argue that the underlying cause lies in a “misalignment” between t he teacher’s understanding and the perspectives of G2 (and G1) regarding G1’s identity. From G1 and G2’s p erspective, this was a group-sharing session, and regardless of who was speaking (since B2 had already spoken once), the speaker was the “representative” of the entire group, and the honor should have belonged to the group as a whole. From the first transcription, G2 demonstrated initial eagerness and active willingness to assist G1 in ex pressing the group’s thoughts clearly. Furthermore,  G1, as the group leader, used the phrase “our group” when spea king. However, the teacher’s statement framed G1 as  an individual. While she initially praised “the idea,” he r finger pointed directly at G1, and all subsequent  pronouns used by the teacher were singular (“she”). The comparative phrasing, “more detailed than all the other discoveries made by the children,” exacerbated the situation for G2, because this was not just an ordinary compliment but the “best” compliment, which G1 had seemingly “taken the honor away” from the group.

## Significance

In this study, we responds to the call made by Moore et al. (2019) to explore when and for whom praise is effective, especially considering the insufficient evidence re garding the impact of praise on students without di sabilities in K-12 settings. Our case exemplifies a failure in teacher praise, demonstrating how even a single word or sentence from the teacher can intentionally —or unintentionally —trigger a chain reaction among peers.  We argue that when praise is directed toward the “wrong” recipient, even highly specific praise loses effica cy. Building on Rymes and Leone-Pizzighella’s (2020) framework of peer-recognized expert and novice identities, our findings reveal students ’ acute sensitivity to representative identities—those perceived as legitimate spokespersons for group knowledge. This sensitivity reflects deeper e xpectations of teachers: students crave not only emotional/intellectual support but also belonging through public recognition, as evidenced in our case.
