# Social Presence with AI-Integrated Student Agents in a Teaching Simulator Leads to Higher Immersion but Not Learning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper reports on an experiment examining perceptions of social presence with AI-integrated student agents in a prototype teacher training simulation. I hypothesized that perceived immersion in the simulation would increase social presence, and that social presence would lead to increased learning gains. A univariate analysis showed that immersion significantly predicted social presence, but perceptions of agent fidelity did not. A regression analysis showed that social presence did not impact learning gains. Design implications and future research directions are discussed.

Simulation-based teacher training is being augmented by artificial intelligence (AI) to increase scalability and authenticity (Barrett et al., 2025). AI-integrated dialogic student agents (student agents) are being developed and implemented in simulated classrooms with a medium positive effect on learning, according to a meta-analysis by Dai et al. (2024). However, it is unclear how student agents influence participation in simulation-based learning systems.

A central issue in designing simulation systems with student agents is which factors most strongly drive participants’ sense of social presence, the subjective feeling of “being with” the agents. Many assume that agent fidelity (how behaviorally individualistic an AI agent is) will boost social presence (Sinatra et al., 2021).  Yet others show that immersi on is often the stronger predictor (Oh et al., 2018).  For example, behavioral realism sometimes fails to produce higher social presence unless participants  feel immersed (Herrera et al., 2018). In the context of student agents, then, immersion will likely exert a stronger influence on social presence than will agent fidelity, and that fidelity’s effect may only manifest when coupled with sufficient immersion. A further question is whether  social presence with student agents  meaningfully contributes to learning gains in simulation-based environments. Many theoretical or design models implicitly assume a mediation chai n where more immersive simulations lead to  greater presence which in turn lead to  enhanced learning, although studies show mixed results (Ochs & Sonderegger, 2022; Stevens & Kincaid, 2015). In educational VR settings, presence has been hypothesized to increase attention, reduce distraction, and thereby support learning (Witmer & Singer, 1998). However, some recent reviews suggest the presence-learning link  is not robust across tasks, content, or designs (e.g. Wei et al., 2025). Thus, while I hypothesize that social presence will positively predict gain scores, I also treat this as an exploratory test given mixed precedent.  Hence our key hypotheses are:  (1) Perceived immersion positively predicts social presence. (2) Social presence will positively predict learning gain scores.

## Method

This study adopted a pre-post experimental design with 52 early-career K-12 teachers. Participants were recruited through self-selection and then screened for suitability. Informed consent was collected from all participants and ethical guidelines were strictly adhered to in accordance with the institutional review board. This study used a prototype teacher training simulation tool called EVEteach. EVEteach integrates GPT4 with the online virtual world OpenSimulator  through a cloud-based program. The program involves a large language model fine-tuned with transcription data from middle-school classroom recordings (see Barrett et al., 2024; Bhowmik et al. 2022). Participants control a teacher avatar and interact with the student agents through text.

Participants individually engaged with EVEteach via Zoom, starting with an explanation and signing the consent form, then taking a teaching knowledge pre-test. During simulated teaching they introduced a topic of their choosing to the student agents and discussed it for a minimum of 30 minutes with the goal of building consensus. Following this, participants took a post-test and survey. Data sources were a pre and post teaching knowledge test (consisting of ten scenario-based multiple response questions) and a post-participation survey measuring social presence (Chronbach’s α =.705), perceived immersion (α =.895), and perceived agent fidelity (α =.865) using a 5-point Likert scale. Social Presence included 5 items which were adapted from Makransky et al. (2017), and both the immersion and agent fidelity scales included 4 items each from Barrett (2025). Analyses included a  univariate general linear model (immersion and agent fidelity, predicting presence), and a linear regression of gain scores on social presence. 2584 The univariate test examined the effects of perceived immersion and perceived agent fidelity on social presence. The overall model was significant, F (3, 48) = 7.72, p <.001, with an R²  of.33, indicating that the predictors explained 32.5% of the variance in social presence. Perceived immersion significantly predicted social presence, F(1, 48) = 7.77, p =.008, η² =.14, such that higher immersion scores were associated with greater feelings of social presence. Perceived agent fidelity was not a significan t predictor, F(1, 48) = 2.60, p =.114, η² =.05, and the interaction between immersion and agent fidelity was also nonsignificant, F (1, 48) = 2.47, p =.123, η² =.05. I ran a linear regression predicting learning gain (post-test minus pre-test) from social presence. The model was not significant, t(1, 50) = 0.103, p =.919, and social presence accounted for essentially zero variance in gain scores (standardized β =.015). The unstandardized coefficient was B = 0.166 (SE = 1.620), with a 95% confidence interval of [–3.087, 3.420]. In sum, social presence did not reliably predict learning gain in this sample. To assess our ability to detect effects, I conducted a post hoc power analysis. Based on our sample size (N = 52) and α =.05, our design was powered 78% to detect effect sizes (f²) around 0.15 (i.e. small-to-moderate). Because our observed effect size (β =.015, R² ≈.0002) is far below that threshold. Together, these results suggest that while simulation affordances (i.e., immersion) influence social presence, social presence does not reliably translate into measurable gains  in knowledge over a short training session.

The analysis supports the hypothesis that perceived  immersion plays a central role in shaping social presence during simulation-based teacher training. Participants who felt more absorbed in the experience also had a stronger sense of “being with” the student agents. In contrast, how much  the AI student agents were perceived as unique individuals did not significantly influence social presence. These findings align with the theoretical view that social presence emerges primarily from cognitive and affective engagement rather than from agent fidelity, aligning with Herrera et al. (2018). In other words, when users are deeply immersed, their sense of social presence can be maintained even when  agents are perceived as less realistic.

The regression analysis revealed that social presence did not meaningfully predict learning gain  in our simulated teacher-training environment. Contrary to the often-assumed mediation chain (i.e., better design  stronger presence   increased learning), our data suggest that social presence may not reliably translate into measurable knowledge gains, at least in this context. The effect size (β ≈.015) was trivial, and the confidence interval was wide, underscoring uncertainty about any relationship in our dataset. Because our design was only powered to detect moderate or larger effects, I cannot exclude extremely small associations; however, the result aligns with a body of work showing mixed or null presence-performance relationships (e.g. Ochs & Sonderegger, 2022).

From a design perspective, these findings suggest caution in assuming that the inclusion of student agents will necessarily enhance learning. Other mechanisms, such as scaffolding and adaptive support, would likely play stronger roles in driving learning gains than merely increasing social presence. If social presence is to play a mediating role, future work should explore when  (i.e. task types, durations) and how (i.e. via motivation or engagement) it connects to learning outcomes.

In sum, our null presence –learning finding does not invalidate the importance of social presence in simulation design, but rather underscores that social presence is a psychological experience, not a guarantee of learning. Its role in teacher-training simulations may be more nuanced: presence may enhance learner engagement or usability perceptions, which support, but do not automatically produce, learning gains.
