# Knowledge Building Design: Bridging Theory and Practice in Teacher Professional Development Through Design Thinking

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In the AI era, Knowledge Building (KB) pedagogy becomes increasingly vital, yet traditional professional development inadequately prepares teachers to facilitate knowledge-creating learning environments. This study examined how teachers engaged in an online professional development course using the Knowledge Building Design (KBD) model, which integrates design thinking processes with KB principles. Teachers demonstrated high collaborative engagement and clear progression through design cycles, shifting from problem definition to lesson prototyping. Qualitative analysis revealed recursive design patterns, with teachers strategically returning to ideation when facing prototyping challenges. Lesson design quality improved significantly across all KB components, with particularly dramatic gains in actions design. The KBD model effectively operationalizes principle-based pedagogy through structured artifacts, addressing the persistent challenge of translating KB's robust theoretical foundation into practical classroom enactment. Findings demonstrate that collaborative, process-oriented professional development enabled by KBD can transform teachers' pedagogical capabilities for the AI era.

In an era increasingly shaped by artificial intelligence, education must shift from knowledge transmission toward cultivating distinctly human capacities —creativity, empathy, and collaborative problem-solving. As AI systems demonstrate growing proficiency in routine cognitive tasks, educators must foster knowledge creation and innovation, engaging students in addressing authentic, ill-defined problems that require collaborative inquiry and iterative refinement.

However, equipping teachers with the mindsets and capacities for facilitating knowledge-creating learning environments remains challenging. Current professional development models are often top-down, prescriptive, and focused on administrative compliance r ather than transformative learning (Issabayeva et al., 2025; McChesney et al., 2024). Delivered through standardized, "one-off" workshops with content-heavy curricula, these approaches leave little room for iterative experimentation and are disconnected fr om classroom practice (Bosch et al., 2025; Chiu et al., 2021). Such structures leave teachers inadequately prepared to foster innovative, knowledge-creating learning experiences.

This study addresses this gap through Knowledge Building (KB), a pedagogical approach centered on collective knowledge advancement. Specifically, we develop and examine a Knowledge Building Design (KBD) model that integrates design thinking's concrete processes to operationalize KB principles in teacher professional development, ultimately preparing educators to lead meaningful learning experiences in an AI-driven world.

## Literature review

KB pedagogy and KBD Knowledge building (KB), developed by Scardamalia and Bereiter (2003, 2006), is a theory of learning and pedagogical approach centered on collective knowledge advancement through sustained collaborative inquiry. This approach emphasizes principle-based practice, where learning is guided by foundational principles rather than rigid procedures (Scardamalia, 2002), enabling flexible adaptation to diverse contexts and emerging challenges.

While KB offers a robust theoretical foundation grounded in principle-based pedagogy, its practical enactment in everyday classrooms remains a persistent challenge (Kashi et al., 2023). This is particularly true for novice teachers who lack concrete models or scaffolding to translate abstract principles into actionable teaching strategies. This gap between theory and practice often results in inconsistent or superficial implementation. 494 Additionally, the literature provides limited articulation of how KB's "design mode of thinking" (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2014) can be systematically enacted in real-world educational settings.

To address these challenges, we propose Knowledge Building Design (KBD), which integrates the design thinking (DT) stages to operationalize KB principles in lesson design through iterative cycles of inquiry and refinement. Below, the KBD model is structured across five interconnected layers:

(1) Epistemic foundation: Three core entities—agents (knowledge workers), ideas (building blocks), and

community (collaborative venue)—create a dynamic system where workers continuously refine ideas within their learning community (Lin et al., 2014; Chen & Hong, 2016). (2) Pedagogical g uidance: Twelve KB principles (Scardamalia, 2002) guide idea-centered activities and promote deep constructivism, shifting away from traditional knowledge transmission models. Research indicates that thoughtful application of these principles fosters adaptive and innovative teaching approaches (Chai & Zhu, 2021; Gan et al., 2021; Tong, 2024).  (3) Contextual affordance: A supportive KB environment with essential social and technological infrastructure enables collaboration, idea sharing, and collective knowledge advancement by mobilizing the core epistemic entities. (4) DT p rocess: Five stages —Empathy, Definition, Ideation, Prototype, and Test —provide explicit, actionable strategies for educators to design and refine KB-aligned pedagogies through creativity, human-centered design, and iterative problem-solving (Brown & Katz, 2011). (5) Design artifact/product: eight design components conceptually derived from the KB epistemic framework (Chen & Hong, 2016) provide a framework for the KB lesson prototype to be finally designed (see below the Method section for operationalized definition of these eight design components). This KBD model bridges theory and practice, offering systematic guidance for effective professional development and broader KB adoption across diverse educational contexts. The present study This study addresses the critical gap in teacher professional development by examining an online course that integrates the KBD model. Traditional professional development models are often top-down and prescriptive, emphasizing compliance over transformative learning and leaving teachers inadequately prepared for designbased pedagogies (Bosch et al., 2025; Chiu et al., 2021; McChesney et  al., 2024). Additionally, while KB offers robust theoretical foundations, novice teachers often lack concrete scaffolding to translate these principles into actionable classroom strategies, resulting in inconsistent implementation (Kashi et al., 2023). The 10-week course designed using KBD engaged teachers in collaborative STEAM lesson design centered on sustainable development goals (SDGs). Two primary research questions guide this study:

• RQ-1: To what extent did teachers, as they engaged with the KBD model through the online professional

development course, actively engage in collaborative KB and demonstrate evolving focus across DT cycles.

• RQ-2: How did teachers’ engagement in KBD contribute to improvements in the quality of their STEAM

lesson designs?

## Student design, participants, and context

This study employed a mixed-methods approach to evaluate a 10-week online KB  professional development course involving 55 teachers (including 6 university faculty members) in collaborative STEAM lesson design centered on SDGs. The course was jointly organized by two established KB research organizations and conducted online from October to December 2023, with all participating teachers from East Asia. Implementation of KBD into teacher professional development This study operationally defines KBD as a process-oriented, epistemically driven approach to learning in which individuals engage with ideas as designable and improvable objects. KBD emerges from learners' perceptions of KB principles, their experiences within a KB environment, and their adoption of DT mindsets. Through iterative cycles of inquiry, critique, and refinement —supported by collaborative discourse and technological tools — learners co-construct understanding and externalize their thinking through the creation of design artifacts. These artifacts serve as tangible representations of idea evolution and collective knowledge advancement within a design-driven KB culture.

The KBD model was implemented in the professional development course through five integrated layers:

(1) Theoretical Foundation: Three epistemic entities—ideas, agents, and community—underpin the framework to

establish effective KB. This foundation develops a culture that empowers teacher learners to assume agency, work innovatively with ideas, and foster community knowledge advancement.  (2) Principle-based KB Guidance: Twelve KB principles (Scardamalia, 2002) provide overarching guidelines for teachers to design effective lessons 495 for eventual classroom implementation.  (3) KB Environment: Knowledge Forum (KF) technology serves as the online collaborative platform supporting the professional development course. This digital environment facilitates collaborative KB among participants to work creatively with lesson design ideas (see Figure 1). (4) DT Process: Five design-thinking steps (empathy, define, ideate, prototype, test) guide lesson development. The professional development course employed five two-week design cycles, allowing teachers to empathize with learners' needs, define themes and topics, generate and refine ideas, develop prototypes, and test lessons when possible. (5) Design Artifact:  Through engagement with the previous four layers during the professional development course, teachers develop and complete their own KB lesson templates. These lesson prototypes embody KB principles and are ready for classroom implementation (see the following section for artifact details). Figure 1 An Excerpted Screenshot Illustrating Group Interaction on KF Platform KBD artifact Eight core design components form the KB lesson template as the main design artifact in this study, conceptually grounded in the KB epistemic framework (Chen & Hong, 2016). These components span three epistemic dimensions and synthesized KB enactment. The Subjectivity dimension (assuming agency) addresses (1) student knowledge worker roles and (2) target competencies, orienting lesson design toward defining student identities and learning goals. The Objectivity dimension (working with ideas) encompasses (3) authentic problem scenarios and (4) an overall mission, guiding teachers to design real-world challenges aligned with SDGs for collective problem-solving. The Sociality dimension (fostering community) includes (5) the community's shared vision and

(6) collective resources, supporting a common goal and the knowledge advancement of the learning community.

Synthesized KB enactment integrates (7) actions to be enacted and assessed and (8) a cohesive KB story that weaves all components into a unified, motivating narrative. Together, these eight components constitute the design core of the KBD framework.

All collaborative online discussions among the 55 teachers were recorded on the KF platform. This dataset provided both qualitative and quantitative data on participant engagement and their design process development. First, a descriptive analysis was perf ormed on the recorded KF dataset to provide an overview of participants' online KB activities. Second, a qualitative coding analysis was conducted to analyze the online discussion content on KF, focusing specifically on original design reflection posts (n=199) in which teachers articulated design ideas during their own lesson development.  Each note served as a unit of analysis and was coded according to the DT 496 process stages: empathize/explore, define, ideate, prototype, and test: (1) Empathy and exploration, in which teachers investigate student contexts and explore KB principles; (2) Course theme definition, in which teachers define the lesson theme aligned with SDGs and set clear learning objectives; (3) Ideation for lesson act ivities, in which teachers brainstorm innovative teaching strategies or resources; (4) Lesson prototyping and reflection, in which teachers complete, implement, and refine the lesson design based on feedback; and (5) Test, in which teachers implement the prototype in an actual classroom to validate the design and collect student feedback. Inter-rater reliability was established by inviting two independent researchers to code a subset of the data. The resulting Cohen’s Kappa coefficients for the reflective posts of the lesson designers were 0.77 and 0.80, indicating substantial agreement.  Third, descriptive statistics were calculated to determine the frequency of the five DT stages in each design cycle.

The eight design elements serve as the KB Lesson Design Evaluation Rubric to evaluate the lesson design quality. Each component was rated on a 5-point scale, where 1 indicated underdeveloped and 5 indicated well-developed, based on content appropriateness. Paired-samples t-tests comparing teachers' initial and final versions of lessons designed around week 5 and week 10 were conducted to examine the quality improvement in the lessons designed. To establish inter-rater reliability, two independent researchers double-coded the total rubric scores for 20 randomly selected lesson designs from the total of 55. Cohen’s Kappa coefficients were 0.83 and 0.78, indicating substantial agreement.

Complementary qualitative analysis of discussion content confirms the quantitative findings across the five design cycles, as elaborated below: Empathy for initial exploration: Direct empathy discussions were minimal because teacher s designed lessons for their current classes and already possessed deep understanding of their contexts. For example, one participant noted: "The children may not yet have the computer skills to fully engage in community sharing, so I thought of switching from an online to an offline approach. I could create a large poster and have the students put 497 their ideas and suggestions on sticky notes, which would then form an interactive network" (S27). Additionally, the course's emphasis on Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) prompted participants to quickly move toward defining concrete problems rather than extended empathy exploration. Problem definition: In Cycles 1-2, discussions were heavily concentrated on the "Definition" stage (83% and 43% respectively), with participants establishing core ideas, learning outcomes, and SDG alignment. For example, one participant outlined: "My cours e theme is global warming and climate change issues. Currently, in addition to teaching basic scientific knowledge about climate systems, greenhouse gases, and global warming in class, I will ask students to play various roles to solve this problem" (S36). Ideation for lesson activities: As the cycles progressed, the focus began to shift. The "Ideation" stage posts increased, notably peaking at 41% in Cycle 4. This increase suggests that participants who encountered difficulties during prototyping returned to reconceiving and adjusting their lessons to achieve higher quality designs. One participant illustrated this: "My current idea is to turn students' road safety suggestions into English road signs, safety videos, and other creative formats. I'd love to hear suggestions from fellow teachers in the community" (S23).

Lesson prototyping and reflection: From Cycle 3 onward, over half of the participants transitioned to prototyping, reaching 65% in Cycle 3 and 82% in Cycle 5. Participants effectively translated problem definitions into detailed lesson content. One participant demonstrated this progression by outlining specific lesson design components: "Problem scenario: Taiwan lacks energy... Action 3: Find ways to resolve disagreements. Activity content: Reference successful and failed cases of using photovoltaic energy abroad to discuss success and failure factors, then find multi-win solutions based on Taiwan's context" (S36). The iterative nature of prototyping is evidenced by continuous refinement. One participant detailed three activities in Cycle 4, then refined them in Cycle 5 by explicitly linking activities to KB principles: "Relationship between the lesson and KB principles: (1) Idea Diversity: Activity 1... aims for students to discuss in meeting format, allowing students to offer suggestions on peers' ideas and achieve idea exchange" (S39).

Test: By the final cycle, some participants reached the "Test" stage, though fewer provided detailed data since the course did not require actual lesson implementation within the limited 10-week timeframe. Problem Scenarios The course begins with a narrative set in a parallel universe, which serves as a gateway to exploring the real-world issue of poverty. As students investigate social realities —such as widening wealth gaps and economic downturns —they will discover that many  people lack access to basic necessities. This scenario guides students to think: how can these problems be fundamentally solved, helping residents in struggling communities, and moving towards a sustainable future?

Community Goals The vision of this course is to create a poverty-free community. Actions

• Action 1: "Understanding the Causes and Impacts of Poverty"

Students explore poverty's multifaceted nature through real stories, videos, and news reports. Through small group discussions, they share insights and document ideas on a collaborative "Knowledge Wall." Students also document community poverty signs throu gh photos, drawings, or videos.

Assessment: Oral sharing, Knowledge Wall records, community observations, and empathy development.

• Action 2: "Experiencing Poverty Simulation Activities"

Through "resource token" simulations, students experience how limited resources constrain life choices and reflect on resource distribution factors. They share experiences and practice perspective-taking through role-playing. Homework involves developing a family savings plan. Assessment: Reflection depth, community observation quality, and savings plan practicality.

• Action 3: "Actions to Reduce Poverty"

Students select feasible solutions through group discussions —such as creating contribution boxes, organizing donation drives, or planning fundraising events. They create posters or digital stories to present their solutions.

Assessment: Solution creativity and feasibility, group cooperation, and knowledge-to-action transformation.

This study examined a 10-week online professional development course involving 55 teachers, demonstrating that the KBD model effectively bridges theory-practice gaps for developing more actionable KB lessons in teacher professional development.

Teachers demonstrated high engagement, averaging 169 notes read and 20 notes contributed per participant, with approximately 75% being build-on notes. Analysis of the five design cycles showed progression from problem definition focus (83% in Cycle 1) to lesson prototyping (82% in Cycle 5). Qualitative analysis identified key design patterns: minimal empathy discussions due to student familiarity and SDG problem framing, heavy problem definition concentration in early cycles (83%), increased ideation when encountering prototyping difficulties (peaking at 41% in Cycle 4), and sustained prototyping refinement (65-82% in Cycles 3-5), reflecting a recursive rather than linear pattern. Lesson design quality improved significantly across all KB components, with actions  design showing particularly dramatic gains (t-values ranging from-10.45 to-12.59). The "Poor Perspectives" case illustrates integration of KB principles with STEAM approaches and SDG content, incorporating knowledge worker roles, authentic problems, community goals, and structured action s with embedded assessments.

Several key findings merit further discussion: First, KBD as an innovation model for teacher professional development. This study addresses the limited empirical research on integrating DT into teacher professional development (Baran & AlZoubi, 2024; Goldman & Zielezinski, 2016) by applying KBD as a novel framework that integrates DT with KB principles as pedagogical guidance. The clear progression from definition-focused complex design proces ses with appropriate scaffolding. The iterative design patterns —particularly increased ideation during prototyping challenges —suggests that KBD's structured yet flexible framework, supported by collaborative discourse, cultivates responsive rather than rig id expertise. Second,  KBD artifact as a bridge from theory to practice. Significant improvements across all lesson design indicators (t-values from-10.45 to-12.59) demonstrate that structured artifacts can operationalize principle-based pedagogy. This offers a promising approach to addressing the implementation challenge (Hong et al., 2011; Kashi et al., 2023), where KB's robust 500 theoretical foundation often remains difficult to translate into practical classroom enactment.  The eight-component KBD artifact provides concrete guidance while preserving teacher autonomy, enabling sophisticated integration of multiple frameworks (KB, STEAM, SDG). Teachers' progression from basic to complex designs incorporating knowledge worker roles and community goals represents substantial professional growth, suggesting that embedding theory in authentic design artifacts facilitates deeper pedagogica l transformation than abstract presentations.

The 10-week timeframe limited classroom implementation, with few teachers reaching the "test" phase. Future research should examine long-term implementation outcomes, student learning impacts, and strategies for bridging professional development experience s with sustained classroom practice. Extended timelines allowing full implementation cycles—including classroom enactment, student feedback, and iterative refinement —would provide deeper insights into the complete design-to-practice transformation. This study demonstrates that the KBD model offers a promising framework for teacher professional development. Integrating DT  with KB principles shows potential for supporting teachers in developing the adaptive mindsets and design capacities necessary for facilitating knowledge-creating learning environments in the AI era.
