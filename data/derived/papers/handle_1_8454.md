# Qualitative Observations of Metacognitive Tendencies of High and Low Foreign Language Anxiety Learners

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Despite the ever-growing necessity to speak in a foreign language (FL), it remains a nerve-wracking task for many. Speaking in a FL, a cognitive task, is under the control of a learner’s metacognition, and research in mathematics anxiety have pointed to metacognition’s role in the possible onset and/or maintenance of negative affect. The link between foreign language anxiety (FLA) and metacognition, however, is currently unclear. To understand this link further, the authors combined the use of novel scenarios and a questionnaire to identify differences in metacognitive thoughts and behaviours between high (HA) and low anxiety (LA) participants. HA participants were found to write less relevant information and be more negative towards themselves, while LA participants mentioned more strategic choices and focused more on the task at hand.

## Introduction

Foreign language anxiety (FLA), an affective condition that affects both a learner’s desire to engage in speaking activities and their performance (Young, 1991), is a particularly pressing issue in our interconnected global society where many people are required to communication in a FL – English. While potential causes are plenty, such as negative past experiences (MacIntyre & Gardner, 1998) and study-abroad experience (Saito & Iida, 2015), there is also a possibility that a thinking element – a metacognitive element – may play a role in FLA. Metacognition encompasses both the knowledge people have of themselves as learners, their strategies in a given task, as well as their planning, monitor ing, and evaluation strategies (Wenden, 1999). Spea king is a skill that requires complex cognitive processes such as message planning, monitoring of oneself and others, and evaluating communication efficacy (Levelt, 1989), a s well as a skill which is also subject to self-eff icacy judgements (Wenden, 1999), thus influenced by metac ognition. While somewhat dissimilar, mathematics is likewise a subject that requires complex cognitive and metacognitive skills, and in the context of mat hematics anxiety, a lack of sufficient metacognitive skills,  or an inability to apply them properly may cause o r further exacerbate anxiety in the context of mathematics test-taking (Veenman et al., 2000). This same theoretical thread may also apply to FL speaking.

Currently, many studies in FLA rely on self-report questionnaires completed by large samples of learners to draw inferences about the nature of the issue ba sed on statistical models and correlations. While t his is often the most ethically sound and generalisable method available, there are obvious limitations. Due to this, there are still many questions unanswered about the cognitive – particularly the metacognitive – element of FLA.  In this study, the authors apply a novel approach to answer the following questions:

1. Is there a relationship between metacognition and foreign language anxiety?

2. What metacognitive tendencies do anxious individuals show?

## Participants

Participants were 68 students (mean age: approx. 19 years) enrolled in both English major and non-English major courses at a women’s university in Western Japan. Of these, high and low anxiety participants were selected for further qualitative analysis (see Results).

## Procedure and Materials

The survey study comprised three parts which were a dministered online over the course of three weeks. In the first week, participants wrote, in free prose, answ ers to six scenarios each designed to elicit participants’ metacognitive knowledge or strategy in a slightly stressful context where English should be used (Table 1). The six components of metacognition chosen were based on Wenden’s definitions (Wenden, 1998). In the following two weeks, the Japanese version of two 5-point Like rt-type scale questionnaires – the Foreign Language Classroom Anxiety Scale (FLCAS) (Yashima et al., 20 09) and a double-back Japanese translation of the Metacognitive Awareness Inventory (MAI) (Schraw & Dennison, 1994) – were administered. These self-report ICLS2022 Proceedings 1233 © ISLS questionnaires are designed to measure the extent of classroom FLA and awareness of metacognitive capabilities, respectively.

Analysis Data-driven thematic analysis was used to identify salient features of the sample’s metacognitive behaviours and tendencies (Tsuchiya, 2016). Clauses expressing sem antically similar ideas were extracted, then groupe d and labelled under headings that captured the main theme. Correlational analysis was conducted on the questionnaire data to examine relationships between FLA and metacognitive awareness to clarify a possible link between these two concepts within the participants.

## Results and Discussion

A significant correlation was found between the FCLAS and the MAI (r = -.39, p >.01), indicating a moderate relationship between FLA and metacognitive awarenes s. To select low and high FLA participants for furt her comparative qualitative analysis, the first and fou rth quartiles (105 and 126, respectively) were calc ulated. Participants whose FLA score included or fell below  105 were considered “low anxiety” and likewise, th ose whose FLA score included or exceed 126 were classed as “high anxiety”. These will be referred to as the LA (low anxiety) group (n = 17) and HA (high anxiety) group  (n = 14, due to non-submissions, this group has fe wer members), respectively. Planning Both groups showed a preference for general plannin g behaviours: researching necessary information pri or to scripting (48%), the creation of a script in Japane se (31%) and subsequent translation into English (4 2%), and memorisation of the script (35%). Notably, many of the LA group showed further aptness of strategy choice that would make the hypothetical classroom presentation easier for them. They exclusively wrote that they w ould write the script in English first (8 responses) and would create bullet points/notes (7 of 10) and identify and focus on keywords (3). Members of this group were aware of the need to make the text easier to memorise while also being aware of the week time limit. Finally, only participants in the LA group wrote about strategies for reducing ICLS2022 Proceedings 1234 © ISLS anxiety during the presentation (2) and considered the needs of the audience (5). The HA group, on the other hand, tended towards more actual planning behaviours such as choosing topic areas (6 of 11) and making summaries (5 of 8) and lacked the same strategic decisions mentioned by the LA group (see example comparison below). LA (4103): I will write out the words and phrases I circled from my script on another piece of paper, and use them as presentation notes, in case I get nervous and forget everything. HA (2222): First I will write a report and summarise my points from that. Monitoring Hypothetical monitoring behaviours common to both groups were making sure they would explain the contents (48%) and features (65%) of the lost item, as well as the places they visited prior to its loss (58%). Like the above, few strategies were salient in the HA group but themes exclusive to this group were: favour confirming possible collection of the item (2 of 3) and the need to pre pare themselves for the worst (2 of 3), while giving the reason that it may have been stolen or that the country is not as safe as Japan (example below). In contrast, the LA group mentioned how they would ask advice from the author ities (2 responses) and use gestures and visual aid s to facilitate communication (5 of 7; example below). W hile shared by a small percentage of the HA group participants, use of visual aids (2 of 7) and the awareness of the need for easy communication categories (3 of 5) were occupied largely by LA participants, who, based on the above, seem to be aware of the authoritative listener in this task and the necessity to communicate effectively. LA (3509): When going abroad, the most important thing in addition to speaking, is to make sure you convey what you want to convey by using gestures. HA (7637): Even if the purse comes back, the contents might not, as Singapore isn’t as safe as Japan.

Evaluation The analysis for the hypothetical evaluation scenario also yielded similar qualities between the two groups: asking the audience directly for feedback (61%), reflecting on their performance by themselves (29%), comparing with others’ presentations (13%), and using the evaluative information for further use (13%). Again, the HA group did not indicate any behaviours that were not seen in the LA group, although they did mention evaluation standards such as the need to be understandable from the perspective of the audience for their presentation (3 of 4). Themes occupied by the LA group participants were: the use of video (7 of 9), monitoring audience reactions (2 of 3), and the use of questionnaires (4 of 6). All of these in dicate ease around actively engaging with their per formance outcomes and the audience. Examples of comparing wi th others appeared in both groups; however, the ton e of the HA group member is far more critical.

LA (4802): I would look at presentations done by people who did well, compare what they did and why, and then see what I could learn from them.

HA (9274): I would compare with others and think carefully about what parts were not good. Task knowledge Both groups mentioned the requirement of sufficient English (23%) and communication skills (31%) in helping to decide whether to participate in the event, although these were more common amongst those in the LA group (4 of 7 and 7 of 10, respectively). In line with the above results, the LA group were found to include other qualities in addition to the shared qualities. These were: me ntioning of specific (4) and general (3 of 5) speak ing skills, affect (2 of 3), and their ability to understand En glish well enough (5 of 6). Both groups responded i ncorrectly with how they would prepare for the event, but the difference here is the LA group’s mentioning of linguistic (5 of 7) and vocabulary preparation (2 of 3) in contrast to the HA group’s preference to write about specific content (4 of 7) and preparing hypothetical materials for t he event (1), which would not likely be necessary. Person knowledge This section was a set of sub-questions that elicited details of learner beliefs. This yielded a particularly rich set of data which can be summarised as follows: the HA group viewed their overall English-speaking skills and selfimpression negatively and especially in relation to  others, with their most salient strength being com pensatory body language. They were particularly harsh about t heir weaknesses in English grammar and pronunciatio n. In contrast, the LA participants mentioned certain aspects of themselves that they viewed as problematic such as a lack of confidence and symptoms of anxiety, but also noted some positive aspects of their overall impression as well, which was exclusive to this group. They were also more focused on their ability to communicate, both linguistically and with gestures. ## Conclusion

While both groups demonstrated necessary metacognitive skills that were relevant to each scenario, presumably facilitating hypothetical success, subtle differences existed between the LA and HA groups indicating that the LA group chose more effective strategies that matched the scenario more closely and were aware of not onl y themselves but the other people they would encounter in each scenario. From these results it appears that there is a qualitative difference in the metacognitive compe tencies of HA and LA learners, and further research  should clarify whether there is a casual relationship between these competencies and its direction of influence.
