### Abstract

This study uses the dialogic mathematics peer learning framework (Ben-Dor & Heyd-Metzuyanim, 202 5) to explore how 6 th grade students learn functions in classrooms. Our findings highlight that students’ perception of authority positioned some peers, despite having the wrong answer, more expert that others and it impacted how the conceptual shift occurred.

A function is a mathematical statement that describes how two (or more) variables change in relation to each other (Cooper & Lamb, 2006). Research shows it is challenging for students to connect functions with real-life situations and locat e variables that represent the function (O’Shea et al., 2016). For example, Oehrtman et al.

(2008) reported that a common difficulty for students is confusing the visual similarity of a physical situation

with the shape of a graph that models the situation. In this regard, peer learning has been shown to  support conceptual understanding of math concepts, but not within the study of functions (Kazak et al., 2015). Peer learning refers to learning that occurs among students in which no one is more expert than the other when exploring a concept (Ben-Dor & Heyd-Metzuyanim, 2025). However, due to peer learning being characterized by no one being an expert, some researchers raise the question of how peer learning reflects students’ perceptions of authority and mathematics content (DeJarnette, 2018, 2022).  More specifically, intellectual authority refers to some students being perceived as knowers and directive authority as when some students are perceived to have the right of telling other peers what to do.  Therefore, in this paper, we ask: What role does perceived authority play in dialogic interactions among 6 th grade students focused on the learning of functions? To study peer learning, Ben-Dor and Heyd-Metzuyanim (2025) constructed a framework known as dialogic mathematics peer learning, which is a discursive lens for studying the intersection of mathematics and dialogic learning. Dialogic learning refers to critical engagement with and openness to another person’s ideas while mathematics learning is operationalized by a shift in discourse and is referred to as meta-level growth. In this paper, we study 6th grade students learning about functions during whole class discussions in two different classrooms. Although the peer interactions in our study are situated in whole class discussions, which are often supported by teacher facilitation, these interactions have minimal teacher involvement. Our study expands upon Ben-Dor and Heyd-Metzuyanim’s (2025) work, which originally studied peer learning in a designed laboratory condition. Thus, we contribute to the extant research  by using this framework in situ classrooms  to analyze student learning of functions in real-world classes.

Our data comes from a larger multi-year project involving schools outside of a large Midwestern city in the United States. The project focused on enhancing teachers’ facilitation of student-to-student talk by positioning students as resources for each other who can guide intellectual explorations in an interaction (Pitvorec et al., 2022). The data for this study includes a total of 6 video recordings of a 3-day lesson on functions taught by the same teacher in two different 6th grade classes (ages 10-11). On the first two days, students worked in groups of 3 to 4 to match eight graphs (ranging from 1 to 8) with eight corresponding real-world scenarios (ranging from scenarios A to H) (see table 1). On the third day, students presented to the whole class with their day-2 groups to share their consensus with everyone and see how others agree or disagree with them.  In our data, we found that several students conflated graph 5 with scenario B because the U-shaped curve in the graph resembl ed a physical half-pipe. In order to analyze students’ understanding of functions we transcribed the video data and identified whether their peer interactions reflected (a) meta-level growth in students’ understanding of functions and (b) openness and critical engagement with one another’s ideas. To identify a shift in discourse we analyzed students’ use of vocabulary (e.g., independent/dependent variable), visual mediators (e.g. graphs), endorsed narratives (e.g. justifications for claims) and routines (e.g. assigning the independent variabl e to the x-axis and 2605 dependent variable to the y-axis). Based on how students used one or a combination of these discourse features to reason about the function either as a physical situation or an abstract relationship between two variables, we coded each student turn as reflective of an abstract or non-abstract understanding of functions.  We developed coding criteria based on the type of reasoning students used. If a student justified their graph choice based on visual similarity, personal experience or general familiarity with a half-pipe we coded the turn as reflecting a non-abstract understanding. In contrast, if a student reasoned about the relationship between speed and time, we coded the turn as reflecting an abstract understanding. Openness to and critical engagement with peers’ ideas were evident in student interactions with each other. We op erationalized students’ openness to peers’ ideas as occurring when students addressed, listened, and responded to each other. We operationalized critical engagement as occurring when students provided disciplinary reasoning to their peers. Further, w e operationalized perceived intellectual authority when in a group of three, peers repeatedly elicited answers, confirmation, or feedback from only one student, and if the student with perceived intellectual authority positioned themselves as a knower. Finally, we operationalized directive authority by observing who took the lead and issued roles and responsibility for others. W e also discussed and compared our data analysis with a research team member who took field notes during data collection to check whether our interpretations of perceived intellectual and directive authority aligned with her observations. Perceived intellectual and directive authority: Across both classrooms, perceived intellectual and directive authority positioned some students as more expert than others in peer learning. In both classrooms, one student was perceived to be the knower and took the role of telling others what to do. For example, in classroom 1, Jerry (all names are pseudonyms) decided that his incorrect mathematical reasoning should be the group’s answer, even when another student had contributed the correct mathematical reasoning. Taken together, these moments show that even within the same grade-level groups, perceived authority shaped participation and supported whose ideas were taken up or ignored in peer learning. This also limited opportunities for some students to articulate their reasoning or engage with peers’ ideas  across both classrooms and made their learning less observable in the interaction.

Issues of authority are not a part of the existing dialogic mathematics peer learning framework (Ben-Dor & Heyd-Metzuyanim, 2025), however they were apparent in both classrooms.  In classroom 1, the emergence of meta-level growth, which is conceptual understanding, was facilitated by students being critically engaged and open to peers’ ideas. But whose ideas actually shaped that growth? We interpret these findings in line with previous research showing that perceived intellectual authority has a significant role in whose ideas count among peers (Cobb et al., 2001; DeJarnette, 2022). Similarly, DeJarnette (2022) reported that in a group of four 7 th grade students working  on a geometry problem  no one engaged with the student who had conceptually advanced contributions but consistently challenged her intellectual and directive authority.  While we acknowledge the contributions of the dialogic mathematics peer learning framework  (Ben-Dor & Heyd-Metzuyanim, 2025), we argue that the existing definition of peer learning operationalized by no one being more expert than the other contradicts students’ perceptions of intellectual and directive authority in classrooms.
