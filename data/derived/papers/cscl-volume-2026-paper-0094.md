# Partnering Through Ceremony: A Multimodal Design for Collaborative Healing and Learning

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This poster presents an early-stage ceremonial design for collaborative learning rooted in Indigenous methodologies and relational accountability. Through multimodal documentation and AI assisted poetic co creation, the work explores how ceremony can support healing centered learning across generations and technologies. The poster highlights design commitments, a simplified conjecture map and emerging expressions that invite discussion about how socio-technical systems can participate in relational meaning making.

This poster offers a design contribution that explores how Indigenous ceremony supports collaborative learning through rhythm, movement and relational accountability. As an Indigenous person, I led a community of CU Boulder alumni, staff and current students of many ages and backgrounds through this ceremony. Grounded in a research practice partnership with alumni and students at CU Boulder, the work introduces a multimodal ceremonial design that integrates slideshow imagery, video documentation, soundscape and poetic co-authorship with AI. The intention is to support healing centered learning through rhythm, movement and shared presence. Reviewer feedback emphasized the need for clearer design rationale, stronger articulation of AI’s role and a more explicit connection to CSCL. This poster responds by presenting a simplified conjecture map and highlighting aspects of the work that will benefit from discussion with conference participants. Contribution and purpose of the poster This work contributes a ceremonial design framework that treats collaboration as a sacred and iterative process involving humans, technologies and more than human kin. The poster highlights three areas for dialogue: how ceremonial intention shapes multimodal learning design, how AI can participate in relational meaning making and how intergenerational participation supports healing centered collaboration. Rather than presenting a full evaluation, the poster offers early expressions that illustrate how the design activated embodied pathways for learning.

Design commitments and modalities The design is guided by relational accountability, cultural responsiveness and embodied knowledge transmission. Ceremony shaped the rhythm and flow of the learning environment. Each modality was selected to activate a different dimension of ceremonial learning and the table below summarizes how each one supported the ceremonial intention.

Figure 1 Simplified Conjecture Map AI co-authorship protocol AI was engaged as a dialogic partner to support rhythm, metaphor, and iterative reflection during the creation of the ceremonial poem. The interaction followed three guiding principles. Prompts were shaped by ceremonial context, symbolic coherence, and cul tural integrity. AI‑generated lines were evaluated for cadence, breath, and emotional resonance. The researcher guided meaning, ensured cultural safety, and treated AI as a participant rather than a neutral tool. This approach invites discussion about how socio‑technical systems can support ceremonial learning without extracting or flattening Indigenous ways of knowing. Emergent expressions These expressions are not presented as findings but as early manifestations of the design. Unedited audio preserved trust and subtle vibrations that carried emotional significance. Children, elders, and peers played drums, clay flutes and shakers together, affirming communal learning. The AI‑assisted poem invited shared breath and reflection during a collective reading. A quiet “I love it” surfaced in the video transcript, signaling emotional resonance. These moments illustrate how ceremony can support collaborative meaning‑making across modalities and generations.

This poster invites conversation about how AI can be engaged as a relational co‑participant, how ceremony can function as a collaborative learning technology, how multimodal documentation supports healing‑centered design, how intergenerational participatio n shapes collaborative learning, and how CSCL might expand to include more‑than‑human kinship.
