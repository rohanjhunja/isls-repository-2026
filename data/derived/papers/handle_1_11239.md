# The Role of Surprise in Learning: Insights from Facial Expression Analysis

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Surprise is a cognitive-emotional phenomenon that triggers attentional and cognitive mechanisms and, as such, can impact learning processes and outcomes. To examine how surprise emerges and affects learning, we captured naturally occurring instances of surprise in 28 students during a VR-based simulation. Using facial expression analysis, our findings challenge prior research by showing that surprise significantly aligns with negative emotions—such as fear, anger, and disgust—and correlates with lower knowledge test scores. These negative emotional states and outcomes may stem from the VR environment's high cognitive demands, which potentially hinder the metacognitive processing required to resolve explanatory difficulties and confusion. Further research should investigate the scaffolding needed to support learners effectively in VR-based contexts to resolve cognitive and emotional disequilibrium.

## Introduction

Surprise is one of the basic emotions shared univer sally across human cultures (Ekman, 1992), along with happiness, sadness, disgust, fear, and anger. Unlik e other emotions, surprise has a neutral valence, m eaning that it is not inherently positive or negative, but acts  as a transitional emotion that varies rapidly depending on the context. Within the realm of learning, surprise is categorized as an epistemic emotion, triggered by a  sudden and unexpected disruption of information. This disrupti on causes a brief cognitive interruption, redirecti ng attention towards evaluating the new stimulus. Such  rapid cognitive reassessment may enable individuals  to quickly reevaluate their environment, prompting them to seek new information and resolve cognitive incongruities, which then leads to better memory of the event (Brod et al., 2018; Foster & Keane, 2019). However, as D’Mello and Graesser (2012) note, surprise can also lead to confusion which, if unresolved, may escalate into frustration when learners are una ble to arrive at  a resolution, leading tonegative learning outcomes. In support of this, Pezzo (2003)  and later Foster and Keane (2015)  conceptualized surprise as a metacognitive signal that indicates difficulty with generating explanations. They proposed a two-stage model: the initial stage involves surprise arising from a violation of expectations o r unexpected novelty, often referred to as prediction errors. The second stage, explanatory su rprise, occurs when individuals struggle to form a coherent explanation for an unexpected event, triggering a m etacognitive process focused on resolving this expl anatory difficulty.

Despite the potential significance of surprise as a cognitive-emotional phenomenon  in the learning process, there is relatively little research examining its role in learning, particularly within complex environments such as those facilitated by virtual reality (VR). Most existing studies focus on simpler contexts, such as one-onone tasks where participants are prompted to make educated guesses about certain phenomena (Brod et al., 2018). This research seeks to fill this gap by investigati ng the role of surprise in a complex, self-directed VR learning environment,  which has been shown to be highly emotionally and cognitively stimulating. Responses to surprising events are dynamic; therefore, to capture their dynamic changes over time, in the current study we use facial expression recognition  combined with self-reports. Facial expressions of surprise typically involve ra ised eyebrows, widened eyes, a slight jaw drop, and facial stillness or brow lowering, reflecting an immediate response to unexpected events and the cognitive effort to ma ke sense of them. By using an automatic facial action coding system to identify and categorize facial expressions based on specific facial action units at a 30 Hz frequency, we aimed to explore how surprise is associated with other basic emotions and cognitive processes, while self-reports were used to validate the association of surprise on learning outcomes.

## Research questions

(1) How does surprise impact the learning process, as evidenced by facial expression analysis and self-reports?

(2) How does surprise impact learning outcomes, as measured by a knowledge test?

## Research design and procedure

This was a prospective experimental study with an intervention component. The intervention involved a desktopbased virtual reality (VR) simulation designed to support nursing profess ionals in medication administration procedures. The simulation featured a three-dimensional (3D) virtual hospital, enabling student s to acquire and practice both procedural and declarative knowledge related to medication administration. The simulation was designed to reduce medication administration errors, which are a common issue in clinical practice  (Dubovi, 2022).

## Participants and instruments

A total of 28 sophomore nursing students from a university volunteered to participate in the study. The study was conducted following approval by the university’s ethics committee (#0008124-1). To assess students’ knowledge, we used pre-post knowledge tests, and to assess students’ surprise dynamic (including other basic emotions), we used facial expression recognition and self-reports. Below, we elaborate on these instruments. The Medication Administration Test (MAT). MAT was used to assess nursing students’ declarative understanding and practical applications of medication administra tion guidelines. The test was validated in previous  stud ies (Dubovi, 2022; Dubovi et al., 2017)  and consists of 13 multiple-choice items. Analysis of the MAT using Cronbach’s alpha yielded a good internal consistency score of.74. Facial expression recognition (FE).  The Affectiva Affdex algorithm, provided by the iMotion s 10.0 (https://imotions.com) platform, allowed this study to collect and extra ct the 7 basic emotions of joy, anger, surprise, contempt, fear, sadness, and disgust (Ekman & Rosenberg, 1997; Kulke et al., 2020). Facial expressions analysis was at a 30 Hz frequency and was collected  for the overall learning experience.  The algorithm uses the Facial Action Coding System, which identifies ‘acti on units’ and then classifies them to provide the l ikelihood that each of the 7 emotions will occur.

Self-report of basic emotions.  Participants self-reported their experience of seven basic emotions —joy, anger, surprise, contempt, fear, sadness, and disgust —by indicating the extent to which they felt each em otion "at this moment" on a 5-point scale. These ratings were collected at three intervals throughout the VR learning experience. Cronbach’s alpha demonstrated good internal consistency, with a score of.733.

## Findings

Descriptive statistics (Table 1) show seven basic emotions that were captured  throughout the overall learning experience using continuous facial expression extraction and self-reports, which were administered three times during learning. As apparent from Table 1, surprise did not prove a dominant emotional response as registered by participants in both self-report and facial expression data. The Kruskal-Wallis H test of facial expressions revealed that participants experienced significantly (χ2(6) = 76.924, p <.001) more joy than other emotions  (Table 1). Similarly, one-way repeated measures analysis of self-reports revealed that the most predominant emotion was joy (F (6, 216) = 74.43, p <.001).

## Discussion

The primary goal of this study was to investigate t he impact of surprise triggered during VR-based simulation learning on both the learning process and outcomes. Surprise, a transitional emotion, is neither inherently positive nor negative; rather, it fluc tuates rapidly depending on context. Our findings reveal ed that surprise  within a simulated VR-based context, as reflected in facial expressions, frequently sh ifted toward a negative emotional (a) (b) (c) state associated with fear, anger, and disgust. Additionally, our case study highlighted that negative emotions such as anger, fear, and sadness, often preceded the expression of surprise, suggesting a complex interplay between negative emotional state and surprise emotions during the learning experience. Our results also indicate d that surprise was negatively associated with learni ng outcomes; participants who experienced surprise more frequently tended to achieve lower scores on the knowledge test. This fi nding contrasts with some previous research suggesting that surprising stimuli can have cognitive and emotional benefits for learning, often associated with more thorough information processing (Brod et al., 2018; Foster & Keane, 2019). Two factors may explain this discrepancy. First, the immersive VR learning environment likely demand s higher cognitive resources, potentially hindering t he metacognitive processing needed to resolve expla natory difficulties and confusion  (D’Mello et al., 2014). Monteiro and Sibbald (2020)  also challenged the belief that surprise generated through simulations  enhances learning by demonstrating that introducing unnecessary complexity without appropriate scaffolds can impede learning outcomes. Second, unlike prior studies that intentionally triggered surprise through specific t asks, our study observed naturally occurring moment s of surprise, allowing for exploration of its effects within a more authentic learning context. This study suggests the importance of surprise as an indicator of learners’  unresolved confusion and frustration, which should be further studied.
