# Advancements in Equity Research in Computing Through Feminist Embodiment Computation Framework

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This symposium introduces Feminist Embodied Computation (FEC), a framework advancing equity in computing by centering epistemological change, identity, and embodiment. Five papers enact FEC across teaching and learning: reframing code as culturally and politically situated discourse; tracing a CS teacher’s journey where e-textiles and material histories make embodiment epistemic; presenting an Indigenous Hawaiian learner’s “rightful presence” through community-centered data science; modeling physics through dance to broaden what counts as rigorous knowledge; and theorizing subversive computational literacy that challenges dominant values of efficiency and abstraction. Collectively, the session demonstrates concrete practices and theoretical moves that honor lived experience, emotion, and culture as central to computation, offering actionable pathways to transform who participates in computing, how knowledge is made, and what computing can become.

Computing culture has long been shaped by historical power structures that define who belongs, whose ways of knowing are valued, and how legitimate participation is recognized (Cheryan et al., 2009; Margolis & Fisher, 2002). Among these power structures, gender disparities offer a well-documented example of how exclusion operates. While women were central to early computing work, the professionalization of the field in the mid-20th century transformed it into a space marked by masculine norms and exclusiona ry practices (Campbell-Kelly et al., 2023; Haigh & Ceruzzi, 2021; Mullaney et al., 2021, Chapter 6). As Ensmenger (2015) describes, computing culture became associated with traits like abstraction, disembodiment, and technical mastery that align with masculine ideals and adopted practices that systematically excluded women and other marginalized groups. This gendered shift did not occur in isolation; it emerged alongside broader societal forces, leading to an environment where women often feel they “do not belong” (Cheryan et al., 2017) and where their ways of engaging with computing, such as relationality, creativity, or embodied practices, are devalued or ignored. The result is a computing culture that perpetuates systemic inequities and shapes both participation and identity development in ways that continue to marginalize women. Gender is only one istantce of the problem. These dynamics also affect other historically marginalized groups, including those who navigate multiple, intersecting forms of marginalization (Margolis et al., 2012). This marginalization affects individuals’ p articipation in the technological and digital movement (Rodriguez & Lehman, 2017). We rapidly notice two main gaps: (1) people with noncomputing backgrounds experience an economic disadvantage (Rodriguez & Lehman, 2017); (2) technological advancements are developed without considering those groups, which reproduces social inequities (Vakil, 2016) 2460 These deeply embedded cultural dynamics inevitably shape learning environments as well as professional practice. In the example of gender, formal computing education often reproduces the same masculine, competitive, and exclusive values found in the broader field (Misa, 2010). For instance, Cheryan et al. (2009) demonstrate that even physical classroom spaces, filled with stereotypical “techy” décor, send signals about who belongs, which can lead women to feel less interested in and less identified with computing. Power and oppression in computing create a cycle of reproduction. On one hand, marginalized students are marginalized by cultural norms that restrict their participation in the field. These same norms are perpetuated as the ones that enter the computing space, internalize dominant value systems, taking on roles that ultimately reinforce their exclusion. For example, socialized into the same cultural frameworks as men, women may internalize gendered expectations that shape how they participate, thereby perpetuating the very structures that exclude them (Hooks, 1988).

To break this cycle of social reproduction, we must fundamentally shift how computing is taught and experienced. This symposium builds on ideas from the Feminist Embodied Computation (FEC) framework, which aims to dismantle oppression by creating space for marginalized students and transforming the field of computing itself. This approach emphasizes honoring students’ embodied knowledge (Solomon et al., 2022), emotions, and cultural practices (Shaw et al., 2023) as legitimate forms of computational engagement, rather than treating them as peripheral or secondary to conventional computing. As D’Ignazio and Klein (2020, p. 61) argue in Data Feminism, “Oppression is real, historical, ongoing, and worth dismantling.” FEC is a framework, introduced in this symposium for the first time, that presents three ideas as a way to offer a shift in what counts as knowledge and how it is produced within the field: epistemological change, centering identity, and feminist embodiment. These ideas are not separate pillars but intersecting threads that push the boundaries of what computing could become, who belongs, and how learning can unfold in more expansive and equitable ways.

Epistemological change Epistemological change in computing education refers to a fundamental shift in what counts as knowledge and how it is produced within the field. Rather than framing computing solely through technical abstraction, this shift centers alternative ways of know ing, such as storytelling, emotion, imagination, embodiment, and cultural experience, as legitimate and valuable. It challenges dominant narratives that position computing as neutral, rational, and detached, and instead affirms that computation can carry emotional, political, and cultural weight. Work by scholars such as Shaw et al. (2023) and Lee et al. (2022) illustrates how computing becomes more expansive and inclusive when students are invited to bring expansive forms of engagement into the learning process. These shifts are not just about adding more voices; they are about transforming the epistemic foundations of the field itself by honoring diverse identities, lived experiences, and creative practices as central to computational engagement.

Identity FEC recognizes identity as inseparable from computing participation, shaping how students engage with, understand, and position themselves in relation to the field. Rather than treating identity as a background context, this approach holds it as an active part of knowledge creation. Students bring their intersecting identities, cultural histories, and ways of being into computing spaces, and in doing so, they reshape what computing can look like. When learners engage through their own cultural narratives, t he work becomes deeply personal, creative, and political. Identity is not something learners leave at the door; it is part of what gives their engagement meaning (Desportes et al., 2022).

(Feminist) Embodiment Feminist embodied computing views the body as central to how learning unfolds, valuing movement, expression, and voice as meaningful forms of participation in computing. Embodiment invites students to bring their whole selves into the classroom, making space for culture and identity to shape their engagement (Solomon et al., 2022). These dimensions, often overlooked in computing, carry powerful possibilities for connection and resistance. By affirming the role of lived experience in knowledge-making, feminist embodiment expands the scope of who and what counts in computing.

Symposium overview Our commitment to inclusion is about transforming the field from within. In Matters of Care, Bellacasa (2017) frames (re)claiming as the act of challenging dominant power structures from within, rather than accepting them as fixed or seeking alternatives outside the system. Applied to computing education, this requires acknowledging 2461 its exclusionary foundations while working to transform them through ongoing, situated efforts. It means positioning students as co-creators of the culture, values, and practices of computing. In this symposium, each presenter will build on one or more ideas from FEC, weaving those ideas into a cohesive whole. By presenting these works together, we aim to showcase the advancements in both theoretical and design perspectives of the computing field, as well as its commitment to equity and inclusion. Together, the five papers exemplify how the FEC framework can be enacted across diverse contexts of teaching, learning, and theory: (1) Daniel, Vogelstein, Vogel, and Hoadley reframe code as culturally and politically situated discourse, expanding teachers’ epistemic resources and opening computing to youths’ languages, histories, and communities.  (2) Fields, Kafai, and Goode trace a computer science teacher’s journey in which e-textiles and material feminist “intra-action” make embodiment central to what counts as CS knowledge, illustrating how teacher identity and craft reshape classroom computing. (3) Tofel-Grehl presents the story of Tahliah, a young Indigenous Hawaiian learner, to show how community-centered data science and “rightful presence” transform STEM from an adversarial to an identity-affirming, community-serving practice.

(4) Champion, Solomon, and Lammey extend the notion of embodiment through dance-based modeling, where

Black and Latinx youth engage deeply with physics ideas while affirming identity through multimodal expression.

(5) Finally, Horn argues for a subversive computational literacy that challenges dominant CS values of efficiency

and abstraction, positioning code as a medium for self-expression, cultural rupture, and feminist embodied empowerment.

Paper 1: What conversation is this code a part of? Bethany Daniel, Lauren Vogelstein, Sara Vogel, Chris Hoadley Computer science (CS) as a field and computer science education (CS Ed) are rapidly growing spaces grappling with ever-shifting questions as emerging technologies shape society and learning. Traditionally, code as a medium within CS and CS Ed has been framed as a neutral, decontextualized tool leveraged for a limited range of purposes (e.g., Benjamin, 2019; Vossoughi & Vakil, 2018). However, this understanding of code perpetuates inequities related to computing and learning to compute (e.g., Margolis et al., 2017; Perrigo, 2022). By contrast, reframing code as a resource for expression creates inroads for computing to become a tool for societal transformation (e.g., Ko et al., 2020; Ryoo et al., 2020). Furthermore, emphasizing coding practices as literacies foregrounds the communities who converse with these languages for a variety of purposes. On the Participating in Literacies and Computer Science (PiLa-CS) project, we have asked teachers learning to incorporate CS into their classrooms, “What conversation is this code a part of?” (Vogel et al., 2020). This deceptively complicated question has helped us work with teachers to discuss the communities of discourse code is and can be a part of and how those communities overlap with the many discourse communities youth are and want to be a part of. Drawing on this symposium’s framework that calls for reimagining epistemological understandings of code and computing, we  describe how our open-source educational resource for CS educators, Advancing Educational Equity in Computer Science, disrupts traditional perceptions of how computing knowledge is created, taught, and used by reimagining code as part of many different conversations (Computer Science Educational Justice Collective, 2025). We synthesize how we pedagogically presented some of these conversations in Table 1 below. We hope the specific moves we make in our book prompt teachers to see code as part of many different relevant and meaningful conversations for youth, leading teachers to create ways of opening up the valued epistemological resources in CS Ed. 2463 Paper 2: A computer science teacher’s personal and professional journey with equity, different ways of knowing, and humanizing computing in teaching CS Deborah Fields, Yasmin Kafai, Joanna Goode In this paper we apply the FEC framework to computer science (CS) teachers’ personal and professional journeys. Like students, teachers tooneed to bring their whole selves into the classroom, making space for culture and identity (Rojas et al., 2025). Further, CS teachers are essential to reshaping the practice of CS, often taking on the role of leading outreach beyond classroom walls to develop courses, recruiting and retaining marginalized students, and educating school leaders about equity in CS (Fincher et al., 2019). In this paper we take a holistic perspective of one CS teacher to inquire into one  teacher’s development in computer science and how that journey was rooted in her own personal, family, educational, and career history. The data for this study consisted of 13 semi-structured interviews (625 minutes total), collected over eight years (2016-2024) as a part of several research projects in which CS teacher, Lydia, participated first as a teacher, then as a facilitator, and fi nally as a collaborative designer of innovative CS lessons for high school courses. We applied case study analysis (Merriam, 1988) to this dataset, utilizing chronological, thematic, and narrative analysis to identify themes across years of Lydia’s CS teaching. Lydia member-checked the full analysis. Here we share some key themes from Lydia’s journey as a CS teacher. At the time of this writing, Lydia (a pseudonym) was a secondary (grades 9-12) nationally board certified teacher in a metropolitan center of the West Coast of the United States, who as of Summer 2024 had 20 years teaching experience, including 12 years teaching computer science. Lydia’s own history with CS reflected her own encounters with inequitable trends within CS.

Issues of equity in CS arose first in Lydia’s college experience, where poor course design and practices (c.f., Fisher & Margolis, 2002) left a lingering negative impression on Lydia. As a curious mathematics major, she took “CS 101 with, you know, 500 boys at [University]. Like what was I thinking? …it was not pleasant at all” (2016). While Lydia persevered through a second semester of CS, the courses left her with very low confidence that persisted even to 2024. At the same time, Lydia thrived on trying new things, and after teaching mathematics for five years found herself ready for “a little change” (2016). When her school district pushed for teachers to pursue additional training in data and computer sciences, Lydia volunteered for training and participated in the Exploring Computer Science (ECS) professional development, which introduced teachers to equitable pedagogies and different ways of knowing CS (e.g., Goode et al., 2014). This gave her a more humane entryway back into CS, and she became the first ECS teacher at her school with the “ highest population of Black students in [our city]”.

A few years later, Lydia signed up to do a unit on physical computing with electronic textiles (Fields et al., 2018). Her interests in doing e-textiles stemmed in part from a family history with crafting: “My mom worked in like a sewing factory, I had to help sew for my allowance money [laugh]... And so I was sewing from since I was a kid” (2016). Sewing was both in her history and family identity and also an everyday creative practice in her household. E-textiles also became a way to incorporate different kinds of knowing into her CS classes: “[E]textiles was a pivotal moment….” that reshaped how she introduced students to CS (2023). The type of pedagogy Lydia developed in teaching the e-textiles over multiple years led her to decenter her role as instructor even more than before: “ it's less of me. So less direct teaching in e-textiles” (2023). She brought many types of materials into the classroom, posted student projects on classroom walls and in school hallways, and encouraged personal expression through physical computing projects. Over time she also developed greater confidence in programming and reshaped computing at her school through a combination of personal outreach and CS teachers are key to making space in CS for marginalized students and transforming what CS can mean for students and as such we need to attend to teachers’ own identities and epistemologies. As a woman of color, Lydia faced many of the same issues of exclusion and inequity that shape CS in many areas (Margolis et al., 2008). Lydia's integration of e-textiles into CS teaching illustrates how embodied practices reshape what counts as computational knowledge. Material feminist theory, particularly Barad's (2007) concept of intra-action, helps clarify this dynamic. Rather than separate entities that interact, intra-action describes how bodies, technologies, and meanings are mutually constitutive. When learners (teachers and students) sew circuits while drawing on family crafting histories, computational understanding emerges through the intra-action of fabric, code, bodies, and cultural practice. This differs fundamentally from viewing materials and culture as additions to predetermined technical content. Instead, the phenomenon of learning itself arises through these entanglements. From this material feminist perspective, embodiment becomes epistemologically central to computing, not merely a pedagogical supplement.

2464 Paper 3: A case example of centered identity in feminist embodiment Creating a diverse STEM workforce and education pipeline is a critical issue of national health and security. Without a diverse STEM workforce, diseases that impact women and minorities will remain understudied, underfunded, and uncured (Tofel-Grehl et al., 2022). Because early exposure to meaningful and personally relevant STEM experiences fosters interest and increases STEM degree attainment (Tai et al., 2004), supporting young people’s STEM interest through meaningful and localized experiences is essential.  However, to do this in equitable ways, one must ensure that learners are wholly and rightly present in their learning spaces (CalabreseBarton & Tan, 2019). To do this specifically for young women within computing requires even more nuanced and thoughtful considerations. Doing so requires the intentional disruption of the normative focus and discourses of computer science, a notoriously hegemonic male discursive space (Convertino, 2020).

In this paper we share findings of the ways in which community centered data science (Tofel-Grehl et al. 2024) can support STEM identity development with a specific focus on computing.  We explore the case of Tahliah, a young indigenous Hawaiian woman. Tahliah spent many of her preteen summers between the age of 10-12 camping on the Mauna Kea with her family to protest the building of a new 30 meter telescope by the NSF (Maly & Maly, 2021). As she described during our work together, Tahliah felt that STEM w as bad for Hawaii. She felt that science and the broader STEM community were active sources of harm to the community and spaces of Hawaii. Engaged in rightfully present making (Calabrese-Barton & Tan, 2019), Tahliah’s connection to and identification with STEM and computing evolved over the course of a summer learning to code maker projects that she conceptualized as in service to the community. Over the course of her learning, Tahliah found that computing could serve her goals, her community, and her identity authentically. Through this discovery, her relationship with STEM and computing changed from one that was adversarial to one that was meaningful and relevant to her. We see STEM, previously absent from her valued set of experiences and spaces, move to a personal position of power as tools to support her personal goals. Through her case we examine the ways that embodiment through rightful presence can foster shifts in identity and engagement for young women. Paper 4: Supporting equitable engagement in STEM through embodied modeling Dionne Champion, Folashadé Solomon, Machienvee Lammey Black youth have struggled to gain access to physics, with their  bodies often positioned as non-conforming in STEM learning spaces. Our research explores what becomes possible when bodies typically framed as problematic are re-positioned as resources for meaning-making and youth are granted access to their full range of modalities–words, vocalizations, facial expressions, gestures, and full-body movement– to explore and express physics ideas. Scholars have examined how cultural, creative, and kinesthetic practices can be leveraged for learning and engagement (e.g., Kolovou & Kim, 2020; Payne et al., 2021; Castro et al., 2022; Allen-Handy et al., 2020). Recent work shows that embodied practices such as dance-making provide youth with powerful ways to communicate scientific understandings through movement, storytelling, and improvisation (Champion, 2018; Solomon et al., 2022). This four-site design-based research study with community-based dance centers engaged Black and Latinx youth (ages 11–18) in embodied explorations of energy, wave behavior, and resonance through dance-making. We found that when their embodied practices were centered, youth experienced authentic connections to physics content, engaging deeply with the material in ways that supported them in sharing their developing ideas, and co-constructing understanding of complex concepts. In the Embodied Physics Learning Lab, dance-making invited youth to engage with content through their full expressive range to develop and reflect on ideas iteratively and collaboratively. Youth were encouraged to create dances that integrated ideas they were learning and questions they engaged with, and to choose movement styles and music that they felt would help them best represent their ideas. This provided opportunities for synthesis, foregrounding reflection, feedback, revision, and collaborative int erpretation, all critical to STEM sense-making.

To understand how dance-making functioned to support learning and engagement, we collected video data of interactions during dance-making activities and employed interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995), analyzing how participants built upon, challe nged, and supported one another’s contributions to advance collective understanding. Through this analysis, we found that centering embodied practices led to deep engagement, authentic connection to and understanding of complex physics ideas. 2465 Embodiment was a meaningful form of sense-making Embodied dialogue allowed youth to express ideas in multimodal ways that resonated with their experiences and identities. The process required translation of physics concepts into the kinetic language of the body, understanding the meaning potential of semiotic signs (Kress, 2011), negotiation and decision-making about which aspects of ideas to communicate and which resources were most appropriate to represent pertinent aspects of phenomena. Embodied engagement with the content raised new questions for youth, allowed them to participate as they learned and work through ideas before fully mastering the vocabulary. It also made room for relational and culturally affirming expressions of science learning. Youth dances about physics were embedded with identity and culture Youth participants utilized their expertise, brought their experiences, their cultural and embodied resources to the process of developing explanations of the science. Their representations, as well as the processes youth engaged in to create and share them, incorporated cultural references, practices, and ways of knowing. Inviting and attending to multimodal and embodied expression broadened what counted as a valid demonstration of knowledge and affirmed and validated their identities and resources. Centering dance-making led to fundamental shifts in what counts as knowledge The opportunity to use gesture, music, spatial arrangements, storytelling, and emotion to express physics ideas deepened and broadened the connections youth made to concepts. For example, youth drew on their personal experiences in social interactions, (e. g., “when people get on your nerves and they just say small stuff,” “when someone whispers in your ear and talk about you that it makes so mad that you want to explode,” “it’s so low effort for them to make you that angry”) to explain the concept of resona nce, the idea that large amplitudes can be created with little effort.

By positioning dance-making as a form of embodied modeling, this work offers an expanded vision of what counts as scientific practice. It enacts a feminist ethics of modeling in which knowledge is produced through embodied inquiry, collective dialogue, and  reciprocal attention. The Embodied Physics Learning Lab demonstrates that physics can be learned and reimagined through movement, care, and relation. For the learning sciences, this reframing invites us to consider how feminist orientations to embodiment and listening candeepen our understanding of sensemaking, making learning both rigorous and just. Paper 5: The case for subversive computational literacy Michael Horn Scholars have long argued that we should think about computation as a kind of literacy (e.g. Berland 2016; diSessa 2018; Kafai & Proctor, 2022; Vee, 2017; Wilensky, Brady, & Horn, 2014). This frame broadens the idea of computational thinking and CS education in important ways, and has implications for how we engage with computational tools, methods, and representations in educational settings. In other words, if we start to view computation as a kind of literacy, we have to think much bigger than teaching kids how to code (as the other talks presented in this symposium beautifully illustrate). DiSessa, in particular, argues that true literacies have societywide impacts that shape work, communication, and learning. Literacies are marked by genres and subgenr es, specialized communities and subcommunities. Further, the process of becoming literate implies a profound transformation of identity (“I am a literate person” or “I am computationally literate”). This process necessarily transpires over a long period of  time (years not months) through a combination of many kinds of learning experiences, both in school and out. Obtaining fluency requires cognitive skill development, proficiency with tools and technologies, immersion in communities of practice, and authentic experiences in which literacy serves as an essential tool for communication, thought, and self-expression. Reflecting on diSessa’s articulation of computation as an “infrastructural” form of literacy, existing at a society-wide level, it’s notable that many genres of literacy have their origin in historical moments of subversion, where the canonical forms are intentionally bent and broken, either as a conscious act of defiance and protest, or as a less conscious reaction to exclusionary systems imposed by the dominant culture. There are countless examples in literature, poetry, art, music, and fashion, where artists / activists break free of academic strictures and define entirely new forms of expression. These moments of rupture are especially important as epicenters of empowerment, where marginalized communities take up a collective voice rooted in cultural identity.

Coming back to computational literacy, which has clearly struggled with an exclusionary culture for decades, we can start to ask important questions: what are the dominant literary forms and values? As 2466 computer science is clearly defined as a domain of engineering and science, there is a heavy emphasis on mathematical elegance, efficiency, neatness, and fault tolerance. What would it mean to subvert those forms and values? What if code was less about functional utility and more about self expression and performance art? Could subversion also become an epicenter for empowerment and new forms of cultural expression? These questions are only partially formed, and the answers less so. Drawing on interdisciplinary onto-epistomological frameworks, the symposium elevates vibes as an educational construct that is consequential to developing equitable learning with students from non-dominant communities.  Through robust analyses of teacher and student interactions that occurred across distinct out-of-school learning ecologies, the respective papers in this symposium: (1) offer a working theory of vibes based on related concepts across disciplines (2) describe teaching and learning experiences in which the colloquial use of vibes supported the development of intuitive pedagogical approaches and; (3) make a case for the continued investigation of vibes in the learning sciences as legitimate dimensions impacting how we teach, learn, and engage in world-making. Symposium overview As educational inequalities continue to be reproduced by structures and practices that employ deficit lenses to the cultural repertoires of non-dominant communities, this paper calls for new sensibilities that attune to the rich cognitive, relational, and affective resources that students leverage to navigate and transform learning ecologies. Our symposium answers this call by naming and examining a key but undertheorized dimension of teaching and learning we recognize as vibes. Drawing on interdisciplinary approaches that leverage Black cultural expression, Chicana feminisms, and sociocultural theories of learning and development, the respective papers in this symposium provide theoretical frames and empirical work that invite deeper reflections of the various dimensions that constitute vibes, as well as their role in teaching and learning. The papers collectively suggest  that vibes are consequential for how communities of learners develop epistemologies, construct relationships, and participate within learning ecologies. The papers also examine how, for Black and other racialized communities, vibes have served as resources for communicating felt experiences within an imagined colorblind society (Miles, 2022). Along these lines of inquiry, Washington et. al (2024) conceptualize a vibe as “a feeling and an intuition about a space and derives from vibrations that signal movement, energy and life” (p. 287). In their research, the authors characterize soulful, healing, and abolitionists vibes as integral to the c reation of community spaces that enable Black and other non-dominant communities to feel safer and more protected from the structural violence that they/we navigate in our everyday lives. We build on these notions of vibes to think about how educational researchers and learning scientists may attune to the “energy,” “vibrations,” and overall feel of out-of-school spaces to co-design learning ecologies that dignify students’ felt experiences. Toward this end, the conceptual piece in this symposium (paper 1) offers a definition of vibes as the unquantifiable, often unspoken yet felt dimensions of interactions and spaces that require cognitive labor to interpret, make sense of, and respond to. Through these theorizations of vibes, paper 2 appropriates the notion of nepantla (Anzaldua, 1987) to reflect on how vibes are co-constructed through tensions and in the in-between spaces of teaching and learning. Similarly, paper 3 draws on the Vygotskian notion of perezhivanie to offer a pedagogical framework to better understand how educators develop critical intuition through the process of learning how to feel and attune to the dynamic cognitive and experiential labor of students that cannot always be captured through conventional and de-personalized teaching methods. Additionally, given the racializing processes that are reproduced in educational contexts, it is important to note that vibes are created within social contexts that unevenly assess whose emotions count and whose experiences are treated as valuable. In this regard, vibes can become sacred in moments of grief, joy, and refusal wherein individuals call forward that which compels their spiritual proclivities —often undergirded by cultural attunement. In the classroom, these sacred considerations provide spaces to learn communal practices of responsibility and care (paper 4). 2470 Our conceptualization of vibes aims to subvert discourses of professionalism rooted in whiteness as the norm that govern what emotions can be expressed and by whom, how racialized feeling is read and regulated, and how these dynamics shape everyday life in schools (Ahmed, 2004; Matias, 2016). As such, the papers in  this symposium offer theoretical, methodological and pedagogical contributions that offer educational researchers a deeper engagement and attunement to vibes. The scholars in this symposium aim to dignify (Espinoza & Vossoughi, 2014) the lived and felt experiences of students through conceptual and empirical contributions, holding the view that, in addition to the overt learning processes present, there are covert dynamics at play affecting how learners and educators construct knowledge and relate to their ecologies. Through these contributions, we aim to dive deeper into the covert dynamics we are naming as “vibes”  that are omnipresent in learning experiences. Specifically, the purpose of this p resentation is three-fold: (1) to describe learning experiences in which vibes emerge; (2) to offer a working theory of vibes based on research and our personal experiences using and witnessing the colloquial use of vibes; (3) to make a case for the continued investigation of vibes in the learning sciences as legitimate dimensions impacting how we teach, learn, and world-make. Format This symposium will consist of individual paper presentations, a discussion among presenters and audience members facilitated by one co-chair, and commentary from our discussant reflecting on the conversation about vibes as inspired by the papers and how vibes can be situated in the learning sciences moving forward. Each of the four papers explore how vibes come into relief in the context of a specific learning experience. However, we present this as a unified collection of pieces developed through ongoing collaborative inquiry and discussion. We build on our previous writing examining preliminary conceptualizations of what vibes are (Aceves et al., forthcoming) to offer a more realized theory-in-progress describing vibes and how they influence learning. We theorize vibes simultaneously as an experience one can observe, tap into, or reflect on, and as an ongoing process of sensing and responding. Paper 1 operationalizes our robust theory of vibes by exploring agentic student learning as leveraged by student attunement to vibes. Paper 2 traces vibes by accounting for the invisibilized labor of care using Chicana Feminist Methodologies and interactional analysis. Paper 3 reflects on the critical intuition educators developed to attune and co-construct vibes in a  tinkering after-school program. Paper 4 offers a methodological intervention through a composite case analysis in which a middle school Latine navigates the contours of vibes they hold sacred as an ongoing process of attunement and reifications. Taken together, we provide theoretical and empirical lenses for the study of vibes as felt sensations constituted by layers of aesthetic and affective experiences, physical and social atmospheres, and sociocultural histories projected by an environment or people.

## Significance

In seeking pathways of education for world-making, we offer solutions from within aspects of education that have yet to be fully explored: vibes. Although “the vibes” of a learning community or program and its impact on learners is rarely considered in academic research, this idiomatic and prevalent concept deserves more thorough investigation. While some parts of the human experience may never fully be named or concretized, they are nonetheless part of the social fabric that is woven by learners, educators  and scholars in the great endeavor of education. There is also an ontological quality to the work of attending to vibes akin to sociocultural theories of learning as social transformation of both learner and environment; it is an attention to that which is in the process of becoming. It is therefore incumbent upon practitioners to pay attention to the aspects of learning that mediate the experiences of those directly involved.

Through theoretical explorations and empirical analyses, the session grapples with the notion that vibes are part and parcel when it comes to teaching and learning. While this symposium brings us closer to a firm conceptualization of vibes, there is still much to be explored. How we tend to vibes as educators and scholars, and how we invite learners to tend to them has consequences for how learning is experienced, especially for students from non-dominant communities. Whether we collectively build toward a future with optimism, imagination, and utopia as our guide may depend on which vibes are nurtured. Paper 1: Vibes; a site of student agency and negotiation of power Jorge E. Garcia

## Purpose and theoretical framework

This empirical manuscript endeavors to serve as a provocation, a call for greater attunement from educators and researchers alike to the labor and power implications of felt dimensions, or vibes, of interactions in learning contexts. Here we are not necess arily talking about solely emotional aspects, but more broadly about the barely 2471 quantifiable yet perceptible aspects of learning as experienced by learners. For the purpose of this manuscript, vibes will be operationalized as the unquantifiable, oft unspoken yet felt dimensions of interactions and spaces that require cognitive labor t o interpret, make sense of, and respond to. It can also be seen as the liminal and generally non-verbal space where negotiation of positioning, affective labor, and learner agency can occur. Learning contexts, and their social milieu, can sometimes be confusing, with curriculum, instruction and learning being overt, hidden, and elusive simultaneously. Learners are not only tasked with making sense of the material presented, but all the subtext relevant to social learning dynamics. It is helpful to consider vibes as akin to affect for the moment. Ahmed’s conceptualizations of affect and affective economies (Ahmed, 2004) are helpful here; affect circulates around, through, and with people, objects and symbols, having material impact for all sharing space, time and activities. Furthermore, affect is social and moves between people, objects and symbols and is a site of negotiation of power. “...[E]motions are not simply "within" or "without" [bodies] but … they create the very effect on the surfaces or boundaries  of bodies and worlds." (Ahmed, 2004). Similarly, I posit that vibes are a social phenomena shared among learners that encompass a broader spectrum of constituents than emotions, or affect alone; using a vibes theoretical lens (as theorized by the members of this panel) requires (but is not limited to) attunement to labor (interpretive, relational, and cognitive), social or relational aspects (including safety, belonging, and care), place, material and/or physical aspects, temporal aspects, elements of refusal and the sacred, and literacies and/or legibilities functioning on cultural, neurodiverse, or political planes in addition to the socio-historically informed present.

This research was conducted in partnership with a community based writing center serving primarily students of color in the 3rd-12th grade. I initially conducted interviews with educators, and joined a student workshop as a (co)Participant Observer, serving as an editorial mentor to a student in the program through the duration of the 12 week workshop. I developed field notes from my jottings as (co)participant observer, and used these in turn to develop dialogical field notes with educators participating in the workshop. All field notes were coded via nVivo and used to develop memos as a way to make meaning of observed phenomena as grounded inductive research. Thematic analysis of qualitative data was used to identify patterns and themes about student agency, learning processes, and affective engagement with process and subject. In analysis of the following vignette, I attuned to the broad, mostly unspoken elements of the interaction and learning setting. This vignette is drawn from an ethnographic field note of a writing workshop, for third through fifth graders in which I was a (co)participant observer. In this vignette, the lead teacher, Ms T, is leading the eight students in the workshop on a lesson about titles and their importance in creative short story writing. She is supported by two assistant teachers, and one (Maria) engages in an intervention that is my focus in this manuscript. The lesson was received in an excited fashion by the students, who are exuberant and animated in their exchanges with Ms T. This energy, though welcomed by Ms T, appears to cause Maria some trepi dation and she intervenes initially by simply naming students she perceives to be doing too much. As all she did was call out names while Ms T continued the lesson, her first intervention was largely ignored. “Students at this point were standing, kneeling on their chairs, pacing or had moved to the cushioned benches adjacent to the central table. Ms T continued her lesson, seemingly unperturbed by this movement. Maria intervened again, this time more assertively, and focused on one in particular, “Jayden, I don’t even know what you’re doing.” Jayden had been moving a bit more than others who were standing. He responded to the lesson with his whole body, and paced back and forth in place. Maria applied the “teacher stare”. Jayden paused, glancing between Maria and Ms T, internally negotiating his preferred mode of participation, on the one hand welcomed by Ms T, on the other feeling the pressure of Maria’s focused attention. He continued to pace and participate, though slightly more subdued.” This moment encapsulates a number of elements that applying a vibes lens makes visible. Firstly, Ms T and her team expended labor in designing and implementing the lesson in a way that invited spirited participation by students, presenting an intentional and inviting vibe. Secondly, but in some ways more importantly, a vibes lens helps us name the elements of the pedagogical environment that Jayden considered as he adjusted his participation. He was participating just as his peers were, yet drew Maria’s att ention, prompting her to address him in a tone of disapproval, via the non-question “I don’t even know what you’re doing,” and her ensuing glare. I recall this moment clearly, as I could almost see the gears turning in Jayden’s head as he weighed his 2472 participation, Ms T’s continued lesson, his peers’ continued participation, and Maria’s utterance coupled with her stare. Jayden reduced his movement slightly, but did not sit down nor did he explain his actions. He continued to participate, calling out answers and moving in his enthusiasm, contributing to the vibe of the room. Though subtle, Jayden made very quick determinations about the nature of the space, his place in it, his expressive choices, and his desire to continue participating in a manner he felt best suited his goals. In this mostly non-verbal dynamic between him and Maria, Jayden negotiated felt dimensions of moment-to-moment interactions and asserted his humanity by claiming agency, and resisting control or carceral logics of containment. Jayden was claiming a role in co-constructing, alongside his peers and the teaching team, the kind of learning space he preferred and enacted the invitation, extended by Ms T and her pedagogical designs and choices, to be fully present. A vibes lens makes more visible Jayden’s cognitive, relational, and positioning labor as he opt s to maintain his participation and enthusiasm for learning. As educators and researchers committed to student agency conducive to world-making anew, it is imperative we place moment-to-moment interactions in contexts that include affect, and less-verbal felt dimensions.

Paper 2: My gut said yes: Attuning for vibes through nepantla, labor, and care Wendy Barrales, Olivia Ortiz

## Purpose and theoretical framework

This paper explores what we might learn when we attune for vibes across space and time through the lens of care as invisibilized labor. Critical educational research cites relationality and embodied knowledge as important facets for more just learning spaces (McKinney de Royston et al., 2020), key aspects of care that support the creation of vibes – a liminal space centered on relationality that is felt and experienced. We conceptualize vibes through Gloria Anzaldúa’s theory of nepantla (1987), a space betw een multiple spaces but nowhere fully all at once. Because of this in-betweenness, vibes have blurred contours that are textured, somatic, and embodied. However, care as a form of labor that sets/creates vibes is often overlooked making vibes invisible and rarely accounted for through colonial frameworks. We define care as "a political act, incorporated into how research is conducted, how social service organizations are founded, and the strategies used in social movements for sovereignty, self-determination, and freedom” (Castillo, 2024).

Our personal political commitments as critical researchers of color from the Chicane/Latine diaspora, acknowledge the histories both violent and built through communal knowledge, that shape the vibes of these spaces. Thus, we center our bodies and attune for the felt dimensions of learning because “the brown body, with its multiple and often oppositional intersections of sociopolitical locations, must be acknowledged in its centrality in creating new knowledges” (Cruz, 2001, p. 657). Throughout this paper, we use Chicana Feminist Epistemologies (CFEs) (Calderon et al., 2012) and decolonial methods to explore how the planning and negotiating of experiencing vibes reflect the often invisibilized labor of care. Building with theories that attend to learners as multidimensional, and learning as connected to cultural, social, and emotional facets of the human experience (McKinney de Royston et al., 2020) we see vibes living within interactions of politicized care (McKinney de Royston et al., 2017) that are authored within a community of practice. When attuning for vibes in our data, we elucidate the invisibilized labor of care and learn how vibes shape learning within/across participant interviews from an informal afterschool STEM program and through mentorship wit hin our research team. We traced vibes across various spaces and timescales, focusing on how participants accounted for care by attuning to how vibes in the interview were co-created through micro moments of care across space and time. First, we use two methods grounded in CFEs where we seek to provide “new narratives that embody alternative paradigms in education research” (Calderon et al., 2012, p.515): autohistoria -teoría (Bhattacharya, 2020) critical autoethnographies rooted in our cultural intuition, and pláticas, politicized intimate conversations rooted in oral traditions (Fierros & Delgado Bernal, 2016). We supplement critical autoethnographies with interaction analysis of video data from the interviews in order to attend to how vibes were co-operatively constructed (Goodwin, 2017) through the interview in order to attend to the micro moments of care. Our analysis desettles Eurocentric research by centering the brown body (Cruz, 2001) through el conocimiento del cuerpo (Juarez Mendoza & Aponte, 2021) encouraging us to be guided by and forefront the knowledges of spirit. After video recording participant interviews, the researchers created autohistoria- teorías on the following prompt: What are you sitting with from this interview and where do you feel it on your body? We traced our autohistoria -teorías for in-betweenness and vibes by accounting for somatics and micro moments of care initiated within the interview and across our years-long partnership. Then, using conocimiento de cuerpo, we scanned and mapped our bodies using a  body Meenadchi’s sensations list and analyzed these scannings and autohistoria-teorías through pláticas. 2473 Our video data comes from an interview held in a classroom on a private university campus with Bianca and Valeria, two queer Latinas employed at Daphnia, an informal afterschool program focused on positive STEM experiences of belonging. There are also two research assistants, one postdoctoral researcher, and one of the coprincipal investigators, sitting around a square-shaped table. The interview’s contents focus on Bianca and Valeria’s personal histories and relationships to science, and their trajectorie s at Daphnia. As the research team prepares for the interview, the moment Bianca and Valeria enter through the glass doors, laughter fills and reverberates through the room. Prior to the interview, the researchers greet one another and briefly catch up on recent events discussed in previous conversations. Even as the first question is asked, the postdoctoral researcher pauses the conversation to ensure that each person at the table has been introduced, interviewees and researchers alike. It is then in this question that Bianca and Valeria first share their entwined histories, where they attended the same undergraduate program, taking the same classes, working with the same advisor, and then recommended to the same internship program at the learning space they work at today. Though Bianca moved through these experiences three years prior to Valeria, they now find themselves at the same time, with Bianca supervising Valeria.

As the interview continues, both the researchers and interviewees negotiate in real time their questions and responses. With careful consideration, we share that the interview culminated in a follow-up question by one of the research assistants, ‘What brings you to social justice?’ To which both Bianca, and then Valeria, share their familial histories steeped in activism, and their lived experiences moving through the social and political ramifications that follow their immigration stories. In these moments, it felt as if we, as researchers, were getting to know Bianca and Valeria as individuals and attending to their responses as entwined, possibly due to the depth of their relationship. Beyond analysis with the video data, researchers noticed patterns of f elt reactions on their body when stories of immigration were shared, warmth on their face when hearing about their mentorship, and their gut saying “yes” when hearing laughter throughout the interview and throughout their post interview pláticas. Emergent findings position 1.) vibes, co-created and present in that space, as spaces of possibility and transformation through micro moments of care seen that are interactional and multidirectional and 2.) pláticas as a critical methodology documenting the many la yers of vibes as care across space and time in learning spaces. This preliminary study promotes vibes as important units of analysis by repositioning the somatic, and what is felt and experienced, as imperative aspects for more just learning environments. In doing so, we recenter relationality and care as an attempt to upend quantitative metrics that simply cannot capture the depth of vibes rooted in care. As we account for vibes, we resist the hegemonic tools of research to measure, quantify, and extract, and instead shift our research to include that which must be felt and experienced — that which cannot be fully measured.

Paper 3: High-school educators attuning to vibes: Weaving critical intuition with tinkering pedagogies Meg Escudé, Edward Rivero

## Purpose and theoretical framework

Perezhivanie is a Vygotskian concept that describes the emotional experiences that develop from a child’s interaction with their environment that shape cognitive and developmental outcomes (Vygotsky, 1994). For Vygotsky (1994),  perezhivanie encompasses the lived experiences of children within and across learning ecologies and details a deeper understanding of the unique needs, motivations, relationships, and emotions of students within these contexts. This paper examines the development of the adolescent and young adult facilitators of an informal after-school tinkering program as they participate in weekly debrief sessions. Analysis revealed that educators often used affective and relational language when discussing their perspectives on student experiences, needs, and growth in the tinkering space. As such, Vygotsky and later scholars theorizations of perezhivanie offer a useful lens for youth educators’ sense-making around the felt dimensions, or “vibes” of the program. Orienting to vibes also contributed to the development of debrief as a space of self-reflection, helping young educators locate their own subjectivity in the interactions they observed and narrated. In the authors’ long history of designing equity-oriented tinkering spaces, we consider belonging as key to supporting students’ creativity and ingenuity, especially those from non-dominant communities (Escudé et al., 2020). We have observed that students who feel stressed, unwelcomed, or marginalized in these spaces will often have a more difficult time developing projects that are meaningful to them and their communities. Increasingly, scholars are recognizing that emotions are inseparable from the epistemic processes of learning for both educators and students (Jaber, 2018, Munson & Baldinger, 2023). We argue that attention to the dynamic and ephemeral dimension of 2474 learning ecologies holds potential as a source of pedagogical development and ethical perception toward conditions of equity (Vossoughi et. al, 2021). We put forth the notion of critical intuition  as a pedagogical sensibility that high school educators developed to perceive the subtle and intangible dynamics of learning ecologies, such as body language, gut-feelings, shifts in playful participation, and overall vibes of the learning ecology, to better affirm and dignify students’ learning experiences. Drawing on this framing, the paper examines how adolescent after-school educators attuned to the vibes of their learning ecology to develop, iterate, and transform their pedagogical approaches with students. The paper examines the following research questions: What dimensions of the learning ecologies did youth educators identify as key to the development of vibes that supported youth learning and creativity? How did after-school educators' reflections on the vibes of their learning ecology inform their own pedagogical development and relationship-building with students? We draw on ethnographic data gathered through a participatory design research (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) study on the pedagogical practices and mentorship of high school facilitators in an after-school tinkering program. The primary data sources for this paper are centered on the end-of-day debrief practices and include high school facilitator journal entries, semi-structured interviews, and audio recordings of weekly debrief sessions.  These sessions included adult educators, researchers, and high school and young adult facilitators. Debrief started with 10-15 minutes of quiet journaling which provided opportunities for high school educators to engage in individual reflexive noticing (Rainio & Hofmann, 2021) and pedagogical sense-making (Munson & Dyer, 2023). This was followed by collaborative group discussion, guided by lead educators and researchers. Audio recordings captured facilitator accounts of students’ experiences with activities, impressions of their affective and social experiences in the space, and the facilitators’ own developing ideas about teaching and learning. Interviews were conducted at multiple points throughout the program to capture the developing pedagogies and sense-making around how high school facilitators contributed to and interpreted the learning ecology. The shared reflections and collective sense-making of these sessions resulted in an intergenerational co-construction of strategies to support students’ creative endeavors. Youth educators had all once been students in the same program and regularly drew on their own memories and developmental trajectories as they attuned to the needs and experiences of the younger students. All debrief participants were encouraged to contribute to iterations on the design of the learning ecology to ensure students’ creativity and sense of belonging was supported. Through thematic analysis, we identified recurring codes in high school facilitators’ reflections on the tinkering afterschool learning ecology and how they perceived students were experiencing the program. Analysis of debrief sessions enabled us to examine how facilitators understood their role in the various dimensions of vibes and affective experiences of learning by focusing on the reflective practices of narration, re-narration, and re-envisioning of talk and interaction (Philip, 2019). While focusing on the narrative shifts in facilitators’ perceptions of student experiences, we noted a recurrence of the use of relational and affective terms when noting student growth or shifts in participation. Facilitators often gauged student interest, increased con fidence with tools and techniques, or shifts in social integration by narrating their perceptions of the students’ felt experiences in the program. In the instances we are calling re-narration, adults and peer facilitators often offered alternative explanations leveraging their own epistemic empathy (Jaber, 2018), and in moments of re-orientation, participants adopted new pedagogical orientations based on the collective sense-making gained from debrief.

The study found that after-school educators frequently described student participation in affective terms to articulate perizhivanie – the felt dimensions of the tinkering program as experienced by students. These conceptualizations became key aspects of high school facilitators’ pedagogical sense-making processes as they reflected on their role in shaping, shifting, and co-constructing vibes with and for students.  As they developed their reflexive noticing through journal entries and discussion, high school facilitators began to sharpen their attunement to affective and relational dynamics to support students’ tinkering and making processes. For example, a youth facilitator shared that he had written in his journal about a student who he noticed was more e ngaged socially that day: “And she was talkative with me  while we were writing. I was reflecting on that and thinking, did I do anything that prompted her to be more talkative with me …I really just think it comes down to her being more comfortable here because I don't think I did anything.” The facilitator’s narration of the student’s social participation and their corresponding self-reflection was met with a subtle re-narration offered by an adult educator: “She had a really good week last week, and I think that's probably maybe contributing to it. 2475 She's growing in her confidence. She was really excited about putting her name in the circuit board when we went to the shop together.” Here, the facilitator’s use of language describing the social, emotional and relational aspects of the student’s interactions with him served to signal that he had noticed a shift in her participation in the space. The educator then offered additional narr ative accounts of interactions with the same student that served to elaborate on what “being more comfortable here” might mean in the context of the student’s intellectual engagement with the projects and herself as a learner. Exchanges such as these led to a collective attunement, or what we are calling critical intuition,  where attention to seemingly undefinable aspects of student experience, or the vibe of an interaction, was treated as relevant to our growing understanding of the teaching and learning processes in the program.

In addition, through intergenerational reflections and experimentation with pedagogical strategies, high school facilitators developed practices that mediated their development as intuitive educators. Through collaborative reflections and joint-activity, high school educators developed more reflexive sensibilities around their own subjectivity in the learning ecology and how students were simultaneously responding to and shifting vibes. As such, part of the development of intuition was also experimentation with new pedagogical strategies and further reflections on the effects these strategies had on the creative processes and felt experiences of students. We argue that attunement to the relational and subjective dimensions of vibes offers educators a powerful discourse for the iterative and experimental nature of designing for equity in tinkering spaces. Moreover, this study has significant implications for how educators can build critical awareness of the intangible aspects of learning ecologies by honoring their intuition and coupling it with pedagogical experimentation over time. Paper 4: Storying sacred vibes: Building on enduring knowledges from and among marginalized youth Nallely Aceves, Aukeem Ballard

## Purpose and theoretical framework

Education scholarship continues to highlight the vast array of knowledge offered in education beyond the normative ‘academic’ knowledge and skills (McKinney de Royston et al., 2020), and highlight utility of emotions (eg. SEL) and care. Yet, scholarship that examines the subtleties and nuances housed within and across ancestral wisdom as stories of time-honored cultural medicine remains scant (Kimerrer, 2013). In concert with recent scholarship at the intersection of cultural emergence and a “life force” th at “vibe connotes a feeling and an intuition about a space and derives from vibrations that signal movement, energy and life” (Majer, 2019; Washington, et al., 2020), we seek new inquires into how educational spaces confer a co-constitutive space of meaning-making in which both students and educators might navigate vibes of a most sacred nature. We define sacred vibes as those the soul is called to honor over prescribed curricular tasks, outcomes, and learning objectives. Sacred vibes are the felt space between stories and bodies, unnamed by nature, as sacred vibes. Often these vibes are intimately tied to cultural and ancestral engagement (i.e. de-colonial), and thus beyond the habit of codification which often takes its cues from coloniality. That is, sacred vibes are those vibes that hold a space within our selves, and across subjects, that those individuals and communities hold with reverence. While vibes are defined as a distinctive feeling or quality capable of being sensed (Merriam-Webster, 2024), we submit that some vibes in educational spaces are sacred in nature due to the content therein,  associated contexts, or constitutive parts of the vibes that remain, at times out of necessity, undefinable by normative tools. In analytical affinity with scholars on the aspects of emotions that not only do things, but have a mediating effect between subjects and within a cultural political contexts (Ahmed, 2004), we engage sacred vibes as a process unraveling the stories, dispositions, values, wisdom, and meaning-making tools that students and educators often engage as a process of healing against extant marginalization and sometimes trauma. Leveraging the generative scholarship of critical affect vis a vis cultural politics of emotions (Ahmed, 2014) together with cultural vibes (Washington et al., 2024) to explore the cultural politics of sacred vibes in classrooms/curricular engagement. Using a composite case from our collective teaching experiences, this paper brings together the cultural politics of emotions (Ahmed,

2014) with storying as a method of meaning-making (Gravett, 2019) for a more just educ ational and research

experience for youth.

Drawing on our combined teaching experiences in public schools, we developed a fictionalized, composite case of Trinity, a middle school Latine youth as a way to examine and unearth the yet to be articulated constitutive parts to working with sacred vibes in learning spaces. We use storying (Gravette, 2019) as a method to explore 2476 sacred vibes that are constituted through youth engaging in practices and narratives built across generations in response to sometimes traumatic experiences. We offer a methodical and pedagogical intervention to honor what scholars and educators have yet to fully articulate: the sacred vibes of learning ecologies that work to (re)imagine and safeguard students’ and educators’ humanity inside subtractive schooling. Borrowing from Trauma-Responsive Pedagogy (TRP) (Casimir & Baker, 2023) as a tool to analyze t he composite case, this paper offers an invitation to education scholars and practitioners alike. We engage TRP as an effective tool to explore the sacred vibes that "take residence between bodies" (Ahmed, 2010) of Trinity and their teacher. Such analytica l tools draw strength and guidance from calls to suspend damage-centered research practices (Tuck, 2009) by offering discursive and humanizing means to highlight culturally mediated emotional meaning-making processes (Ahmed, 2014). Ultimately, this offering stands to constitute sacred vibes of the most human order towards worldmaking from within the worlds of youth. Trinity is tasked with writing about a cherished memory. Trinity’s writing centers around the cultural and emotional experience of being in the hospital with a family member that is preparing to transition away from this life, and into the role of ancestor. Trinity’s writing does not follow the prompt exactly as described.  In turn, the teacher honors the writing and takes Trinity’s lead on how they would like the writing represented in the class (e.g. hung up with others, spoken about in discussion, etc). They work together to decide how to support Trinity in their processing of both the original experiences as well as what it means to write this story outside of Trinity's mind. Whereas TRP may invite us to “turn wounds into wisdom” (Casimir & Baker, 2023), we leverage Ahmed’s consideration of social-structural aspects of emotions to meditate on the space between student and teacher that confers potential wisdom through the sacred vibes that take residence. Bringing storying to TRP offers tools to examine sacred vibes of experiences that may be shaped, (re)imagined, and inscribed between space, time, and personhood through the practice of storying (Gravette, 2019). Gravett’s methodological framework can be leveraged alongside TRP and Affect (Ahmed, 2014) to capture and honor the sacred vibes with which marginalized youth are coming into schools. The turn towards full complexity empowers students to have power over the narration, re-narration, and imagination.

In the emotional world of Black and Brown youth, Trinity’s case creates space to position youth and educators themselves as leaders who reject exploiting or hiding the stories of pain and joy of Black and Brown youth. First, we find that agency can play a critical role in the constitution of sacred vibes. Trinity and the teacher choose to participate in the emotional exploration beyond the initial assignment. While the container itself (e.g., the assignment) elicits memories of sacred stories for Trinity, the series of collaborative and agentic decision-making processes constitute a broader sacred vibe of storying as practice and pedagogy. Storying offers a space in which Trinity and the researcher/practitioner co-mediate the process and (re)imagine the purpose of the learning space towards healing (Casmir & Baker, 2023). This relational space becomes the container of sacred vibes as development in process. The resulting space can be thought of as the liminality of vibes that are at once central to the learning process and, perhaps necessarily, never fully concretized. In turning attention away from bifurcating storying as method and affect as the cultural politics of emotions. Here, both Trinity and the educator make discrete and thoughtful choices that center and honor the delicate nature of the story itself. The space between the bodies involved, as Ahmed articulates, is more than a series of choices. The evolving sacred space created between Trinity and their teacher, from within the choices made in response to a story of trauma, is the sediment of cultural politics of emotional experiences that cannot be justly articulated through words alone, such as grief, healing or even love. Yet, upon reflecting on the case, one can readily conjure those all-but-stated moments when one is presented with a vibe. That dynamic space of “knowing” the vibe of extant emotionality between and among bodies  indexes a sacred nature worth a space of meditation and examination. For historically marginalized youth across the globe, such meditation becomes solutions towards more dynamic learning spaces from within the youth themselves.

Second, beyond managing emotions (i.e. SEL), Trinity and the teacher’s choices constitute a rebuff to the narrow scope in which emotions in education have been articulated through normative research and educational practice. Whereas SEL may guide towards n aming and managing emotions, TRP highlights the centrality of self-knowledge in Trinity, and their teacher’s decision to regard emotions as “not simply within or without, but that they create the very effect of the surfaces or boundaries bodies and world” (Ahmed, 2004). The sacred nature of the emotions and choices therein function to affect the surfaces and boundaries of this new sacred terrain. Trinity’s case offers a (re)imagining of how storying can be leveraged to illuminate the sacred vibes between the individuals in a learning community. In highlighting the dynamic space of boundaries and surfaces between bodies, Ahmed’s articulation of the cultural politics of emotions offers a bridging between the 2477 phenomenology and the sociality of bigger emotional experiences. In building on Ahmed’s notion, Trinity and their teachers’ unspoken and spoken interactions bring affect into conversation with the unnamed, felt sense between them that not everything needed to be named in order for it to confer meaning and wisdom. In leveraging these multiple frameworks, we position an epistemological and ontological orientation that honors the full complexity of youth unfurling into their whole selves inside traumatic experiences (Casmir & Baker, 2023) that are often codified by a higher order of their revered cultural, spiritual, and ancestral gifts of wisdom that cannot always be clearly defined.. We invite the use of new analytical tools that index meaningmaking from within stories (Gravette, 2019) and the space between bodies. The resulting cross-cutting axiology invites the authors of stories themselves to excavate and honor the complexity of discourses that confer meaning and inscribe complex cognition. In heeding Tuck’s call for a “moratorium on damage-centered research” (2009), an investigation of sacred vibes from within the brilliance of youth storying offers new pathways of world-making towards sustainable education solutions for a globe in crisis.
