# Coherence in Online Micro-Credential Professional Development

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Coherence supports meaningful teacher professional learning. While micro-credentialing offers flexibility, it may lead to fragmentation. This study (N=541) identified three dimensions —Curricular, Transitional, and Epistemic. Findings suggest a multidimensional structure, with Role –Cluster Alignment as the strongest predictor of Curricular Coherence.

Empirical studies consistently highlight coherence as a central condition for successful teacher education and professional development. Coherent knowledge   structuring in teacher knowledge coherently enables teachers to connect elements of learning in a logical and continuous manner (Anderson & Taner, 2023; Stamovlasis et al., 2013). Against this backdrop, the question arises of how coherence can be maintained within micro-credentialing programs, which offer flexibility and autonomy but also risk producing fragmented knowledge (Gan, 2019).

## Theoretical background

From a theoretical perspective, coherence is commonly defined as the degree of connection, continuity, and consistency among elements of knowledge and program components, forming a meaningful whole (Hammerness, 2006). Prior research has identified coherenc e as an important component of effective teacher professional development (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017; Nordine et al., 2021). In this context, studies suggest that professional development programs that integrate theoretical knowledge with practical expe rience, and that are guided by an overarching vision of teaching, are more likely to support meaningful learning processes (Hammerness & Klette, 2015). The assessment of coherence in such programs is often based on multiple sources of evidence, including self-report data and field-based analyses (Canrinus et al., 2017). Micro-credentialing environments introduce a distinct context for examining coherence, as teachers independently construct their learning pathways. Previous work suggests that coherence may emerge not only from formal program structures but also from learners’ active integration of external requirements with their own conceptions (Hammerness, 2006; Darling-Hammond et al., 2017). At the same time, engagement in multiple, loosely connected learning units has been associated with the risk of fragmented knowledge that is not fully integrated or transferable (Gan, 2019). From a curricular perspective, coherence has been described as involving connections across content areas and the translation of theoretical knowledge into practice (Shulman, 1986; Hammerness, 2006). Within flexible professional development frameworks, teachers may take a more active role in shaping their learning trajectories (Darling-Hammond, 2020), which positions coherence as an outcome of how learning choices are constructed and connected.  Coherence was conceptualized as three dimensions: Curricular (Hammerness & Klette, 2015), Transitional (Smeby & Heggen, 2014), and Epistemic (Heikkilä & Hermansen, 2024

## Research context and data sources

The study was conducted within the Israeli Ministry of Education’s micro-credentialing platform, where teachers select 10 short learning units each semester to construct personalized professional development pathways. The sample included 541 teachers. Data were drawn from course selections, expert evaluations of cluster alignment (Delphi), teachers’ professional roles, and written explanations.This triangulated design integrated behavioral data, expert judgments, and reflective meaning-making. Curricular Coherence was measured use Cluster Average (dispersion/concentration) and Cluster Alignment (expert-rated alignment). Transitional Coherence was assessed through Role–Cluster Alignment. Epistemic Coherence included Internal (reasoning across courses) and External (alignment between explanations and selections) components, enabling a multidimensional assessment of learning pathways.

2722 Analytical approach To address RQ1, descriptive and inferential statistics were used to evaluate the five coherence indicators.

To address RQ2, PCA (Varimax) examined the underlying structure of coherence.

To address RQ3, correlations and hierarchical regression were conducted, with Cluster Average as the dependent variable, predicted by the remaining indicators.

RQ1, descriptive statistics of the study variables are summarized below. Curricular coherence, based on the Cluster Average (M=2.73, SD=1.37) was moderate, with only 33% of teachers concentrating an average of three or more courses on the same topical cluster. Cluster-combinations Score (M=1.23, SD=0.63), was moderate with 40% of teachers choosing clusters combinations which were rated as highly coherent. We note high variability in Role–Cluster Alignment (M=1.69, SD=2.21), while 51% of teachers selected at least one course aligned with their role, only 13% of the teachers chose five or more courses aligned with their role. These findings indicate gaps in the alignment between teachers’ professional roles and their course selections. Epistemic coherence was moderate in the internal dimension (with 61% of explanations coded as articulated) and higher in the external one (73% of explanations were coded as aligned with actual selections). RQ2, the results of an exploratory Principal Component Analysis indicated the presence of two complementary dimensions. The first dimension included Cluster_Average, Cluster_Score, and Role –Cluster Alignment. The second dimension included Internal_Epistemic and External_Epistemic. This distinction suggests that the explanations teachers provide for their course selections represent a separate interpretive dimension, which can be triangulated with the curricular dimension. The results of the variance components analysis show that two components with eigenvalues greater than 1 jointly explained 54.3% of the total variance, with Component 1 representing the curricular dimension and Component 2 the epistemic dimension. It is interesting that transitional coheren ce, which relies on data external to the course selection (teachers’ roles) loaded on the same factor as curricular coherence, perhaps hinting that teacher role is an essential factor behind courses selection coherence.

RQ3, the bivariate correlation analysis revealed that the associations among the variables were statistically significant but modest in magnitude. Curricular coherence (Cluster_Average) correlated moderately with Role–Cluster Alignment (r =.416, p <.001) and weakly with Cluster_Score (r =.207, p <.001). Internal epistemic coherence correlated only weakly with curricular coherence (r =.112, p =.013) and showed no significant associations with Cluster_Score or Role –Cluster Alignment. External epistemic coherence was unrelated to curricular coherence (r = –.010, p =.831), and its only significant association was a weak correlation with Role–Cluster Alignment (r =.097, p =.031). This pattern suggests that the practical–curricular measures are more strongly interrelated, whereas the epistemic indicators form a distinct dimension, reflecting a distinction between teachers’ actions and their articulated reasoning, which may operate as partially independent processes. Thus, epistemic coherence can be triangu lated with curricular measures, enriching the overall understanding of teacher learning pathways. A hierarchical regression was conducted to predict Cluster_Average. The first step, including Role–Cluster Alignment, was significant and explained 17% of the  variance. Adding Cluster_Score in step 2 improved the prediction modestly, raising the explained variance to 20%. Adding Internal_Epistemic in step 3 provided a small but significant contribution. External_Epistemic in step 4 was not significant, and the final model explained about 21% of the variance.

This study identifies key predictors of curricular coherence in teacher-driven micro-credentialing, highlighting the roles of structural alignment, reflective reasoning, and inter-cluster connections. Findings suggest that coherence can be operationalized as a multidimensional construct, combining Curricular and Transitional aspects with Epistemic reasoning. Teachers tended to align course selections with practical demands, while reflective reasoning emerged as a distinct and less strongly connected dimension, consistent with the modular nature of micro-credentialing. Curricular coherence (Cluster_Average) was used as the main outcome. Regression results indicate that Role–Cluster Alignment was the strongest predictor, followed by Internal Epistemic Coherence and Cluster_Score, suggesting that both professional role alignment and reflective reasoning support coherent learning pathways.These findings extend prior research by applying coherence constructs to personalized learning environments. Practically, they  support maintaining flexibility in micro-credentialing while incorporating reflective prompts and cross-cluster connections. At the policy level, coherence should be assessed through complementary structural and epistemic indicators.
