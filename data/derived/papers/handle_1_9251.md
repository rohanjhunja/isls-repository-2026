# Exploring the Interplay Between Domain Knowledge and Collaborative Problem Solving Skills Through User Profiling

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Collaborative problem solving (CPS) skills gain great attention in recent years as people are often required to work in teams to solve problems in the complex working environment. Even though CPS is usually treated as a domain-general competency in assessment, many CPS tasks may be closely related to the team member’s domain knowledge. This research aims to explore and disentangle the relations between CPS skills and domain knowledge. Cluster analysis on 378 individuals from 126 teams solving an online electronics task discovered two types of collaboration patterns, which also differ on the prior knowledge levels. However, these two subgroups were not significantly different on the task performance. This finding uncovered interesting interactions between CPS skills and domain knowledge, and emphasizes the importance to consider the task features and domain knowledge in CPS assessment and learning.

## Background

Collaborative problem solving (CPS) is considered one of the critical competencie s for 21st-century talents as increasingly complex tasks emerge in workplace contexts. Therefore, fostering and assessing students’ CPS skills gained increasing academic attention in recent years. Even though CPS skills are domain-general (OECD, 2017), the collaboration and problem-solving processes may also be influenced by the team member’s prior knowledge and experiences, especially domain knowledge related to the CPS tasks they work on (Funke  & Frensch, 2007). Despite the effort of designing domain-general CPS tasks (Sun et al., 2020), e.g., P ISA 2015 (OECD, 2017) and ATC21S (Griffin et al., 2015), many CPS tasks may still be heavily related to specific knowledge domains. This study explored how team members’ domain knowledge interplays with the CPS skills in a simulation-based CPS task on electronics. We coded the team member’s activities using the PISA  CPS framework (OECD, 2017) and then conducted cluster analysis to build the team member’s CPS activity profiles. We then explored the relationship among the domain knowledge, the CPS activity profiles, and the CPS task performance. The findings of this study expand our understanding of the interactions between domain knowledge and CPS skills and may help us to disentangle these two distinct sets of skills in CPS.

## Participants

All participants were recruited from universities and community colleges in the USA, who majored in electronics, engineering, and physics programs. 378 completed the test. 76% of the students r eported their gender as male, 21% as female, and 3% chose not to report. 62% reported their race as White, 7% as Black, 8% as Asian, 10% as multiple races, 1% as other races, and 4% as unreported. The age of the participants ranged from 18 to 20. Task and prior knowledge The students were asked to collaboratively complete a simulation-based 3-resistor task (Andrews-Todd & Forsyth, 2020), in which 3 resistors were connected in a series circuit. Each student in a team of 3 controlled one of the 3 resistors from a separate computer, and had to reach a goal voltage on his /her resistor. Students had to communicate via a chat box to share information, maintain consensus and make  decisions. This task was composed of 4 levels ordered by increasing difficulty. All participants were gi ven a prior knowledge test on the series circuit generated by a group of experts, with the full score of 23. Coding process and analyses To identify the CPS skills reflected in the process data, this resear ch adopted the CPS framework in the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) (OECD, 2017; P137), which takes a matrix format of 12 categories at the intersections of four problem-solving dimensions, e.g., (A) exploring and understanding, (B) CSCL 2023 Proceedings  © ISLS 401 representing and formulating, (C) planning and executing, and (D) monitoring and ref lecting, and three collaborative dimensions, e.g., (1) establishing and maintaining shared understanding, (2) ta king appropriate action to solve the problem, and (3) establishing and maintaining team organization. For instance, (D1) would be “Monitoring and repairing the shared understanding”. The log data of the 126 groups’ chats and actions contained 51,805 rows, and were coded by two trained human coders (Kappa=0.82). Eight groups showed to o little interactions and thus not included in the final analysis. After the coding, the f requencies of the coded CPS skills were summed for each student, and the percentages of each CPS categorie s were then calculated. Then we performed a hierarchical clustering process on the percentage of the C PS skills to identify different behavioral patterns. Further comparisons were made on the subgroups using t-tests. ## Results and discussion

Cluster analysis resulted in 2 unique clusters. On the percentages of the  12 CPS skills (Figure 1), only C2 of Cluster 1 was significantly higher than that of Cluster 2 (t = 13.89, df = 339, p <.001), and all other CPS skills were either lower or similar to the other cluster. C2 indicates “enacting plan” in the PISA frame work, and represents the participants' actions to executing plans while all other skills involved discussion and planning. Therefore, we named the participants in Cluster 1 “actionist” (n = 170) and Cluster 2 “planner” (n = 184). On team performance measured by the attempted level (AL) and the succe ss level (SL), there was no significant difference in both AL (t = 0.29, df = 349, p = 0.77) and SL (t = 0.46, df  = 349, p = 0.65) between the two clusters. On prior knowledge (PK), it was found that the correlation between PK and SL is 0.27 (p < 0.001). In addition, even though the planners’ prior knowledge levels were significantly higher th an the actionists (t = 3.75, df = 343, p < 0.001) they failed to achieve more. Further analysis discovered a negative correlation between PK and C2 frequency indicating that team members with better PK are less  likely to enact plans. The results indicate that some collaborators may not be equipped with as much domain knowledge, they can achieve similar performance with the strategy of trying more. However, further studies need to be carried out to explore why knowledge-equipped collaborators act like “planners” and achieved less.
