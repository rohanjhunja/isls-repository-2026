# Being Body-Conscious: A Trauma-Informed Inquiry into Elementary Students’ Collective Embodied Learning Experiences

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper presents a case study on integrating trauma-informed design into embodied science modeling activities for upper elementary students. The study, conducted with fifth-grade students, examines their experiences with and reflections on embodied modeling activities, focusing on their expressions of need, preference, and comfort with various available modes for participation in collective, full-body, and playful embodiment. The findings offer insight into the complex dynamics of embodiment, (dis)comfort, and trauma and underscore the necessity of infusing trauma-informed pedagogies into embodied learning environments.

## Introduction

Embodied learning, central to educational discourse, views the human body as key in creating meaning t hrough cognitive and sociocultural lenses (Glenberg, 2008;  Shapiro, 2019) while inadvertently carrying implic it assumptions about learners' physical and v isible performance abilities. Recent critical schol arship interrogates these normative views of embodiment and recognize the dynamic interactions among bodies, spaces, movements, and time (Mathayas et al., 2022).

We present a case study integrating trauma-informed design in embodied science modeling activities for upper elementary students While this study is not a comprehensive guide for activity design for traumatized youth; rather, it investigates how trauma-informed approaches might be infused into embodied act ivities towards a broader ethos of 'doing no harm' in educational con texts. For the purposes of this analysis, we invest igate how a class of fifth-grade students attended to and reflected on their embodied modeling experiences concerning comfort with degree and mode of participation.

## Theoretical framework

Embodiment scholarship has long leveraged the body's role in inspiring, mediating, and communicating thoughts and actions to enhance learning (Shapiro, 2019). Hi storically influenced by Western dualism and the industrialization of education, which often downpla yed the body's role in thought processes (Nathan, 2 021), the field has seen evidence of how embodiment benefits learning and motivation across disciplines (Glenberg, 2008; Goldin-Meadow, 2011).

While initially focused on cognition, the field now  increasingly recognizes the sociocultural and interactional dimensions of embodied learning (Ma, 2017; Solomon et al., 2022; Vossoughi et al., 2020)  with particular attention to how diverse physical abili ties, cultural backgrounds, and the politicization of bodies influence embodied learning (Marin, 2020; Takeuchi & Ishihara, 2020). These experiences necessitate de signs that actively build and affirm learner dignity, countering the cumulative dehumanization of learners, characterized as the accumulation of instances where political, mental, social, and biological processes impact bodies (Espinoza et al., 2020). Here, we marry frameworks of embodim ent alongside critical perspectives on dignity to a ttend to these internalized and often-overlooked experiences to better understand how mediating processes of our activity designs may have amplified, subdued, or reinforced learners’ discomfort and autonomy. Trauma-informed pedagogies offer such a framework and reco gnize the profound impact of trauma on students' learning and behavior (Bogard & Carr-Chellman, 2023). In educational settings, trauma can manifest in various ways, including attention difficulties,  emotional dysregulation, relationship challenges, d isruptive behaviors, self-harm, misinterpretation of social cues, avoidance, trust issues, and cognitive challenges to name a few. Children with trauma histories may find it cha llenging to articulate thei r experiences and needs, often resorting to expressing preferences or making speci fic choices as a form of communication (Woodgate et  al., 2017).

As is relevant for embodied learning contexts, trauma-informed educators recognize when stressors (i.e. external ‘activators’ or ‘triggers’) may be present  and activate vicarious (re)traumatization. This aw areness then should inform the intentional design of the physica l/cultural setting to be psychologically safe. The nature of ‘psychologically safe’ learning spaces can contextu ally vary but often prioritize students’ social-emotional regulation and healthy interpersonal coping strateg ies during times of i ncreased vulnerability. (Darroch et al.,

2020) Apprehension to participate in activities can also be the product experiences that cover a wide spectrum of

severity; learners may opt out of participation due to factors that span from chronic illnesses and complex traumas to more mild or short-term physical/mental states Embodied learning activities often include structur es with trauma-activating characteristics; without alternatives or opportunities for refusal, these ac tivities may inadvertently coerce participation and  vicariously (re)traumatize youth while also posing a risk of peer critique or harassment, particularly for multiply marginalized youth with non-normative bodies. Needless to say, the infusing of trauma-informed approaches to embodied activity design must respect, trust, and validate learners' expressed preferences and needs without requiring further disclosure or evidence of traumatization.

Scholars in trauma and disability within physical education have further embraced a holistic view of the body as simultaneously thinking, feeling, moving, and learning while incorporating discursive methods, materials, and participation/task structures that enable and e mpower learners to act in accordance with ever-changing circumstances, times, preferences, and spaces (Darr och et al., 2020; Goodwin, 2020; Pickett & Cun ningham, 2017). ## Methods

In Fall 2022, we conducted a study with 19 fifth-grade students at a rural Midwestern U.S. public sc hool who engaged in a nine-session unit using a mixed-reality environment to explore ecosystems and matte r cycling. The embodied modeling activity used a classroom-level mixed reality learning environment called GEM-STEP (GEMSTEP, 2021) that incorporates a motion-tracking computational modeling simulation to facil itate elementary students' playful exploration of agent-based models of ecosystems. In this environment, students take on the role of an agent, such as a fish, either by physically m oving within a tracked space with a wearable tag at tached to a hat or lanyard. Through six tracking anchors set up  around the classroom, the positions of tags on eac h student's hat are monitored and the location information is t ransmitted to a computer model. This allows student s to walk around the classroom and observe themselves as fish  while interacting with virtual algae in the model.  We additionally provided students with an alternative option for character control via an iPad. For research purposes, all classroom activities were video-recorded and interviews were conducted with 17 (con sented) students before and after the unit.

Our analysis drew on grounded theory methods (Charm az, 2006) and interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) of classroom video data featuring students’ navigation and enactment of choice over t heir modality for participation (a hat, lanyard, or iPad) and segments of students’ pre/post interviews tha t featured responses to the following questions: (1) How do/did you and your classmates feel about using  their bodies and movement as a way of learning science?  (pre & post) (2) You had the choice to use your body or the iPad. Ho w did you decide which one to use? If you used both, how were they different? How did they help you lear n differently?  (post only).

## Findings

We first note how, when questioned about their pref erred modes for participation in the embodied model ing activity, all 17 students expressed enjoyment and a preference for learning science in this manner. We underscore this discovery as it not only aligns  with existing field insights but also opens questio ns into the complex considerations and conditions of students' agreemen t. These considerations are captured in the followi ng two themes.

Theme 1: Students recognized their non-normative bodies as in tension with normative expectations Inductive coding of student interview responses revealed that they were aware of the physical, attentional, sensory, emotional, psychological, and educational differences of bodies and that these differences could shape experiences within embodied learning  environments. For instance, four students noted that  physical dis/ability may impact how their peers participate in the activities, and two students attended to more nuanced implications of tasking students with physical performance and how that might place additional labor onto ‘non-athletic’ bodies: “Like I said some people might not be very athletic and it's hard for them to move around (laughter), it's not that hard for people to move a round but maybe like they are sweating. They don't really want to move right now. Maybe they just need to rest…” One student also described their body as ‘atypical’ in contrast to dominant narratives of what constitutes a ‘normal’ body’s behavior: “…because I don’t exercise and I know I’m a kid but like I’m not a kid who like runs around with their dog. I’m the kid who the most exercise she does is jumps at the screen and yells at the screen because she didn’t get the battle royale in Fortnight. That’s my workout.” Students repeatedly expressed awareness of the socially and culturally constructed expectations for what bodies ‘should’ be able and willing to do. Furthermore, several students alluded to their body falling short of some socially constructed expectation and assumption that children want to and are willing  to be physically active in a particular way.

Several students (including one student who voluntarily disclosed their ADHD diagnosis) also discussed the affordances of embodied activities for directing and regulating their attention in a traditional classroom setting. For these students, embodiment enables them to sati sfy their body’s attentional and sensory need to ‘f idget’ and ‘move;’ even further, this enabled a meaningful eng agement with the content in a way that was not poss ible in more traditional classroom settings: “Because sitting down is not my favorite thing and sitting down sometimes I get distracted and like fidgeting and moving a lot.  and like I feel like it transferred in my brain. A nd made sense and moving around how to take information in better.” Such reflections reveal an understanding of the soc ioaffective and cognitive dimensions of embodied experiences and its relationship with their learning. Theme 2: Students were conscious of their bodies as under observation and judgement Students articulated a heightened awareness of being observed, both by themselves and others, within the context of embodied learning activities. When asked to speculate about the viability of a new GEM-STEP feature utilizing projected self-views, three students expressed concerns about how self-viewing sessions might activate feelings of self-consciousness and discomfort, with one student sharing how “...some people may be afraid, they don’t like to see their reflection…” Five separate students raised concerns about the impact that self-views might have on students’ attention and focus, arguing that a perpetual self-view would be a distraction from learning for both themselves and their peers as illustrated in one response: “[the self-view] is bad because you are supposed to be modeling and not just looking at yourself. (play-acting) ‘Ooh I look at this hat on and this lanyard !’” These observations reveal that even in the abstract, students were conscious of ho w such technologies might shift focus away from the  learning objectives towards self-preoccupation and activate self-corrective behaviors. They also underscore the comp lex intertwining of students' experiences with embodiment, self-perception, and external observation. Student responses also revealed how students consid ered the risk and consequences of inviting peer critique of embodiment; it might result in an appre hension to experiment with ‘new’ approaches to embo diment in such a public setting. As one student discusses: "...some people might disagree with what we are trying... If you didn’t do something right with the strategy some pe ople would criticize themselves." This inhibition of experimental embodiment methods extends beyond the need for practice in traditional scientific competencies but got conflated with and complicated by a pressure to socially perform in ways that did not come at the cost of social capital.

## Discussion / Conclusion

This study investigates how elementary students eng aged with and navigated embodied experiences spanni ng from full-body motion tracking to gestures within the context  of embodied science activities. Across video and student interview data, students conveyed the value and appreciation of using such physical and visible modes of embodiment for their content learning, yet attended  to the multifaceted ways in which certain modes pr ivileged the needs, preferences, experiences, and abilities tied to different bodies. One crucial insight led us to scrutinize the static  and binary ‘yes/no’ model of presenting choices to students as inadequate for students to experiment with or ascertain their preference/need for the available modes. Students were consistently eager to ex periment with different modalities and revoke these  decisions as circumstances called for it. Merely waiting for stu dents to express a ‘need’ falls short of addressing  their comprehensive psychological, physical, and emotiona l needs; rather, we advocate for an educational eth os that places unwavering trust in children to advocate on behalf of themselves without obligatory probes into  the underlying reasons or demand for disclosure. Recogn izing that when children articulate a preference, t hey are doing so for reasons deeply significant to them, is  paramount. This approach is essential for the deve lopment of educational frameworks that are genuinely responsive and adaptive to the many ways students express their needs towards cultivating learning environments that are truly inclusive, dignified, and supportive of each child's holistic development.

Our research illuminates the necessity of adopting consent as an integral pedagogical framework that empowers children to govern themselves and shifts decisions on how students’ bodies should behave and present in the classroom away from facilitators and towards those who have unparalleled insights into th eir bodies needs

– the students themselves.
