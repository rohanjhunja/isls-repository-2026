# Teacher Engagement in Online Micro-Credential Professional Development

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Micro-credentials promise flexible, competency-based teacher professional development, yet understanding teacher engagement within these platforms remains a challenge. This study explores teacher engagement by analyzing large-scale digital trace data (N=5,271) from eight courses on Israel’s national micro-credential platform. It investigates how engagement can be empirically conceptualized, what are its underlying dimensions, how they predict learning outcomes, and what distinct engagement profiles emerge. Factor analysis revealed three engagement dimensions: forum activity, quiz performance, and learning effort. Regression analysis showed that forum activity was the strongest predictor of course and task completion. Cluster analysis identified three teacher profiles, revealing a misalignment between effort and achievement. These findings provide a data‑driven conceptualization of engagement and inform the design of online professional learning environments.

Teacher professional development (TPD) is central to educational improvement, as it enhances teachers’ knowledge, skills, and practices (Avalos, 2011). TPD has undergone a profound transformation in recent years, with digital platforms offering flexible and scalable opportunities for learning through short, competency-based modules, such as micro-credentials (Louws et al., 2017). Despite their promise, current research often relies on surface-level metrics, such as completion rates or self-reports, overlooking the nuanced behavioral and cognitive processes that unfold during learning. This study addresses these gaps by examining teacher engagement in Israel’s national accredited micro‑credential system-“Hakaveret”. Using digital trace data, we aim to empirically conceptualize engagement, test its relationship to learning outcomes, and identify participation patterns.

## Theoretical background

This study is grounded in adult learning theory, underpinning effective online TPD, and the multidimensional construct of learner engagement. The promise of online TPD is rooted in its alignment with adult learning theory, which highlights that teachers, as adult learners, are driven by autonomy, experience, relevance, and self‑direction (Knowles, 2013). Digital platforms excel at providing this flexibility, offering scalable, personalized access that reduces costs and logistical barriers (Dede et al., 2009; Lay et al., 2020). However, a significant gap persists between this potential and the reality of practice, where online environments often struggle with challenges of limited collaboration and shallow participation (Hao & Gu, 2023). Learner engagement, broadly defined as the extent to which learners are involved in learning tasks, offers a robust framework for addressing this gap. Engagement is a multidimensional construct comprising four components: cognitive (investment in time and strategies), behavioral (observable actions such as attendance or contributions), emotional (affective reactions), and social (interaction with peers) (Finn & Zimmer, 2012). In online environments, learning analytics might enable empirical examination of engagement through activity logs, with recent approaches including indicators of usage, performance, and communication (Ahmadi et al., 2023). However, research has criticized overreliance on simplistic behavioral indicators, such as click counts, that do not necessarily reflect genuine learning (Hershkovitz et al., 2025; O'Brien et al., 2022). Recognizing that engagement patterns vary based on motivation and context (Hershkovitz et al., 2011), recent studies have moved toward more sophisticated, multimodal approaches (Bernacki, 2025). By combining multiple indicators, such as time on task, active presence, interactions, performance, and self‑reports, and employing learning, researchers can model engagement more holistically and demonstrate its predictive power for performance (Zhuang et al., 2017).

This methodological challenge is particularly acute in the context of micro-credentials, which are short, modular, competency-based learning opportunities often recognized through digital badges. They are valued for 1124 flexibility and relevance in adult learning (Tamoliune et al., 2023).  Yet, their structure heightens the risks of superficial engagement, where learners may pursue completion without meaningful learning (Abramovich et al., 2013; Pollard & Vincent, 2022). Research on engagement in micro-credentials is limited and often operationalized using the same narrow metrics, such as logins or completion rates (Hao & Gu, 2023). The empirical structure of engagement as a multidimensional construct remains an underexplored area. This study directly addresses this gap by leveraging learning analytics to conceptualize teacher engagement within a national micro-credential platform.

## Research questions

The study addresses three research questions: RQ1: What latent dimensions of teacher engagement are reflected in digital trace data? RQ2: To what extent do these engagement dimensions predict learning outcomes? RQ3: What profiles of teacher engagement emerge from the data, and what are their characteristics?

## Research Context and Data Sources

This study was conducted within the context of Israel’s national micro-credential platform-“Hakaveret”. It is a centralized TPD micro-credential system developed in collaboration with the Ministry of Education and the Center for Educational Technology. The system holds extensive data on teachers’ demographics, goals, and learning behaviors, posit ioning it as a representative model for studying the under-researched world of micro-credentials. Courses are typically equivalent to three hours of learning. Completion of ten courses provides TPD credit. A typical course includes at least one quiz, a discussion forum, and a section dedicated to reporting practical applications of the course content. Some courses include more than one forum or additional assignments. Currently the platform includes 839 courses with approximately 46,849 enrollments. This study reports on an initial stage of a larger research program that examines the full dataset; thus, it is a work in progress. The dataset for this study consisted of digital trace logs from eight courses over the years 2021-2025, representing 5,271 valid teacher records after data cleaning. We extracted and standardized (as Z-scores) variables reflecting learning behaviors and outcome measures. Cluster analysis produced a stable three-cluster solution, revealing distinct engagement profiles. Model quality showed good separation between clusters (Table 4). Cluster 1: “Active collaborators” characterized by above-average forum activity (Z= +1.01) and learning duration (Z= +1.11), but average quiz scores. Cluster 2: “Struggling strivers” included teachers with the highest log records (Z= +0.88) and longest learning duration (Z= +1.92), combined with high forum comments, but below-average quiz score (Z= –0.75). Cluster 3: “Efficient achievers” had the lowest log records and quiz attempts, slightly below average forum activity, yet achieved the highest quiz score (Z= +0.78). These profiles highlight that observable engagement is not always aligned with academic achievement; hence, observable effort is not a reliable proxy for course completion in this context. This study challenges the simplistic view of teacher participation in online professional development that focuses on activity counts. The findings reveal that engagement in micro-credential courses exhibits multiple patterns that influence outcomes in different ways. One contribution is the strong predictive power of forum participation: teachers who contributed more actively to discussions were more likely to complete the course. In addition, the learning effort factor, which captures extended time on the platform, task submissions, and forum involvement, was also positively associated with course completion, assignment submission, and reported applications in practice. By contrast, quiz performance had only a limited role in explaining these outcomes. Cluster analysis revealed distinct participation profiles. Some teachers invested significant time and activity without achieving high quiz scores (struggling strivers), while others attained high scores with relatively little observable activity (efficien t achievers).  While adult learning theories emphasize collaboration (Knowles, 2013), these high achievers bypass forums, effectively withdrawing their expertise from the community and limiting collaborative knowledge-building. The presence of this high-achievement, low-activity group might suggest that some important learning processes occur outside the logs, such as offline reflection or the use of prior expertise. Conversely, the high-effort, low-achievement group highlights the importance of improved scaffolding and adaptive feedback, enabling learners to translate their effort into effective progress. For the learning sciences, this work presents a replicable, multi-method approach that extends beyond simple activity counts, utilizing factor analysis, regression, and clustering to reveal nuanced patterns of participation. It underscores the need to design online professional development for interaction and support, while acknowledging the diverse pathways teachers take through such environments. This study represents an initial stage of a larger research program; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary. While the current analyses provide a robust, data-driven conceptualization of engagement based on log data from eight courses, they do not yet capture the full range of teacher participation in micro-credentials. Future work will expand to additional courses to test the stability of the three-factor structure and cluster profiles. The next phase will also incorporate multimodal learning analytics (Berncki, 2025), including textual analysis with Natural Language Processing (NLP) to analyze the semantic content and pedagogical depth of forum discourse, moving beyond surface-level behavioral counts to capture the true quality of engagement, and practical application reports to provide a more comprehensive understanding of teacher engagement in these courses.
