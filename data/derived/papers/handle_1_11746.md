# Emerging Debugging Strategies of Novice Programmers: The Role of Self-Efficacy

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Self-efficacy in novice programming affects problem-solving approaches. Analysis revealed three clusters (underconfident, confident, overconfident) based on debugging self-efficacy versus ability. Misalignment between perceived and actual ability may reduce future programming interest. Sequence analysis showed strategy quality, not quantity, determined debugging success, with confident students showing adaptive behaviors. Findings suggest interventions promoting well-calibrated self-efficacy and metacognitive skills.

## Background

Research on programming attrition (Tek et al., 2018) underscores the role of self-efficacy in shaping student motivation, interest, and retention (Bandura, 1986; Law et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2024). In programming, self-efficacy significantly affects debugging, a critica l skill many novices struggle with (Yang et al., 20 24). It influences how learners approach debugging tasks, requiring adaptive strategies like systematic analysis, resource utilization, and help-seeking (Yang et al., 2024). Misaligned self-efficacy, often reflected in the Dunning-Kruger effect (Kruger & Dunning, 1999)—lower performers overestimate their abilities, while higher performers tend to underestimate theirs—negatively impacts learning processes and outcomes (Winne & Hadwin, 1998; Morphew, 2024; Sobral, 2021). Our study examines clusters ba sed on debugging self-efficacy and ability, explores differences in programming interest, and investigates how debugging strategies vary across these clusters.

## Methods

We collected data from 43 novice students in an int roductory Python workshop taught across two iterati ons (3-4 days each, approximately 8 hours of hands-on programming). Using surveys and video recordings, we identified clusters of debugging self-efficacy and performance through hierarchical clustering, se lecting a three-cluster model based on dendrogram and elbow graph analysis.  We then examined problem-solving patterns using sequence analysis based on Loksa et al.'s (2016) fr amework. Two researchers coded  videos for debugging strategies (κ =.820), identifying six categories: analysis, debugging, switching, independent work, online resource utilization, and interpersonal strategies. For each  cluster, we analyzed strategy sequences using desc riptive and first-order lag sequential analysis, exploring significant transition patterns (p <.05, minimum frequency = 3).

## Results

We identified three clusters: underconfident (58.1%, n=25), confident (23.3%, n=10), and overconfident (18.6%, n=8). Figure 1 shows debugging self-efficacy versus demonstrated ability (ratio of solv ed bugs) for each group. The underconfident group had moderate ability but lowest self-efficacy, the confident group showed mid-high self-efficacy with highest ability, and the overconfident group reported highest self-efficacy despite lowest performance. We found no statistically significant differences in future programming interest between clusters (F(2,40)=0.96, p=0.49, η²=0.05). However, only the confident group showed increased future interest (M=0.11, SD=0.52), while interest decreased in both underconfident (M=-0.16, SD=0.65) and overconfident (M=-0.23, SD=0.86) groups—a trend warranting further investigation with larger samples. We observed divergent patterns across clusters. Table 1 shows underconfident students used conservative approaches with lowest metrics, confident students maintained moderate metrics, and overconfident students showed intensive behavior with highest metrics. Figure 2 highlights different strategy patterns: underconfident learners showed repeated cycles in analysis and deb ugging, moved from analysis to independent work, and had paths from interpersonal help and online resources to debugging; confident learners demonstrated connections among multiple strategies, with analysis leading to  independent work and interpersonal help, online re sources connecting from independent work and to interperson al help, interpersonal help leading to debugging, a nd repeated cycles in analysis and debugging; and overconfident learners linked analysis to independent work, had paths from online resources and interpersonal help to debugging, with uniform transition strengths and no repeated cycles or dominant transitions.

## Conclusion

We identified three novice programmer clusters: underconfident, confident, and overconfident, revealing patterns beyond simple high/low classifications. It highlighted adaptive and maladaptive confidence lev els, emphasizing metacognition's importance in developing appropriate self-efficacy. Increased programming interest only among confident students suggests self-efficacy/ability alignment enhances future engagement. Subsequent research should examine contributing factors and long-term impacts. Lag sequential analysis revealed distinct  patterns. Confident students showed sophisticated problem-solving—their analysis led to both independent work and interpersonal help, using online resources strategi cally. This integrated approach contrasts with underconfident learners' direct paths to debugging from support resources  and overconfident learners' uniform, less engaged transitions. While overconfident students used most strategies and underconfident students fewest, the confident group's moderate but diverse approach proved most e ffective, highlighting strategy quality over quanti ty. Our findings suggest effective problem-solving stems from appropriate self-efficacy supported by metacognition that enables adaptive strategy use (Yang et al., 2024), indicating the need for personalized interventions.
