# Constructing Mathematics Learning Environments where Students Feel Safe to be Wrong: A Mixed-Methods Investigation of Educator Behaviors in Classroom and Tutoring Settings

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates educators’ communication to students that the mathematics classroom or small group tutoring is a safe space to make errors or seek support as needed. Our mixed-methods approach includes quantitative data from 220 transcripts along w ith two case studies that highlight best practices. Results indicate that both classroom teachers and paraprofessional tutors typically incorporate a small number of verbal indications that they welcome mistakes (mean of 4.9% for teachers and 4.5% for tutors). Although both teachers and tutors who talked more than average used disproportionately more “safety to be wrong” moves, teachers (but not tutors) who talked less than average had lower proportions of these moves. The case studies suggest that when teachers and tutors provide frequent reminders that being wrong is safe, students share their ideas, articulate areas of confusion, and persevere through challenging tasks. We conclude with suggestions for supporting professional learning.

Researchers in mathematics education have long argued that welcoming and addressing student errors can play a valuable role in ensuring a productive learning environment (Borasi, 1994; NCTM, 2000). All mathematical communications generated by students – particularly those with pervasive errors due to underlying misconceptions – present an opportunity for generative inquiry and collective knowledge construction (Brodie, 2014; Smith et al., 1994). Attending to errors in an educational context involves both ma thematical and social processes, yielding content-focused resolutions along with markers regarding acceptable mathematical norms. Investigations into how teachers handle errors when they arise in the classroom indicate wide variation, which has been labeled the “error climate” (Steuer et al., 2013). Numerous teacher behaviors shape the degree to which there is a positive error climate, including how they (implicitly or explicitly) frame student ability, such as by reminding students of their accomplishments  on similar problems or by questioning their basic math skills (Battey, 2013). These behaviors have been classified as adaptive/productive or maladaptive/unproductive with regard to their impact on the classroom’s error climate (Alvidrez, 2024; Tulis, 2013). Ongoing scholarship continues to define specific pedagogical actions that invoke an epistemological framing of errors as resources, specifically by highlighting their value in collaborative knowledge construction (Alvidrez et al., 2024). Research that defines and explores positive relational interactions more broadly between teachers and students offers critical insights. Scholars in this area typically position classroom learning as an inclusive social process reinforced by intentional interpersonal dynamics and communication patterns (Asterhan et al., 2015; Mercer & Littleton, 2007). There is an emphasis on teacher-prompted communication as the driver of positive interactions, ensuring that all students engage and thrive in classroom environments, especially those who might otherwise feel marginalized or excluded (Bartell, 2011; Battey et al., 2016; 2018). For example, Maloney & Matthews (2020) emphasize the need for educators to deliberately and consistently demonstrate empathetic care, which includes acknowledging the challenges their students face and explicitly addressing their well-being, not just their academic performance. Their research found that when students perceived their teachers as offering genuine care, the students felt a g reater sense of classroom connectedness and had improved perceptions of the value and relevance of the mathematics.

In this study, we consider how often educators verbally articulate that the instructional environment is a safe space to make mistakes during periods of  mathematics instruction, including a comparison of classroom and tutoring learning environments. Furthermore, we illustrate what exemplary teaching and tutoring sessions in this regard look like, including the type of language educators use to ensure a positive error climate and how their efforts impact students’ mathematical engagement.

## Related work

Attending to students’ mathematical errors Student errors are a natural and expected part of the learning process; however, they can be handled by teachers in very different ways. Based on a case study investigation, Gardee and Brodie (2015) identified four ways that teachers might interact with students' mathematical errors: (1) ignoring, (2) correcting, (3) probing with follow-up questions, and (4) embracing to generate new knowledge. The teacher they studied most often ignored or corrected, a finding reinforced by other empirical investigations (Kaufmann et al., 2023; Son, 2013). In Kaufmann and colleagues’ (2023) study, teachers typically corrected errors during individual or small-group work and were most likely to hold productive discussions based on errors during whole-class instruction. This research suggests that within a given classroom, the structure of the activity serves as important context when considering the treatment of errors.

Belonging-centered instruction In recent years, Belonging-Centered Instruction has become recognized as a critical framework that attends to students’ perceptions of inclusion and empowerment through specific instructional techniques and interpersonal interactions (Matthews et al., 2021). The notion of belonging in schools was first highlighted and defined in the early 1990s by Goodenow and Grady (1993), with increasing attention to the importance of belonging for racially marginalized students whose schooling experiences can present ide ntity threats through stigmatization and discrimination (Matthews et al., 2024). Belonging is a multifaceted social and psychological construct that is understood as a basic human need and associated with overall well-being (Allen et al., 2022; Gray et al., 2018). Multiple studies have found that students’ sense of belonging in school classrooms is associated with educational flourishing as evidenced by increased effort, engagement, and academic performance (Allen et al., 2022; Barbieri & Miller-Cotto, 2021; Gray et al., 2024; Penuel et al., 2024). Matthews and colleagues (2021) conceptualized two domains of teacher actions that foster students’ perceptions of belonging: instructional supports (bridging course content with students’ identities) and interpersonal supports (facilitating social ties and interconnectedness). In this paper, we focus on one subdomain included in the instructional supports domain: Safety to Be Wrong (STBW). Our choice to focus on this dimension is largely pragmatic – it was one of the most frequently observed in our dataset,  contained a straightforward and clear definition, and was relatively simple to obtain high levels of interrater reliability. There is strong evidence that experiencing an emotionally safe learning environment promotes productive risk-taking behaviors, suc h as sharing and defending ideas that may contain errors (Sharma, 2015). To foster belonging, educators can actively push against a prevailing view that good mathematics students follow instructions and listen to their teacher rather than offering information, asking questions, and engaging in argumentation (Hunter & Anthony, 2011; Stevens et al., 2007).

In their Belonging-Centered Instructional Protocol, Matthews et al. (2021) define Safety to Be Wrong as teacher discourse moves that ensure students do not feel stigmatized for encountering difficulties, being wrong, or needing support. Safety to be Wrong moves include normalizing difficulties, creating space for questions, taskfocused encouragement, and celebrating mastery-focused behavior, as shown in Table 1. Used in combination multiple times across a lesson period, these actions can promote a classroo m culture that disentangles mathematical correctness from inclusion and belonging.

Comparing classroom and small-group tutoring environments Most research on how educators handle mistakes, and relationship-building with students more broadly, has been conducted in traditional classrooms. However, there is a growing interest in incorporating tutoring periods into the school day, especially to support students’ learning of mathematics. In particular, high-dosage tutoring (during school, at least three times a week, in 30-50 minute sessions) has been shown to have strong positive impacts on learning outcomes (Nickow et al., 2020; Guryan et al., 2021). A key benefit of human tutoring is the potential for students to develop interpersonal relationships with their tutors as part of their small-group, content-based interactions (Rothman & Henderson, 2011). However, the presence of a tutor could potentially undermine student agency if they take on the role of expert and simply tell students how to solve problems or ensure that students do not make errors by immediately stepping in to correct them. Particularly for tutors who are at the beginning stages of their work, specific challenges include forming strong relationships with all students, facilitating productive that even experienced tutors struggl e with the socio-emotional components of their relationship-building efforts, especially when tutoring takes place online (Gortazar et al., 2020). This study includes classroom teachers and human tutors who provide small-group mathematics instruction during the school day. We consider the total frequency of their utterances indicating that it is safe to be wrong, the proportion of total utterances that fall in this category and the distribution of STBW utterances based on educators’ total utterances. We anticipate that both groups of educators will explicitly communicate the importance of errors and seek to create a positive error culture to some degree. Due to the fact that our sample of tutors comprises primarily novice, paraprofessional educators (i.e., not certified teachers) who interact with their students remotely, it is possible they might have less firmly developed pedagogical skills, including those associated with relationship-building. On the other hand, a key benefit of tutoring is the opportunity to form closer bonds with students; therefore, it is also possible tutors might be more intentional in using verbal indicators to support students’ belonging, including reinforcing the value of mistakes. Classroom and tutor session data This study involves the secondary analysis of two datasets, both of which were collected by members of the authorship team as part of larger research projects. These datasets included recordings from convenience samples of (1) classroom mathematics lessons  and (2) mathematics tutoring sessions. The uniform context across both projects is public education spaces where students receive mathematics instruction as part of their everyday school experience. The classroom lessons were collected as part of a study on the design, implementation, and impact of an application that provided automated feedback to mathematics teachers on their instructional practices (Jacobs et al., 2025). Twenty-one teachers self-recorded 373 lessons from 2019-2021. The teachers were fro m two U.S. school districts and spanned grades 4-12, with most teaching upper elementary school (71%). The tutoring sessions come from a study of similar automated feedback made available to instructional coaches who supported U.S. high school mathematics tutors. These tutors engaged with students in a hybrid format; tutors were remote while students were in person. As part of the tutoring study, 2,350 tutoring sessions from 28 tutors were recorded during the 2022-23 school year (Jacobs et al., 2025).

A subset of these classroom and tutoring recordings were manually transcribed and hand-coded by individuals trained to apply the Belonging-Centered Instruction protocol. Prior to coding, the two coders achieved 419 high reliability (Cohen’s k = 0.83, percent agreement = 98%) on the STBW dimension. They then individually applied this dimension to a set of 220 lessons (156 classroom lessons, 64 tutoring sessions). Every teacher utterance (i.e., sentence) was coded usin g binary labels “Not-Present” or “Present” to indicate whether the utterance qualified as STBW.

## Analytic approach

This mixed-methods study involves quantitative and qualitative analyses to distill the frequency and nature of educators’ use of Safety To Be Wrong utterances during their mathematics instruction. Quantitative analyses are based on 220 transcripts from classroom lessons and tutoring sessions, described above. We then used  “extreme case” purposive sampling (Etikan et al., 2016) to select instructional periods from one teacher and one tutor that represent best practice in constructing an instructional environment welcoming of mistakes. Periods with higher than average frequencies of STBW detected were deemed candidates. A review of these transcripts by the authorship team led to the identification of cases we agreed were exceptional due to the consistency and quality of their efforts to affirm the value of mistakes throughout their instruction.

Exemplary cases of articulating students should feel safe to be wrong An exemplary elementary school lesson 420 Ms. Landon was an elementary school teacher with four years of classroom teaching experience when she taught the fifth grade lesson that is the focus of this case study. On average, Ms. Landon’s 13 recorded lessons had a slightly below average number and proportion of STBW utterances (M =15.2 & 4.5% respectively) although her total talk was slightly above average (M=424, SD=189). It is important to note that her lessons exhibited high variability; some lessons were 1-2+ standard deviations above and below the mean for total teacher talk, total STBW utterances and proportion of teacher talk coded as STBW. In the selected lesson Ms. Landon made 56 utterances coded as STBW representing 9.9% of her total utterances. Exploring outlier lessons such as this one, with much higher than average total talk and STBW, can offer insights into the trends portrayed in Figure 1. The lesson stood out as an exemplar of fostering a caring and inclusive learning community in which the teacher frequently guided the class to recognize how challenging the mathematics content was, validating students’ difficulties and encouraging them to help one another. We describe selected moments in the lesson where Ms. Landon made STBW utterances (in italics) alongside an analysis of how these verbalizations appeared to positively influence the error culture in her classroom. The class period begins with the students using individual white boards to write the “word form” of given decimal numbers. For example they are expected to write that 4.9 is “four and nine tenths”. Students then show their answers to a partner or the entire class, as prompted, and indicate whether they agree with one another. After spending about five minutes on activity, Ms. Landon asks, “What is the difficult part about writing down these numbers? Where do you think a common mistake is usually made?” When a student responds that it’s tricky to talk about decimals in the tenths place, Ms. Landon agrees and adds that it’s common for people to get “tens” and “tenths” confused. She then highlights the use of “-ths” to refer to decimals in the tenths, hundredths, and thousandths place.

After students have worked on several sets of problems, Ms. Landon brings the class together to talk through one of them: 3 ⅓ - 1 ½. As they go over the steps and arrive at the correct solution, Ms. Landon frequently asks whether students agree or disagree  with specific steps and what others did similarly or differently. An exemplary high school tutoring session Ms. Koda had been a full-time mathematics tutor for two years when her focal tutorial session with 9th graders was recorded. Ms. Koda had the highest average frequency of STBW (M=53.5, SD=37.5) in the tutoring dataset and one of the highest proportions of STBW utterances (M=5.5%, SD=2.7) with only minimal variability across her four recorded sessions. We selected the focal tutorial session because it had STBW throughout and exemplified Ms. Koda’s approach to facilitating math instruction with students. In this session Ms. Koda made 77 utterances coded as STBW, representing 8.5% of her total utterances. Similar to Ms. Landon’s case study lesson, Ms. Koda’s tutorial session offers insights into outlier periods with a large amount tutor talk and a high proportion of STBW utterances (i.e. data in the farthest right bin in Figure 1). For the first two minutes of Ms. Koda’s tutorial only one student, Jasmine, is present and she begins working on the warm-up problem. When Max enters the session, Ms. Koda immediately notices his unhappy expression and asks, “What’s up love?” After Max res ponds by saying,“Nothing,” Ms. Koda probes, “Why do you seem sad honey? Are you ok?” Max explains that he is feeling done with school and wants to go home. Ms. Koda offers sympathy and encouragement saying, “One more period. You got this. You so got this.”  She then thanks both students for being on their best behavior with a different tutor when she was absent, sharing that she “heard nothing but good things” about them.

From the beginning of the tutorial session, Ms. Koda sets the tone as one of care and attentiveness for her students, especially towards Max who arrives a bit late and looks disgruntled. Although Ms. Koda’s remarks 422 at this point are not considered STBW (i.e., they are unrelated to the math content), they represent a clear effort to build positive relationships with her students. Using the language of the Belonging-Centered Instructional Protocol (Matthews et al., 202 1), Ms. Koda projects empathetic awareness as she shows genuine concern for Max’s emotional well-being. Her warmth at the start of the session paves the way for the STBW utterances that occur throughout the tutorial, all of which convey the message that this instructional period is a welcoming and safe space if students are feeling unhappy in general or frustrated with the mathematics content. After completing the warm-up problems, Ms. Koda reads the next task out loud: a 2 + b2 = c2. Solve for b in terms of a and c, assuming all values are positive. She points out a hint that she added to their workspace, telling the students their last step in the solution process should be to remove the exponent using the square root, which is the inverse operation. Ms. Koda then reassures the students saying, “If you’re stuck, let me know. I’ll come to your page and help you. If we’re all stuck, we cando it together.” Here Ms. Koda is making space for student questions by acknowledging that they might experience difficulties with this more challenging problem and should let her know if that is the case. She sets the expectation that Max and Jasmine will try to work on the problem and offers a hint for a step that involves a higher sophistication level than their previous problems (i.e., it includes exponents). After she sets up the task in this manner, it is not surprising that both students feel comfortable to ask numerous questions as they work through it. Ms. Koda responds by providing scaffolding and asking questions back that prompt them to verbalize what they are thinking and consider why they are doing each step. At one point, when Jasmine shares that she is confused about what it means to “remove the square root,” Ms. Koda again offers reassurance that she is there to help and then enlists Max to explain. Max tells Jasmine, “Since you're trying to solve for B, you need to take away the other variable that's next to it.” Ms. Koda expands on Max’s advice and checks in with Jasmine to make sure that she is following. With the support of Max and Ms. Koda, Jasmine persists through the problem and arrives at the correct answer. Ms. Koda then continues to prompt the students to pose more questions if they have any and finally asks if they will be okay if she gives them a similar problem to solve. These ongoing prompts for student questions serve multiple purposes; in addition to highlighting Ms. Koda’s willingness to provide support, they help to slow the pace of the lesson and bring the students together as learning partners. Toward the end of the session, the students begin talking about an upcoming standardized achievement test, expressing concern that the test problems might be confusing and that they won’t do well. Ms. Koda agrees that it might be challenging but encourages  them by saying, “It’s going to be fine. You’re going to be prepared. First of all, both of you are very advanced, all right? A lot of students don’t even get to this part. So take a deep breath, right? That’s why I’m here to help you. If you don’t understand something now, I will go over it [later in the week].” The students seem to appreciate this confidence-building conversation. They willingly go back to working on their assigned problems, remaining engaged and soliciting help as needed. This study attends to one component of Belonging-Centered Instruction (Matthews et al., 2021), specifically the communication patterns that promote a positive error culture in mathematics learning settings. The results offer information about how often mathematics teachers and tutors convey to their students that it is safe to be wrong (STBW). We found that STBW utterances occur relatively infrequently and their frequency and proportion are correlated with total educator talk. Despite their infrequency and  an association with larger amounts of educator talk, they appear to have a strong impact on the learning community. The two groups, teachers and tutors had relatively similar amounts of STBW verbalizations, especially when computed as a proportion of thei r total talk. However a subset of teachers and tutors in our sample more frequently attended to this component of relationshipbuilding, explicitly calling attention to the value of mistakes, reassuring students that errors and uncertainty are normal, and inviting questions or other help-seeking behaviors as needed. Our two case studies illustrate how both teachers and tutors can infuse their instruction with this type of communication. The case study teacher and tutor both wove STBW utterances into the beginning, middle and end of their instructional periods. These educators set their students up to feel comfortable sharing ideas, highlighted the value of their participation, encouraged them to persevere when solving difficult problems, and praised them for contributing. Their cases provide a sense of the wide variety of approaches that individual educators can use to establish a positive error culture, regardless of grade level (elementary school or high school) and instructional setting (classroom or tutoring). Although we did not break down our findings to the “action indicator” level for STBW utterances (Table 1) due to the low occurrence of individual indicators in our dataset, an informal analysis suggests that normalizing difficulties is most common, followed by creating space for questions and task-focused encouragement, with celebrating mastery being the least common. Examples of most of these action indicators are evident throughout both of the case study lessons.

423 Another key takeaway from the cases is how deliberate educator behaviors to ensure that students feel safe to be wrong can yield a highly productive and discursive mathematics learning environment. In these two lessons the students frequently verbalized their mathematical thinking and reasoning, engaged with their classmates' ideas, articulated areas of confusion, and persevered through challenging tasks despite expressing frustration. All of these behaviors are aligned with effective mathematics instructio nal practice as recommended by experts (e.g., National Council of Teachers of Mathematics, 2014; National Governors Association, 2010). Suggestions for supporting educators’ professional learning Engaging educators in practice-based professional learning (Koellner et al., 2020) that centers their efforts to promote a positive error culture in their mathematics instruction may be beneficial for raising awareness of these behaviors and increasing their frequency, particularly for paraprofessional tutors who general have limited pedagogical training. In line with current recommendations for structuring teachers’ learning of ambitious and equitable mathematics instruction (Horn & Garner, 2022), we suggest educators engage with rich images of their own teaching, or that of their colleagues, that highlight occurrences of STBW through video, transcripts, or other evidence. This type of asset-based approach to professional learning (Celedon-Pattichis et al.,  2018) explicitly attends to teachers’ instructional strengths, highlighting practices they are likely already familiar with and implement to some degree, in order to promote their continued and more intentional use. Visualizations of practice, generated either by hand or automated, are also increasingly recommended as a way to support educators’ reflective practice, particularly with regard to increasing the use of targeted discourse practices (Chen & Chan, 2022; Correnti et al., 2015; Jacobs et al., 2025).

## Study limitations

The present study helps to expand an emerging line of research on the extent to which educators communicate that mistakes are welcome and provides illustrations of how mathematics teachers and tutors can infuse their instruction with this type of language.  However our study is limited to a relatively small sample of transcripts, collected from convenience samples, with many more from classrooms than tutorial sessions. Furthermore, data from a majority of the classroom teachers includes at least two recordin gs (18/20 teachers), whereas only a relatively small number of the tutors were recorded more than once (15/41 tutors), meaning that the teacher data is likely more representative than the tutor data. In addition, grade level is a confound in this study; most of the classroom data comes from elementary school classrooms whereas the tutoring data is from high school sessions. Future research is needed to ensure the generalizability of these findings and attend to additional nuances that may exist across educational contexts including teaching/tutoring and elementary/middle/high school instruction. Finally, future research should explore the relationship between anomalously high educator talk (2+ SD above the mean) having disproportionately higher STBW utterances. What other types of talk patterns occur (or do not occur) and what information about high quality instruction can we glean from these anomalous examples? Study results underscore the importance of specific discourse moves in ensuring inclusive learning environments. Although Safety to Be Wrong moves were generally low frequency, representing only a small percentage of the talk by educators during their mathematics instruction, these moves likely have a strong impact on the lesson climate. The case study lessons included in this paper illustrate how intentional communication about the value of mistakes supports students’ engagement, tolerance of challenging m athematical activities, and active participation in the learning community.

Prioritizing instructional behaviors that foster students’ sense of belonging is especially critical for historically marginalized students, including students of color and linguistically diverse students (Gray et al., 2018; Moschkovich, 1999). Our study attends to teacher behaviors that promote the construction of an environment where all students feel safe and valued, especially through the strategic use of statements that convey a positive attitude towards errors. At the same time, it is necessary to ack nowledge that this research is confined to classroom level, and more specifically teacher discourse, which is likely to yield actionable insights for educators. However, those educators work within the confines and constraints of broader institutional and socio-political contexts that play a key role in ensuring that students are immersed in caring and safe learning environments.
