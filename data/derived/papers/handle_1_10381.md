# Collecting College Students’ Funds of Knowledge in a Language Program

**Conference:** ISLS 2023

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Based on the theoretical perspective of Funds of Knowledge, this pilot study examined interview data from three pre-service teachers in the area of English for Specific Purposes (ESP) to explore how higher education language professors discover learners’ lived experiences to mediate curriculum and household scholarship. The findings posit that ESP pre-service teachers draw on assessment strategies as well as empathy for household practices and cultural capital to bridge content.

## Introduction

Higher education plays a consequential role in a human being’s educational journey. As it is considered the last step of formal education, this prepares undergraduates to deal with complex issues for their future job market and develop hard and soft skills  for life. Nevertheless, discussion about how academia develops the undergraduates’ learning process by connecting learners’ lived experiences, life skills, and cultural factors within formal education curriculum, and what the development of learning entai ls within the higher education ecologies is still limited. The review of the literature demonstrates how scholars utilize the Funds of Knowledge (FoK) framework to design curriculum units for bridging college-underrepresented learners’ household practices and formal teaching practices to enhance learners’ academic success and achieve the endpoint of higher education (see; Mora & RiosAguilar, 2017; Neri, 2017; Kiyama, Rios-Aguilar, & Deil-Amen, 2017). However, there is little research on how the connection between learners’ home  experiences, cultural backgrounds, and wisdom with  formal education curricula occurs within foreign/second language classroom ecologies; as a result, this pilot study focuses on two main themes. First, how higher education language professors find out about their students’ household experiences, skills, a nd cultural capital that aid to navigate their lifelong learning for designing curricula units. Second, how the influence of empathy on educators works as a key element for unit designing. Framework This research draws on the theoretical perspective of Funds of Knowledge (Moll, Amanti, Neff, and Gonzalez, 1992), which honors learners’  family practices and experiences, cultural resources, and life knowledge. The intersection of this framework with formal higher education pedagogical practices provides an opportunity for academia to connect learners’ lives and articulate curricular practices (see; Bensimon, 2007; Umbach & Wawrzynski, 2005; Barnett, 2010).

## Methods

Data were collected from a public university in Costa Rica. The participants were three Costa Rican language faculty members, who have vast experience in Teaching English as a Second and Foreign Language (TESOL/TEFL) in higher education. I conducted online  structured interviews in the English language using the ZOOM platform. I used an interview protocol, which contained predetermined questions. The average length of each interview was an hour long. Utilizing a qualitative design, this study conducted a the matic analysis of the data. The data sets were transcribed by using ZOOM audio transcript and then cleaned manually. After that, the data sets were codified by using MAXQDA to identify meanings and patterns. Finally, it was arranged in themes.

## Findings

The findings in this study bring up two main themes. First, forms of knowing students’ cultural capital theme posit that ESP pre-service faculty implemented diverse assessment strategies to know their students’ needs, preferences, personal and cultural backgrounds, identities, and other relevant resources before they start designing lesson plans and materials.   For instance, Participant 1 relied on the use of a diagnostic test, which consists of asking learners to fill out a written questionnaire. “For me, it’s mandatory to have a diagnostic test before I start any class and in that diagnostic test I include things like telling me about your life story, tell me about your preferences, about your hobbies, about your sports you practice, and define yourself in  a word.” This instrument allows Participant 1 to gain access to pupils’ cultural resources and household practices to be taken into consideration in-class lessons. In contrast, Participant 3 preferred to carry out getting-to-know-you activities (e.g. survey, Kahoot, listening activities, and conversational topics) with the entire class, which allows him to ask pupils how they would like the class to be organized, what kind of activities they like, and what their expectations are as well as diving into learners’ cultural capital and lived experiences. This professor takes notes about learners’ ideas and comments and later, he reviews these annotations before designing and planning a class. On the other hand, participant 2 does not administer any specific assessment practice but he relies on the hypothetical thought if he were his student, what activities he would like to see in class. “I know I’m different to them, Okay? but I think that we still have some things in common, like a human being.” Participant 2 assumes that learners’ needs, preferences, and personal and cultural backgrounds are similar to his based on his teaching trajectory. The second theme has to do with the concept of empathy.   Two participants highlighted the word empathy several times as a consequential element in the lesson planning. Nevertheless, their perception of this concept is different for each of them. For Participant 1, empathy is a way to remind her of her responsibility as a professor by including their learners’ cultural capital and lived experiences in planning a lesson. “My duty is to show empathy during the process of planning. While others are pretty busy planning activities, I am pretty busy learning about my students’ life to include in the planning.” This meaning denotes the level of regard that participant 1 shows for her learners’ cultural assets and family practices and brings up these elements in the lesson planning. In contrast, for participant 2, empathy is an important criterion for designing lesson activities. “I use a basic concept that is empathy. If I were in Portuguese, French, or German, I wouldn’t do certain activities that don’t motivate.” For participant 2, empathy is a manner of putting himself in his learners’ shoes based on his previous experiences as a language learner and professor. This implication is ingrained more with the concept of prolepsis as “the representation of a future act or development as being presently existing" (Webster’s dictionary cited in Cole, 1996, p.183), which reveals how Participant 2 relied on his past as a future orientation in teaching language practices.

## Discussion

This study contributes to the growing body of research on funds of knowledge in higher education by offering an in-depth understanding of how foreign language faculty members bridge students’ experiential knowledge with the formal curriculum within higher education. More broadly, this study adds to the recent commitment to advocating social-justice efforts and issues of equity, power, and pedagogical change in the field of learning sciences. The connection between learners’ lived experiences and higher educ ation instruction contributes to underscoring respect and honoring the cultural background features of diverse students within a venue where they are learning a foreign language. In addition, this study shed light on good practices that foreign language fa culty professors implemented to unveil their learners’ lived experiences and cultural capital for planning a class. The implication of closing the gap between scholarship practices and home generates opportunities to implement assetbased teaching in future research work of foreign/second language classrooms.
