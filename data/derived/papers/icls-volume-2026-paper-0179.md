# Design and Perceptions of a Science Conversation-based Assessment with AI Agents

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Conversation-based assessments (CBAs) incorporate conversational agents into computer-based tasks to elicit evidence about learners’ knowledge and skills in a domain. We describe the design of a science conversation-based assessment incorporating AI agents that engages high school students in a scenario related to storm formation and storm prediction. We further discuss results from a usability study in which students completed the CBA task and subsequently responded to interview questions about their perceptions related to the design and usability of the task. Students generally had positive experiences with the task and its design, though challenges related to information clarity and AI interaction authenticity were noted.

Advances in learning sciences emphasize the importance of assessing students’ ability to apply disciplinary knowledge in authentic contexts  (Markauskaite et al., 2024). Theoretical frameworks such as sociocultural learning theory (V ygotsky, 1978) highlight the role of discourse and collaboration in shaping how students construct and demonstrate knowledge. These perspectives inform assessment approaches  that prioritize evidence of “knowledge in use” and leverage technology to support meaningful engagement. STEM assessment innovation focuses on how students actively use  their knowledge in interdisciplinary contexts, emphasizing technology use and classroom discourse to connect scientific and mathematical concepts to relevant problems (Harris et al., 2023). Performance tasks can support student engagement with authentic scenarios that are complex and multidimensional, enabling them to develop practices and skills that are less emphasized in shorter tasks or multiple-choice items. When embedded in instruction, performance tasks support students’ engagement  in sensemaking of authentic science problems and are best suited for practices that are difficult to measure through single-response items (Darling-Hammond, 2017; Shepard et al., 2018). Conversational agents can enhance the impact of performance tasks by supporting student engagement and learning (Ortega-Ochoa et al., 2024). These software programs (e.g., chatbots) communicate with users using natural language (Yildirim-Erbasli & Bulut, 2023). Conversation-based assessments (CBAs) integrate conversational agents into computer-based assessment tasks to capture students’ knowledge and skills through interactive dialogue. Compared to traditional assessment formats, CBAs offer richer insights  into the decisions students make and can foster discursive practices that encourage students to articulate, refine, and reflect on their ideas (Zapata-Rivera et al., 2023). In this paper, we describe the design of a CBA task and discuss results from a usability study with high school science students where they provide perceptions and suggestions for improvement for the task. Our CBA leverages AI-based conversational agents to elicit, scaffold, and assess student reasoning in real  time through prompts that encourage explanation, justification, and revision of ideas during data interpretation and prediction  making. Rather than evaluating learning outcomes, this study focuses on students’ experiences with AI-mediated interactions and opportunities for sensemaking. The current study was guided by the following research question: How do high school students perceive the design and overall experience of a science CBA incorporating AI agents?

Conversation-based assessment task design Our design team included expertise in learning sciences, cognitive science, STEM education, technology, and machine learning to develop a CBA performance task for assessment and instructional purposes. The design of the CBA integrates the evidence-centered design (ECD) framework (Mislevy et al., 2003) with the AutoTutor framework for dialog and teaching tactics (Graesser et al., 2001), using theory-driven speech acts  to elicit interpretable evidence of student reasoning during AI-student interactions. The task, named The Weather Task, engages high school students in exploring storm formation and using data from weather stations to predict the likelihood of a storm in the U.S. Southern region  (see Figure 1 for a 1377 screenshot of the task). The task is grounded in Earth science content and incorporates science practices from the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS), a U.S. -based framework emphasizing science and engineering practices, such as  arguing from evidence and planning and carrying out investigations. By promoting climate literacy (McCright et al., 2013) and awareness of weather-related phenomena (Kessler, 2021), the task sequence (Table 1) prompts students to make evidence-based predictions. Activities include  an animated video on storm formation, animated slides introducing a weather index scientists use to predict the likelihood of storms, the Total Totals Index (TTI; Miller, 1972), and web-based activities for concept mapping, data collection from weather stations, index calculation, and AI agent interaction.  The final component is a writing task aimed at communicating storm risks to the public.

Each activity features conversation prompts with two AI agents: Dr. Garcia, a scientist and authoritative figure, and Art, a student peer. We used an LLM-based approach to program agent interactions through prompt engineering, with  the goal of eliciting students’ thinking, guiding data collection and analysis, a nd supporting collaborative discourse. The system also includes three agents operating in the background: the watcher, formative assessor, and summative assessor. The watcher manages conversation flow and monitors for rule compliance, the formative assessor categorizes student responses to inform agent feedback, and the summative assessor aggregates evidence to show alignment with NGSS  performance expectations  (Hou, 2025). To strengthen instructional validity, three high school teachers reviewed multiple iterations of the task and provided feedback to ensure alignment with STEM curricula and to reflect the diverse student experiences and reasoning approaches. ## Participants

Thirty-seven high school students were recruited for the usability study using a digital flyer on our organization’s website. Participants represented all grade levels  (9 th (13), 10 th (5), 11 th (9), 12 th (10)) and included a  mix of genders (female (13), male (24))  and diverse ethnic backgrounds  (White (8), Hispanic  or Latino (9), Black (3), Asian (14), two or more races (3)). Students also represented a range of educational backgrounds, with GPAs between 2.7 and 4.3. Before starting  the Weather Task, students completed a demographic survey collecting information on age, gender, and attitudes towards math and science.

## Procedure

Each participant joined a virtual session with a facilitator and observer where the study’s purpose was explained, and students received a link to the Weather Task along with an anonymous ID. To maintain privacy, cameras remained off throughout the session. Students shared their screen while completing the task which was recorded for analysis. After completion, students participated in a structured interview  about the task’s usability and connection to science learning experiences.  Sessions lasted about 90 minutes, with roughly an hour for the task and half an hour for the interview.

## Measures

Facilitators used an interview protocol with  12 multi-part questions focused on task design and usability. Topics included clarity of the task, how helpful the task was perceived to be, what was confusing, how easy to use/helpful particular task features were (e.g., Notes tool, calculator, concept map), interpretations of features and how they were used (e.g., plots, data tables), suggestions for improvement, and perceptions of the agents. Thematic Analysis (TA) is a method which identifies and organizes patterns of meaning across a qualitative data set. The approach generally follows six phases: familiarization with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing potential themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report (Braun & Clarke, 2012). Recently, researchers have used LLMs to help expedite the TA process through human-in-the-loop coding approaches (Dai et al., 2023).  This approach allows the LLM to speed up the process for initial code generation while still keeping a human-in-the-loop for code refinement and theme verification. For example, the LLM may provide themes that are either too broad (e.g., entire dataset) or narrow (e.g., specific interview questions) that do not align with the goals of the analysis. In this paper, we utilize LLM-enhanced TA to synthesize the results from the usability study.

After data were collected, participants’ interview responses were compiled into three  spreadsheets aligned to the sections of the structured interview (Overall usability of the task, Data familiarity and interpretation, and Use of AI agents), with individual questions in each column and individual participants in each row. To create the initial codes and thematic categories for each question, the responses were analyzed using a secure, enterprise version of Bing Copilot. The researchers prompted the LLM by asking it to provide the steps it would take to conduct the thematic analysis. Then, the researchers attached the data and described the content and format of the files. The LLM then created codes for the first question for review.  These initial codes were evaluated by researchers to ensure accuracy and appropriateness  in scope by reviewing students’ original responses. After a researcher provided feedback to the LLM that the initial codes were appropriate, the LLM continued the analysis with the remaining questions. Finally, the LLM provided a summary of themes across the entire set of questions. Once this was completed, a researcher reviewed the themes for general accuracy. While accurate, many could be collapsed into broader themes. For example, the LLM provided themes related to specific activities within the task. “Graph Interpretation” and “Data Analysis” were combined to create the broader theme “Engagement with Content”, as both specifically involved understanding and interpreting data related to the TTI, leading to redundant codes. After revising the initial themes provided by the LLM, the researchers re-coded each participant response to the revised themes to ensure that every response belonged to a particular theme for each question. Five broad themes emerged based on students’ responses to the interview questions after completing the Weather Task, which were then grouped into two overarching categories: Usability and Perception. Usability theme s 1379 reflect experiences with task features and interface elements, while the Perception themes capture classroom comparisons and how AI-mediated conversational interactions supported or constrained sensemaking. Usability The first theme was Information Clarity and Sufficiency, which emerged based on students’ understanding of the task, sufficiency of provided information, and clarity of instructions. Students generally report ed receiving sufficient information to complete the task  (78%), while specific task elements or technical vocabulary caused confusion for some students  (e.g., the Total  Totals index, data analysis). The second Usability theme  was Task Design and User Experience, which emerged based on students’ responses regarding task layout, colors, design, and technical usability of the platform.  Students generally liked the overall look and design of the task (75%), describing it as clean, well-structured, and easy to navigate, but suggested improvements for layout and design (e.g., use more color, reduce amount of text, use bigger font) and functions (e.g., clarify concept map functionality, support ability to go back to previous pages).

Perception Perception themes included Interaction with AI Agents which emerged based on students’ responses about comfort and realism in interacting with the agents, responsiveness of the AI, and comparisons with real-life teachers and classmates. Students generally reported the AI agents provided a positive interactive experience  (72%), noting that interactions were easy to follow, with agents often explaining errors and prompting revision. Students also felt comfortable typing in the chat box (85%), though some students felt the agents lacked realism or responsiveness (e.g., repeated questions, not enough interaction with the peer agent). There was also mixed feedback on how much the agents resembled real-world teacher and peer interactions. The second Perception theme was Engagement with Content, which emerged based on responses related to interest and engagement with different aspects of the task. Students generally found the task helpful  in learning about weather concepts (94%) and noted data analysis, including calculating the weather index  from the data, the animated video on storm formation, and interacting with the agents as particular favorite parts of the task. The final Perception  theme was Classroom Relevance, which emerged from responses related to comparisons of the task to traditional classroom activities and other online tools. Students were divided in their responses: 47% found the task similar to classroom activities, citing familiar  features such as videos, whereas 53% reported that the task was not similar to other classroom activities, emphasizing AI-mediated dialogue as a particularly novel element. The present study examined the development and evaluation of the Weather Task, a CBA designed for high school science students specifically focusing on storm  formation and prediction. With  this paper focusing on students’ perceptions, the findings generally suggest that the Weather Task, which integrates AI conversational agents, was well-received by students who expressed appreciation for its design and interactive elements.  Students’ perceptions suggest that AI-mediated dialogue supported opportunities for sensemaking by prompting explanation, evidence use, and reflection within an authentic science context, even as students identified limitations in interaction realism. Such integration of AI conversational agents in digital performance tasks such as CBAs offers an innovative approach to collecting and analyzing student responses in a more nuanced and interactive manner (Zapata-Rivera et al., 2023).

Though the CBA was well-received overall, some issues were noted regarding the clarity of the information provided, as well as the authenticity of the interactions with the AI agents. Further research should seek to address such issues, for example, by exploring approaches for fine-tuning the AI agent s, particularly in terms of developing the appearance of human-like empathetic qualities (Casas et al., 2021). Future studies could explore the impact of CBAs on learning outcomes across diverse subjects and learning contexts. Drawing insights regarding these issues will require the recruitment of a larger sample of students, as well as  the engagement of educators to support an equity focused co-design process (Kang & Nation, 2023). Overall, we believe this work contributes important insights regarding the design and use of CBAs, thus paving the way for future advancements in interactive educational technology and assessment.
