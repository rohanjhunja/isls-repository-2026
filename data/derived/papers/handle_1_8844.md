# Genres of Discussion-Based Social Emotional Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Social and emotional learning (SEL) scholars advocate discussion as a promising instructional method yet rarely specify how such discussions should be conducted. Furthermore, SEL literature contains contradictory discursive imperatives and typically overlooks the ways emotional experience and expression are shaped by culture, class, and gender. We addressed these issues in analyses of two primary language arts lessons drawn from a two-year ethnographic study. Each case study lesson illustrated a different interactional genre for discussing emotions: (1) inclusive emotional dialogue, in which students shared emotions experienced in their everyday lives; (2) emotional inquiry, in which students explored emotions and their social meanings. Both types generated informative discussions about students’ emotions but put the teacher and students in challenging positions as students publicly exposed their hardships. Both types were also, at times, limited by norms of hegemonic positivity, gender-based assumptions, and teachers’ promotion of a white, middle-class emotional code.

## Introduction: Promoting discussion through social emotional learning

Social emotional learning (SEL) is widely promoted as a means to improve students’ well-being, academic achievement, mental health, and civic engagement (e.g., Domitrovich et al., 2017; Jones et al., 2017). One strategy for advancing SEL is to integrate social, emotional and academic development (Aspen Institute, 2019), for example, by ‘enhancing’ the language arts curriculum with SEL (Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning, 2017). A central method for advancing these aims is discussion-based instruction (e.g., Assor, 2020). However, scholars advocating classroom discussions for SEL rarely specify how they should be designed and facilitated.

We explore discussions about emotions in the analysis of two case study lessons drawn from a twoyear ethnographic study of a dialogic pedagogy intervention designed to foster productive classroom dialogue in the language arts. The two lessons were conducted in a primary school in a mixed Arab-Jewish socioeconomically disadvantaged Israeli city. Each case reflected a different interactional form for discussing emotions: (1) inclusive emotional dialogue – a form of dialogue in which participants are expected to share their own emotions and to support others’ sharing of their emotions; and (2) emotional inquiry – a form of dialogue in which participants are encouraged to explore emotions and how they and others experience and express them. Social emotional learning discursive imperatives SEL, like other educational trends, emerged within a certain cultural and historical context and shoul d be understood as part of a broader therapeutic turn in  education (e.g., Ecclestone, 2012). For present pu rposes, we focus on how scholars envision SEL discussions. We identify three main discursive imperatives: ‘Don’t bottle things up’ SEL guidance emphasizes that pupils should learn ho w to express their emotions (Collaborative for Acad emic, Social, and Emotional Learning, 2013; Jones et al.,  2017; Merrell & Gueldner, 2010) and advocates shar ing emotions as a desired practice (Department for Education and Skills, 2005; Merrell & Gueldner, 2010). Cameron

(2000) critiques the imperative ‘don’t bottle things up’, noting that its goal is limited to venting bottled up feelings,

and does not include reflecting on those feelings: ‘to speak them so that they may subsequently be spoken about’ (p. 157).

Inclusiveness SEL programs place a strong emphasis on sharing emo tions. Students are guided to share their emotions and teachers are expected to encourage such sharing, wi thout restriction or direction (Gillies, 2011). Wil ce and Fenigsen (2016) conclude that ‘emotion pedagogies e vince a need to recognize—and, often, fully accept— all emotions, including “bad” or “negative” ones such as anger or shame’ (p. 84). The authors point out that the idea of emotional inclusiveness is historically recent, as many cultures have traditionally tended to direct people toward desired emotions, while avoiding and even condemning the expression of others. Hegemonic positivity SEL programs tend to promote what Stearns (2017) calls ‘hegemonic positivity’, based on ‘an assumption that children should feel good in school and about school’ (p. 265). Hegemonic positivity is expressed in SEL by proclaiming the primacy of joy and by putting a strong emphasis on training pupils to control anger and stay calm. Gillies (2011) calls it a ‘“feel good” language... that deflects argument and disarms criticism’ (p. 188). ICLS2022 Proceedings 59 © ISLS Hegemonic positivity, however, has been criticized: for example, Dutro (2008) writes, ‘if wounds are not welcome, children will correctly sense that what school wishes to hear is the banal, the safe, the bland, and they will leave what matters most muted beneath a sterile, clean bandage’ (p. 18). Hegemonic positivity is in tension with inclusiveness and the call to accept all emotions. For example, teachers in a classroom observed by Stearns (2017) explicitly told students that bad feelings ‘should only last for a certain amount of time’ (p. 277). Stearns asserts that the directive to focus on positive emotions made teachers overlook students’ considerable interest in discussing ‘bad’, difficult, emotional states.

## SEL in context

SEL not only expects and promotes a certain emotional code; it also assumes a cultural world in which it wishes to operate, addressing and ignoring other social identities and discursive genres. Following Stearns (2017), who identifies a gap between the rare explicit mentions of race, class, and gender during SEL discussions and their ‘ineffable presence’ in them (p.152), we discuss here how emotional dialogues interact with gender and socioeconomic status. Gender Evans (2017) shows how perceptions of gender roles generate different expectations for boys’ and girls’ participation in SEL. Girls are invited to ‘suspend affect, and to engage in the intellectual exercise of analysing and articulating emotions … In contrast, boys [are] … permitted to passively participate, with their docile bodies serving as a signifier of success’ (Evans, 2017, p. 198). Similarly, Wood and Brownhill (2018) show how teachers evaluate pupils’ needs vis-à-vis gender, depicting boys, mainly those with ‘anger problems’, as needing strong boundaries.

Socio-economic Class Hoffman (2009) criticizes the common SEL demand that ‘children in the elementary grades should be able to recognize and accurately label simple emotions’, arguing that ‘not all cultures interpret emotional experience in the same way, nor do they assign the same kinds of regulatory or expressive responses (such as talk) commonly shared by the White, American middle class’ (p. 540). Gillies’s (2011) ethnographic study exposes a multilayered gap between the everyday needs of underprivileged pupils and the values promoted by the influential Social and Emotional Aspects of Learning (SEAL) program. The students she studied often discussed difficult events, such as local teenage stabbings, in a ‘seemingly emotionless manner’ (2011, p. 200). From an SEL perspective, such a response is ‘indicative of poor social skills and low emotional intelligence’ (ibid). Yet Gillies suggests the students used this emotional code to cope with a reality they could not affect or escape, and it ‘effectively worked to defuse difficult feelings while fostering supportive social bonds’ (ibid). In sum, scholars call on teachers to use discussions to promote SEL but prescribe different and often contradictory discursive imperatives. How are such discussions enacted in practice in classrooms in diverse cultural contexts?

## Research context and methods

The data were collected as part of a design-based implementation research project designed to foster productive dialogue in primary language arts classrooms. Participating teachers aimed to facilitate discussions that afford students opportunities to express their thoughts, build on others’ ideas, and reason about their own and others’ perspectives (Alexander, 2020; Authors, 2021a). During the 2019-2020 school year, the program included ten schools, most of which were in underprivileged neighborhoods, including the school at the center of this paper. This paper focuses on lessons in two fifth-grade classrooms, documented in March 2020. The two classes shared similar demographics: most of the 31-32 students in each class were Mizrahi Jews, several pupils recently emigrated from the former Soviet Union, each class contained one Arab student, and 4-5 students were identified as having special educational needs. Both classes had balanced distributions of boys and girls. Most students came from lower-middle-class families and, according to the teachers, some experienced financial difficulties and domestic violence. The two classroom teachers, whom we are calling ‘Miriam’ and ‘Osnat’, were both highly respected veterans. The first author conducted weekly observations at the school, documenting teacher team meetings and Hebrew language arts lessons in Miriam’s and Osnat’s classrooms. Our two case study lessons, taught in the two fifth grade classes, were dedicated to a preparatory discussion conducted prior to reading the short story ‘The King Who Could Not Cry’. The story tells of a king whose kingdom is extremely prosperous and whose subjects are wonderfully happy. Yet one problem vexes the king: he cannot cry. The story follows the king’s futile attempts to cry, helped by his advisers who try to make him cry by showing him sad sights such as a black ICLS2022 Proceedings 60 © ISLS clouds, dry thorns, and a lizard that lost its tail. These attempts fail one after another. Finally, after many years, upon reaching old age, the king sees a young couple in love; this reminds him of his youth and evokes his tears. We chose to focus our analysis on the lessons dedicated to ‘The King Who Could Not Cry’ as the classroom discussions revolved around crying and emotional states related to crying, thus lending themselves particularly well to SEL topics and aims. We applied principles and methods of linguistic ethnographic microanalysis (Rampton et al., 2015) in analyzing the cases. Contrasting two forms of classroom emotional dialogue We examined how two classroom discussions unfolded vis-à-vis teachers’ anticipations and SEL goals, cultural assumptions, and contradictory imperatives. In our data we identified two types of discussion relevant to SEL: inclusive emotional dialogue and emotional enquiry. In inclusive emotional dialogue students share their experiences and feelings with the teacher’s encouragement, support, empathy, and approval. In emotional inquiry, teachers and students investigate their emotional experiences and perspectives through the use of openended questions, probing one another’s ideas, weighing evidence, and drawing connections. Each form of dialogue sheds light on different aspects of the challenges facing classroom discussions about emotions. Yet both types of discussions require that students share personal emotional stories and associated ideas about emotions and their expression. In this way they may expose gaps between SEL-preferred white, middle-class emotional codes and how students from diverse backgrounds experience and express their own and others’ emotions.

Inclusive emotional dialogue in Miriam’s class Miriam opened the discussion by asking the class: ‘Ok, is there someone who is willing to share when he cried last? And why?’ Thereby she set in motion a round robin discussion in which students shared in turn their personal experiences of crying. Each turn was followed either by clarification questions or empathetic approval.

Miriam’s question aroused the class, generating much chatter and attention. The first four students shared short anecdotes to which Miriam replied with playful banter, thereby signaling that the students’ contributions were welcome. During these short dialogues, a girl remarked that ‘boys cry because, when they lose in something’. A boy commented, ‘I’ve never seen a boy cry”.  John, a fairly tough and popular boy, said that he cried on his birthday, when he heard that his friend’s mother had a baby. The sixth student to take the floor said she cried yesterday, just to let things out. Here, Miriam built on the student's talk to express a general argument: “You say that you had something, that was kept inside, very, very much, and you did not let it out. Not by talking or by crying, and it just came out. Because it was night. I can understand what she’s saying. At night, it’s quieter around us and we slowly, slowly gather into ourselves towards – towards the night and sleep and dreams, and then many thoughts well up within us.” Miriam then returned to John: ‘Wait a minute I want to go back to what I heard John say earlier, you [plural] were at John’s birthday party right? And Zohar was there too, right? And then they told Zohar his mother had given birth? So why did this make you cry?’ ‘It's moving,’ he replied.

In these two interventions, while abiding with the inclusive norms of sharing and approving, Miriam accentuated ideas that aligned with the emotional code she wanted to promote. The conversation with John also demonstrated that while the conception that boys don’t cry was present in the discussion, some boys, including tough and popular boys, talked about crying openly, even in the traditionally feminine context of a friend’s significant life event.

Miriam’s response distanced the discussion from domestic violence through two discursive moves. First, she focused on Liron's emotional state. This echoes one of the key principles of SEL and emotional knowledge: focusing on naming emotions and reflecting on them. By so doing, Miriam turned Liron’s story into a story about his internal emotions as opposed to the external events to which he was exposed. Moreover, Miriam suggested Liron was angry, and anger made him cry (line 9), without considering more passive emotional states, such as sadness, fear, anxiety, distress, or sorrow, which would reflect an innate helplessness Miriam’s interpretation of Liron's emotional state as anger might have been affected by a gender-based perception of emotions and their expression (Evans, 2017; Wood & Brownhill, 2018) which sees anger as an active masculine emotional response to getting hurt (although we cannot know whether Miriam would have treated a female student differently).

Second, Miriam built on her focus on Liron’s emotional state to conclude the entire discussion by pointing to the range of emotions that may bring people to cry, one of the main goals of the discussion. Turning a story about domestic violence into a discussion on the varied emotions that may evoke crying may be thought of as a form of defensive teaching (McNeil, 1982), aimed to defend the teacher, as well as the students, from public exposure of and discussion about students’ hardships. Similar dynamics have been identified in the sparse ethnographic literature on SEL in other classroom discussions. For example, Coe and Nastasi (2006) documented a classroom discussion in which a student told her class that her grandmother ‘sometimes used a stick or cable wire’ to beat her. The teacher replied by asking her if the situation was really stressful and if she was upset. Miriam was not trained for SEL, but she used the same ‘name-your-emotional-state’ practice and by using it, like the teachers documented by Coe and Nastasi, overlooked and perhaps normalized a story about domestic violence (see also Gillies, 2011; Stearns, 2017). To understand why this happens, we need to examine the structural conditions of emotional classroom discussions; we return to this issue in the Discussion.

Emotional inquiry in Osnat’s lesson Osnat initiated her classroom discussion with the question: ‘Imagine that I have a magic wand and everyone I touch will never cry again. Who wants me to touch him?’ The question yielded eight responses, with students saying they did not want to stop crying. The first student who answered the question explained that we can cry because of joy or happiness. While she spoke, several students offered additional examples of positive experiences that generate crying. One said ‘laughter’, which the speaking student embraced and repeated, and at least two others said ‘[out of] emotions’. The students in effect followed the logic of the dominant cultural code, according to which emotions and emotional expression are good and, since crying is the product of emotion, crying is also good.

Students also addressed crying as a means of communication. The first speaker who promoted this line of thought said that if she did not cry, others would not understand how she felt. A boy who spoke after her said that when he cries, ‘everyone rushes to me and asks me what happened’, thus attributing a specific message to crying: it calls for and generates positive attention. This was supported by other students. The next student (male) to speak said, ‘If someone does something to you then… crying says, like, leave me alone. ## Genres of classroom discussions about social and emotional issues

Discussion is seen as a favorable instructional method for SEL, but we found the term ‘discussion’ was too vague in the SEL literature. We identified in our data set two very different types of discussion, each with distinct goals, norms, affordances, constraints, and challenges. In inclusive emotional dialogue, students were encouraged to narrate personal, sometimes difficult stories, thereby enabling the sharing of emotions as experienced in students’ everyday lives. However, as indicated by Liron’s story of how his brother choked him on his birthday, this genre can put teachers and students in a challenging position. On the one hand, sharing personal stories entails exposing fears and hardships in the class plenum, an environment in which it is hard to ensure that students receive the appropriate response to such exposure. On the other hand, if the teacher glosses over these issues, she implicitly conveys that such issues are either not important or unsuitable for discussion, should be kept secret, are not the school’s business, and so on (Dutro, 2008).

Emotional inquiry promoted exploration of children’s understandings and expressions of emotions. It generated informative discussions about emotional states related to crying and their diverse social meanings and may thus be considered especially compatible with SEL (as well as other pedagogic) goals. However, norms of hegemonic positivity can limit or silence the expression of emotions that deviate from official school expectations.

Faced with these challenges, the teachers we observed reacted in various ways. One reaction was to casually acknowledge the content of students’ experiences by thanking them for sharing, indicating that what they shared was important, or by playfully bantering with them, thereby suggesting the sharing was in and of itself the end goal of the discussion. However, when sharing does not lead to reflection, SEL’s effectiveness is questionable (Cameron, 2000; Nils & Rimé, 2012). A second reaction was to probe students’ responses, figuring out together what made them cry or why they wished to continue or give up crying. A third reaction was to emphasize and generalize certain answers, thereby endorsing these answers as the teacher’s and school’s official position. Finally, in some cases, the teachers held back and chose not to discuss sensitive issues such as domestic or school violence.

## SEL’s cultural assumptions reflected in the classroom discussions

We found the white, middle-class cultural code, critically identified with SEL (Hoffman, 2009), conflicted with many students’ cultural codes. For example, the SEL literature emphasizes inclusiveness (Wilce & Fenigsen, ICLS2022 Proceedings 64 © ISLS 2016). From this perspective, crying is encouraged. For Yotam, however, crying entailed internalizing bad things others had said about him, which gave him a solid reason to refrain from crying (see also Gillies’ (2011) discussion of anger). Yotam was strong enough to insist on his perspective, and the classroom culture allowed him to take a critical stance on the teacher’s position. In other instances, students may learn that their emotional code is inadequate, even when it serves important goals in their lives. This case thus serves as additional support for culturally relevant SEL (Mahfouz & Anthony-Stevens, 2020).

Our findings indicated gender-based assumptions can interact with classroom emotional dialogues. The conception that ‘boys don’t cry’ was present in the classroom discussions and was even expressed directly. However, many boys actually reported having cried, and some even reported crying in stereotypically ‘feminine’ contexts, such as the birth of good friend’s baby brother. The findings thus demonstrate that the classroom reality produces diverse manifestations of the relationship between gender, age, and the way emotions are talked about. Gender was also evident in the way Miriam constructed Liron’s emotional state after he was attacked by his brother, calling it anger (a stereotypically masculine emotion), not sadness or helplessness.

We found the teachers were guided by hegemonic positivity (Stearns, 2017, 2018), in that they avoided talk about negative experiences and emotions and attempted to (re)interpret these as positive or neutral events or feelings. This phenomenon was manifested in several ways. First, a teacher’s response to a student’s story about domestic violence, while aligned with the SEL agenda of focusing on students’ emotional responses, did not touch on some disturbing aspects of his story-his brother fighting with his mother over money and then choking the student for interfering. Second, in an instance of teacher-pupil miscommunication, a student’s description of crying as ‘asking for tsumi’, a slang phrase that indicates pitifully asking for ‘socially undeserved attention’, was reframed by the teacher as ‘asking for tsumet lev’, a neutral phrase meaning ‘asking for attention’.

The contradiction between hegemonic positivity and inclusiveness was reconciled by the general insistence that crying is essentially good. Teachers and students repeatedly addressed crying as desired using two main arguments: we also cry because of joy, and by crying, we ‘let things out’. This may indicate a broader trend to affirm all emotions; if emotional states which initially seem bad are ultimately deemed positive, then naturally we need to accept and include them.

When hegemonic positivity and inclusiveness were disregarded, the teacher took a negative position on a student’s stance (Osnat and Anat). This generated a classroom discussion in which students jointly inquired into the social meanings of crying. This finding suggests SEL could benefit from taking into consideration the pedagogic potential of encouraging conflicting voices when discussing emotions in the classroom (Authors, 2013; Authors, 2016).

## Conclusion

Miriam and Osnat are both highly dedicated and experienced teachers who care deeply about their pupils. Why didn’t Miriam develop the discussion on Liron’s story of domestic violence (much like the teachers documented by previous ethnographers, such as Coe & Nastasi, 2006; Gillies, 2011; Stearns, 2017)? Why did Osnat miscommunicate with her students and transform tsumi into tsumet-lev? Classroom discussions, particularly discussions in which pupils express original and unexpected ideas, complicate teaching. They increase uncertainty as the students’ thoughts and ideas are expressed in them, not just the teacher’s (Cohen, 2011). In emotional dialogue, which aims to discuss students’ emotional codes, it is not only students’ thoughts and ideas but also the content itself that is determined by students. In these discussions, almost any topic can emerge. The study shows that emotional dialogue requires teachers to facilitate extremely challenging discussions while encouraging students to share, supporting them when they share difficult stories, and defending them from exposure to criticism, threats to their status, and other social implications. To exploit the full potential of discussions, teachers alsoneed to identify when students express emotional codes that differ from the teacher’s and to encourage these expressions. Overall, the findings indicate that integrating SEL into the ‘regular’ curriculum can enhance it (Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning, 2017. p. 2). They also shed light on some of SEL’s blind spots and suggest that while SEL may enhance language arts, language arts instructional methods may also enhance SEL.
