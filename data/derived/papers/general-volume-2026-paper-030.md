# Introducing Scratch-Quantum: A Block-Based Interface for Quantum Computing Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Quantum computing is increasingly recognized as a critical technological domain; however, its mathematical formalisms and Python-based programming interfaces pose substantial barriers for K–12 learners. To address these challenges, this study introduces Scratch-Quantum, a block-based environment that integrates quantum computing capabilities into Scratch. Leveraging IBM Qiskit as a backend, Scratch-Quantum enables learners to construct executable quantum circuits using visual blocks, allowing them to explore qubit initialization, gate operations, and measurement without writing code. This paper presents the design principles and system architecture of Scratch-Quantum and illustrates how learners can explore core quantum phenomena—such as superposition and entanglement—by constructing and running circuits within the tool. We propose this tool as an entry point for introducing quantum concepts into elementary and middle school classrooms by lowering syntactic difficulties and broadening access to quantum computing education.

As global interest in quantum technologies accelerates, fostering quantum-literate future citizens has become a major educational priority (Bova et al., 2021; Nita et al., 2023). The Q-12 Education Partnership's K–12 Quantum Information Science and Engineering (QISE) Framework identifies foundational concepts—such as qubits, states, measurement, quantum gates, and entanglement—as essential components of early quantum learning (Q-12 Education Partnership, 2025). Several countries have recently begun incorporating quantum computing into public education, increasing demand for accessible tools that non-specialist teachers can use in classrooms (Seskir et al., 2022). Yet most existing instructional tools rely on text-based programming. These environments require learners to simultaneously acquire programming syntax and abstract mathematical representations, which generates extraneous cognitive load that diverts attention from quantum concepts themselves (Sweller, 1988; Yusuf et al., 2025).

To address these challenges, we developed Scratch-Quantum, a visual programming environment that embeds quantum computing functionality into the widely accessible Scratch platform. Scratch-Quantum allows learners to build quantum circuits through a familiar block-based interface and observe measurement outcomes, helping them focus on conceptual understanding. While the conceptual difficulty of quantum phenomena remains an inherent challenge, the block-based interface reduces syntactic barriers so that learners candevote more cognitive resources to grasping fundamental quantum concepts through experimentation and observation. ScratchQuantum primarily targets elementary and middle school students (grades 5–9) who have basic familiarity with block-based programming and introductory probability concepts. The tool aims to broaden access to quantum computing education and provide a foundation for integrating quantum concepts across the STEM curriculum.

## Theoretical framework

Scratch-Quantum is theoretically grounded in Constructionism, which holds that learners actively form knowledge by building and manipulating external artifacts (Papert, 1980; Papert & Harel, 1991). Scratch embodies this philosophy, enabling learners to actively construct computational concepts by creating meaningful projects such as animations and games (Resnick et al., 2009). Two challenges characterize quantum computing education. The first is the syntactic barrier, stemming from the complexity of text-based programming languages. According to Sweller's (1988) cognitive load theory, recalling and typing code syntax generates extraneous cognitive load unrelated to the learning objective. ScratchQuantum replaces code writing with block assembly, reducing this extraneous load and allowing learners to focus their working memory on understanding quantum concepts. The physical shapes of blocks provide visual cues about grammatical validity, shifting learners' attention from low-level syntax to higher-order reasoning (Bau et al., 2017).

The second is the conceptual barrier, arising from the counterintuitive nature of quantum phenomena such as superposition, entanglement, and probabilistic measurement (Seegerer et al., 2021). This barrier exists r egardless of the programming medium and cannot be resolved by a block-based interface alone. ScratchQuantum addresses it in two ways. First, measurement outcomes are rendered as sprite behaviors, transforming abstract quantum states into observable phenomena. Second, histogram visualizations of repeated circuit executions and classroom dashboard aggregation allow learners to experience the relationship between the indeterminacy of individual measurements and collective probability distributions. Existing web-based circuit simulators support learners in assembling and running quantum circuits but limit learners’ interactions to circuit construction alone. QScratch (Escanez-Exposito et al., 2025) offers metaphorical experiences of quantum concepts but does not provide an environment for building and executing actual quantum circuits. Scratch-Quantum goes beyond these limitations by directly integrating executable quantum operations into a block-based programming environment. Quantum concepts are difficult to understand through explanation alone, and passive learning can increase the risk of misconception formation (Hu et al., 2024). In Scratch-Quantum, quantum blocks serve as what Papert (1980) called "objects to think with": learners construct circuits, predict outcomes, and explore the probabilistic nature of quantum measurement through repeated execution. For example, students can create a project in which a sprite's appearance changes based on quantum measurement results—applying an H gate places a qubit in superposition, so each execution produces a different sprite appearance depending on the collapsed state. Adding a CNOT gate creates entanglement, causing two sprites to always change to the same appearance, making the correlation between entangled qubits directly observable. This perfect correlation is possible only in a quantum context, giving learners a tangible experience of the difference between classical and quantum phenomena. Furthermore, by supporting hybrid programming that combines classical control structures (loops, conditionals) with quantum gates, the tool establishes a conceptual bridge toward text-based quantum frameworks such as Qiskit (Papert & Harel, 1991).

Design and features Scratch-Quantum is a browser-based application hosted on a dedicated web platform built upon the open-source Scratch codebase, requiring no installation. The environment is designed to let students build and simulate quantum circuits. This section describes the core features of Scratch-Quantum and the learner experience it supports.

The central component is the Quantum Blocks library, which represents key Qiskit operations through an intuitive drag-and-drop interface. Single-qubit gates, multi-qubit gates, and measurement operations are each represented as distinct blocks. By assembling these blocks, learners can construct and execute quantum circuits without writing code (see Figure 1).

Figure 1 Scratch-Quantum Interface Showing a Superposition Circuit and its Measurement Results Scratch-Quantum integrates quantum operations with classical Scratch logic, allowing learners to embed measurement results in conditional statements or run repeated executions through loops. Each block-based circuit is automatically translated into Qiskit code in the background, creating a pipeline between visual programming and standard quantum frameworks. This builds conceptual continuity and provides a steppingstone toward textbased quantum programming for learners who advance to higher levels. To support classroom use, Scratch-Quantum includes a cloud-based Classroom Orchestration system that allows teachers to monitor learner progress in real time (see Figure 2). Students can share their screens using the Send Screen feature, and the Student Gallery dashboard enables the class to browse one another's projects, compare circuit designs, and reflect on outcomes. These features facilitate timely teacher feedback and create opportunities for peer learning. Together, these features position Scratch-Quantum as both an accessible introductory environment and a pathway toward more formal quantum programming. By situating authentic quantum computing within the familiar creative context of Scratch, the tool supports inquiry-based learning while maintaining conceptual fidelity to the underlying physics. Figure 2 Student Gallery Dashboard Displaying Superposition Measurement Results Across Learners System architecture and implementation The architecture of Scratch-Quantum connects a familiar block-based programming environment with a highperformance quantum simulation engine, allowing learners to engage with quantum computing without installation or configuration barriers. The front-end is built on the Scratch GUI and Blocks framework, augmented with a custom quantum block library. Using blocks such as H gate, X gate, and measure qubit, learners can assemble visual representations of quantum circuits entirely within the Scratch canvas. A middleware layer translates each block configuration into Qiskit-compatible Python code. When a learner runs a program, the translated script is sent to a server-side quantum simulation environment, where the circuit is executed and measurement results are returned to the Scratch interface. These results update sprite behavior or trigger conditional logic within the learner's project, enabling quantum circuit simulation within a visual programming paradigm.

To support real-world classroom use, Scratch-Quantum incorporates cloud-based real-time synchronization. Leveraging Supabase, the system manages project data, execution traces, and screen-sharing events. When learners use the Send Screen feature, their current state—including circuit configurations and experimental outputs—is uploaded to the cloud database. Teachers access this information via the Student Gallery dashboard, which visualizes the class's collective progress in real time. This orchestration infrastructure transforms Scratch-Quantum from an individual learning tool into a collaborative classroom platform, supporting monitoring, formative feedback, and whole-class reflection around quantum experimentation. Interactive demo session In the interactive session, participants will experience Scratch-Quantum from a learner's perspective. No prior knowledge of quantum computing is assumed; the session begins with a brief, jargon-free introduction to superposition and entanglement. For example, superposition is described as "a coin spinning in the air before it lands." Participants then explore the quantum block palette integrated into the familiar Scratch interface. Participants first assemble an H gate followed by a Measurement block to implement simple quantum superposition, observing superposition and state collapse through sprite responses and histogram displays. Those who complete this activity then use a CNOT gate to create entanglement between two qubits. Finally, participants transmit their experimental results to the cloud using the Send Screen feature. These results are displayed in the Student Gallery, where the group can observe how repeated measurements collectively approximate a probability distribution. The session concludes by revealing the Qiskit code automatically generated from the block-based circuit, demonstrating the gradual transition from block-based to text-based quantum programming.

## I mplications and conclusion

Scratch-Quantum provides an environment in which elementary and middle school learners can approach quantum computing concepts through block-based programming. By replacing complex programming processes with manipulable visual structures, it reduces syntactic difficulties and allows learners to experience abstract quantum principles through concrete experimentation. While the block-based interface does not eliminate the inherent conceptual difficulty of quantum phenomena, it allows learners to focus cognitive resources on understanding superposition, entanglement and probabilistic measurement through direct experimentation. Scratch-Quantum also holds promise as a research tool. Its integrated cloud infrastructure provides systematic records of learners' circuit designs, execution patterns, and measurement histories. These data offer researchers new opportunities to investigate how students construct quantum circuits and how misconceptions arise.

Scratch-Quantum currently supports only basic quantum gates (H, X, Z, CNOT) and measurement and has not yet been empirically validated in classroom settings. Future development plans include adding quantum protocols suitable for more advanced learners—such as quantum key distribution and simple search algorithms— and conducting empirical studies to verify learning effects in practice. Through these expansions, ScratchQuantum aims to develop into a sustainable platform for both teaching and research in the emerging field of quantum education.
