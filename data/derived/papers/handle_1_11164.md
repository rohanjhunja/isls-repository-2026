# “In the lived experience of the lesson”: A Case Study of Novice Teacher Improvisation

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Improvisation is an essential aspect of teaching, but has not typically been prioritized in teacher education. The prevalence of procedural approaches to teacher education reflects a prevailing notion that novice teachers should master predetermined moves and apply them automatically before beginning to improvise. An emerging line of research counters this view, arguing that novice teachers can and should improvise. This case study of a novice teacher’s practice contributes to that research by drawing on jazz improvisation to highlight improvisational teaching. Starting from the premise that novices can be responsive and improvisational, this study asks: how do novice teachers engage in improvisation in their initial teaching experiences? Analysis shows that this novice teacher improvises purposefully to further instructional goals and support student learning, and that he employs reasoning that is typically associated with experienced teachers. These findings have important implications for theorizing teacher improvisation and for supporting novice teacher learning.

## Introduction

Improvisation has been identified as an essential a spect for teaching  (Sawyer, 2011). The interactive and co-constructed nature of teaching is inherently improvisational (Loughran, 2019), and teachers’ ability to improvise thoughtfully and effectively is central for cultiva ting more responsive  practice (Jurow & Creighton, 2005). Developing improvisational skills, however, presents significant challenges for teachers. Improvisation entails inthe-moment interpretation and reasoning that is not ext ernally visible, and it is co-constructed with other participants (i.e. students) within constantly evolving social contexts. The practices involved are therefore difficult to describe, and the circumstances needed for teachers to practice improvising are difficult to replicate. This issue is especially pronounced in pre-service teacher education, where more prescriptive, procedural appr oaches are prevalent. In some instances, these prescriptive ap proaches reflect a prevailing notion that pre-service teachers must master predetermined teacher moves and learn t o apply them automatically, a nd that the interpretation and judgment needed for responsive teaching can only emerge later in a teacher's career (Ball & Forzani, 2011; Lemov, 2014). This study contributes to an emerging body of research that challenges these deficit views (e.g. Kavanagh et al., 2019; Philip, 2019). To counter procedural conceptions of teaching practice and deficit views of what novice teachers are capable of in the classroom, this stud y analyzes the improvisational practices and reason ing of a novice teacher who previously participated in a responsive and improvisational teaching fellowship. Drawing on parallels to improvisational practice in jazz provides a framework to identify improvisational actions as they arise in his teaching and to highlight ways of reasoning and decision making that reflect an improvisational  approach. Through observations in  his student teaching placement and video-stimulated recall interviews, this case demonstrates his ability to engage in improvisation even at this relatively early stage of his teaching development. Improvisation in teaching Improvisation is challenging to discuss in teaching because it is difficult to define in concrete and bounded terms. Jazz pianist Chick Corea, who is recognized as one of the world’s greatest improvisers, said at the end of a fiftyyear career, “I’ve never  seen a satisfactory definition of improvisation” (C orea, 2016). To that point, some definitions of improvisation describe the products of improvisation rather than the practices that constitute it. For instance, Jurow & Creighton (2006) define improvisation as “creatively using the resources at hand to de vise an action or response that allows one to develop new p ossibilities for participation and understanding” (p. 275). While this account does describe what improvisation accomplishes, it offers little guidance in terms of  how it comes about, i.e., what an improviser actually does to devise actions and develop new possibilities. One of the more detailed and widely cited definitions of impro visation comes from Berliner’s definitive work “Thi nking in Jazz”, in which he states that “improvisation involves reworking precomposed material and designs in relation to unanticipated ideas conceived, shaped, and transfor med under the special conditions of performance, th ereby adding unique features to every creation” (Berliner, 1994, p. 241). (Note that Berliner also shies awa y from defining improvisation conclusively, instead descri bing features that it involves.) This definition has been taken up in literature on teacher improvisation (e.g., Sa wyer, 2011), which notes  that teaching involves each part of Berliner’s description: teachers are required to respond to unanticipated ideas, they use precomposed designs and materials to organize activity, their performances are subject to the special conditions of the classroom and school system, and so on. These key factors in teacher improvisation invoke o ther closely related constructs that concern how teachers make sense of classroom activity and determine their role and actions within it. The literature on teacher noticing, for example, examines how teachers attend  to, interpret, and make decisions about responding  to classroom activity “within the fast-paced, complex space of daily schooling” (Patterson  et al, 2021, p. 506). The construct of pedagogical reasoning characterizes teaching in terms of managing a continuous stream of dilemmas, each of which creates an unclear situation in which  the teacher must respond to maintain equilibrium a nd move forward (Mansfield & Loughran, 2018).  Literature on adaptive expertise addresses how teac hers draw on knowledge contingently and contextually to respond to such unclear situations, in contrast with “routine” expertise that can be determined in advance (Bowers et al, 2019; Hatano & Inagaki, 1986). The field of responsive teaching examine questions like how teachers can center stud ents’ sensemaking to guide classroom activity (Levi n et al, 2013; Ritchhart et al, 2011) and how teachers can shape instruction in response to students’ identities, experiences, communities, and customs (Gay, 2018; Ladson-Billings, 2014). While there are important differen ces among these approaches to teaching practice, one feature they share is an emphasis on how teachers make decisions and take action based on what transpires in the moment. As such, they indicate both the centrality of improvisation in teaching practice and the importance of understanding how teachers develop and apply their abilities to improvise. Novice teachers’ capacity for improvisation The construct of improvisation, however, has not histor ically been central to teacher preparation. The fie lds of teacher education and professional development have  often prioritized teachers’ content knowledge, pla nning, and control over students, i.e., “classroom managem ent” (Emmer & Sabornie, 2015; Irby, 2014). Even as the teaching profession has moved away from the strict discipline and authoritarian models born from the i ndustrial revolution (Freire, 2017), teacher education has continued to reinscribe the se issues as it has wrestled with how to prepare new teachers for the classroom (Philip, 2019). At the forefront of this issue is the practice-based teacher education movement. Practice-based teacher education (abbreviated as PBTE) first developed from the “practice turn”, in which teacher education shifted its focus from t eachers’ theories and beliefs to their actions (Bal l & Cohen, 1999; Zeichner, 2012). Authors like Ball & Forzani (2011) call for articul ating and disseminating “highleverage practices” to enforce a “focus on the actual tasks and activiti es involved in the work [of teaching]” (p. 19). In its starkest manifestations, this approach has resulted in “teacher proof” curriculum, scripted lesson plans, and lists of predetermined teacher moves that novic es are encouraged to implement reflexively despite changes in context (Keefe & Miller, 2021; Lemov, 2014; Neer, 2014). These imply that improvis ation is something to be avoided, construing it as the opposite of preparati on, structure, or deliberate and thoughtful action.  That is, improvisation means creating something out of nothing, making it up as one goes along, or abandoning structure, presumably to compensate for some failure to prepare. In the words of Holdhus et al. (2016), this naïve conception of improvisation considers its purpose to be “mak[ing] the best of things when plans fail or something unforeseen happens” (p. 4).

Some scholars have more recently challenged this view of improvisation, arguing that improvisation is not opposed to preparation or structure but is in f act an essential aspect of how teachers plan and pr epare, how they enact plans and structures, and how they build  on and adapt plans and structures in response to c lassroom activity (Sawyer, 2011). These growing calls to recognize the importance a nd nature of teacher improvisation have come in part from critics of PBTE.  At the same time, similar arguments are being made by practitioners of PBTE, many of whom argue that their efforts, or at least their intentions, have been mischaracterized or co-opted by other interests. (Neo-liberal school reform and business-model charter schools are frequently named in this respect.) In the former camp, Philip (2019) notes that “the rich and nuanced usages of practice have been reduced in popular reform discourses to ‘practicing’ skills  and techniques” (p. 3), while  Zeichner (2012) argues that practice–based approaches to teacher education undermine soc ial justice efforts by proceduralizing practice and minimizing the role of context, collaboration, and responsiveness. In the latter, Kavanagh et al (20 19) describe the aim of PBTE as moving novice teachers’ enactmen t of practices “from rote to reasoned”. These practitioners of practice-based teacher education maintain that they are not calling for a scripted or rote style of teaching. Instead, they hold that good-faith advocates for “core practices”  still allow for teacher discretion and reasoning; in short, their views are compatible with improvisation and even facilitate it. Understanding and cultivating improvisational teaching The same nuance and ambiguity that makes improvisat ion challenging to define makes it challenging to l earn. Improvisation is difficult to see clearly, partly because it hinges on the capacity to exercise informed judgment and draw on knowledge and resources without interru pting the flow of activity. In other words, the difference between a planned action and an improvised action l ies in the reasoning that motivates it, which is embedded in the moment of instruction  (Kavanagh et al., 2019). This kind of internal reas oning is difficult to articulate, and teachers seldom have occasion to revisit their in-the-moment reasoning. In his work on pedagogical reason ing and equilibrium, Loughran notes: “in the normal cou rse of events, the search for pedagogical equilibri um is not typically organized as a formalized pro cess…teachers’ professional knowledge develops in a manner that keeps it tacit” (p. 528).  Because teacher education relies on making teaching  practice visible so that novices  can recognize, analyze, and cultivate the relevant skil ls (Grossman et al., 2009) the tacit nature of teachers’ in-themoment reasoning can render it inaccessible for novices who may not yet appreciate its importance. Moreover, exercising professional judgment in the moment requires deep knowledge and familiarity with content, context, and learning processes  (Shulman, 1987). Consequently, even some scholars who support improvisation and responsive teaching in general ar gue that it can only emerge from long experience, and that novices therefore are not capable of improvisational practic e. For instance, in a paper analogizing between jazz musicians and mathematics teachers, King (2001) des cribes “the conceptually-oriented mathematics teacher as proficient jazz improviser, in contrast to an inexp ert or learning player, or one who d oes not improvise” (p. 9). Sawyer (2000), however, counters this framing on th e grounds that “there is no sharp diving line betwe en ‘improvisation’ and ‘not improvisation’” (p. 182). To take King’s jazz metaphor, novice jazz musicians improvise from the start of their practice because jazz music ians recognize their discipline as inherently impro visational; there is no “non-improvisational” way to play jazz, and learning to improvise can only result from improvising (Berliner, 1994). As teaching fundamentall y involves respond ing to continuously evolving situations (Sawyer, 2011), the same logic should apply to novice teachers. In a similar debate, some of the aforementioned advocates for PBTE argue that novices cannot exercise judgmen t or discretion in their teaching actions until the y can first implement proven and predetermined “teacher moves” faithfully and without thinking (Keefe & Miller, 2021; Lemov, 2014). Philip (2019) critiques this “skills-first approach” as an unnecessarily narrow definition of teaching preparation and an obstacle to novice teachers’ development, arguing instead that novices are capable of creativity in their teaching and require opportunities to exercise creativity if they are to learn to teach responsively. There are scholars of teacher education who believe in novice s’ capacity for improvisation and aim to support it. Philip  (2019) and Kavanagh et al. (20 19) stand out in this regard, along with scholars lik e Kang & Anderson (2015), Kazemi et al. (2015), and van Es et al. (2017), all of whom work with preservice teachers to develop responsive teaching practice.  Many of these studies, however, ask ho w teacher education programs can introduce the concept of responsive teaching to novices and prepare them to improvise; few studies examine whether and how novices are already improvising. This study aims to contribute to and extend this emerging line of research by looking at how a novice teacher engages in improvisational practices and reasoning in the context of independent classroom teaching.  A thorough and practical understanding of how novic es approach doing improvisational teaching-what their improvisation looks like, its essential components, and how they fit together-is essential for preparing new teachers. In order to support a novice teacher’s learning of improvisation, teacher

educators require a clear conception of the practices and ways of thinking they are working toward. These efforts call for developing more precise and nuanced langua ge for talking ab out teacher improvisation, particularly in ways that are relevant and actionable for novices.  For instance, when Kavanagh et. al. (2019) talk about moving from rote to reasoned, what is the meaningful difference between a rote application and a reasoned application of the same practice, knowledge, structure, or plan? What is the nature and character of that reasoning, and how does one hone the ability to engage in it? What does it look like to use structure, planning, and preparation in an improvisational way?  How do plans and structures h elp an improviser make decisions and take action in  the moment? The urgency of these questions, and the pot ential utility of the answers, call for continued e fforts to describe the role and character of im provisation in teaching practice.  In this study, I analyze how a preservice teacher who was introduced to improvisational teaching in a summer program worked to conceptualize and enact improvisation in his student teaching placement. Th is study takes a strengths-based approach to novice teaching practice, starting from the belief that novices can be creative, responsive, and improvisational in their practice and that it is worth examining their efforts to understand how they are approaching this challenge. Given the premise that novice teachers can, should, and do improvise, I ask the questions: how do novice teachers  engage in improvisation in their initial teaching experiences ? In particular, how do novice teachers navigate te nsions between structure and creativity, or planning and responsiveness? To answer these questions, I draw on jazz improvisation as a framework to identify key improvisational practices and ways of thinking. As an improvisational discipline that is particularly attentive to the role of technical knowledge and structure in collaborative in-the-moment action, jazz has numerous parallels to teach ing practice that have not been adequately explored. Jazz improvisation highlights the manner in which creativity and in-themoment action are not opposed to structure and prep aration. In fact, jazz improvisation relies on structure and preparation, and in many ways the improvisation itself consists of creating, maintaining, and adapting structures. In addition, j azz is a technical artform that values virtuosity, precision, and the creative enactment of technical knowledge and skill. In this regard, jazz is similar to teaching, which prioritizes establishing and meeting learning standards, achieving excellence, and demonstrating mastery of content. The emphasis on rigorous and vi rtuosic improvisation leads jazz players to talk about their practice in detailed  and concrete ways-an attitude that this paper hopes to cultivate in teaching and teacher ed ucation. By looking more closely at these parallels, I aim t o characterize novice teacher improvisation at a similarly specific and actionable level.

## Methods

This paper is a single case study of Brian, a pre-service teacher who participated in a Responsive Te aching Summer Fellowship during his credential program. I designed the fellowship to introduce pre-service teachers to improvisation as an aspect of teaching and give the m opportunities to observe, discuss, and apply key concepts and practices for improvisation. These events took place during the summer of 2022, beginning at the v ery start of participants’ enrollment i n their credential program and concludin g at the start of the fall quarter. During the following spring, in the final months of the creden tial program, I recorded classroom observations of two of the fellowship participants in their student teaching field placement and conducted video-stimulated recall interviews, or VSRIs (Martinelle, 2020) after each observation. A VSRI is a form of clini cal interview (Hunting, 1997) in which the interviewer selects video clips to watch with the interviewee as a mechanism for prompting d etailed and specific accounts of the events in question. As such, VSRIs are especially suited to eliciting the interpretations and reasoning that underlie a practitioner’s specif ic actions (Nguyen et al., 2013). The data for this study consi st of two VSRIs with one of the participating novice teachers, which together comprise 3 hours of video. To analyze this data, I began by writing summative analytic memos (Miles et al., 2019) immediately following each interview. Next, I created content logs describing the main points of discussion, which I segmented by changes in topic (Jordan & Henderson, 1995). This divided the interv iews into sections that each addressed a particular classroom event. I then wrote additional analytic memos about sections that addressed improvisational practices or reasoning in more detail. At this point, I turned to the literature on jazz improvisation to identify key improvisational practices and ways of thinking. Berliner’s seminal work “Thinking in Jazz” (1994) was invaluable in this regard, as the author interviewed more than  50 world-class jazz musicians as the basis for his analysis. Because this study is focused on how practitioners reason and make decisions in the moment of practice, I placed a particular emphasis on practitioner accounts (whe ther through interviews, lectures, or written work). The data in this study are themselves practitioner accounts of improvisation; tracing par allels across these two sources revealed similar reasoning and practices that indic ated improvisational ways of thinking and doing.  To that end, I used Berliner’s analysis as a framework, suppleme nted by other practitioner interviews, writings, and talks. As this literature evoked connections to Brian’s actions in the classroom and his accounts of his reasoning, I compared his accounts with those of jazz musicians to describe improvisational practices that Brian engaged in and highlight distinctly improvisational rea soning. I continued iterating between the jazz lite rature and the interview data, writing and revising analytic memos throughout the process to continue developing and refining explana tions of Brian’s emerging improvisational practice (Engle et al., 2007; Locke et al., 2022).

## Findings

I focus here on two main findings. First, I address how Brian conceptualized lesson plans and structures in a way that is compatible with and supports improvisational practice. Second, I describe how the reasoning behind Brian’s improvisational decisions show ed a combination of horizontal and vertical thinking t hat is consistent with how jazz musicians interpret the evolving circumstances in musical performance. Lesson plans as a form for improvisation Rather than treating a lesson plan as a script, a set of instructions, or a sequence of tasks to follow, Brian’s explanations described the lesson plan as a structure or form that support s and shapes improvisational decisions. This orientation is consistent with the way that ja zz musicians talk about conceptualizing the structu res they rely on in improvisational performance. A typical jazz performance centers on playing a tune, which has a melody and a series of chords that correspond to it. The harmo nic structure established by the sequence of chords  is referred to as the “form” of the tune. To help musicians learn these forms and keep track of them as they improvise, tunes are often represented in a “chart”. In teaching, le sson plans also provide a structural form that teachers use to organize their activity; written lesson plandocuments play a parallel role to jazz charts. There are some common and popular forms that novices typically learn and rely on as they are developing their practice, and they come to recognize the underlying structure of those lesson forms in other contexts, which  helps them improvise through different lessons. Brian applied this reasoning during an interview in which he intr oduces the lesson plan he was using for an upcoming class: “At th is school, we often do almost like a three act struc ture, you know, opening, body, closing. This is going to be a little…maybe a more simple execution of that than some of my other lessons.” Brian’s distinction between a “simple execution” and a “complex execution” of the same lesson plan str ucture echoes how jazz musicians describe variation in forms. Berliner shares that musician Josh Schneider “first learned ‘simple blues changes’ then ‘jazz blues changes’, that he describes as ‘the same as the basic blues but with a few additional chords thrown in.’” (Berliner, 79). Note  that the complex structure is still recognizable as a vari ant of the simple execution, which Schneider used to make sense of the former and navigate its additional complexity-just as Brian recognize d his “three act structure” underlying both simple and more complex instances.  To this point, bassist Chuck Israels stressed the importance of “the abili ty to recognize a parallel case when you're confronted with one. If things remind you of other pieces when you approach a new piece, you generally catalog them very quickly so that you candraw upon your accumulated knowledge” (Berliner, 1994, p. 78). Like jazz tunes, lesson plans are often formally represented in writing  to document the sequence of activities and their intended timing. That said, many jazz musicians and teachers  prefer to use alternative representations of the form to structure their less ons, depending on what is useful in the moment to s upport improvisation. Once they are familiar with a tune, musicians often  don't need a formal chart, using other conceptual and/or graphic representations to navigate the structure-something Berliner describes as “negotiating through the ever-changing patterns around them fro m the perspective of their personal structural maps ” (p. 9293). Just as jazz musicians often only write formal chart s when needed to share the tune with other musicians, Brian comment ed that he only writes formal lesson plan  documents if requested by an evaluator. Without an external reason to document their lesson plans in t he traditional format, improvisers often rely on other representations (both graphic and conceptual). For instance, Brian preferred to mark the structure of his lessons with projected slides. In the interview, he explained that slides provide an accessible and plastic representation of form as he teaches: they advance in accordance with the progress made during the lesson and are easily consulted without interrupting the flow of activity. Moreover, the slides are a representation of form that is a lso visible for students, which serves to orient them to the course of the lesson. These features enabled him to remain immersed in classroom activity even as he attended to the structure, and to use that structure to guide student activity. Slides are also easily reorganized (e.g., skipping slides, reordering, backtracking), which e nabled Brian to improvise with structures as well as within them. In learning to play jazz, novices “soon discover that experts transform the harmonic structures of a piece as routinely as they  do their melodies” (Berliner, 1994, p. 82). Similarly, Brian ’s willingness to rearrange and adapt the structural components of his lesson reflected an improvisational approach. Thinking vertically and horizontally The jazz practices of “vertical” and “horizontal” reasoning to support improvising within and with structures were also visible in Brian’s explanations of his improvisation in the classroom. When improvising, jazz musicians may conceptualize structure vertically or horizontally.  Vertical playing attends primarily to the details of the current chord, enacting and navigating the structure at hand in that moment. Horizontal playing, on the other hand, looks “across” the improvisation by attending more to the broader structure of the progression and shaping the direction of the improvisation as it develops over time. Berl iner states that “some [musicians] may favor a vert ical concept…interpreting a progression’s individual cho rds faithfully, ‘articulating each chord that comes  up’.... Other performers may favor a horizontal concept, which they view as providing greater ‘freedom of movement’” (p. 128). While some may favor one over the other, it is important to note that players “commonly mix vertical and horizontal musical elements in their conceptions.” (Berliner, p. 128). As in jazz, Briandemonstrate d both vertical and horizontal thinking in explaining his improvisational decisions. In vertical terms, he attended to what was happening in the classroom at that moment and how  to respond to it. In horizontal terms, he attend ed to the progression-both the temporal progression through the structures or activities and the conceptual progression of students’ content learning. The combination of these orientations was central for his structural improvisation, as they enabled him to situate the activity of a given moment in th e context of how it fits in the progression.  In response, he could choose actions vertically in the moment to support the progression he wanted to enact, and he could adapt structures horizontally to reorient around what came up in a given moment. In an example that highlight s horizontal thinking, Briandiscussed an instance in  which he needed to decide in the moment whether or not to show a video he had selected for the lesson. Because a video fills a fixed amount of time with little need for facilitation, h is choice to cut it or insert it back involve d a horizontal view of pacing based on the relative importance and time needs of the sections before and after. When we have video clips, it always looms large in my head, because I know they take a certain amount of time. You know, they don't go faster or s lower, they're just there. So definitely, I think at this point I was like, I know we can't get  to the video, right, I'm not go ing to start if I thought there's 2 minutes left. We're not starting a video that you know is 3 minutes long. When included, Brian’s video could extend time and fill space in the form without requ iring any additional structure to support it. On the other hand, it could be cut out to advance directly to the next section without sacrificing any coherence or function in the form. Brian therefore use d the video as a “back pocket” feature to improvise temporal structure, either extending the time spent on the topic or accelerating progress in the lesson. Brian explained that as he taught this lesson, he first decided to cut the video and then added it back into the lesson. This change back and forth was prompted by a point of confusion regarding the day’s schedule. Having glanced at the class times for a d ifferent day of the week, Brian was under the impre ssion he was running significantly short on time. As a result, he was impelled to restructure the rest of the lesson into the time he thought remained, and then to restructure back to a longer timespan when he realized his error. That point of confusion and adaptation led him to discuss his reasoning about structuring the ends of lessons, which raised additional ideas about the relationship between vertical and horizontal thinking. I start looking for off-ramps that make sense…something for closure at the end of a day of lesson. Ideally, we have them summarize what they've learned, or something. Or at least, if we can't get to that summary, at least it's cool to en d on them sharing, you know, doing one of the interactions sharing something. I don't love it when I have to end just reading a line from a slide or something, and then they go out. I feel like that didn't sink in when I'm the last person to say something. So yeah, in this moment I'm looking for an off ramp. Brian’s reasoning about providing an “off-ramp” demonstrates the relationship between the vertical elements and the horizontal elements of improvising structure. Where the  class was in the lesson temporally and conceptually had different implications for how he wanted to shape the current structure vertically. Certain content or activities might have been suitable at earlier points in the lesson but would have been out of place at the ending; when and how he included them depended on how he understood the class’s progression through the form of the lesson. In another example, Brian chose to insert a brief think-pair-share activity where his original plan began a lecture segment. His vertical thinking was evident, as he was responding to a need that came up in the moment by using a bounded structural component that he could enact and complete before moving on to the next. At the same time, he explained that this improvised change to the less on form was based on students’ need to revisit prior context for new information he planned to cover.

I thought to do [the think-pair-share] as I was getting into it, in the lived experience of delivering the lesson. We did the first half of this lesson and then had some project work days, and now we're going back to it. In theory, that sounded good. And then as I started the lesson, I was like, we're about to talk about the ways Gorbachev respon ded to problems that we haven't talked about for a couple of days. I should have them just look over the front half of their notes again, and remember what those problems were.

Here, he situated his decision at that moment in the context of the previous lesson and what stud ents would need to make progress through the current one. In horizontal terms, he attended to how this new structural component fit into the progression, both in terms of justifying the component’s inclusion (i.e., it will make the lecture portion more generative) and in terms of how t he activity transitioned into the next segment; using a think-pair-share provided the class with a clear sequence of activities that segued smoothly into a lecture portion.

## Discussion

Most importantly, this case study demonstrates that  novice teachers can  and do improvise, and they do so purposefully and with positive effects. Brian’s teaching particularly counters the view tha t improvisation serves to “make the best of things when plans fail” (Holdhus et al., 2016, p. 4), as his improvisations were not motivated by patching up problems and he did not view his moments of improvisation as signs of a failure. On the contrary, he expected to improvise while teaching and planned to improvise. He accounted for his improvisations in ways that are well-reasoned and deliberate, and his improvisational decisions were targeted to further instructional goals and support student learning. To accomplish those aims, he drew on ways of thinking that are typically associated with more experienced teachers. For instance, van E s et al. (2022) propose the construct of “multidime nsional noticing for equity” to describe how teachers situa te their noticing of in-the-moment activity in the context of students’ personal histories, sociocultural backgro unds, and potential futures; their account dovetail s with the practices of horizon tal and vertical thinking that Brian’s teaching dem onstrated. This evidence of advanced reasoning and skills in a novice teacher’s practice  challenges the idea that novices must learn to reproduce practices first and improvise later, as suggested b y some PBTE advocates (e.g. Ball & Forzani, 2009; L emov, 2014). Rather, this case shows Briandeveloping his ability to improvise by improvising. This case thereby rai ses questions about how novice teachers should be s upported and what kinds of activity should be expected from them. For instance, the matter of conceptualizing form in this case h ighlights a tension between how teacher education presents lesson plans and how lesson plans are actually used by teachers. This tension has been noted in the literature on te acher education. Sawyer (2011) found that teacher e ducation programs tend to place an “emphasis on detailed les son planning and vignettes of teaching in which the improvisational elements are not made salient” (p. 39), and Grossman et al (2009) cautions that “the lesson plan, as practiced in teacher education, both captures and misrepresents the practice of experienced teachers” (p. 2075). While their comments are oriented toward understanding the practice of experienced teachers so that novices are not led astray by a misrepresentation, Brian’s discussion of lesson planning indicated that he was already sensitive to the realities of how lesson plans are used. He did not write or use lesson plans in the rote procedural ways that Sawyer and Grossman allude to, instead improvising freely with the structural components of his lessons, and he noted that he only performs the expected method of lesson  planning when it is demanded by evaluators. This misalignment is a source of concern for novice teac her evaluation, which may not accurately reflect either experienced teachers’ practice or novice teachers’ learning trajectories. Finally, this case study contributes to theorizing the role of structure in teacher improvisation. While the importance of structure is well established, many accounts of teacher improvisation (including those that analogize to jazz) characterize structures as static objects within which improvisation is performed.  This analysis suggests that teacher improvisation often consists of enacti ng, maintaining, and adapting structures in the mom ent to respond to evolving circumstances and activity. This view is consistent with scholarship in practice theory, which maintains that structures are not objects, but situated processes that are dynamically and collabo ratively constructed, negotiated, and maintained (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011; Gherardi, 2001). Conse quently, I posit that structuring as an active practice is central to teacher improvisation. The practice of improvised structuring is well known in the world of jazz, especially among rhythm section musicians (e.g., piano, bass) who play the role of “comping”, or enacting the harmonies for melodic  soloists. This aspect of jazz improvisation, h owever, is seldom explored in literature that uses jazz as a m etaphor for teaching practice. Future research in t his area has the potential to develop more thorough and complete  theories of teacher improvisation, as well as prov iding generative new directions for novice teacher education and professional development.
