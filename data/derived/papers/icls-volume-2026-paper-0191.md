# Does More Exploration Mean Better Learning? Rethinking VR Log Data Through Instructional Sequences

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines how identical VR log events can reflect different cognitive states depending on instructional sequence. We analyzed detailed logs from a high school cell division VR simulation, comparing the Paper→VR and VR→Paper sequences. Students in the Paper-first condition explored more, but exploration time was not related to achievement. In contrast, for VR-first students, longer exploration and more transitions were associated with lower scores. Round-specific reading and behavioral coupling also differed: in VR-first, reading was linked to immediate correct actions without broader exploratory moves, whereas in Paper-first, reading integrated with a wider range of behaviors. These results suggest that VR traces are not fixed indicators but acquire meaning from instructional context. Rather than arguing that one sequence is superior, we show that VR log features are context sensitive and emphasize that log data should be interpreted within context to guide learner-specific feedback.

Immersive technologies are steadily entering classrooms, giving students opportunities to observe and manipulate complex scientific phenomena beyond what static media afford (Merchant et al., 2014; Radianti et al., 2020). In virtual-reality (VR) simulations, every gaze, grab, and panel view is logged at millisecond resolution, providing a rich substrate for learning analytics. Yet these traces are not self-interpreting: extended “exploration” can reflect hypothesis testing or disorientation, while prolonged “reading” may indicate deep comprehension or repeated searching for guidance. Meta-analytic work on VR shows that presence and motivation often increase, but learning gains vary—patterns linked to cognitive-load and guidance design rather than raw activity counts (Makransky et al., 2019; Makransky & Petersen, 2021).

Learning analytics scholarship likewise cautions that trace data gain meaning only when grounded in theory and context. Winne (2020) highlights the need for construct and consequential validity, arguing that log features must align with instructional intents. Reviews of higher-education analytics show that early studies often treated clicks and dwell time as generic proxies for engagement, producing inconsistent findings when context was ignored (Viberg et al., 2018). A systematic review of immersive-VR analytics reached the same conclusion: most studies still rely on aggregate counts and rarely consider design factors such as sequencing (Tao et al., 2025). Other work finds that relations between behaviors and outcomes can even reverse across settings (Karumbaiah et al., 2022). As VR enters its “big-data” era, analysts risk repeating these pitfalls unless analyses explicitly account for instructional design and learner context (e.g., sequence, prior knowledge, task goals, interface constraints). Research on instructional ordering clarifies why sequence shapes the meaning of VR behaviors. Introducing ideas before a complex task helps learners build schemas that guide later actions, as captured by the pre-training principle (Mayer et al., 2002). In contrast, beginning with exploration can foster productive failure if subsequent instruction helps organize what was tried and why (Kapur, 2008). A Time for Telling similarly shows that expository explanations are most useful after students have wrestled with a problem space, when they have questions the explanation can resolve (Schwartz & Bransford, 1998). From a cognitive-load perspective, guidance that benefits novices may diminish or even reverse as expertise grows, which is known as the expertise-reversal effect (Kirschner et al., 2006; Kalyuga et al., 2003). Taken together, these strands suggest a simple premise: identical VR actions such as exploring, reading, and transitioning carry different cognitive implications depending on sequence and prior knowledge.

Despite these insights, sequencing is rarely considered in VR log analyses; behaviors are often aggregated across participants, assuming a single interpretation. This study addresses that gap in a high-school biology lesson on mitosis delivered in two sequences: Paper→VR (worksheet first, simulation second) and VR→ Paper (simulation first, worksheet second). We analyze exploration time, information-panel reading, and transition structures across three simulation rounds, linking them to posttest performance. Our aim is not to declare one order superior, but to test whether the interpretation of the same behaviors depends on instructional sequence. By identifying sequence-aware behavioral motifs, we seek to inform adaptive VR systems that tailor scaffolds to 1437 both what students do and when they do it.  Therefore, we frame our inquiry around two research questions that link sequence, behavior, and learning outcomes: RQ1: How do exploration time, information-panel reading, and transition structures differ when the VR simulation appears after versus before paper-based instruction? RQ2: Within each sequence, how are these log features related to post-test performance, and do specific behavior chains provide predictive value beyond simple counts? VR simulation and paper-based material We developed a VR simulation using the Oculus Quest to teach high school cell division through interactive, gesture-based learning. The system, equipped with built-in hand tracking and grounded in embodied, distributed, and experiential learning theories, was refined through multiple cycles of user testing and redesign (Kim et al., 2023). The simulation covers all five phases of mitosis (interphase, prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase), each with targeted interactions. For example, during interphase, students collect floating nutrients and deliver them to the mitochondrion to generate energy for growth. Repetition across three cycles, in which students played the simulation three times, helped them adapt to VR and grasp the continuous nature of cell division. ## Study design

Thirty-five students from a midwestern high school participated in this quasi-experimental study, which followed a structured one-on-one interview format. Students were assigned to one of two sequences. In the Paper →VR sequence (N = 20), students completed a pre-questionnaire and a pre-verbal explanation of cell division, studied the paper material, gave a first post-verbal explanation, then engaged with the VR simulation, and finally provided a second post-verbal explanation. In the VR→Paper sequence (N = 15), the order of the middle steps was reversed: students first completed the VR simulation, gave a post-verbal explanation, then studied the paper materia l, and concluded with a second post-verbal explanation.

The study focused on the five phases of cell division, each with a key element students were expected to learn, and both the paper materials and VR simulation emphasized these same elements.  The paper materials mirrored the VR content with a standard cell division diagram, and VR logs were collected during simulation use. To assess knowledge before and after the intervention, students provided verbal explanations rather than multiplechoice an swers, as explaining phenomena is a primary goal of science (McCain, 2015). A coding scheme developed with high school teachers and detailed in a prior study (Kim, 2024)  awarded 1 point for each of five key elements, emphasizing conceptual understanding over exact terminology. For example, describing mitochondria's energy production as “bringing green capsule-shaped nutrients to a yellow object to create energy” was considered correct. Twenty-five percent of responses were double-coded (85.8% agreement, Cohen’s K = 0.72), and the first author coded the remainder.

## Log Data Analysis

To examine how interaction behaviors in the VR simulation related to learning outcomes, we analyzed system log data from the ChromosoME environment. Student behaviors were coded with a structured scheme (Table 2), and transitions between coded states were tracked to capture behavioral dynamics. For each student, we calculated the 1438 duration of behaviors (e.g., exploration, reading), frequency and diversity of state transitions, and the number of unique interaction patterns, with particular attention to Reading as an indicator of information-seeking. Transition pattern s were further examined by reading status and sequence group to explore how reading related to other engagement forms (e.g., object manipulation, movement). This analysis revealed nuanced behavioral trajectories associated with different learning outcomes. Students in the Paper→VR sequence spent significantly more time exploring the VR environment compared to those in the VR→Paper sequence, t = -2.70, p <.01. However, within-group analyses revealed different patterns. For Paper→VR students, exploration time was not significantly associated with posttest performance (r =.26, p =.27). In contrast, for VR →Paper students, there was a marginal negative association, suggesting that longer exploration corresponded to lower scores (r = -.48, p =.08). Reading behaviors in later rounds also showed differential associations with achievement. Because each student was required to play the simulation three times, the data span Rounds 1– 3. In Round 3, among students whonever engaged in reading, Paper→VR students achieved significantly higher scores than their VR →Paper peers, t = -2.34, p =.05. By contrast, when the analysis was restricted to students who did engage in reading during Round 3, the between-group difference was not significant (p >.10). Within the Paper →VR group, an additional pattern emerged: students who, in Round 2, read for at least five seconds at each phase had significantly lower achievement than peers who did not meet that threshold. Patterns of state transitions highlighted differences between readers and nonreaders. Collapsing across groups, readers exhibited significantly more transition types than nonreaders, t = - 3.59, p <.001, reflecting the added reading state and broader behavioral variety. Within the VR →Paper sequence, students with higher achievement showed significantly fewer total transitions than lower-achieving peers, t = - 3.09, p <.01. This relationship did not appear in the Paper→VR group, where the difference was nonsignificant (t = -.78, p =.44). Finally, the specific contrasts between readers and nonreaders varied by sequence. In the VR →Paper condition, all significant differences involved transitions that included reading, typically flanked by correct object grabs, object touches, or movement, and reading did not co-occur with more exploratory transitions such as exploring, incorrect grabs, or object gazing. In the Paper→VR condition, significant differences included not only six reading-related transitions but also seven additional non-reading transitions, suggesting a broader reconfiguration of behavior beyond reading alone (see Table 3 for some examples of transitions). In particular, whether students engage with a simulation before or after studying key concepts seems to shape how their actions might best be interpreted. For students who used VR before learning from paper, longer exploration was associated with lower achievement, which could indicate a trial-and-error approach without clear direction. In contrast, for students who learned on paper first, longer exploration was not related to achievement and may reflect deliberate checking, verifying whether what they had learned matches the simulation. This highlights that behaviors like “more exploration” cannot be universally interpreted as deeper learning. In some contexts, it may reflect confusion; in others, it could signal purposeful engagement. Reading behavior showed similar context sensitivity. For the Paper→VR group, students who spent more time reading the information panel during Round 2 tended to score lower. This might indicate that constant rechecking disrupts smooth task execution and reflects gaps in understanding. However, for VR →Paper students, ongoing reading is likely part of the learning process itself, as these students had not yet studied the underlying concepts. Here, reading may serve an instructional role rather than a sign of uncertainty. Transition patterns align with this view: in VR →Paper, reading transitions were narrowly tied to correct object actions (grabs, touches, movement) rather than exploratory behaviors, a “read-then-execute” tendency. By contrast, the Paper→VR group showed significant differences in both reading-related and non-reading transitions, consistent with more flexible, knowledge-informed action.

This study does not aim to determine which learning sequence is categorically more effective. Rather, it highlights a more nuanced insight: the same VR log behaviors can reflect entirely different cognitive states depending on the learning context. Recent work shows that log traces must be interpreted “through the lens of learning design,” because the same clickstream patterns can map onto different strategies under different task structures (Wang, 2021). In our data, students’ prior conceptual preparation and the moment a behavior occurs within the instructional sequence shape its meaning. Exploration and reading, for example, displayed sequence-specific links to achievement, and the transitions connecting actions revealed distinct ways  learners wove information into interaction, echoing evidence that the direction of log –outcome relations can change with instructional conditions (Gašević et al., 2016). These findings warn against one-size-fits-all interpretations such as “more exploration equals deeper learning.” As big-data studies of higher education note, behavioral indicators gain value only when situated in clearly articulated pedagogical contexts and learner characteristics (Daniel, 2015). Because immersive-VR log analysis is still in its infancy, it is prudent to avoid early  learning-analytics pitfalls, over-reliance on raw counts and context-free heuristics (Viberg et al., 2018). We therefore advocate context-sensitive analytics that read behaviors in light of learners’ position in the sequence and their evolving conceptual readiness.

While the findings provide useful insights, they should be interpreted in light of  several limitations. It examines one topic in a single simulation with a relatively small sample and uses correlational analyses of naturally occurring behavior. We also lacked direct measures of cognitive load and metacognition. Future work should manipulate panel design, access timing, and prompts; develop sequence-aware adaptive scaffolds keyed to transition motifs; and combine VR logs with physiological or self-report data to enable real-time, contextsensitive personalization. 1440 Daniel, B.
