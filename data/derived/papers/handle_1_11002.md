# Indigenous Learning Lab for Inclusive Systemic Design and Expansive Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Native American youth receive exclusionary discipline more frequently and severely for more subjective reasons such as disrespect in the United States. Racialization of discipline is a contextually situated problem whose patterns and predictors change from one social, historical, and spatial context to another. Addressing inequity requires a historicized and situated understanding of important relevant issues to help develop localized solutions in response to school communities’ diverse and often conflicting histories, needs, and goals. In this study, we investigated how Indigenous Learning Lab, a community-based problem-solving intervention, facilitated collaborative transformation effort at a rural high school to design a culturally responsive behavioral support system in partnership with the Anishinaabe nation, school staff, and researchers from a local university.

Decolonizing systemic design and expansive learning In the United States, Native American youth receive exclusionary disciplinary actions more frequently and severely for more subjective reasons such as disrespect (Whitford et al., 2019). Disproportionality is a result of historically accumulated contradictions arising from a racist, capitalist, and settler colonial society. The resolution of disproportionality demands expanded community engagement, critical dialogue on race and white supremacy in designing justice-oriented solutions led and owned by community members.  In this study, we examined how Indigenous Learning Lab (ILL) facilitated collaborative transformation to design a culturally responsive schoolwide behavioral support system in partnership with a band of Anishinaabe nation and school staff at a rural high school, Northwoods High School, in Wisconsin.

Indigenous learning lab: Collaborative design toolkit toward transformation ILL is employs formative intervention methodology to facilitate praxis-a continuous cycle of collective critical reflection and action among local stakeholders (Freire, 2000). ILL  provides an epistemological shift by not only tapping into cultural wealth and spirituality but also validating indigenous epistemology and tribal sovereignty to countervail negative impacts of settler colonization and racism. It builds upon “refusal” of metanarrative in social science research and leverages “resistance, reclaiming, recovery, reciprocity, repatriation, [and] regeneration” (Tuck & Yang, 2014, p. 244) as a transformative tool to fight against damage-centered epistemology.  ILL redirected researchers’ and school leaders’ gaze toward Indigenous prolepsis  to foster collaborative agency and vision of a possible future to develop locally meaningful solutions developed by Indigenous community members. Members follow a cycle of expansive learning actions consisting of questioning, analyzing, modeling, testing, and stabilizing new solutions to resolve the systemwide problems of practice (Enges tröm, 2016). The city of Northwoods: The battlefield of the walleye war The city of Northwoods, where the school is located, was the epicenter of the Anti-Indian movement in the 1990s. Only a generation ago, tribal members encountered angry local White protesters who gathered at boat landings as they fished, meeting hostile crowds of local White men, women, and children who threw bottles and held signs that said, “Save a Walleye. Spear an Indian!” 2333 Nort hwoods High serves as the only choice for Anishinaabe youth living on the reservation to attend high school, as the reservation school serves only K-8. The school is about 13 miles away from the reservation. For most Indigenous youth, ninth grade is the first time they attend school with nontribal peers. Northwoods’ student body comes from four public feeder schools, only one of which has a substantial portion of American Indian  youth. Other feeder schools provide almost exclusively White students from varying economic backgrounds. There have been conspicuous disparities in academic achievement, school dropout, and exclusionary discipline. T hough Indigenous population was 21.3%, they received over 60 % of the suspensions and 100 % of the expulsions.

## Participants

The university research ers including Indigenous scholars worked with the school and community liaisons to recruit potential members who have expertise and experiences related to school discipline and community-school relationships.

Data sources and analysis ILL members participated in ten sessions and one subcommittee meeting from October 2019 to May 2020. Sessions were video recorded. To analyze the collaborative design process, we employed an expansive learning action analytic framework (Engeström, 2016).

The research team collaboratively develop  meeting agendas for each session (e.g., meeting flows, meal plan, participant recruitment) to empower local educators to lead the systemic transformation process. At the beginning of each session, we started with a pipe smudging ceremony by lighting up traditional tobacco and sage. Jeremiah Lefebvre, American Indian education mentor, serv ed as the project liaison led the opening pipe ceremony as a starting ritual to fill the meeting space with spiritual positivity and dispel negative energy such as mistrus t, conflicts, confrontations.

ILL utilized multiple mediating tools (school discipline data, existing discipline plan, school system map), participation structure (small group or whole group discussions) to facilitate a collective critical examination. Disaggregated outcome data was used as a reflective stimulation in the next meeting to help members critically interrogate school’s outcome data. M embers identified multiple systemic breakdowns in the existing system that contribute to (re) production of the racialization of school discipline. They found that deep mistrust stemming from historical trauma of racism, cultural and linguistic erasure, white supremacy aggravates relationships between school and the  tribal community. Deficit ideology, manifested in low expectation and delegitimizing tribal ways of learning and behaving, functioned ideological tool that mediates everyday institutional, interactional, and pedagogical practices of the school staff. On M arch 13, 2020, the state of Wisconsin declared a public health emergency amid the  COVID 19 pandemic. The school was closed in late March 2020. ILL continued its work through online meetings (e-ILL). members worked in small groups and generated lists of innovations and ideas for improvements to collectively design a culturally responsive school-wide behavioral support system. During the reexamination process, the research team suggested utilizing space s (e.g., hallways, tribal community) as epistemological axes to identify new solutions. Through critical examination, members enriched the culturally responsive behavioral support system. The United States was founded on settler colonialism and slavery. Native American youth has experienced a hostile schooling system that invalidates sovereignty and self-determination of the tribal nations (Tuck & Yang, 2014). As a collective systemic design process, ILL can be utilized to generate a praxis to address historically accumulated systemic contradictions through a strategic university-school-family-community partnership for building a socially just school systems for all.
