# Embodied Cognitive Pathways from Dynamic Geometry Software Access to Deductive Geometric Reasoning

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examined whether Dynamic Geometry Software (DGS) access influences deductive geometric reasoning through embodied cognitive pathways. Structural equation modeling of data from a randomized within-subjects experiment (n = 1,239 responses) revealed a modest positive total indirect effect of DGS access on deductive reasoning (β =.052, p =.006), mediated through task-relevant imagery formation and sensorimotor simulation. Both constructs showed strong associations with reasoning, consistent with embodied cognition predictions.

Mathematical proof is essential for generalization and abstraction (Lakatos, 2015), yet students struggle to produce valid deductive arguments (Dreyfus, 1999). Dynamic Geometry Software (DGS) enables visualization and direct manipulation of geometric diagrams, with meta-analyses reporting positive effects on mathematical outcomes (Chan & Leung, 2014; He et al., 2025). However, few studies model the cognitive mechanisms through which DGS may support deductive reasoning specifically; most rely on pen-and-paper outcomes or lack control conditions (Chan & Leung, 2014; Walkington et al., 2024). Embodied cognition theories posit that cognitive processes are rooted in bodily motor and perceptual systems (Barsalou, 2008). The Gesture as Simulated Action framework proposes that gestures arise from sensorimotor simulations activated during mental imagery (Hostetter & Alibali, 2008). Students who gesture during geometric reasoning produce more correct insights and valid proofs (Nathan et al., 2021; Walkington et al., 2022), suggesting that DGS environments supporting physical manipulation may activate embodied pathways that enhance reasoning.

We developed a structural equation model representing three latent constructs: task-relevant imagery formation (indicated by intuition and insight), sensorimotor simulation (indicated by dynamic and non-dynamic gestures), and deductive geometric reasoning (indicated by generalizable, logical, and operative reasoning). The model specifies that DGS access influences deductive reasoning indirectly through these embodied constructs (Figure 1). We ask: How does physical access to DGS impact deductive geometric r easoning? We hypothesize that DGS access positively influences deductive reasoning through increased imagery formation and sensorimotor simulation.

Figure 1 Hypothesized Relationship between Constructs Undergraduates (N = 158; mean age = 20.19; 92 female, 65 male, 4 non-binary) from a large Midwestern university participated in a randomized within-subjects experiment. Each participant proved or disproved eight geometry conjectures —half with access to GeoGebra 3D Calculat or simulations on a touch-enabled digital whiteboard, and half without. Conjecture order was counterbalanced via a Latin Square. Within the DGS access condition, students' interactions were further classified as dynamic interaction, non-dynamic interaction, or passive viewing based on observational coding; these categories entered the model as separate dummy-coded 2548 predictors to enable estimation of the overall DGS access effect, but their differential associations are beyond the scope of this report.

Each conjecture-level response (n = 1,239) was coded from video for five verbal reasoning characteristics and two gesture categories, all as binary indicators (0/1, not mutually exclusive). Verbal reasoning characteristics included Intuition (immediate correct judgments without extended deliberation), Insight  (explicit retrieval and correct application of mathematical properties), and three deductive reasoning attributes: Generalizable reasoning (addressing entire object classes rather than specific instances), Logical reasoning (coherent inferential chains), and Operative reasoning (goal-oriented, mathematically purposeful thinking) (Harel & Sowder, 2007). Gesture categories included Dynamic Gestures (hand movements showing object transformations across different states) and Non-Dynamic Gestures (representing static objects or properties). Interrater reliability ranged from κ =.84 to.96 across all categories. Baseline measures of spatial reasoning, spatial anxiety, geometry knowledge, and attitudes toward geometry served as covariates.

We estimated a structural equation model using diagonally weighted least squares with a probit link for binary indicators and robust standard errors (lavaan 0.6-19 in R). The total indirect effect of DGS access on deductive reasoning was computed as a sample-proportion-weighted combination of specific indirect effects across all mediating pathways.

The model demonstrated acceptable fit: scaled χ²(41) = 206.55, p <.001; CFI =.94; RMSEA =.052, 90% CI [.044,.059]; SRMR =.041. Standardized factor loadings ranged from.75 to 1.00. Both imagery formation (β =.57, p <.001) and sensorimotor simulation (β =.45, p <.001) showed strong positive associations with deductive geometric reasoning, with the model accounting for 58.3% of variance in the reasoning construct. The total indirect effect of DGS access on deductive reasoning was β  =.052 (p =.006), supporting a modest positive causal effect operating through embodied cognitive pathways. The positive total indirect effect of DGS access on deductive geometric reasoning (β =.052, p =.006) is consistent with prior meta-analytic evidence (Chan & Leung, 2014; He et al., 2025) and with embodied cognition theory (Barsalou, 2008; Hostetter & Alibali, 2008). This effect is modest, but notably, the treatment consisted of mere DGS access wi thout instructional scaffolding — a far more minimal intervention than is typical in the DGS literature. Both embodied constructs showed strong associations with reasoning (β =.57 and.45), confirming their relevance as explanatory mechanisms. However, the path from imagery formation to sensorimotor simulation was negligible (β = −.006, p =.917), suggesting these constructs operated as independent channels rather than the sequential cascade predicted by the GSA framework. This may reflect boundary conditions on the imagery-tosimulation link in technology-rich environments, where externally displayed geometric objects partially substitute for internal motor simula tions. Limitations include a single-university undergraduate sample, and the operationalization of sensorimotor simulation through gesture production — a measurement dependency the GSA framework's authors have themselves identified (Hostetter & Alibali, 2019).
