# Learning From Teachers' Concepts of Dialogic Pedagogy

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Advocates of dialogic pedagogy advance a certain set of ideas about productive classroom talk. Associated research processes usually begin with researchers' concepts of what dialogue should look like and then examine whether teachers understand and enact them. Following Vygotsky's ideas that concepts are formed primarily through social interaction and that learners are never tabula rasa, I investigate teachers' concepts of dialogic pedagogy as they emerged during a professional development workshop. I use thematic analysis to identify seven teachers' concepts, examining whether and how they were advanced during the workshop and investigate the broader contexts that shape them. My examination of teachers' concepts shows that (a) teachers have an eclectic and flexible approach toward dialogic pedagogy, pointing to conflicting pedagogical needs and pressures; (b) teachers' concepts are embedded within classroom affordances and constraints, pointing to their potential role in bridging between researchers' concepts and the realities and limitations of classroom practice.

Objectives A growing body of evidence points to the potential of dialogic pedagogy for improving thinking and learning in the classroom (e.g., Resnick et al., 2015). Advocat es of dialogic pedagogy promote ideas about product ive classroom talk (e.g. Alexander, 2020; Mercer et al., 2019) and try to persuade teachers to adopt and enact them. The following paper aims to examine teachers' understandings, assumptions and objectives – their own concepts – of dialogic pedagogy. This investigation builds on Vygotsky's (2012) and his follower's ideas regar ding concept formation (e.g., Wertsch, 1991). Accor ding to Vygotsky, concepts are formed primarily through int ermental processes, grounded in and mediated by soc ial interaction. Importantly, learners do not arrive tabula rasa  to such processes but have already accumulated experiences, developed ideas and objectives which bound their learning and their appropriation of new concepts. Investigating teachers' perspectives and understandings of dialogic pedagogy, as they emerged during professional development (PD) interaction, would benefit the field in several ways. First, such exploration may help recognize the disparities between researchers' and teachers' concepts. Second, teachers' concepts are embedded w ithin classroom and institutional contexts, giving insights into the challenges of disseminating and enacting researchers' concepts of dialogic pedagogy. Third, teachers' con cepts may shed light on shortcomings and blindspots  of researchers' conceptual and practical tools and point to directions and sites for future research.

## Theoretical perspectives

In recent decades numerous researchers have suggested various conceptual approaches to dialogic pedagogy (e.g., Lefstein & Snell, 2014; Kim & Wilkinson, 2019). In the PD processes investigated here, facilitators pr esented dialogic pedagogy mostly in general principles as teaching that affords opportunities for students to express their thoughts and ideas, to inquire, challenge, or build  upon others’ ideas and to reason about and critica lly weigh multiple arguments and solutions (e.g., Alexander, 2020; Resnick, et al., 2015). Which of these ideas resonated with the teachers? How did teachers relate them to their existing experiences and perspectives? Follow ing Vygotsky, Wertsch (1991) elucidates that a process of concept formation "results in a mastery of abstract forms of reasoning… units are abstracted from their communicative contexts and become objects of reflection" (p. 2930). Vygotsky indicates that a mastery of new conce pts requires addressing the differences, and someti mes conflicts, between learners existing concepts acqui red in their everyday experience and the correspond ing academic concepts. Following a Vygotskian framework  to teacher professional learning (e.g., Grossman e t al., 1999), in this paper, instead of beginning with the principles or concepts of what researchers believe productive dialogue should look like, I map teachers' concepts of dialogic pedagogy as they emerged during PD processes, and endeavour to understand them on their own terms. I discuss how teachers' concepts relate to the PD processes they emerged in, the contexts that influence them and what lessons can be learned to advance research and practice in the field.

## Methods

Research Contexts ICLS2022 Proceedings 1061 © ISLS This research was conducted within a yearlong PD workshop in Israel focusing on dialogic pedagogy in language arts lessons. The process included eight language a rts teachers, five instructional coaches and four r esearchers learning and working together to codesign and devel op dialogic instructional units. During the 2018-20 19 academic year, I video-recorded 20 biweekly meeting s (70 hours total), collected materials and resourc es, and summarized events in reflective memos. The PD proce ss included iterative cycles of codesigning, enacti ng, inquiring, reflecting, and refining dialogic instru ctional units in the language arts (Barak & Lefstei n, 2021). Facilitators led the group meetings in line with th ese different stages, usually not allocating time f or directly advancing or deliberating concepts of dialogic peda gogy. However, beyond presenting dialogic pedagogy in general terms as mentioned in the theory section above, during the sessions the facilitators explicitly highlighted three central ideas: (a) that productive discussions involve prompting students’ thoughts, ideas and reasoning; (b) that teachers should connect between different line s of inquiry that emerge during discussions and (c)  that assuming critical stances is a central facet of dialogic teaching.

## Data Analysis

To identify and map teachers' concepts I investigat ed the workshop data thematically. This analytic pr ocess included the following stages: a. Reviewing the whole data set. This review included moving slowly through the video-recordings of the meetings, identifying interesting episodes and inte ractions (Rampton, 2006) relating to conceptualizations of dialogic pedagogy. b. Identifying preliminary codes (Braun & Clarke, 2012) for teacher utterances that relate to classroom talk, noting different purposes and functions that teachers attribute to dialogue. c. Reviewing the data corpus again, in search of recur ring themes that shed light on the teachers’ views, ideas and understandings. This process included identifying episodes in which different ideas and views about classroom dialogue were directly discussed by  teachers during the PD sessions (a total of 26 episodes of about 15-30 minutes each were identified). d. Assembling the emerging themes into seven categories that reflect distinct concepts of dialogic pedagogy. After mapping the concepts, I reviewed the data set  to examine pedagogical assumptions and tensions and how the teachers' concepts relate to ideas that  the facilitators advanced. I integrated into my an alysis also written material produced in the workshop context, against the backdrop of the broader ethnographic data, such as material from the curriculum and other national education resources.

## Findings

By following the four-stage process described above, I identified seven teacher concepts expressed during the PD program.

1. Getting students to talk. The core of this concept is that getting student to talk is important feature

in of itself, irrespective of the content or the quality of dis course. While regularly stressing the importance of increased student participation, teac hers acknowledged that opening the floor for students is not without its challenges, such as bal ancing student talk with other pedagogical needs (e.g., lesson structure, teacher authority or educational purpose).

2. Encouraging students to talk among themselves.  This concept highlighted the importance of allowing

students to talk autonomously, without teacher guid ance. The underlying assumption of this stance is that teachers should consciously relinquish the flo or and reduce control over topics, structure and purposes of the discussion.

3. Developing speaking skills. This objective relates to the first concept – getti ng students talking – but

highlighted a specific focus and justification: getting students to talk to hone their oral language s kills. On many occasions, teachers related these skills to  formulations outlined in official guidelines for classroom discussions (e.g., Ministry of Education, undated).

4. Cultivating polite discussion norms. Teachers claimed that classroom discussions involve establishing

a respectful classroom environment. An underlying a ssumption of this perspective is that teachers and schools are responsible for imparting prosocial behaviors, and indeed teachers agreed that maintaining polite norms is a basic, unambiguous requirement of classroom interaction.

5. Students arguing their points of view and offering reasons. Teachers assumed that meaningful

classroom discussions involve students expressing i deas, arguments and reasoning together. They stressed that learning to lead such discussions, and being granted the legitimacy to do so, was a significant aspect of the PD program.

6. Creating interpersonal encounters. Some teachers related to classroom discussion as an  opportunity

for encounters on a personal, emotional level. Whil e teachers expressed the notion that such ICLS2022 Proceedings 1062 © ISLS communication cultivates inclusive classroom environment, they also raised pedagogical tensions it brings about, pointing to difficulties in maintaining focused classroom activity and meeting institutional requirements.

7. Assuming critical stances. This concept underlines the notion that assuming critical stances toward texts

promotes productive discussions and enhances studen ts' engagement. Interestingly, teachers related to this idea mostly by invoking their own experiences as learners in the PD workshop, especially following sessions that included interpreting, criticizing and questioning texts as preparation for classroom dialogue. Note that even though some of these concepts interrelate, they point to different underlying purposes and functions of classroom talk and are thus mutually e xclusive. Notably, some concepts were more prominent than oth ers in the numbers of times they were expressed, th e numbers of episodes they emerged in and the number of teachers expressing them. For example, many different teachers expressed concept 5 (students arguing their points of view and offering reasons) as it was central to the PD program and was raised repeatedly by facilitator s. Concept 6 (creating interpersonal encounters) wa s expressed on very few occasions and only by two teachers. On the other hand, concept 1 (getting students to talk), emerged on several occasions but was expressed only by three teachers who repeatedly voiced this idea. Some of the concepts the teachers expressed, namely  concepts 5-7, resonate quite closely with ideas facilitators tried to promote and the research literature. However, at times the teachers ignored some of the notions the facilitators underlined. For example, the facil itators recommended that when students argue their points of view and offer reasons (concept 5) the teacher's role is to bring together the different lines of inqu iry – and that aiming for such coherence is fundamental to productive classroom dialogue. The teachers hardly echoed the latter notion during the sessions. This may be explained by the fact that teachers need to simultaneously take on multiple roles as managers of classroom discussions, as evid ent in the multiple concepts and tensions they expr essed. Adding yet another role of connecting between students’ ideas may seem an excessive load. Concepts 1-4 echo also requirements, pressures and educational discourses that relate to classroom talk. For example, the goal of increasing student talk during lessons (concepts 1) resonates with recent reform efforts in Israel to promote a more constructivist pedagogy (Ministry of Education, 2014). The facilitators did not advance this concept in of itself, but acknowledged it as a  basic, implicit condition for leading productive c lassroom discussions. On the other hand, the facilitators en couraged the teachers to avoid concentrating on stu dents' formulations as means for developing their speaking skills (concept 3), instead to try and recognize their thinking and arguments, even when they seem ill-structured o r confused. The teachers associated this concept wi th institutional expectations to appropriate students into academic language and formulations that reflec t adequate disciplinary vocabulary and ways of thinking.

## Scholarly significance

The premise of this study, following Vygotskian ins ights into processes of concept formation, is that teachers have important pre-existing concepts with regards to the pedagogical agenda they encounter; that it is important to study and understand (rather than evaluate) teachers' concepts if we wish to introduce them tonew ones; and that a crucial site to investigate teachers' concep ts, and the negotiations about them, is social inte raction (for example, during PD processes). Three central insights can be derived from the above investigation into teachers' ICLS2022 Proceedings 1063 © ISLS concepts of dialogic pedagogy, their emergence duri ng the PD workshop and the broader contexts influen cing them.

First, thinking about dialogue through the concrete  classroom lens allows (and indeed in many cases, demands) teachers an eclectic approach. The multiple concepts teachers expressed, and the pedagogical tensions they raised about them, indicate their acute awareness of classroom intricacies. Their approach indicates, in-effect, that leading classroom discussions demands of teachers to take on multiple roles, stances and to lead diverse, at times contradictory, practices. To better understand teachers' possible adoption of researchers' ideas  and recommendations, more studies should focus on the n itty gritty interactional realities within the clas sroom and promote dialogic pedagogy taking these realities and limitations into consideration. Second, teachers'  concepts are flexible. Many of the teachers readily espoused  different concepts during different sessions, refl ecting the contradictions and tensions inherent in school realities. Two central questions for investigation are, what are the conditions that promote particular concepts over ot her? And how can promoters of dialogic pedagogy enh ance teachers’ dialogic repertoires?

Third, teachers' concepts relate to broader educati onal discourses and agenda. In recent decades, classroom talk is receiving more attention from dec ision-makers who have begun to target it as a lever  of instructional change. Ideas and policies, translate d and filtered through PD processes and resources i nfluence teachers' understandings and perspectives regarding dialogic pedagogy. This paper points to diverse re sources, such as reforms (e.g., Ministry of Education, 2014), official guidelines for classroom discussion (e.g., Ministry of Education, undated) and other national programs, as resonating in teachers' concepts of dialogic pedagogy. The findings presented here calls for more trans-contextual studies in the field, to investigate how different discourses shape and bound classroom talk, and for promoters o f dialogic pedagogy to engage more closely with the se educational trends.
