# Examining High School Teachers’ Frames in Science Teaching

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Using interviews analyzed through comparative case study methods, the study examined themes in two high school science teachers thinking about the topics they’re teaching and synthesized their frames of the topics from these themes. Results revealed distinct frames: one anthropocentric, emphasizing human success, and the other ecocentric, centering planetary interconnectedness and success. The collective nature of these frames and the role of culture in their shaping for the teachers is discussed.

The present moment in the Anthropocene, a term used for the current geological epoch shaped heavily by human activities (Crutzen & Stoermer, 2021), comes with profound challenges for the planet and all its inhabitants in the light of human-caused changes in climate and its cascading impacts for various planetary processes. In the light of these challenges, there is a need for an analysis of social, institutional, and global power relations and inequalities that simultaneously drive climate change and shape our responses to it (Newell et al., 2021). Such spaces where the future global citizenry is engaging, or not engaging, in it. However, research on how teachers think about such power relations and create opportunities for their sensemaking by the students in science classrooms is sparse but critical from the perspective of shaping future global citizens. Responding to this gap, this study seeks to understand the frames of two high school science teachers in Southern California and asks the question-how do two science teachers participating in the study frame the topics they teach? Work on teacher frames has so far has focused on epistemological frames (Alvidrez et al., 2024; Louca et al., 2004; Redish, 2004; Russ & Luna, 2013), contextual frames (Engle, 2006), and positional frames (Greeno, 2009, Hand et al. 2012). Such frames offer  valuable insights into how teachers organize students’ learning experiences in the classrooms and what they notice and attend to, including specific aspects of student thinking. However, the focus of this study is on ‘what’ the teachers are (un)intentionally teaching the students through the use of their resources as outlined by Schoenfeld (2011) along with ‘how’ they’re facilitating the learning. For example, Gresalfi et al. (200 9) illustrated how students were differently framed as authors or receivers of mathematical ideas in two different classrooms. This impacted ‘how’ students learned in these classes. However, this study argues that it ought to have also impacted ‘what’ the students learned to think about themselves as students and as individuals who do mathematics. And this ‘what’ has a bearing on the futures of the students as learners and how they frame their roles as mathematicians in interpreting their future experiences with mathematics.

## Conceptual framework

Frames are “schemata of interpretation” that allow individuals or groups to “locate, perceive, identify, and label” events and activities, thus rendering meaning, organizing experiences, and guiding actions (Goffman 1974, p. 21). The way a teacher frames a particular topic can be understood as a function of their resources, goals, and orientations which together make some pieces of information and knowledge salient, help them set goals, and make decisions consistent with these goals (Schoenfeld, 2015). Framing, therefore, is not only a mental activity but also a cultural-historical one because the teachers' resources, goals, and orientations are culturally and historically constituted through their experiences in society (Scheiner, 2021). Drawing from Busch’s (2016) study where they examined how teachers framed climate change when teaching climate science in the classroom, this study conceptualizes two steps in teachers’ framing in the classroom-first, the process of frame building by the  teachers using their past experiences and present sensemaking (arrow 1, fig. 1) and second, the process of using the frames built to guide their actions in the classroom (arrow 2, fig. 1). While the broader study that this paper draws from focused on both arrows, this paper reports findings on arrow 1 by synthesizing teacher interviews about the topics they taught into their frames. This study used a comparative case study methodology while exploring two high school science teachers’ framing of science topics using their interview data. Both research participants, Radha (grade 11, biology) and Sheela (grade 10, chemistry) taught at the same charter school in Southern California. Names used are pseudonyms. To examine teachers’ frames of their topics, three rounds of interviews were conducted with each teacher participant 1991 adding up to approximately 4 hours of interview time for each participant. The interviews inquired about the various connections teachers made between the topic they were teaching and other related ideas and concepts. It also examined teachers’ views about science and the applications of the science topics that they were teaching. The interviews were transcribed and analyzed using a priori coding schemes based on and emergent coding schemes and themes were generated for each teacher.

I applied a combination of a priori codes using Choi et al.’s (2011) character and values framework (parent codes-ecological worldview, socioscientific accountability, and social and moral compassion) and Dagher & Erduran’s (2016) NOS components (parent codes-knowledge and skills, social values, financial systems, political power structures). Excerpts for each of the codes for each teacher were then extracted and open coded (example codes-capitalism, human benefits,..) and patterns were identified in each teacher’s interview data to generate themes. The themes identified for each teacher were then juxtaposed and synthesized into teacher frames using constant comparison method (Glaser & Strauss, 2017) and analytic memoing. Synthesis of frames Such simultaneous reading of excerpts and analytic memoing of the interview data revealed that Radha adopted an “anthropocentric success” or a human-centered frame of genetics and biotechnology while Sheela adopted an “ecocentric success” or an environment-centered frame of climate change as a topic. Radha’s frame of anthropocentric success through science was evident in how she positioned the environment mainly in its relationship to humans, imagined possibilities of environmental modification through gene editing in ways that will be beneficial for humans, and critiqued mainly the role of capitalism in preventing access to all humans to the benefits of science.

Sheela’s frame of teaching climate change was also aimed at success, but instead at ecocentric success through science. She centered the interconnectedness in the environment and humans as being only one node in these connections. She emphasized the role of humans in disrupting some of these ecological interconnections through science and the need to be solution-oriented in preventing these disruptions and, perhaps, even repairing some of these disruptions through the science of climate change. In that, her focus was really on ecological success which will also contribute to human success.

These frames have a substantial power to shape teachers’ practice in their classrooms and to foreground particular stakeholders in decision-making about socioscientific issues while backgrounding others. Wood et al. (2013) use the notion of cultural ‘software’ proposed by Balkin (1998) in the context of science and science education and discuss shared cultural heuristics as ways of understanding and engaging with the world that are transmitted through social learning or communication. They posit that as this cultural software is transmitted from one individual to another, individuals understand and modify the software in unique ways but also achieve a similarity in the software through ongoing interaction involving the use of the software and adaptations based on the use.

Thinking with this idea, the frames employed by Radha and Sheela are a part of the cultural software of the group(s) they belong to. Radha’s undergraduate academic background is biology. As such, her anthropocentric success frame of teaching genetics and b iotechnology can be construed as cultural software transmitted through her experiences in training, first as a biologist and then as a biology teacher. Through such framing, Radha foregrounds human concerns while backgrounding, not necessarily ignoring or denying, ecological concerns. Sheela on the other hand came from an undergraduate academic background in environmental science and forestry. Therefore, there is a reflection of more-than-human priorities in her ecocentric success frame of climate change which are likely a feature of the cultural software of the spaces that she was a part of in her training as an environmental scientist.

Thus, if we construe these frames as cultural software that was gradually transmitted to Radha and Sheela through their participation in specific activity systems, then it helps us to shift the focus away from these teachers as individuals and shift attention to the activity systems that help individuals become science teachers. Such a collective reorientation towards the processes through which certain frames are made available to students in the classroom through their teachers also reframes the pursuit of moving anthropocentric frames in the science classroom towards more expansive frames as a collective endeavour, rather than an individual one to be pursued by the teacher.

Additionally, the limitation of anthropocentrism has also been highlighted in relation to NGSS (NGSS Lead States, 2013). Hufnagel et al. (2018) employed critical discourse analysis to examine how the environment is positioned in the NGSS and the found that it constructed environment as an entity separate from people through exclusion and ambiguity, ascribed agency more often to actions or activities (manufacturing) rather than people (individuals or people who choose to engage in such manufacturing), and when proposing environmental solutions it focused on technoscience while sidelining the social and political dimensions of the problem. (2012), and Bang & Marin (2015) have demonstrated how the frames of Western science as reflected in documents like NGSS are in tension with the conceptions of human-nature relations and their foregrounding of human concerns compared to the unsettling of anthropocentrism in indigenous ways of knowing.
