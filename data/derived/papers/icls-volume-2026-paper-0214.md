# The Biology of Belonging: Mapping Racialized Assemblages in High School Science through Actor-Network Theory

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study uses actor-network and assemblage theory to trace how race becomes entangled with science curriculum, materials, and classroom interactions. Drawing on ethnographic data from an AP biology classroom, we examine moments where race emerges through both sanitized curricular content (e.g., Asian earwax, Punnett squares) and disruptive student outbursts (e.g., “Sit down, brown person!”). These vignettes are mapped as heterogeneous networks of human, nonhuman, and discursive actors. Here, race is conceptualized as a shifting assemblage enacted through every day charged interactions. This posthumanist framing rethinks how race is produced and negotiated in STEM classrooms and points toward more just, relational pedagogies.

In U.S. high school biology classrooms, race is often rendered invisible within a discipline framed as “objective.” Yet students’ lived experiences surface in ways that assemble race as both concept and identity. This study examines how race and racialization emerge in classroom moments where topics like genetics, human variation, and bodily features take on sociocultural meaning. In these moments, classrooms function as borderlands where identity, belonging, and disciplinary knowledge are negotiated. Drawi ng on ethnographic vignettes (e.g., “Asian ear wax,” “There are only you and I who have blue eyes!,” “Sit down, brown person!”), this work shows how scientific and racial logics are co-assembled in dynamic, situated ways. Using actor-network theory and Deleuzian assemblage, the study reimagines classrooms as sociomaterial entanglements rather than neutral sites of knowledge transmission.

## Theoretical framework

This study is grounded in Actor-Network Theory (ANT) (Latour, 2005) and Deleuze & Guattari’s theory of assemblage (1987), which together support a posthumanist framing of race as emergent, relational, and enacted. ANT invites a constellation  of associations across human and nonhuman actors: students, teachers, Punnett squares, ancestry testing charts, classroom tools, cultural discourses, and emotions. A ssemblage theory enables an analysis of how these actors territorialize and deterritorialize racial meaning in the moment. Departing from essentialist or reductionist accounts of race as biological or demographic, this work treats racialization as a dynamic and material-discursive process. Moments like the invocation of “Asian ear wax” or referencing “brown parents” in radiation discussions are not peripheral but central to how identity and content interweave. Drawing on MacLure’s (2013) concept of “glowing data,” the study centers affectively charged vignettes to examine the micro-politics of racial discourse within science education.

## Context and methodology

This study draws on a year-long ethnography in an Advanced Placement (AP) biology classroom at a racially diverse urban high school in the southeastern United States. Students (e.g., East and Southeast Asian, South Asian, Latinx, African American, White, and multiracial) navigated complex, often multilingual identities. Data included fieldnotes, classroom audio, informal conversations, student artifacts, and analytic memos. T he classroom functioned as a charged site of racialized meaning-making. Guided by post-qualitative inquiry, this study attends to rupture, emergence, and what “glows” in data (MacLure, 2013), tracing moments where scientific discourse ignites racialized affect, tension, or humor. These moments are analyzed not as representations of belief or bias but as assemblages  or entanglements of human and nonhuman actors (e.g., genetics diagrams, language norms, cultural memories). Actor-network theory and Deleuzian assemblage frame agency as distributed and relational. Seven vignettes were selected for their affective and sociocultural complexity (e.g., “Asian ear wax,” “Sit down, brown person!”) and they form a rhizomatic map of race-in-action, showing how race is assembled, disrupted, and reconfigured through the everyday materialities of science education. Sanitized way of discussing race is to embed the discussion as part of the curriculum 1552 These vignettes show how race surfaces in science classrooms through biology and genetics content, not formal equity curricula. They reflect sanitized racial discourse, where race becomes mentionable through “objective” science. Each is a glowing interaction (MacLure, 2013), where sociomaterial relations briefly reconfigure race, science, and belonging (Figure 1).

Well, that’s a plus While teaching about genetic inheritance, Barbara shared a personal story about undergoing IVF and showed a photo of her children’s embryos. She remarked that although her kids don’t look like her, “at least they’re white.” Zola, a Black student, quipped, “Well, that’s a plus!” Her comment was followed by a stunned silence. Zola later explained she meant to lighten the mood, but her humor collided with an unspoken racial boundary. This moment represents a rupture in the actor-network, where the acceptable curriculum (reproduction, heredity, IVF) became disrupted by a student’s reframing of whiteness as privilege. The materials involved, such as the petri dish image, the embodied children, the race-as-resonance, co-assembled into a volatile network. This interaction exemplifies how scientific discourse invites race only when depersonalized and emotionally distanced; once race is personalized or values-laden, discomfort renders it unspeakable. Asian earwax Barbara, during a unit on gene expression, stated that “Asians tend to have dry, crumbly earwax, while others have sticky, yellow earwax.” Presented as scientific fact, the statement elicited no verbal response. The class continued, unperturbed. This moment exemplifies how race enters science class through biological determinism: a non-threatening, fact-based route that flattens complexity. Framed as neutral and empirical, the earwax example effectively functions as a curricular proxy for discussing race, allowing students and teachers to acknowledge difference without tension. Yet, the actor-network, comprising Mendelian traits, scientific authority, and classroom affect, doesn’t just reflect race, it produces race as legible, knowable, and bodily. The silence in the room is itself an actant, reifying the naturalization of racialized biology. Let’s both talk about x-rays, physics, and having brown parents A discussion about radiation led to Ravi joking that his parents wouldn’t let him stand near microwaves. Priya echoed him, referencing “brown parent rules.” Their playful exchange triggered laughter and a short thread of cultural storytelling among South Asian students. This vignette demonstrates how students appropriate curricular content, in this case, radiation biology, as a platform for cultural self-recognition. Race emerges not through direct instruction, but through affectively shared logics of parenting, protection, and body management. The actor-network includes students’ family rules, physics content, and cultural scripts about skin color and health. These fleeting moments allow brownness to be acknowledged, not as phenotype, but as shared epistemology. There are only you and I who have blue eyes!

In a casual moment during lab cleanup, Lauren noted aloud that only she and the teacher had blue eyes, unlike everyone else with “black hair and black eyes.” A classmate later added, “We all know the right difference is White!”— a joke met with laughter and silence. Lauren’s comment positioned whiteness as both visual difference and baseline norm, rendering race visible without naming it explicitly. The joke that followed, while flippant, crystallized what Deleuze and Guattari might call a territorialization of whiteness: a moment where the racialized body becomes mapped onto visible traits. Scientific tools like eye color inheritance diagrams and phenotype charts enable this discourse while cloaking it in curricular legitimacy. The silence and laughter function as additional actants in stabilizing whiteness as the “default” in the biology classroom. Let me tell you who you are going to be In a performative burst during lab cleanup, Ravi assigned social roles to his peers based on race and gender: “You’re the mom,” “You’re the kinky aunt,” “You’re the genius cousin.” He named himself “the brown one.” Though presented as a joke, Ravi’s commentary was a live act of racial scripting, producing social identities through peer surveillance. This impromptu role assignment, happening amid informal chatter and shared labor, was not policed by the teacher and went uncontested by peers. Yet the affective texture of the room shifted. Here, race is not referenced: it is enacted. The actor-network includes Ravi’s embodied self-awareness, the cultural scripts available to him, the classroom silence, the physical space of lab cleanup, and the perceived absence of consequence. These elements together materialize race as  both category and performance, revealing the porous boundary between science learning and racial identity-making. Conversations surrounding race take lines of flight that appear unexpected, demanding quick attention of the observer In contrast to “sanitized” moments, these interactions show how race erupts unpredictably, disrupting classroom discourse. Emerging through peer dynamics, digital media, and affect (not curricular content), race is destabilized through humor, confrontation, and circulation. These are not conventionally “teachable” moments but sociomaterial events shaped by power and precarity, best understood as lines of flight (Deleuze & Guattari, 1987) that briefly expose underlying structures of race and belonging (Figure 2). Examples of unexpected outbursts A seemingly innocuous exchange about grocery store brands, such as “You’re Walmart, I’m Sam’s Club,” quickly pivoted when Ali declared, “Black Muslim women have it the worst… White supremacy!” Another student responded, “Well then… patriarchy!” This unscripted exchange unsettled the classroom’s social terrain. Though framed as humor or satire, the statements carried the force of lived reality. These moments are affectively loaded and ontologically multiple: they are both jokes and truth-claims, disruptions and enactments of care. The network here included peer dynamics, the spatial arrangement of seats, shared language around systems of oppression, and the social capital of being allowed to say such things out loud. Rather than existing in isolation, these outbursts map relationally onto previous and future classroom interactions, forming an emergent assemblage of youth critique, resistance, and coded solidarity.

Sit down, brown person!

During a moment of technical troubleshooting, Barbara asked for help with the classroom sound system. Ravi and Thomas both got up to assist, but Dinh called out to Ravi: “Sit down, brown person. It’s always the White people that get to help.” Laughter followed. Ravi sat down. Though joking, this moment enacted a material-discursive critique of who gets to be positioned as competent in science spaces. Dinh’s racialized command simultaneously referenced racial hierarchy and challenged it, exposing an embedded actor-network where access to technological knowledge, and the teacher’s implicit trust, may align with whiteness. The phrase “Sit down, brown person” is not just speech; it activates a set of affective and epistemic hierarchies, where roles of knower and helper are unevenly distributed and instantly recognized by peers. The whiteboard, the br oken speaker, and teacher’s gaze were all silent actants stabilizing this hierarchy, even as the utterance momentarily unsettled it. It’s just a video or a word on an iphone In a particularly charged episode, Ali and Bill laughed while watching racially provocative YouTube videos, repeating slurs, and searching for offensive content on their phones. They joked loudly about race, gender, and religion: “I am a big Brown boy,” “Let’s blame China!” “You know who really takes the jobs, Mexicans.” Some students appeared visibly uncomfortable. Barbara, the teacher, said nothing. This was not a singular event, but part of a patterned enactment where digital tools, student status, and silence converged to create a permissive zone of ambient racial harassment. These interactions were not curriculum-related, yet they were not separate from learning, either. Instead, they shaped who felt safe, heard, or hyper-visible in the classroom. In this actor-network, the iPhone, the video content, the uns poken rules of peer humor, and teacher inaction were all powerful actants 1554 shaping racial discourse. These events demonstrate that race is not always taught, but it is always present, circulating, modulating, and pressing against the edges of classroom governance. This study reveals how race is not an external variable to science education but is actively assembled through classroom interactions, curricular artifacts, and affective atmospheres. Whether emerging through the sanitized language of genetic traits (e.g.,  “Asian earwax”) or in disruptive moments of humor and resistance (e.g., “Sit down, brown person!”), race is continually enacted through entangled relations among human actors, scientific tools, and sociocultural discourses. These findings challenge domina nt notions of the science classroom as a neutral space of knowledge transmission and instead reposition it as a borderland (Anzaldúa, 1987): a space where power, identity, and knowledge are contested through everyday material practices. Rather thandefining whiteness upfront, whiteness is  described in terms of how it functions or emerges within the network. In line with actor-network theory, whiteness is mapped as it materializes through relations: through who is seen as helpful, who remains silent, whose discomfort matters, and what forms of speech are permissible. Whiteness becomes visible not through direct articulation but through its effects, as a discursive-material force that circulates across curricular tools, classroom talk, and affective atmospheres. Rather than a stable identity category, it is enacted in fleeting moments of normativity, silence, and presumed authority (see vignettes). This framing resists essentialism and aligns with this work’s  commitment to viewing classrooms as dynamic assemblages of human and nonhuman actors, co-producing racial meaning through everyday practice. By leveraging actor-network theory and Deleuze’s concept of assemblage, this analysis departs from frameworks that treat race as a fixed attribute or solely representational discourse.  Instead, it demonstrates that race emerges through relational ecologies of affect, silence, humor, content, and institutional logics. For example, a Punnett square does not merely teach inheritance: it configures which bodies are legible, which narratives are welcomed, and which identities are silenced. These insights have implications for both research and practice. Methodologically, the use of post-qualitative inquiry invites learning scientists to attend to “glowing” moments (MacLure, 2013) that resist codification but illuminate how science learning is always already racialized. Pedagogically, the findings urge educators to move beyond representational inclusion of “diverse scientists” and toward disrupting the material-discursive assemblages that sustain whiteness as default. This may involve rethinking what counts as participation, which stories get heard, and how race is being enacted through silence as much as through speech. In short, science c lassrooms are not immune to the sociopolitical. They are micro-sites of racial meaning-making, complex, fragile, and often contradictory. Designing for equity thus requires not only reforming content but reconfiguring the relational architectures of the classroom such as what is visible, sayable, and shareable. This study contributes to learning sciences by demonstrating how race is not a backdrop to learning but a constitutive force within it.
