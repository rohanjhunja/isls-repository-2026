# Innovative Game-Based Fraction Curricula for All: Design, Development, and Feasibility of Dream2B

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Simulated learning environments have the potential to significantly enhance engagement and conceptual understanding for all students, including students with learning disabilities and difficulties. Dream2B, a web-based video game designed with the Universal Design for Learning framework to maximize access, advancement, and power, includes for fourth through sixth grade students in supplemental mathematics instructional settings. Considerations for Dream2B’s design and development are shared as well as planning for and results of feasibility testing in 4th grade classrooms.

Keywords:  mathematics, universal design, supplemen tal instruction, gam-based instruction, fractions, learning disabilities, equity Promoting diversity in the workforce is paramount for U.S. innovation in Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) and Information and Communicatio ns Technology (ICT) fields. Individuals with disabilities are underutilized members of the STEM and ICT workforce (e.g., National Science Foundatio n’s National Center for Science and Engineering Statistics, 2019), even though these individuals exhibit unique traits and skills that are particularly beneficial in these careers (Dreaver et al., 2020).  For example, Scott et al. (2019) noted these individuals often exhibit: 1) sustained  attention to detail, 2) systematic procedural know ledge and skills, and 3) the ability to conceptualize outcomes and solutions to complex problems. The underutilization leads to an inverse relationship between worker supply and workforce demand and perpetuates disparities, especially for underrepresented groups.

Students with learning disabilities (LD), in partic ular, are one of the largest subgroups of individua ls with disabilities and receive supplemental services to r emediate areas of difficulty, such as fractions and  rational numbers.  Yet, this group exhibits reduced engagement and attendance in mathematics and science courses when compared to their peers in tier 1, often due to boredom (Sparks, 2015).  The reduced engagement is likely an issue of access. Students with disabilities often require  access to different tools to learn or express thei r STEM knowledge (Author). They also benefit from problems that allow for novel solutions because of the unex pected prior knowledge they bring to the classroom (Author). Both instructional design elements position stud ents’ knowledge as important and valuable. Unfortunately, many learning environments afford students with disabilities a one-size-fits-all procedural instruction with little opportunity to engage in and express their uniq ue reasoning (Lambert & Tan, 2017).

In this paper, we report on the design, development, and initial testing plan of Dream2B, a game enhan ced supplemental mathematics curriculum. Developed using the Universal Design for Learning (UDL) framework, Dream2B is designed to maximize accessibility and engagement by providing fraction conceptual understanding challenges rooted in authentic STEM careers. Tutorials, action adaptive prompting, and authentic formative and summative assessments are included within the user interface. Game-based problem-solving and after-game play discussion activities promote connections and strategically link to academic curriculum standards. The research questions are: (a) What were student perceptions of  the Dream2B intervention?, (b) What were teachers’ perceptions of the educational value of the curriculum?, and (c) What was the impact of the Dream 2B intervention on student outcomes (i.e.,  mathematics engagement/ conceptual understanding/STEM Interest)? Below, we unpack Dream2B’s core components. Then, we discuss our ongoing usability and feasibility testing of Dream2B in three fourth grade classrooms.  Data collection is ongoing. Completed results will be shared in session. Access, advancement, and power: Dream2B Dream 2B is a narrative-based mathematics game wher e students play the role of “Bunny” who helps vario us STEM/ICT career specialists complete tasks by demon strating their conceptual understanding of fractions. The ICLS2022 Proceedings 1085 © ISLS game narrative describes how Bunny attends career d ay at school and is introduced to five different ca reers in STEM and ICT fields. Bunny returns home, goes to sl eep, and begins to dream about the different career s discussed that day. In the dream, Bunny is introduced to different career specialists who are each encountering an issue at work and are seeking Bunny’s help to fix i t. Therefore, Bunny’s role in the dream (or game) i s to solve these challenges. Each challenge requires Bunny to engage in problem tasks (see below) that can be com pleted by solving fraction problems.

There are six worlds in the game, with the first fi ve worlds corresponding to the different STEM/ICT c areers: wind technician, solar engineer, fire inspector, ph otogrammetrist, and programmer. These five careers were chosen based on projected career increases over the next 10 years by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. Each of the first five worlds corresponds to a specific career; world six is a combination of tasks from all careers. The use of game narratives have yielded mixed findings about their effectiveness on learning outcomes (e.g., Clark et al., 2016). However, introducing a narrative has also be en found to positively impact learning and motivati onal outcomes (Plass et al., 2015; Wouters et al., 2013). Therefore, we believe that providing a base narrative in Dream 2B will positively impact learning aligned with the mathematics curriculum we included (see below) and interest in STEM/ICT careers.

We take an innovative approach to the design and development of supplemental math interventions by integrating the following core components into Dream2B: (a) Gam e Tasks along a Fraction Learning Trajectory, (b) Universal Design for Learning, (c) Action Adaptive Self-Regulation Prompts, and (d) After Game Concept/Skill Connections.

Understanding dream2B’s usability and feasibility Our project is collecting and analyzing data relate d to the feasibility of Dream2B.  This work is supp orted by a concurrent mixed methods design with one phase of d ata collection and analysis. Qualitative data will be embedded into the quantitative design. Data and analyses from this year will be used to inform a final revision to the supplemental curriculum before pilot testing.

Recruitment and training. We recruited three fourth grade teachers and their students to implement the Dream2B curriculum. Teacher and student partici pant selection was purposive in that inclusive math classrooms were selected that include 5-10% of students with disabilities for the feasibility testing. Participating teachers attended four one-half day training sessio ns on implementation of the curriculum. Day 1 of tr aining opened with the purpose of the study, the logic model, and the target population. Teachers will become familiar with the design of the new curriculum, its features, and the overall learning trajectory that grounds the task sequence, prompts, and teacher talk moves. Over the second and third day of training, each task category and its written lessons acted as structural guides. The guides were designed to help teachers deepen their understanding of how students might think within the tasks and understand how the core components are used to bolster student learning. Teachers worked with each other to discer n possible nuances in students’ reasoning and promp ts that are embedded into the game and curriculum. On the 4 th and final day of training, teachers practiced us ing Dream2B through role playing. Teachers formed a sma ll group with their peers and the research team, ro tating between teaching roles and student roles, hypothetically engaging in the tasks, and implementing mocked student strategies. Teachers then engaged in discourse and used identified talk moves taught during the traini ng to facilitate a sample student conversation.

Data collection. We are collecting five kinds of data to gauge the f easibility of the curriculum to answer the research questions (student perceptions,  teacher perceptions, student outcomes).  Each area  is addressed below.

In terms of student and teacher perceptions, we wil l meet with our teachers in a 1.5 hour long semi-st ructured focus group to gather feedback on STEM career chara cters, guides, and motivational game components (i. e., resource management, narratives, character unlock). We will discuss how content is currently taught in classroom instruction, difficulties students may be experiencing with the content, and teacher familiarity with pedagogical moves used in the curriculum (e.g., worked examples, number strings; game prompts and task sequencing)  and the extent to which, if at all, these design components were being used in their classroom. Finally, we will meet with 4th grade students with and without LD to conduct semi-structured interviews to gauge students’ strengths (i.e., what students already know about ratio and proportion; what gestures they may already employ as they solve problems). We will also gain feedback on the preliminary list and design of STEM careers, game characters, and elements to align the game to preferences of elementary school students. ICLS2022 Proceedings 1086 © ISLS In terms of student learning outcomes, defined as engagement/conceptual understanding/STEM Interest, we will use several measures.  First, the Engagement in Science Learning Activities survey, written for use with 10 to 14year-old students immediately after an in-class or informal activity, will be used to measure student engagement with the curriculum. The survey measures a student’ s cognitive, behavioral, and affective engagement. Participants respond on a Likert-type scale ranging  from 1 (YES!) to 4 (NO!).  Both Cronbach’s α and the polychoric coefficients yielded acceptable reliability when using all eight scale items (0.80 and 0.85, respectively). In addition to the survey, click trails will be used to determine in-game engagement. We will use 60 second game intervals without activity as the basis for engagement based on Author’s prior research. Conceptual understanding will be measured before and after the intervention using the Test of Fraction Schemes (Wilkins et al., 2013) and problem solutions generated during the games. The Fraction Schemes test is a proximal measure of the effects of the intervention on stude nts’ fraction conceptions. It consists of 12 items;  4 items for each of the following fraction concepts: unit fract ion concept, partitive fraction concept, and splitt ing that underlies the recursive fraction and iterative frac tion concepts. Internal consistency reliability for  the test was reported as 0.70 for the unit fraction concept and splitting. Criterion-related validity with an associated clinical interview score was reported with coefficients of 0.52 (p < 0.01) for splitting and 0.58 (p < 0.01) for the unit fraction concept. Following Wilkins et al. 89, test scores will be supplemented by student inter views with an additional six items to assess the nature of students’ pre-post fraction concepts. Students’ STEM interests will be measured with the Upper Elementary School (4-5) and Middle High School (6-12) Student Attitudes Toward STEM (S-STEM) (Friday Institute for Educational Innovation, 2016). It contains 56 items across six constructs: math at titudes (8 items), science attitudes (9 items), engineering and technology attitudes (9 items), 21st century learning attitudes (11 items), interest in STEM career areas (12 items), and 7 “About You” items that measure short-term expectations for course success and exposure to STEM careers. Responses are supported by a five-point Likert scale, with response options ranging from “strongly disagree” (1) to “strongly agree” (5). Higher scores reflect the greater perceived value of participants. Cronbach’s α of the SSTEM ranged from 0.84 to 0.86 for the grade 4-5 subscales and 0.89 to 0.91 for the middle high school subscales. Feasibility data analysis. We will take various strides to ensure the credibil ity, transferability, and dependability of the collected data. The prolonged engagement with teachers and students, persistent observation of problem-solving strategies, self-regulation utterances, and discourse representations, language, and observable operations), triangulation (i.e., 3 coders, 3 sources of data, 3 methods to collect data-video, student work, field notes) and member checking with teachers and studen ts will address credibility of the data. Describing  not just the behavior of students in the intervention but al so the context supports transferability; we will al so report examples of our coding processes and rationale for what codes were clustered together to form the basi s of a theme to ensure dependability. We will also organize and analyze collected process and product data utilizing an array of tools. We will transcribe (verbatim) all video recordings that capture students’ self-regulatory utterances during problem solving (“Think”), their discourse (“Pair” and “Share”), and teachers’ employed talk mo ves (“Share”). We will also use Excel to organize students’ self-reported use of checklist prompts, student and teacher self-regulation survey data, and pre-and post-test (product data) scores on the paper and pencil tests. We will conduct 3 sets of data analysis: (1) assess  teacher and students’ perceptions of the curriculum,

(2) assess pre-mid-post changes in self-reported engagement, conceptual understanding, and self-reported STEM

and ICT interest, and (3) assess within-student fluctuations in time stamped behaviors over the learning sessions. To understand student and teacher perceptions of Dr eam2B, we will qualitatively analyze the focus grou p data. Specifically, we will transcribe and qualitatively code the data for  recurring themes. To investigate changes in students’ reported engagement, their conceptual understanding, and reported STEM and ICT interest from pretest, mid-study, to posttest between conditions, we will conduct a series of repeated measures ANOVAs with time as the within-subjects factor and teacher as the between-subjects factor. We will ex amine the main effect of time (pre vs. mid. vs. post), the main effect of condition (teacher), and the interaction between time and condition on engagement scores, conceptual understanding scores, and STEM/ICT interest scores. We will also run linear regressions (single and multiple) to determine if engagement, conceptual understanding, and STEM and ICT interest at pretest are predictive of these measures at mid-study or posttest, or if measures at midstudy are predictive of measures at post-test. As the study includes a nested design (students within teachers), we can investigate the between-and within-student or teacher variance in time spent engaging in game activities or game levels, and their association with our posttest measures. The trace data will generate a stacked dataset with multiple rows of data per participant, where one row can represent a game session or game level, as opposed to aggregating all this information into a single row (i.e., mean game session duration). In addition, we will use sequential pattern mining to examine if we candetect sequences of in-game actions that are common across participants, and then determine the frequencies of those sequential patterns per student and compare those frequencies using chi-square analysis  differential sequence mining. In addition to exami ning overall group differences between test times, we ca n examine processes of engaging in-game behaviors a cross class sessions or game levels.

Final interpretation.  Results of each analysis will then be merged to c ompare and interrelate the feasibility of implementing Dream2B in fourth grade  classrooms. Once the data are analyzed and organiz ed, results will be used to make necessary adjustments to the curriculum.  In session, we will report on t he analysis and curriculum revisions.
