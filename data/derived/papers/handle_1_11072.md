# “Pencils Down!” A Critical History of Timed Mathematics Exams

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Timed mathematics exams have been prevalent throughout the history of public schooling in the United States as measurements of achievement and accountability. However, investigations into the origins and use of timed mathematics exams are still missing from the history of school mathematics. We present an in-process historical literature review that seeks to uncover the origins of timed mathematics exams, analyze the ways their use has changed over time, and discuss their implications for educational equity.

## Introduction and relevant literature

Timed mathematics exams are a defining feature of m athematics schooling in the United States. There ar e a variety of timed mathematics exams that have evolve d over the history of public schooling. For example, oneminute timed arithmetic facts tests are wi dely used across the US in elementary mathematics s chooling as both formative and summative assessments. Additional forms of timed tests include high-stakes assessments, ranging from yearly standardized and promotional state exams to competitive national and global exams. Recently, studies have highlighted some of the pote ntial negative impacts of timed mathematics exams on student learning and performance. The stress ind uced by having a time limit has been documented to lead to math anxiety (Boaler, 2014) and to decrease working memory, thereby impacting performance (Foley et al., 2017; Kellogg, Hopko, & Ashcraft, 1999). Scholars have argued for a moratorium on timed math exams in schools, citing several limitations and questioning the unde rlying rationale for their use. Gernsbacher and colleagues

(2020) present empirical evidence that test-taking pace is not a valid reflection of student ac hievement, the

reliability of timed tests is inflate d, timed tests often exclude students with (dis)abil ities, and timed tests are inequitable to marginalized student s. They advocat e assessing mastery through nontest mechanisms, asynchronous tests,  or untimed power tests. Removing time limits from tests has been shown to i mprove the performance of marginalized students and add validity to test results (Gernsbacher et al., 2020). Given the ubiquity of timed mathematics exams  and what the literature indicates about their impac t on student learning, we seek to understand why their use is prevalent in mathematics schooling today and theorize their implications. To this end, our research focus es on two questions: 1) What is the origin of the t imed mathematics exam? 2) How has the timed mathematics exam evolved? We address these questions through a historical literature review project.

## Method

We searched Google Scholar and Scopus using the ter ms “timed mathematics test,” “timed mathematics tes ts,” “timed math test,” and “timed math tests.” To get a t the changes or continuity over time (both in rese arch and practice), we looked for examples in e ach proceeding decade from the earliest writing (1887). For this initial search, we sought to identify the earliest writings or research studies related to our topic. The selection criteria for eligible articles during this piloting phase were b road and included articles or reports related to th e development or use for research or educational practice of timed mathematics exams. Our initial search resulted in 461 items.

## Results: A historical account of timed mathematics exams

We analyzed and classified the literature into four broad categories, briefly outlined below. Psychology research—An overwhelming majority of the studies found in our search were psychological studies using timed math tests as a stressor, the connection between timed math tests and math anxiety, or studies surrounding testing accommodations tonegate the ti med factor of tests (e.g., Thomas, 2018; Boaler, 20 14; Lewandowski et al., 2007).

Competitive exams —Established prior to 1870, these were used as civil-service examinations. Preparation for these exams typically relied on rote memorization and led to the coining of the term “cramming” (Carr, 1887). Despite debate about their use, researchers around the turn of the century utilized timed comparative standard tests to examine ways to measure the efficacy of instructional methods through educational outcomes. Comparative exams and achievement standards —In 1902, J. M. Rice demonstrated a wide range of abilities among students in arithmetic, the extent of that range, and the overlap of ability in grades  by comparing student data from exams (Brown, 1925). Rice’s resea rch led him to believe that establishing  achievement standards and using scientifically prepared tests to measure student progress would result in improved arithmetic performance (Brown, 1925). His work became the foundation on which other educational researchers would build. Standardizing tests—The Courtis Standard Tests were the most widely use d in school s between 1900 and 1920. Courtis sought to improve instructional m ethods to reduce or eliminate  individual variation in scores amongst students (Courtis, 1914) by developing a series of standard tests and testing conditions in 1910 (for more detail, see Courtis, 1914). It must be noted that C ourtis intended these standardized timed tests to b e used as an assessment of the efficiency of schools, not student achievement or ability. Finding the time component to produce variable and inaccurate results, Courtis abandoned the timed element as his tests evolved. Standardized arithmetic tests developed for researc h purposes paved the way for similar tests to be developed in Algebra and Geometry  (Rogers, 1919). Specifically, the Rugg & Clark (1917) Algebra tests, we hypothesize, inadvertently created the shift from timed tests as  measures of school efficiency to measures of student achievement.

## Discussion and implications

It is clearly stated within the literature by all rese archers who designed early timed tests that their u se was for developing more efficient teaching methods, not to measure individual student achievement. Contrary to their progenitors’ vision and intentions, timed mathematics tests carry increasingly higher stakes in their continued use as measures of accountability and mathematical knowledge and mastery. Test-based accountability has been the longest enduring policy in the field of education  (Fuhrman and Elmore, 2004), despite warnings  that attaching incentives and sanctions to high-stakes tests can result in some students being intentionally excluded from testing (Hout and Elliott, 2011). In a top-down schooling system where policymakers connect accountability with timed tests, the weight of accountability for successful schooling outcomes has been progressively passed do wn to the students.

The use of these exams as formative and summative assessments has been under debate and speculation since the 1870s. There is evidence surrounding the inefficiency of such exams in reflecting mathematical knowledge and mastery, that they contribute to student math anxiety  and negatively affect mathematical performance (Boaler, 2014; Foley et al., 2017; Kellogg, Hopko, & Ashcraft, 1999). Furthermore, timed tests hold (dis)ability, racial, language, cultural, and gende r biases, weaponizing mathematics to perpetuate disparities that historically marginalized students face (Gernsbacher, et al., 2020). This annual meeting’s theme calls us to reflect on how learning can lead to healing, resilience, and community. Understanding the origins and use of timed tests in math classrooms is important because the current scholarship on the history of school mathematics omits this ubiquitous and potentially deleterious feature of math education. Are teachers willing to shift their prac tices away from timed math exams, and what are the potential positive consequences? These are questions researchers and practitioners should sit with. Time’s up!
