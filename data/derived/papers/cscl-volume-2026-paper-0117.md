# Yang Jiang, Stephen Elliott Collaborative High School Teacher Learning in Bichronous Online STEM Professional Development

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how a bichronous model supports collaborative teacher learning in a four-week online bioinformatics professional development. Through analysis of nine high school teachers’ 1,363 asynchronous forum posts and 220 minutes of synchronous sessions, we identified complementary pathways: asynchronous forums supported knowledge building through archived threads and peer resource sharing, while synchronous sessions facilitated real-time collective sensemaking and expert scaffolding. Findings offer design principles for online teacher professional development in emerging STEM domains.

Findings offer design principles for online teacher professional development in emerging STEM domains. Designing online teacher professional development (oTPD) to support both sustained reflection and real-time collaboration within time constraints is a fundamental challenge (Haug & Mork, 2021; Meyer et al., 2023; Mulaimović et al., 2024), particularly in emerging interdisciplinary STEM fields like bioinformatics (Yoon et al., 2022). Without standardized curricula, teachers need to acquire both bioinformatics content knowledge and pedagogical content knowledge which involve designing curricula using authenti c data sets, simulations, and computational tools to engage students in scientific knowledge building in multiple domains (Huang et al., 2022; Yoon et al., 2025). oTPD models that blend delayed reflection and real-time interaction with colleagues and experts within a sustained professional learning community (PLC) help optimize teachers’ use of time while meeting core principles for teacher learning for STEM integration (Jeong et al., 2019; Yoon et al., 2025). Existing online learning environments have demonstrated the potential of virtual space to transform teacher learning, yet the field lacks instructional models showing how to orchestrate multiple online modalities for meaningful oTPD experiences (Bragg et al., 2021; Jin et al., 2024). We conceptualize bichronous learning as the purposeful integration of asynchronous and synchronous modalities to sustain engagement in fully oTPD (Martin et al., 2023). Asynchronous spaces offer temporal flexibility, access to an accumulating archive, and opportunities for iterative reflection and peer-curated resources (Clark & Mayer, 2024; Hernandez et al., 2024; Moore et al., 2024). Synchronous sessions afford immediacy for real-time collective problem-solving, just-intime expert feedback and modeling, and community building that generates momentum and accountability (Clark & Mayer, 2024; Jin et al., 2024; Mulaimović et al., 2024). We ask, how does a bichronous model support collaborative learning as teachers develop expertise in emerging STEM domains? The bichronous model was instantiated on edX Edge in July 2020. The asynchronous layer used threaded forums with search functions and embedded multimedia. It allowed teachers to initiate conversations, revisit prior contributions, and build shared resource  collections. The synchronous layer employed integrated video sessions with screen sharing for collaborative problem-solving, facilitator-guided Q&A with subject-matter experts, and a live chat to parallel discussion. The design blended asynchronous module s with weekly live sessions totaling approximately 60 hours of learning.

Nine carefully selected high school (9th-12th grade) biology and environmental science teachers from Northeastern US participated (IES & NSF, 2013). Their teaching experience ranged from 2 to 14 years (m = 5 years). All teacher participants completed asynchronous assignments and joined weekly synchronous meetups alongside the research team, subject matter experts, and two teacher facilitators from the previous cohort. Two primary data sources included: (1) asynchronous discussion forum prompts (n = 52) with participant responses (n = 1,363), categorized as top-level posts, second-level comments, and third-level replies; and (2) synchronous session recordings (n = 5), including one orientation and four weekly Friday meetings. The synchronous sessions, totaling 220 minutes, included content discussions, announcements, check-ins, Q&A, and feedback.

We conducted a hybrid deductive-inductive thematic analysis (Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006) of asynchronous and synchronous data that integrated theory-driven constructs from teacher PD (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017) with inductive codes grounded in teachers’ interactions and practices. 671 synthesis, which sparked deeper problem-solving across modalities. This cyclical progression supported teachers in co-constructing bioinformatics content knowledge and pedagogical content knowledge within the virtual PLC. Phase 1 was asynchronous foundation building. Teachers used asynchronous forums for sustained, situated reflection for developing competence in teaching bioinformatics. The temporal flexibility allowed them to explore new concepts, contextualize challenges, and connect content to students’ lived experiences. For example, one teacher reflected on the local prevalence of asthma among their students, “I would estimate over 80% of my students had been diagnosed with asthma … looking into it deeper, I found this  article [saying] 1 in 4 kids in Philadelphia are diagnosed with asthma [due primarily to poor air quality].” With sufficient time given, they were able to construct content knowledge by researching and drawing connections between real-world data and classroom instruction. The forum became a dynamic archive for exploring unfamiliar STEM content and sharing teaching resources contextualized for their classroom use. Phase 2 was synchronous synthesis through expert scaffolding and peer collaboration. Facilitators strategically monitored asynchronous threads to identify emergent themes that required synchronous attention. Each synchronous session began with reflections on recent forum activity to strengthen social ties and shared purposes within the PLC. When teachers expressed instructional challenges asynchronously, facilitators addressed these in real-time with conceptual scaffolding. For instance, one teacher had expressed concerns about using problem-based learning (PBL) with students who lack a sufficient foundation of academic skills. In the synchronous discussion, a facilitator responded, “[PBL] is highly scaffolded … You’re scaffolding the groups, the questions …  you’re embedding this in a problem that they have a choice in solving.” This immediate reframing helped teachers perceive PBL as structured and adaptable and created a sense of co-presence and urgency that propelled the group toward actionable planning and implementation. Phase 3 was collaborative problem-solving across modalities. Following synchronous clarification, teachers returned to asynchronous forums with enhanced understanding. Topics raised tentatively in Phase 1 were revisited with greater clarity and confidence.  The remote learning solutions that were generated synchronously re-emerged in later asynchronous modules as teachers shared refined implementation plans, now incorporating specific strategies for data collection and student inquiry into PBL instruction. T he asynchronous modality continued to afford teachers the time and autonomy to explore, synthesize, and enact new noncanonical STEM knowledge with growing competence. This ongoing cycle of inquiry, peer feedback, and facilitation, now informed by synchronous insights, established a pattern where each modality's affordances reinforced one another. In sum, the bichronous design enabled continuous cycling between modalities. Asynchronous learning supported conceptual exploration, resource sharing, and idea generation while synchronous learning catalyzed clarification, problem-solving, and implementation. This strategic sequencing transformed tentative exploration into confident implementation planning, establishing the core pillars of high-quality PD: iterative sense-making, authentic problem-solving, and continuous PLC building. Deliberate coordination across time and modality fostered a cumulative learning ecology where sustained reflection and real-time collaboration reinforce one another (Huang et al., 2022; Meyer et al., 2023; Mulaimović et al., 2024; Yoon et al., 2025). Three design principles emerge from this model. First, integrate platforms for cognitive continuity (Bragg et al., 2021; Jeong et al., 2019). Hosting discussion forums, live sessions, resources, and artifacts within one learning environment reduces context switching, preserves interactional history, and turns movement between “any-time” and “real-time” into part of the learning flow rather than a friction point. Second, leverage continuity as a collaborative scaffold (Clark & Mayer, 2024; Hernandez et al., 2024; Moore et al., 2024). A searchable, preserved archive enabled teachers to resurface ideas across weeks, to justify design choices with prior discussion, and to onboard peers into ongoing threads. Continuity supported both individual sense-making and collective progress (Haug & Mork, 2021). Third, design for complementary, not redundant affordances (Jin et al., 2024; Martin et al., 2023). Cross-modal scaffolds such as prompts that seeded live agendas or synchronous inquiries that returned to the forum transformed separate channels into one coordinated learning ecology. Our study advances CSCL theory by shifting focus from comparing different modalities to specifying strategic coordination mechanisms. Future research should examine how the bichronous model scales in larger, more diverse teacher PLCs, how they impact on classroom enactment and student learning, and how AI might enhance these feedback loops. Platform decisions are pedagogical decisions: they shape when and where learning happens and sustain collaboration across time.
