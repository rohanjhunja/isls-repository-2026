# Teachers Bridging Technical and Reflective Stances in K–9 AI Education

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As AI tools enter K-9 classrooms, teachers face the challenge of supporting both technical understanding and critical reflection. This study examines how lower secondary teachers integrate technical and reflective stances of AI through participatory lesson design with simplified Large Language Model activities. Analysis of classroom enactments, lesson plans, and interviews shows that: First, technical and reflective stances can be bridged in discrete, well-structured activities. Second, teachers stress technical insights before critical reflection. Third, abstract reflection concepts can cause classroom detachment, and fourth, bridging technical and reflective stances requires more than explanation. Additionally, these findings lead us to suggest implications highlighting awareness of teachers’ assumptions, deliberate activity scaffolding, and pedagogical strategies to support teachers in balancing technical and reflective stances of AI literacy.

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly entering K-9 classrooms, reshaping curricular priorities and pedagogical possibilities (e.g., Lee et al., 2021; Wu, Wang, & Shih, 2025). This trend poses a dual challenge: fostering students’ technical understanding of AI systems while cultivating critical reflection on their ethical, cultural, and societal implications (e.g., Holmes et al., 2022). Teachers are central to this ambition  (e.g., Lin & Brummelen, 2021; Velander et al., 2024), yet AI literacy introduces a dual pedagogical demand, engaging students with technical dimensions such as datasets, probabilities, and algorithms, while simultaneously promoting reflective perspectives on fairness, bias, and societal impact (e.g., Touretzky et al., 2019; Long & Magerko, 2020). Although both dimensions are widely recognized, little empirical research has examined how teachers attempt to bridge them in practice (e.g., Lee et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023). This poster presents preliminary findings from a participatory design project investigating how lower secondary teac hers (grades 7-9) integrate technical and reflective stances of AI literacy in classroom teaching. Rather than evaluating student outcomes, we examine teachers’ planning, enactment, and post-lesson reflections to understand how bridging is attempted, where it becomes fragile, and what forms of scaffolding appear necessary. Our guiding research question is:  How can teachers bridge technical insights and critical reflection on AI in K-9 classroom practice? AI literacy often develops along two parallel trajectories. The technical stance emphasizes computational thinking, data literacy, and understanding algorithmic processes (e.g., Zhang & Nouri, 2019). Students engage with simplified models to explore how AI systems are trained and how outputs are generated. The reflective stance, in contrast, foregrounds fairness, transparency, bias, and broader societal implications (e.g., Veldhuis et al., 2025), positioning AI education within critical digital citizenship.  Despite the recognized value of both perspectives, they are frequently addressed separately  with few studies explicitly focusing on bridging these two stances in classroom practice (Lee et al., 2021; Bilstrup et al., 2022). This separation risks framing technical insight and reflection as sequential phases rather than  intertwined dimensions of learning. Drawing on Dewey’s

(1903) notion of experiential learning and Schön’s (1983) theory of reflective practice, we conceptualize bridging

as a pedagogical process in which technical experimentation and critical reflection are integrated within activity structures. In this framework, reflection emerges from engagement with systems, not merely as a follow-up to technical instruction.

## Research context

The findings stem from a professional development project involving eight lower secondary teachers, recruited from two criteria: Experience in K-9 technology education (2-10 years of experience), and teaching grades 7-9. The teachers participated in workshops focused on designing AI lessons about Large Language Models (LLMs). 2674 Teachers used a non-programming tool enabling students to experiment with simplified text-generation processes, exploring datasets, probabilities, and model behavior. They were also introduced to a structured reflection model to support analysis of embedde d values and societal implications.  For this poster, we focus on data from two teachers including lesson plans, classroom video recordings, and pre-and post-interviews. Analysis employed emergent and axial coding to identify patterns in teachers’ intentions, enacted practices, and reflections. Our analysis indicates that bridging technical and reflective stances in AI literacy is possible but fragile. Reflection was most effectively integrated when embedded in discrete, well-structured activities that allowed students to explore ethical implications alongside technical mechanisms. For instance, Teacher A used a fairytale as dataset to highlight narrative bias, stating: “Then we can reflect on how this data influences us-is it always the man who is the hero?” Teacher B designed a probabilistic s entence-generator task without female pronouns  to expose gender bias, prompting students: “Who is able to hate someone in this dataset?” In both cases, students engaged in reflection through direct interaction with LLM processes rather than abstract discussion, demonstrating that reflection can emerge through bounded technical engagement. Despite these successes, teachers had tendencies to prioritize technical understanding before engaging in reflection. Teacher A noted, “T he students focus isn’t necessarily on that  [reflection]. It was for them to understand how to interact with it [LLM] and see what kind of texts it generates.” Teacher B grappled the balance between technical and reflective stances in his planning: “ Working with some of the technical aspects on one hand, and working with reflection on the other points in slightly different directions…  so I find it a bit difficult to have a completely clear flow through it [the lesson]” Abstract reflective concepts such as fairness, transparency, and societal impact  remained largely unaddressed in  the classroom activities. D espite teachers  expressed intensions to include such concepts, they were often overshadowed by technical content. Teachers planned f or student-centered reflections through dialogues but ultimately reverted to demonstrating and explaining what could be reflected. Teacher B emphasized, “Putting reflection into the conversations around the tables requires that you [the teacher] have time to dwell in those discussions. ” Overall, these findings suggest that AI literacy emerges not from technical fluency or ethical awareness alone, but from carefully designed activities that integrate experimentation and reflection, scaffolding students’ critical engagement alongside technical exploration. These preliminary findings suggest several implications for supporting teachers in AI literacy. First, show how bounded, well-structured bridging activities are effective for integrating technical and reflective stances, allowing students to experience ethical implications alongside technical experimentation. Second, surface and discuss teachers’ default prioritization of technical mastery before reflection. Third, support the design of activities where abstract reflection concepts can be operationalized and progressed along a continuum. Fourth, underpin the importance of and enable designing activities with student-centered dialogue and exploration that supports authentic reflective practice rather than relying on teacher explanation. These findings underscore that AI literacy  emerges from the interplay between how AI systems work and why they matter. Supporting teachers in orchestrating this interplay represents both a central challenge and an opportunity for K-9 AI education.
