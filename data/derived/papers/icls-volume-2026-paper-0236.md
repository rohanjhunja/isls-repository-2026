# Examining AI as Design Partner in Creative Computing: Digital Dialogue to Embodied Expression

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study investigates how AI-supported creative computing fosters computational thinking (CT) and creativity among rural middle school students. Thirteen students (ages 12-14) participated in a six-session Creative Computing Workshop using ArtAlgo, a p5.js-based platform with Aiko, a conversational AI, alongside Makey Makey physical computing kits. Analyses revealed significant learning gains and three themes: (1) Exploring Color as a Computational Medium, progressing from syntax verification ("show me #b3c8ff") to aesthetic expression (generating color palettes); (2) Iterative Remixing and Creative Debugging, where students showed two distinct engagement patterns: extended AI dialogue versus minimal engagement; and (3) Translating Digital Art into Embodied Interaction, bridging computational abstractions with tangible experiences. Aiko functioned as a dialogic partner rather than tutor, maintaining student agency. Findings demonstrate how conversational AI can scaffold technical skills and creative expression when designed as an optional dialogue resource, suggesting possibilities for expanding computational participation in under-resourced contexts.

As artificial intelligence (AI) systems increasingly shape how ideas are generated and shared, from art recommendation engines to generative tools, learners must understand not only how these systems work but also how to think creatively and computationally with them. This capacity extends beyond technical literacy to include the ability to express, critique, and reimagine computational processes as creative media (Resnick, 2017; Kafai & Peppler, 2011). Yet many young learners, particularly those outside formal computer science education, have limited opportunities to explore how computational logic and artistic expression intertwine (Margolis, 2017). Aligned with efforts to expand computational participation in K –12 settings (Brennan & Resnick, 2012; Yadav & Cooper, 2017), this study investigates how AI-supported creative coding helps students foster computational thinking (CT) and creativity. Existing computing curricula often emphasize technical fluency such as syntax, algorithms, and problem-solving, while overlooking the imaginative and reflective dimensions of creative production. Constructionist environments (Papert, 1980), by contrast, position computation as a medium for personal expression, enabling learners to explore how logic, aesthetics, and emotion interact through making. The Creative Computing Workshop engaged students with ArtAlgo, a p5.js-based platform featuring Aiko, a conversational AI designed as a design partner. Aiko, Japanese for 'beloved child', embodies constructionist principles as a caring companion rather than an authoritative tutor. Aiko serves as a resource students can seek when needed, maintaining their creative agency while providing responsive scaffolding through dialogue about color, mood, and design choices. Through this partnership, students encountered computational structures (loops, variables, conditionals) as expressive tools for emotion, rhythm, and symmetry. Building on Guilford's (1967) creativity dimensions (fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration) and recent work in computational creativity (Israel-Fishelson & Hershkovitz, 2024), we conceptualize creativity as an emergent process visible in how learners generate, vary, and refine ideas through code. These practices unfold alongside CT dimensions, abstraction, iteration, and debugging, illustrating the synergy between creative ideation and computational reasoning. In this study, we ask two research questions: (1) To what extent does the creative computing workshop support students' learning of computational thinking and creativity? (2) What learning practices emerge as students engage in the AI-supported creative computing workshop? By examining pre-and post-tests, chat transcripts, and artifacts across six workshop sessions, this study provides empirical evidence for AI-mediated creative learning and implications for designing human-centered AI tools that nurture creativity, reflection, and agency.

## Theoretical framework

This study is grounded in constructionism, which holds that learners build knowledge most effectively by creating personally meaningful artifacts that can be shared and reflected upon (Papert, 1980). Constructionist environments emphasize learning through design, iteration, and public expression, foregrounding the social and affective 1662 dimensions of making. Resnick (2017) extends this through his creative learning framework, positioning creativity not as an innate trait but as a cultivated capacity that develops through cycles of imagining, creating, playing, sharing, and reflecting, which he calls the creative learning spiral. Creative computing operationalizes these principles by treating CT as an expressive practice rather than mere problem-solving. Through Resnick's Four P's —projects, passion, peers, and play —students engage with computational abstractions (loops, variables, conditionals) as media for artistic and personal expression (Brennan & Resnick, 2012). This aligns with Kafai and Peppler's (2011) vision of computation as a literacy for creative production, where coding becomes a means of reimagining and critiquing computational processes. Recognizing concerns that many AI educational applications risk reducing children's agency and undermining personal expression (Darvishi et al., 2024), we conceptualize Aiko not as a tutor delivering instruction but as a design partner that preserves students' diverse ways of thinking, playing, and expressing. This positioning is crucial for human-centered AI in education: by designing Aiko as a conversational AI that responds to student-initiated requests (e.g., "show me #f50094 (hot pink)" or "make a warm color palette") without dictating solutions, we maintain the constructionist commitment to learner agency while providing responsive scaffolding. To examine creativity within this framework, we adopt Guilford's (1967) dimensions: fluency (quantity of ideas), flexibility (diversity of approaches), originality (novelty of solutions), and elaboration (depth of refinement). Recent computational creativity frameworks (Israel-Fishelson & Hershkovitz, 2024) adapt these dimensions to analyze student code and artifacts, treating creativity as a visible, measurable process rather than an outcome. This lens reveals how students' iterative work in ArtAlgo demonstrates the synergy between creative ideation and computational reasoning.

## Participants and workshop

The Creative Computing Workshop was a six-session informal learning program designed to support middle school students' computational thinking (CT) and creativity through AI-supported coding and physical computing. Thirteen students (ages 12–14) from a rural southeastern middle school participated in spring 2025. Sessions were held after school in a laptop-equipped classroom with Makey Makey  kits available for physical computing integration. Students worked in ArtAlgo using Aiko to support their creative coding process through dialogue-based assistance with design goals and debugging. The workshop followed a constructionist, studio-style progression across six sessions: (1) Exploring Color in Digital Art, (2) Exploring Color Harmonies and AI-Generated Palettes, (3) Designing Interactive Pixel Mosaics, (4) Bringing Digital Art to Life with Physical Computing, (5) Refining Interactive Musical Mosaics, and (6) Project Refinement and Showcase. The culminating Interactive Mosaic Art projects (see Figure 1) combined custom p5.js visuals with Makey Makey (see Figure 2) touch inputs to trigger visual and musical responses.

Data sources and analysis Pre-and post-tests measured students’ CT learning and creativity using validated instruments adapted from prior studies (Huang et al., 2024). Ten students who completed both tests were included in the quantitative analysis; three were excluded due to missing pre-or post-responses. Responses were coded using a rubric aligned with CT practices (abstraction, iteration, decomposition, debugging) (Huang et al., 2024) and creativity dimensions (fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration) derived from Guilford (1967). Two researchers independently coded 20% of the responses, achieving substantial inter-rater reliability (Cohen’s κ = 0.79); discrepancies were resolved through discussion.  A paired-sample t-test was conducted to examine potential changes in CT and creativity from pre-to post-test. To triangulate these results, we conducted thematic analysis of chat transcripts, code iterations, and project artifacts from all 13 students following Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six-phase approach. Deductive and inductive coding captured both theory-driven and emergent patterns. Inductive codes included politeness markers in AI interactions  (e.g., “please” and “thank you”), exploratory typos  (repeated invalid HEX values), and conceptual bridging (connecting color to emotion). Through iterative categorization and theme refinement, we consolidated codes into themes that represented how students’ CT and creativity developed through dialogue, experimentation, and reflection.

Learning gains in creativity and computational thinking Our analysis revealed significant learning gains in both CT and creativity, with students developing these capabilities synergistically through AI-supported creative coding and physical computing activities. Pre-and posttests showed significant improvements in students' creativity and CT. A paired-sample t-test revealed statistically significant gains with a large eff, t (9) = 4.13, p =.003, with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.31). Mean scores increased from M = 15.7 (SD = 3.6) pre-to M = 26.9 (SD = 4.2) post-test, demonstrating measurable growth in students' creativity and CT in the context of creative computing. Learning practices and interactions with AI as creative partner We analyzed chat transcripts (n=13 students across 6 sessions), iterative code versions, and final Interactive Mosaic Art projects to identify three interconnected themes illustrating students' learning trajectories. While Themes 1 and 2 demonstrate Aiko's  role as a design partner in supporting CT and creativity, Theme 3 illustrates how the broader workshop design promoted embodied learning through the combination of digital coding with physical computing. These themes reveal how AI as a design partner and hands-on making create complementary pathways for creative computing.

Theme 1: Exploring color as a computational medium Students' interactions with color evolved from syntax-focused queries to aesthetically-driven explorations. Early sessions were dominated by verification requests, with 11 of 13 students initiating conversations with direct translation queries (e.g., "show me #b3c8ff," "what color is #207341"). The AI responded with both visual code examples and contextual explanations, scaffolding technical understanding alongside aesthetic awareness. By session 3, chat patterns shifted toward relational and intentional color work. Students like Jessica (all names are pseudonyms) progressed from asking "What is the RGB of this color?" to requesting "Generate a monochromatic palette based on #fdac92 with more than three colors," demonstrating both computational understanding (color space navigation) and creative intent (palette harmony). Similarly, Shania's repeated queries about warm versus cool colors ("is this a warm or cool color #9466a1") evolved into generative requests for complete palettes, indicating movement from classification to application. This progression revealed CT practices of pattern recognition (identifying color relationships) and abstraction (understanding color as numerical values), while creativity dimensions emerged through fluency (multiple color variations per session) and elaboration (refining palettes for specific moods).

Theme 2: Iterative remixing and creative debugging Students demonstrated two distinct orientations toward unexpected results. Chat logs revealed that approximately half the students (n=7) engaged in extended dialogue with Aiko (averaging 15-30+ exchanges), using sustained conversation to work through error s and discover alternative strategies. For instance, Kayci's design process in ArtAlgo exemplifies this dialogue-intensive approach. Her chat logs show that in early sessions, she attempted to create custom colors using invalid HEX values (#fr9375, #ur9371, #tt9375), which produced syntax errors. Rather than abandoning her color experimentation or working independently, she continued engaging Aiko in dialogue 1664 to understand why these codes failed and what constituted valid HEX format. Through this iterative conversation, she discovered that Aiko could generate validated color palettes on request. By session 6, her chat logs document a functional 6-key piano wher e each key was assigned an Aiko-generated pastel color, transforming her initial error-prone manual approach into a systematic method of requesting and implementing valid color schemes. Conversely, students with minimal chat engagement (n=6, averaging 3-5 exchanges) showed different learning patterns. Samantha's interaction with Aiko consisted of only 4 exchanges total. After receiving error correction about an invalid HEX value (#hh7865), she did not continue the dialogue to explore alternative strategies through conversation. The distinction lay not in AI response patterns, which remained consistent across all interactions, but in students' choices about whether and how extensively to use AI dialogue as a problem-solving resource. Students who engaged in sustained conversation with Aiko had more opportunities to iteratively discover alternative approaches, while those who engaged minimally may have pursued independent experimentation or r elied on other resources such as direct code trial-and-error, teacher support, or peer collaboration. Through iterative dialogue, high-engagement students demonstrated CT practices including abstraction (modular thinking about color systems), pattern recognition (identifying valid HEX format rules), and debugging (systematically testing and refining approaches). Their creativity manifested through elaboration (refining color relationships) and originality (personalizing aesthetic choices), with the conversational AI serving as a responsive partner in their self-directed design process. Theme 3: Translating Digital Art into Embodied Interaction In later sessions, students extended their computational designs into physical form using Makey Makey kits, connecting coded mosaics to touch and sound. These activities transformed computation from screen-based abstraction into an embodied, multisensory process that bridged digital and physical expression. An example of embodied computational learning occurred when sixth grader Olivia discovered her metal braces could complete a circuit. As documented in the showcase, she demonstrated how "anything metal can be part of a circuit," turning her orthodontic hardware into an unexpected interface component. This moment of "improvisational tinkering" exemplified the project's constructionist approach, where unplanned discoveries become pathways to deeper understanding. By incorporating her body into the circuit, Ol ivia demonstrated both creative flexibility and computational reasoning about conductivity and electrical flow. Other students created equally inventive physical-digital hybrids. Sixth grader Victoria, inspired by "the galaxy and stars," designed an interactive piece that produced "different songs" when touched, explaining "I could make images and different songs. That's what I like the most." Her project combined visual aesthetics (galaxy imagery), sound generation (musical sequences), and physical interaction (conductive touch points), demonstrating sophisticated abstraction as she mapped physical inputs to multiple coded outputs. under-resourced settings. Students showed significant pre-to post-test improvements, while thematic analysis revealed how AI interaction, iterative  making, and embodied exploration shaped learning. Aiko served as a dialogic co-designer rather than an instructional tutor, supporting experimentation through natural language dialogue without overtaking student agency. Unlike traditional AI tutoring syst ems that control learning sequences, Aiko's conversational interface allowed students to maintain creative agency while accessing support when needed. Analysis revealed two distinct engagement patterns: students who engaged in extended conversation (n=7) i teratively discovered alternative strategies, while those with minimal engagement (n=6) pursued independent experimentation or peer collaboration. The findings revealed that engagement differences stemmed from student agency in choosing when and how to use  AI dialogue rather than opportunity constraints. These results extend the creative learning spiral (Resnick, 2017) by demonstrating how conversational AI can function as a responsive partner within cycles of imagining, creating, and reflecting. Future wor k will examine through co-design how AI tools can adapt to diverse engagement preferences while upholding constructionist values of agency, creativity, and play. Understanding what predicts engagement choices and whether different patterns can lead to successful learning outcomes will be essential for ensuring human-AI dialogue enhances rather than constrains creative expression.
