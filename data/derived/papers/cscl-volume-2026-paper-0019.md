# Student, Hybrid, or AI?: Examining Authorial Roles and Network Structures in GenAI- Supported Collaborative Learning

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As generative AI (GenAI) reshapes collaborative learning, understanding how authorial roles interact within evolving network structures is crucial. This exploratory case study explored authorial roles and social network structures in a Knowledge Forum-based computational thinking course. We analyzed 527 discussion notes from 54 undergraduates using Social Network Analysis (SNA) and deep learning classification. Notes were categorized into four roles: pure student (Stu), direct GenAI outputs, Student-GenAI (SG) hybrids, and Student-GenAI-Student (SGS) iterations. Results showed student-only contributions dominated (73.8%), with GenAI-involved notes at 26.2%. Network analysis revealed Stu roles as primary information hubs with high centrality, while hybrid SG/SGS roles were less central than expected. Findings suggest that without explicit guidance, students remain core connectors and AI-facilitated knowledge brokerage is underutilized. Educational practice must intentionally scaffold hybrid roles to foster balanced, integrated knowledge flow in GenAI-supported collaborative learning.

In the evolving educational landscape, collaborative learning has emerged as a cornerstone for developing essential 21st-century skills, including computational thinking (CT) and problem-solving. Frameworks such as Knowledge Building (KB) emphasize the collective advancement of ideas within a community, where students engage in authentic discourse to refine and expand shared knowledge (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006). Platforms such as the Knowledge Forum (KF) facilitate this process by enabling students to post notes, discuss solutions, and iteratively build upon each other's contributions, creating a dynamic environment for idea exchange (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2014). However, traditional collaborative settings often face limitations in scalability and personalization, prompting the exploration of technological integration to enhance engagement and outcomes. The emergence of Generative AI (GenAI) tools like ChatGPT offers new opportunities for collaborative learning. GenAI acts as a virtual collaborator, generating ideas and providing feedback, thereby extending human capabilities in knowledge co-construction (Chan & Hu, 2023). Studies have shown that GenAI enhances creativity and problem-solving in group tasks by reducing cognitive load and fostering diverse perspectives (Wei et al., 2025). However, its integration raises questions about authorship and the ba lance between human and AI contributions, particularly in cases where students may not be able to distinguish their ideas from AI-generated content.

This study examined GenAI-supported collaborative learning within a KF-based course aimed at cultivating CT. As GenAI assumes increasingly diverse roles in educational contexts —evolving from a passive information provider to an active co-constructor of knowledge —understanding the dynamics of human-AI collaboration becomes crucial (Chan & Hu, 2023; Lee et al., 2023). In this learning environment, students collaborated on problem-solving tasks while incorporating GenAI interactions into their discussion notes. To capture the varying degrees of human-AI integration, pedagogical scaffolds were designed to differentiate four distinct authorial roles: pure student (Stu) contributions representing independent human thought, direct GenAI outputs showcasing AI-generated content, Student-GenAI (SG) hybrids reflecting initial human-AI integration, and refined Student-GenAI-Student (SGS) iterations demonstrating critical engagement with AI outputs.  Owing to the varying scaffold proficiency, deep learning methods were employed to classify discourse into these four roles, followed by Social Network Analysis (SNA) to uncover interaction patterns. To guide this investigation, the following research questions were posed: RQ1: What is the distribution of the four distinct authorial roles—Stu, GenAI, SG, and SGS —within the collaborative discourse in this AI-supported collaborative learning environment? 90 RQ2:  What typical interaction network structures emerge within student groups when considering the different authorial roles adopted by students?

RQ3:  What structural roles (e.g., centrality, brokerage) do author-role nodes with different authorial role configurations play within the collaborative network? Specifically, do nodes representing Student-GenAI-Student author role combinations occupy critical bridging positions in the KB network?

## Literature review

Collaborative learning and KB Collaborative learning theories highlight the importance of social interaction in acquiring knowledge, wherein learners co-construct understanding through dialogue and shared activities. KB, a framework by Scardamalia and Bereiter (2006), views education a s collective idea improvement, not mere transmission, emphasizing idea diversity, improvable ideas, and synthesis. In platforms like KF, this appears as threaded discussions where students post notes, reference peers' ideas, and refine solutions, fostering  computational thinking in problem-solving (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2014). Studies have shown that such environments enhance metacognitive skills and group efficacy, although challenges include ensuring equitable participation and distinguishing individual contributions (Grunspan et al., 2014).

The integration of AI into these settings extends traditional collaborative paradigms through hybrid human-AI discourse. In KB scenarios, AI serves as a scaffold for idea generation, aligning with the principles of authoritative sources (Lee et al., 2023).  This hybridization complicates authorial roles, as students may paste AI outputs into their notes, blurring the lines between original and generated content. Deep learning classification techniques parse such discourses by categorizing contributions based on linguistic patterns (Wang et al., 2023). This reveals that SG hybrids predominated in creative tasks, suggesting that AI's role is to amplify ideas while risking over-reliance.

Genai in educational environment GenAI has transformed educational practices by enabling personalized support in learning environments. Tools such as ChatGPT facilitate content creation, brainstorming, and feedback, thereby enhancing student engagement (Chan & Hu, 2023). In higher education, GenAI supports discourse by summarizing discussions, generating recommendations, and synthesizing knowledge, aligning with knowledge creation goals (Chen et al., 2023; Cress & Kimmerle, 2023). Experimental studies have shown that GenAI boosts collaborative problem-solving and creativity, as seen in digital storytelling, where tools like Midjourney and Runway aid visual and narrative development (Wei et al., 2025). However, perceptions vary; students appreciate efficiency but express concerns about accuracy, ethics, and diminished critical thinking (Chan & Hu, 2023). In KB contexts, GenAI acts as a co-constructor, prompting reflection and refinement, although its lack of understanding requires human oversight (Lee et al., 2023). Frameworks such as Know-Build-Critique guide ethical integration, emphasizing AI literacy (Chen et al., 2023). Authorial roles emerge as critical, with classifications of pure students versus AI-augmented input revealing patterns in which refined SGS contributions foster discourse (Cress & Kimmerle, 2023). Challenges include cognitive offloading, where reliance on AI weakens personal development, requiring scaffolding for active refinement. SNA in learning environments SNA provides a robust method for mapping interactions in educational networks, revealing structures such as centrality and brokerage that indicate influence and bridging roles (Grunspan et al., 2014). In AI-supported learning, SNA uncovers human-AI dynamic s by clustering participants based on interaction patterns and epistemic networks to assess community sense and performance (Wang et al., 2023). In GenAI contexts, SNA identifies emergent structures where hybrid roles, such as SGS, occupy bridging positions, facilitating knowledge flow across groups (Feng, 2025). Network methods analyze small-group interactions, providing evidence of how GenAI influences discourse patterns and equity (Feng, 2025). Gaps persist in applying SNA to classify authorial roles in KF, where deep learning aids in delineating nodes, potentially revealing whether refined hybrids enhance centrality and brokerage in computational thinking tasks. Despite these advancements, the existing literature lacks integrated analyses of authorial role distributions and network structures in GenAI-supported KB. This study aims to address this gap by classifying discourse via deep learning, applying SNA to explore patterns, and elucidating the structural significance of hybrid roles in collaborative networks. 91 This study employed an exploratory case study methodology (Yin, 1981) with quantitative social network analysis (SNA) and deep learning techniques for automated text classification to investigate AI-supported collaborative learning environments. The integration of generative AI into collaborative learning represents a phenom enon in which the boundaries between human and artificial agents are increasingly blurred. The case study design allowed for a contextual examination of human-AI interaction dynamics, while SNA provided an analytical framework to visualize collaborative networks. Deep learning-based text classification enables the systematic analysis of educational discourse by connecting qualitative understanding with quantitative modeling.

## Participants

The study involved 54 second-and third-year undergraduate students from a key public university in China, enrolled in the general education course "Computational Thinking and Problem-Solving." The participants represented diverse academic backgrounds, including software engineering, artificial intelligence, cultural industry management, and financial management, ensuring disciplinary heterogeneity within the cohort. Students were organized into 10 interdisciplinary groups of 5-6 members each to facilitate collaborative problem-solving activities.

The learning environment The study was conducted during the Spring 2025 semester over a 12-week period within a Problem-Based Learning (PBL) framework guided by knowledge-building principles. All collaborative activities occurred on the KF platform, augmented by two core pedagogical components: Pedagogical Support for GenAI Use  (see Figure1): Students were encouraged to integrate various external GenAI tools (ChatGPT, DeepSeek, Doubao) into their inquiry processes. Specialized scaffolds within the KF platform structured these interactions, including prompts such as "The explanation for this concept from ChatGPT is...", "I think ChatGPT's answer/explanation is still lacking...", and "Here is how I evaluate the accuracy and reliability of ChatGPT's proposal." Second, group interaction networks were constructed and analyzed to reveal their structural patterns and role functions.

Group-level network structure analysis 93 To address RQ2, we computed group-level structural metrics, including network density (reflecting the tightness of interactions) and degree centralization (capturing centralized exchange patterns), and used a visualization of the interaction distribution to identify decentralized, peer-driven, or AI-centered structures (Wasserman & Faust, 1994). Metrics were calculated and layouts rendered with Python’s NetworkX library, revealing emergent network patterns under different authorial role dominance.

Node-level centrality and brokerage role analysis With respect to RQ3, we computed individual-level centrality measures in the directed interaction network: degree centrality (with in-degree and out-degree assessing influence and proactiveness, respectively), betweenness centrality (identifying brokerage roles), and closene ss centrality (indicating the efficiency of information reach) (Chen & Poquet, 2022). We then compared the mean centrality scores across student-led authorial role types, with particular attention to whether the SGS role occupied key knowledge-broker positions. Statistical significance was tested using the Kruskal–Wallis test.

## Result and discussion

RQ1: Distributional characteristics of roles in collaborative discourse The distribution of the four authorial roles was markedly non-uniform (Figure 2 a). Student-authored notes dominated, accounting for 73.8% of all contributions, whereas GenAI-involved notes comprised 26.2%. Students thus engaged primarily in independent sense-making, with AI contributions present but far from dominant. This pattern is consistent with recent findings. Wei et al. (2025), for example, reported that groups using GenAI tools in a digital storytelling experiment achieved significant gains in collaborative problem-solving and team creativity, yet human contributions remained central; GenAI enhanced efficiency and ideation rather than replacing student input. Together, these results suggest that GenAI functions as a cognitive augmentation tool in col laborative settings, extending rather than supplanting human reasoning and thereby reducing the risk of cognitive offloading. 94 Between-group differences further highlighted this heterogeneity (Figures 2b–2c). For instance, the GenAI role was entirely absent in some groups (e.g., Group B) but reached 16.9% in others (e.g., Group F). SGS had the lowest prevalence across all groups (mean, 4.7%). This may be related to the scaffolds’ design, suggesting that students face cognitive challenges when attempting to integrate AI outputs more deeply. Survey evidence by Ruiz-Rojas et al. (2024) indicates that although 87% of university students are familiar with GenAI tools, only 64% believe these tools substantially enhance critical thinking —consistent with the low share of SGS and potentially attributable to unresolved issues of bias and ethics that complicate effective integration in collaboration. Similarly, Zhai (2024) argues for a shift in teacher roles from observers to innovators, underscoring the need for ongoing professional development in the GenAI era to help students overcome these challenges and promote equitable AI use across groups.

From the proportional trend plot (Figure 2 d), the share of Stu remained relatively stable across groups (≈ 0.70–0.80), whereas SG varied the most, peaking at ~0.21 in Group B and falling to ~0.08 in Group F. This trend reveals the dynamic nature of GenAI involvement, which is potentially shaped by within-group interaction patterns or task design. Prior work supports this view; for instance, in GenAI-supported knowledge construction, student role allocations may be influenced by chatbots, yet the effects are  often statistically non-significant and more salient at the perceptual level (Gong et al., 2024). Overall, the distribution indicates an assistive —not substitutive—positioning of GenAI in collaborative discourse, providing a diverse role landscape for subsequent network analyses.

RQ2: Identifying prototypical interaction-network structures SNA results revealed diverse interaction networks in GenAI-supported collaborative learning. The mean network density was 0.408 and degree centralization was 0.367; most groups achieved full connectivity (connectivity = 1), as shown in Figures 3a, 3b, and 3 e. This moderate density indicates interactions that avoid isolation while preventing information overload (Feng, 2025). Groups A and J showed densities of 0.583, forming grid-like structures that reflect frequent exchanges among roles—consistent with Wei et al. (2025), who found GenAI tools enhanced CPS and team creativity through increased interaction without displacing human contributions. Conversely, Group B showed lower density (0.333) and network fragmentation, suggesting interactional atomization due to over-reliance on GenAI or insufficient scaffolding (Zhang & Wang, 2025). This fragmentation risk is higher in AI-centered patterns, where students interact more with chatbots than with peers, dampening the peer-to-peer knowledge flow (Feng, 2025).

High-reciprocity groups such as Group D (0.667) displayed balanced knowledge exchange, aligning with Zhang & Wang (2025), who found GenAI effective for information processing and critical reflection but less supportive of social interaction— 95 potentially leaving low-reciprocity groups in a one-way AI-consultation mode that limits collaborative depth. Low reciprocity may reflect cognitive offloading to GenAI during complex tasks rather than the active cultivation of reciprocal ties, echoing concerns about overdependence noted by Wei et al. (2025). From a nodes-and-edges perspective (Figures 4d and 4f), all groups shared four nodes—Stu, GenAI, SG, and SGS—but the edge counts varied substantially. Groups A and J had seven edges, indicating rich interactions that support decentralized structures and the idea that role diversity can enhance the learning outcomes. Multidimensional scaling (MDS) analysis (Figure 3; stress  < 0.01) supported the structural reliability; the larger positional variance of the student role suggests within-group dynamism, whereas the relative positions of GenAI and hybrid roles indicate context-dependent interaction patterns. For instance, in high-density groups, AI roles are more embedded within human networks, facilitating a shift from AI-centered to distributed patterns (Feng 2025).

RQ3: Structural functions and centrality profiles of authorial roles Students functioned as the primary network hub, with the highest degree (1.367 ± 0.189) and betweenness (0.300 ± 0.058) centrality, whereas GenAI and the system-generated scaffold occupied peripheral positions (betweenness = 0), confined to content generation and record-keeping. The student-generated scaffold showed moderate degree centrality (0.733 ± 0.466) but negligible betweenness (0.067 ± 0.110), indicating limited bridging capacity. This pattern underscores the difficulty of positioning GenAI or its outputs as knowledge integrators in the absence of targeted instructional scaffolds (Figures 5a, 5b, 5d).

This asymmetry (Figure 5 c) indicates that students shoulder the primary bridging and coordination burden in knowledge-building discourse, consistent with findings that human agents occupy network hubs in hybrid teams (Feng, 2025) and that GenAI's low betweenness reflects inadequate scaffolding rather than inherent limitations (Zhang & Wang, 2025).

This study analyzed 527 discussion notes from 54 undergraduates using authorial role classification and social network analysis to examine how GenAI participates in collaborative discourse within a computational thinking course. Results demonstrate that students function as primary network hubs while GenAI and system-generated scaffolds occupy peripheral positions with zero bridging capacity, revealing a critical gap: current scaffolds fail to position GenAI as a knowledge mediator. This finding underscores the necessity of refined prompt design and instructional scaffolding strategies to transform GenAI from a content provider into a collaborative partner that supports peer knowledge integration. Limitations include classifier generalizability, reliance on discussion notes without learner interviews, and static network analysis that cannot capture temporal dynamics.
