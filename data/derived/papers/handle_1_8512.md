# Case-Based Scenarios for Supporting Equitable Mathematics and Science Instruction: Articulating Design Conjectures and Emerging Tensions

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Cases have been used in teacher education to engage pre-service teachers (PSTs) with realistic situations that they might encounter in the classroom. While research suggests affordances of using cases for PST learning, little research has systematically explored productive features of cases or principles for case design. We use design-based research to develop design conjectures about the use of cases to support equitable mathematics and science instruction among PSTs. We describe a project in which we engaged in-service teachers in the development of cases grounded in their own teaching experiences and then implemented those cases as pedagogical tools in elementary mathematics and science teaching methods courses for PSTs. We share two design conjectures emerging from this work and discuss paths forward.

## Introduction

Case-based scenarios, or cases, have been used to engage pre-service teachers (PSTs) with challenging classroom situations they might encounter. A case is typically presented with questions, asking PSTs to analyze the scenario and discuss potential responses. Cases have been shown to represent the complexities of teaching (Baier, Maurer, Dignath, & Kunter, 2021; Shulman, 1992), create opportunities for reflection (Abell et al., 1998), elicit PSTs’ beliefs (Katsh-Singer et al., 2016), and enable PSTs to build connections between theory, practice, and teaching contexts (Shin, Brush, & Saye, 2019). Yet, little research has systematically explored productive features of cases and principles for their design. Here we see the potential for the learning sciences and, in particular, design-based research (Cobb et al, 2003; diSessa & Cobb, 2004; Sandoval, 2004). In this work, we focus on cases as a pedagogical tool to engage PSTs in exploring issues at the intersection of equity and elementary mathematics and science instruction, given persistent historical inequities in these domains. Case-Based Scenarios in Teacher Education Cases, or narratives depicting specific teaching situations, critically engage and challenge learners (Shulman, 1992). Scholars have employed cases for many purposes, from promoting growth of teachers who design cases (Arellano et al., 2001) to collecting data on teachers’ beliefs (Katsh-Singer et al., 2016) to illustrating exemplary practices (Carter, 1999). We argue that cases could foster learning environments for PSTs that allow for links between theory and authentic practice. Yet, the development of cases is rarely discussed or problematized in existing research, leaving questions about how to produce cases that could support such an environment. While some aspects of case development have been identified, such as authenticity (e.g., Shin et al., 2019), these have not emerged from systematic investigations into design principles. Further, few studies have examined cases that forefront equity, yet developing equitable teaching practices is a key priority of teacher education. Design-Based Research Approach We use design-based research (DBR) to iteratively generate cases that foster PST learning. Aligning with the goal of DBR to build theory (diSessa & Cobb, 2004), in this paper we present design conjectures (Sandoval,

2004) that contribute to knowledge about how to design cases that forefront issues of equity in STEM

education. We conceptualize equity in line with Aguirre et al. (2013) who describe the need for responsive instruction that centers learners’ identities, competencies, and varied knowledge resources, while providing opportunities for all learners to engage deeply with mathematics.

## Case Development, Implementation, and Data Collection

A team of teacher educator-researchers worked with in-service elementary teachers (ISTs) to design cases in three virtual workshops. ISTs first explored ideas about equitable instruction from Aguirre et al. (2013) and discussed their relevant experiences. ISTs then used their experiences to design cases for use with PSTs. ICLS2022 Proceedings 1457 © ISLS Here we draw on data from two cases designed by two pairs of ISTs during the workshops. In the Daily Schedule case, a new teacher in a Title 1 elementary school with a large multilingual population is given their schedule and notices that they have been allotted only 1 hour for science each week and 45 minutes a day for math, compared to 3 hours per day for literacy. When the teacher inquires about the lack of science and math time, a coach replies, “That’s not what these kids need.” Discussion prompts explore the coach’s perspectives and actions the teacher might take moving forward. In the Group Dynamics case, three students work together on a science activity about plants’ water and nutrient needs. A dialogue is presented in which two students, Ramon and Roman, start working with materials rather than first doing the required computations stated in the instructions. Meanwhile, the third student, Keza, quietly works on the computations until the boys realize what she has done and take her work. Discussion prompts ask PSTs to analyze group dynamics and potential contributing factors and to discuss pathways to equitable group engagement. The Daily Schedule case was administered in an elementary math methods course in two consecutive semesters. The Group Dynamics case was administered during two consecutive semesters in an elementary science methods course. PSTs first responded individually to prompts in writing and then engaged in discussion in small groups, followed by the whole class. Discussions were audio recorded, and written responses were gathered virtually. After each implementation, our team reviewed the data, noted key themes, discussed themes as a group, connected with prior research, and finally made changes to each case. To support our conjectures, we draw on artifacts, notes, and video recordings from our workshops with ISTs; documentation from research group meetings; written responses and audio recordings from implementation with PSTs; and prior research. Design Conjectures and Lingering Tensions Here we articulate two conjectures for case design at the intersection of equity and STEM instruction, providing supporting evidence and articulating tensions related to each. We also describe ways in which the conjecture can support deep engagement with cases at the intersection of equity and mathematics and science education. Our ultimate goal in this research is to share formalized design principles to be used by others; this work is a first step in that direction, proposing in-progress conjectures and describing initial evidence to support them. Design Conjecture 1: To engage PSTs in deeper critical thinking and offer a framing of teaching as a complex activity involving continual learning, case prompts should ask PSTs to unpack and analyze situations, not come up with solutions. While cases often ask participants to develop a solution to the presented challenge, we have observed that moving towards a solution limited PST thinking and encouraged simplification of the situation. For example, in the Daily Schedule case, the original third prompt asked PSTs how they might provide students with rich math and science learning opportunities despite stated constraints. All students who submitted written responses to that question mentioned integration as a/the solution – several excitedly so (e.g., “Integrating with other subjects!” and “Integrate!!! We cando reading comprehension with science and math with phonics!”) These responses seemed to diminish the richness of responses to the first two questions, conveying a sense that the challenge is easy to solve. Here, we do not blame the PSTs; rather, we felt that asking for a solution to such a complex situation was unfair, as even the ISTs who designed the case based on their experiences were at a loss for what to do. In fact, they had previously discussed the possibility of integration but felt that the situation was more complicated than that. Given PSTs’ tendency to turn to this solution, in part reducing the complexity and thinking brought forth by the first two questions, we revised our initial prompts (Figure A). In particular, we removed the final question and expanded the prior questions to draw out more deep thinking. We concluded with a prompt for PSTs to think about how they might work to advocate for students’ math and science learning moving forward. This question conveys a sense of action as a process, rather than a simple solution, and encourages PSTs to brainstorm various types of information they would want to gather to engage in advocacy. In response to question three, many PSTs cited their own experiences as evidence for the drawbacks of emphasizing literacy at the expense of STEM subjects (e.g., “I have had a lot of language arts and English experiences...Mathematics was not a focus...I would argue that I feel more comfortable with writing and reading than with mathematics for this reason.”). Additionally, question four elicited many responses about the value of data gathering and finding research to support your arguments with stakeholders (e.g., “I think that if I could present research and data that shows that there are not only studies outside of this school that support the idea of teaching math and science as a way to assist in reading, but that this concept has been applied and is working with my own classroom as well, it would be pretty helpful in making my point that we can use math and science as a way to help teach reading.”). While some students did still write about integration, a wider variety of approaches were mentioned, and many PSTs focused on areas for teacher learning and strategies for arguing against the current schedule, rather than simple solutions to fix the problem. Design Conjecture 2: Provide comparable demographic details about all students depicted in the case.

While we know specificity in cases is important (Nemirovsky & Galvis, 2004), prior research lacks theory around what kinds of details to include and how to include those details. One aspect we have investigated is the comparability of detail provided about each student. This conjecture emerged as our research team discussed the following paragraph that teachers wrote about the three students depicted in the Group Dynamics case: Keza, Roman and Ramon were put in a group together. Keza, a refugee from Rwanda, is good at math, has been in the U.S for a year and is part of the ELL class. She is the only student from an African country in a class mixed with other students of color. The two boys in the group are rowdy on the playground and good at math in the classroom. One key challenge we noted is that details provided about the students in this description are not equivalent. Keza’s refugee status, country of origin, and language are highlighted; while the boys’ rowdy disposition and math competency are highlighted. In our prior work, we have seen PSTs perceive demographic details as cuing the solution pathway; such assumptions seem even more likely given the unevenness of the descriptions. This concern is supported by research about the tendency to name minoritized identities but not white students’ identities (e.g., Gilliam et al., 2016), as well as how such naming can cue implicit biases and perpetuate stereotypes (e.g., Godsil et al., 2014). Subsequently, we rewrote the paragraph as follows: Keza, Roman, and Ramon were put in a group together. Keza tends to perform well and seems comfortable in math. She is a refugee from Rwanda, has been in the U.S for a year, and is multilingual while still learning English. Roman and Ramon are good friends, and they both perceive themselves as good at math. Roman is white and grew up on the Wasatch front. Ramon is Chicano and grew up in the Salt Lake area. Both boys are monolingual English speakers. Here we attempted to provide comparable details about all students, including language spoken, ethnicity, where they grew up, and comfort level with math.

While our group agreed that presenting comparable details supports more equitable discussion, we continue to grapple with many tensions related to student demographic details. For example, we wonder which demographic details to include and how much detail to include overall such that those details are not perceived as the only – or even primary – keys to the solution, thus removing agency and critical thinking from PSTs. We ICLS2022 Proceedings 1459 © ISLS are currently exploring these questions by examining PSTs’ responses to prompts in which we ask them to consider what factors might be playing a role in the situation. We will explore whether factors emerge that are not included in the case description, and how such answers differ in cases with few versus many details. Also, we have included prompts asking PSTs to discuss what else they would want to know to better understand the situation; we will analyze those responses to see if inquiries arise regarding additional demographic details.

## Conclusion

In this work, we offer preliminary design conjectures for systematically designing case-based scenarios at the intersection of equity and STEM instruction. While the context for which we are designing is university methods courses for PSTs, we aim for these conjectures to be applicable to cases designed for a variety of purposes – for example, for both PSTs and ISTs. These conjectures can also be applied to cases designed by a range of stakeholders, from researchers and teacher educators to practicing teachers. Additionally, as we work to solidify these conjectures, we believe they might prove useful for designing activities beyond the types of casebased scenarios we describe, such as video cases and sample anecdotes provided in teacher education textbooks. Moving forward, we hope that these conjectures will serve as a springboard for other scholars to systematically explore the design of case-based scenarios and prompts. While these conjectures certainly are not sufficient to cover all issues related to case design, we hope they offer a fruitful first attempt at moving the research field towards a rigorous, robust process for developing cases.
