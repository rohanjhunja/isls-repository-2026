# Side Quests: Black Futures Technology-mediated Thinking and Practice in a Justice-oriented Future Educator Learning Environment

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper explores Black futures tech nology-mediated sensemaking and practice within the context of a redesigned future educator technology course at a Primarily White Institution (PWI) that brings together Sociocultural theories of learning with Afrofuturist principles of learning and design. Through an analysis of the design and enactment of a core course task and participation structure, side quests, this paper addresses two research questions:1) What kinds of thinking around technology emerge within a Black future oriented learning ecology?; and 2) How did the side quest’s task and participant structures mediate this thinking?. Findings illustrate joint critical explorations of everyday technologies, such as artificial intelligence, to foster Black futures technology thinking and practice.

Side Quests: Black Futures Technology-mediated Thinking and Practice in a Justice-oriented Future Educator Learning Environment Ashieda McKoy, University of Colorado Boulder, Ashieda.mckoy@colorado.edu Abstract: This paper explores Black futures tech nology-mediated sensemaking and practice within the context of a redesigned future educator technology course at a Primarily White Institution (PWI) that brings  together Sociocultural theories of learning with Afrofuturist principles of learning and design. Through an analysis of the design and enactment of a core course task and participation structure, side quests, this paper addresses two research questions:1) What kinds of thinking around technology emerge within a Black future oriented learning ecology?; and 2) How did the side quest’s task and participant structures mediate this thinking?. Findings illustrate joint critical explorations of everyday technologies, such as artificial intelligence, to foster Black futures technology thinking and practice. The learning sciences (Politics of Learning Writing Collective, 2017), teacher education (Cochran-Smith and Villegas, 2015), and the National Academy (NASEM, 2024) have all elucidated a need to attend to the historical, social, cultural and political experiences and realities of our world and to our growing diverse student population. Inspired by repeated calls for more political and justice-centered study and design of teaching and learning, particularly in pre-service educator learning, I investigate the conjecture that teacher learning ecologies organized around Afrofuturist principles and sociocultural theories of learning afford educators opportunities for justice-centered, (Black) future-oriented, technology-mediated, and collective learning. This conjecture was embodied in the design of a future educator course on learning technologies, in which preservice educators were invited to engage with texts and activities grounded in Afrofuturist principles and design (McKoy, 2025). Of the four Afrofuturist guides (briefly explained below), this paper explores tech nology-mediated thinking through the design and enactment of one of the course’s main task and participation structures, side quests. Specifically, this paper addresses the research questions:1) What kinds of thinking around technology emerge within a Black future oriented learning ecology?, and 2) How did the side quest’s task and participant structures mediate this thinking?. To answer these questions and investigate my conjecture around tech-mediated learning, I first summarize Afrofuturist tech-mediated thinking and practice, then explain the design of side quests, paying attention to key task and participation structures. Finally, I explore one side quest and enactment from the course to conclude with key aspects (and revisions) of the design that afford Black futures tech-mediated thinking and practice.

Learning scholars have built on this notion of learning and development with regard to tools (Vygotsky & Cole, 1978), naming that artifacts and their design can influence the shape and direction of learning, but also the learners themselves (Pea, 1993; Wer tsch, 1991; 1998). These scholars help us understand how artifacts or tools –and for this paper, technologies –carry histories, uses, and innovations that both afford and/or constrain learning. The recent sociopolitical turn in the learning sciences also emphasizes the importance of re-mediation (Gutiérrez et al., 2009); I build on recent scholarship calling on intentionally changing and reimagining infrastructures of learning, including artifacts, to foreground equity and justice, particularly toward communi ties valued practices (Jurow et al., 2018). This notion of re-mediation is critical for re-designing teacher learning environments, helping to reimagine educators' learning toward students’ and their community’s lived experiences and ultimately more just and equitable ends.

Previous literature has also explored how various technologies, including artificial intelligence, have been riddled with constraints for communities of color (Benjamin, 2020; Noble, 2018), yet might  help us move toward the futures of our dreams, particularly through the everyday practices of young people of color (Tanksley, 2024). Further, Afrofuturism’s interdisciplinary fluidity combined with its potential for critical reimaginations of technology afford a reflexive conversation between learners, their lived experiences, and the society in which we live (Carter, 2022). Afrofuturism uses technology and science as forms of “critical praxis,” where reflection and action work together to address harmful structures of our present (Dando et al., 2019) and move towards more just and equitable futures. This paper builds from this literature to describe the ways in which future educators 292 make sense of how our everyday technologies can both harm and help communities of color move toward the just (learning) futures of our dreams.

An Afrofuturist framework for technology-mediated thinking and design Four design guides for Black futures thinking Afrofuturist scholars have highlighted its epistemological intersections of (collective) imagination, technology, the future, and liberation, as well as its ability to turn theory, imagination, and literature into action (Womack, 2013). Inspired by these four epistemologies, I integrated Afrofuturist principles and aesthetics into the design of an undergraduate course for future educators. I organize d these principles and aesthetics as four design guides: justice-centered, (Black)future-oriented, technology-mediated, and collective. Previous work (McKoy, 2025) details all four design guides in greater depth, with an attention to how these guides were  reflected in  future educator thinking and practice at the end of the course in final projects or prototypes with young people. Briefly, the justice-centered guide reflects future educators realizing the ways race and power show up in our past, present and future realities, and working to undo constraint by (re)imagining and (re)making alternative ways to be (Love, 2019; Zamalin, 2022); the future-oriented guide supports recontextualizing futures as “authorable”—we can write ourselves into futures, but they are always grounded in Black history, agency, and cultural tradition (Eshun, 2013; Yaszek, 2006);  technology-mediated thinking and practice involves future educators’ critical sensemaking of expansive and everyday technologies for how they help or harm communities of color move toward more equitable and just futures  (Carter, 2022); finally, the collective guide illustrates future educator thinking and practice that recognizes freedom dreaming towards more just futures as a community endeavor with diverse learners and expertise (Baumann et al., 2017; Holbert et. al., 2020). Analyzing where students “ended up” at the evident thinking around the four guides. This paper builds on that work, specifically taking up a conjecture around technology-mediated thinkin g and practice by exploring  a task and participation structure (i.e. side quests) designed for Afrofuturist tech-mediated thinking and its subsequent enactments. I delve deeper into the third guide, tech-mediated, as it serves as a focal analytical tool for this paper. Tech-mediated thinking for Black futures Afrofuturism is a perspective mediated by the fields of science and technology to both critique but also reimagine our world in service of more just futures by offering a lens for specifically thinking about concerns and constraints in our everyday techno-rich environments and the role of technology itself (in reimagining these environments) (Dery, 1994). Afrofuturism, along with Black tech scholars (Benjamin, 2020; Noble 2018; Tanks ley, 2024) and Africana Digital Humanities scholars (Carter, 2022) help us understand that technology is not neutral, rather it can and has been used expansively to both harm and help communities of color move towards just futures that resist their erasure.

Specifically, Afrofuturism helps situate technology as “an evolution of what Black people have been doing for generations, across the Africana diaspora, in different forms but accomplishing the same goals” (Carter, 2022, pg.15). These goals include understanding ourselves and the world around us, communicating with each other and ancestors, and asserting ourselves in the future even as we face persistent erasure (Carter, 2022). In this way technology is broad, but always grounded in Brown and Black experiences, and more importantly, warrants critical consideration of both its design and “use”, particularly in its ability to harm or help communities of color organize toward collective liberatory futures. Consequently, Afrofuturism has been used to inspire the design and (re)imagination of expansive technologies that help our diverse communities (Baumann et al., 2017; Dando et al.,

2019) and all humanity (Winchester III, 2019) work toward alternative and just ways to be. Thus, Afrofuturist

tech-mediated thinking and practice is an attention to how technologies have harmed and erased communities of color, and despite this harm, how technology can be (and has been) expansively (re)imagined in pursuit of (collective) liberation for us all.

Side quests: Task and participation structure for tech-mediated thinking Side quests are a participation structure designed to support future educator Afrofuturist tech-mediated thinking and practice, concerned with an expansive view of technology and with respect to how it can (and has been used) to both help and harm Black and Brown folx move towards collective liberation. Like side quests in video games, this activity involves working through a small task or mission that is partly outside the main storyline in a game. Often in side quests, players earn valuable tools, points,  or rewards that help them in achieving their overall goal in gameplay. Side quests can often be collaborative or team-building activities that stress different skills than a main storyline (which is often more individual-oriented). In this course, I designed side quests to be weekly 293 collaborative tasks aimed at: 1) exploring technology within everyday (learning) contexts, 2) elucidating and navigating issues folx of color experience around technology, 3) working collectively with peers to reimagine and move through these constraints and, 4) introduce new tools–such as new texts or digital technologies–to help them design/imagine anew. Side quest activities at the beginning of the course were designed for evaluating historic and everyday technologies, then transitioned toward reimaginin g (with) technology for collective liberation toward the middle and end of the course.

## Data context, sources, and analysis

The context for this paper is a re-designed Spring 2024 education technology course at a  central US public university that is a Predominantly White Institution (PWI). Like many preservice courses, the student population of the course consisted of mostly white femmes—out of seven total undergraduate participants, only two were of color, five were white, four were women, one man, and one future educator identified as non-binary. This largely white femme preservice population follows the trend of the nation’s teaching force that  is likewise white and female, teaching an increasingly diverse student population (Hussar et al., 2020). In previous iterations, this course focused on exploring learning in and out of schools with attention to digital media and technology. Redesigning this education course to focus on leveraging technology to imagine and design for more liberatory learning futures, particularly for students of color, as technology has always been bound up in narratives of futurity for Black and Brown folx (Dery,  1994), was important for these future educators who have been steeped in a university environment and professional landscape that has historically privileged whiteness. This study might be best understood as a social-design based experiment inspired collective case study (Merriam, 1998) where I make sense of learning as a result of multiple designs, tools, and participation structures within the (re)design and implementation of this 15-week undergraduate education course. I drew on social design-based experiment literature (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016) to facilitate a learning space where future educators specifically explored justice-centered issues in learning. Social design-based experiments allow for studying how educator participation, learning, relationships and sensemaking transform over time, particularly through tool-mediated interventions (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016), an important design feature for my work and in Afrofuturism which deeply considers the role of tools and technologies in supporting the joint creation of just new futures (Dery, 1994).

Data sources and analysis As side quests were a recurring participation structure in the course, data for this paper was collected from weekly classroom video and artifacts  gathered over the 15-week semester; there were 8 total side quests. Specifically, video data sources include 8, 2.5-hour 360-degree whole group discussion recordings and 20 small group audio/video recordings that ran between 30-60 minutes. Data sources also included any artifacts from assignments and activities completed during side quests  (e.g. google slides, docs, images, drawings). Finally, because class slides and instructional materials were designed and iterated on as the course progressed to gather learners’ inputs and participations, I collected and analyzed all these artifacts to  showcase the side quest’s particular task and participation structures and student contributions during those activities. For this paper,  both whole and small group videos were logged for every 5 minutes of activity and transcribed. These video/content logs primarily oriented me to toward deeper moments for analysis. Once I had key episodes identified, I sought to understand through interaction analysis (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) how participant and task structures mediated future educators' thinking around the Black futures technology guide I introduced in the course. Thus, after identifying moments (from logs) that provided some evidence related to the Black futures tech thinking, I began to analyze key interactions for: 1) engagement with course tools and technologies (as this was a major design of the course, and rotated each week), 2) joint sensemaking and practice around the technology-mediated guide. My interpretations focused on elucidating which ideas  from the course students took up and how. For this paper I developed an interpretive argument around these moments, presenting dialogue, notable nonverbal interactions, and any artifacts designed for or created during these conversations in order to make and support claims related to how the side quest’s tasks and participant structures mediated engagement with my tech-mediated guide.

In this section I move through an extended example of a side quest and enactment from the beginning of the course as an ideal case of Black futures tech nology thinking mediated by joint critical evaluation of everyday 294 technology. Through an analysis of side quest artifacts and interactions, I explore tech-mediated thinking and practice as well as key participation and task structures that illuminate this thinking. The AI side quest: Designing for tech-mediated thinking as critical evaluation of everyday technology This side quest was designed to help students critically evaluate the world as it is, paying attention to how current/historic structures and technologies can both help and harm communities of color. Inspired by the work of Drs. Tiera Tanksley (2024), Sofiya Noble (2018), and Ruha Benjamin (2020) and their critical explorations of digital algorithms, I designed this side quest for students to explore everyday a rtificial intelligence with a new, more critical lens. Students were asked to interact with generative AI and interpret their results through the lenses of Black Tech and Afrofuturist scholars. The slide presenting instructions for this side quest is below (See figure 1): Figure 1 Instructional Slide for the AI Side Quest The side quest was simple: collaboratively explore AI, paying close attention to what AI generates as a result. Then, leverage two tools, a text–Ruha Benjamin’s (2020) Race After Technology, which explores humancentered racial design and implications of algorithmic technology –as well as the AI itself to critically evaluate their results toward implications for nondominant communities and learning environments. Students also had to revise prompts or (re)imagine how the AI might need to function to generate more “realistic” (e.g. diverse) results. In this vein, I gave them a specific set of prompts to try with AI first that would get them to consider everyday learning contexts before encouraging them to try prompts that were related to something that would be interesting to them. On the slide above you can see a variety of prompts like “ learning, school, teacher, student ” that might be readily associated with education and schooling, however, I also included some prompts less commonly associated with schooling, such as “fun, strong, and youth”, to see what additional connections or inferences future educators would make, based on their results. In comparison, the only time Black or Brown figures or communities were mentioned, were by Groups 2 and 4. Group 2 only generated an image of a Black man when prompting AI with the word “rapper” and received a Black man standing in dark baggy clothes in the middle of a street crosswalk. Lana says that they kept putting in different general terms to see what they would get but they “knew what the search would turn out every time”. For Lana, being able to predict what the AI would generate for each term spoke to “something wrong” or a normalized exclusion and erasure of marginalized folx. For Ben, the AI’s predictability signaled racism, one that reifies stereotyping as a kind of intentional erasure and oppression of Black and Brown folx. Afrofuturist tech-mediated thinking in this excerpt, then, is exemplified in how future educators move from echoing a more general sentiment that technology is “supposed” to be this more neutral and positive thing, or in Lana’s words   “inclusive and accessible”, but ult imately conclude that it is not; rather, the AI’s predictability signals that technology carries many of the same biases people do as “it’s clearly excluding a majority of the population”. iteration Side quests scaffold Afrofuturist tech-mediated thinking through two key structures: first,  supporting future educators in critically evaluating technology’s impact on communities of color using justice-centered frameworks; and second, engaging in collaborative design tasks that require (re)imagining technology as a tool for navigating and transforming everyday constraints. These structures help future educators move beyond critique toward expansive, liberatory possibilities in teaching, learning, and our w orld. In the student artifacts and enactments presented above we can see evidence of future educators ’ tech-mediated thinking and practice supported by the first structure. Specifically, leveraging Afrofuturist and Critical Black tech course texts in reflection on everyday technologies, particularly those we more readily associate as technologies like AI, help future educators recognize that their everyday technologies and technological practices are not neutral, rather they have the potential to harm Black and Brown folx (and especially their future students) through normalized stereotyping, invisibility, and erasure. As Lana mentions during this AI side quest, current and future narratives around technology stress that it is “inclusive and accessible, ” but the side quest critical reflection activity helped her challenge this notion and acknowledge that there was “obviously something wrong” with technology, pointing to its repeated erasure and stereotyping of communities of color. Being able to identify this harm in/with technology, in its various forms, will be important for using and (re)imagining technology away from these constraints and toward more equitable and just ends  for our current and future students. Analysis of additional side quests, particularly those that elucidate the collaborative design (towards new futures/technologies) task and participation structure of this activity for tech-mediated thinking will be a feature in future work. Lastly, based on these findings, future iterations of the side quest structure could consistently incorporate task and participation structures involving joint making and design (instead of scaffolding them for later side quests); Afrofuturist justice work implores always being in the practice of (re)imagining and (re)making the world as we know it (action-oriented tasks). Thus, in revision of this side quest, I would also ask future educators to create their own AI algorithm, with critical considerations f or where the data comes from, who designs it, and what it is supposed to do/what can you do with it, based on Benjamin’s main concerns with technology. In this way, the side quest could be immediately both reflective and imaginatively action-oriented. Ultimately, the Afrofuturist tech-mediated design of the course, particularly around the joint critical exploration of everyday technologies, offers a new  approach to justice-centered future educator learning within the context of PWI university-based teacher education aimed at liberation for all our future students.
