# Embodied Improvisation: Exploring How Teachers Support Young Children’s Engagement with Intelligent Agents

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Early childhood teachers are increasingly introducing intelligent agents (IAs) into their classrooms but often face challenges in balancing structured lessons with the spontaneity required to address children’s natural curiosity and unexpected exploration with IAs. To address this challenge, we propose an Embodied Improvisation framework to investigate how teachers support young children’s engagement with IAs. The Embodied Improvisation framework adapts embodied responsive teaching and the “Yes, and…” improv principle to highlight the embodied and fluid nature of teachers’ scaffolding. Through interactional video analysis of teacher-child interactions with a robot in a toddler (2-3 years old) classroom, we demonstrate how teachers address children’s cognitive and emotional needs and enhance their engagement and agency through embodied improvisation. Based on the findings, we advocate a flexible approach to integrating IAs into developmentally appropriate classroom practices for young children.

## Introduction

Intelligent Agents (IAs), typically supported and accompanied by Artificial Intelligenc e (AI) technology, are defined as entities that can perceive, respond prom ptly, interact with other agents (including humans), and adapt to their environment (Rudowsky, 2004). Appearing in forms like smart speakers, chatbots, a nd interactive toys, IAs are increasingly integrated into various aspects of  young children’s daily li ves, including homes, schools, therapy, and entertainment (Van Straten et al., 202 0). As a regular part of young children’s experience s, IAs are influencing how they learn, play, and communicate (Druga et al., 2019; Rapti & Sapounidis, 2023; Samue lsson, 2023). The widespread presence of IAs in early chil dhood settings presents notable challenges for educators. Unlike structured subjects like math or science, teaching about IAs and AI is not yet an established discipline with familiar content and resources for teachers (Kim & Kwon, 2023; Su et al., 2023). Rigid, structured tea ching methods often fall short of accommodating the necessary flexibility and responsiveness in IA lessons, highlighting the need for teachers to incorporate improvisational skills when engaging young children with these technologies. Furthermore, young children’s natural curiosity oft en leads them to explore technology in unexpected w ays (Arnone et al., 2011), creating the challenge of ba lancing structured lessons with spontaneous explora tion. This context prompted our investigation into teachers’ i mprovisational practices and their impact on IA lea rning in preschool classrooms. When we applied existing improvisational frameworks to analyze teacher-child interactions in these settings (Dahn et al., 2021; Holdhus et al., 2016), we observed a limited emphasis on the nonverbal aspects that are essential for young children’s learning and development.

To address these challenges, this paper proposes an Embodied Improvisation framework to investigate how teachers  support young children’s engagement  with IAs. The framework is built on  Flood, Shvarts  and Abrahamson’s (2022) embodied responsive teaching and the “Yes, and…” improv principle (Gesell, 1997). The former emphasizes teachers’ embodied support, while the latter focuses on the fluid and affirmative res ponses. Our framework highlights how teachers actively engage y oung children by incorporating nonverbal, embodied interactions alongside verbal cues to balance struc tured lessons with the flexibility and affirmation needed to respond to children’s and IAs’ spontaneous actions or needs.  Informed by this framework, we conducted interaction analysis of video recordings from a pre school classroom where children aged 2 to 3 interac ted with a robot. Findings of this study can offer valuable insights for early childhood educators, particularly in guiding and supporting young children’s exploration and interactions with IAs in classroom settings.

## Theoretical framework and literature

Improvisation (or “improv”), defined as intuitive  and spontaneous action (Crossan & Sorrenti, 1997),  is deeply connected to our everyday practices and fosters openness tonew perspectives and actions and antici pating what has yet to be realized but can be brought into exis tence (Dehlin, 2012). In the education context, improvisation serves as a powerful metaphor  and tool for teaching  (Berk & Trieber, 2009; Erickson, 1982), highlightin g the collaborative and emergent nature of effective clas sroom practice (Sawyer, 2004b). Improvisation in the classroom could encourage students to respond freely, with the teacher facilitating rather than judging responses, guiding students to self-discovery (Sawyer, 2004a) and co-constructing knowledge (Sawyer, 2004b). It allows students to express ideas, collaborate, and learn from mistakes (Koppett, 2001), fostering their problem awareness, and ideas fundamental to their intellectual development (Berk & Trieber, 2009). Research linking teaching and improv often draws on principles from theatrical or musical improvisation to identify effective teaching strategies (Borko & Living ston, 1989; Sawyer, 2004a; Yinger, 1987) or to guid e teachers in incorporating improvisational technique s into their lessons (Dahn et al., 2021; Sawyer, 20 04b). For example, acceptance, the “Golden Rule” of improvisa tion, requires a “yes, and…” approach, where teache rs embrace and build upon new ideas from students and colleagues (Gesell, 1997). This mindset fosters collaboration, as each member contributes to brainstorming and inn ovative solutions (Koppett, 2001). Other essential improv principles in teaching include trust, spontaneity, attentive listening, storytelling, nonverbal commun ication, and warm-ups (Adu & Adu, 2014). Attentive listening enhances communication and understanding, while spontaneity allows teachers to respond naturally, fostering genuine interactions and trust (Keefe, 2002). Trust among teachers supports productive collaboratio n, encouraging risk-taking and exploration. Nonverbal communication  conveys meaning and supports co-creation through expressions and body language, while warm-up activities set a safe, supportive environment, enabling teachers to improvise confidently and creatively (Koppett, 2001). When improvisation is adopted as a guiding theory in education, a critical gap emerges in addressing the nonverbal, embodied aspects of communication. While modern improvisational theater emphasizes participation through both verbal and nonverbal interactions, the  nonverbal elements are often overlooked or treated  as peripheral in educational applications.  For example, Holdhus et al. (2016) reviewed the roo ts and application of improv in education and teaching, summarizing four key components: communication and dialogues, structure and design, repertoire, and context. Although they described communication and dialogues along a continuum— from internal processes to external outcomes—the focus stayed mainly on the verbal responses of and to students. Mæland and Espeland (2017) further expanded on these ideas by incorporating pre-service teachers’ perspectives, yet still emphasized verbal improvisation from teac hers, overlooking nonverbal, embodied response s that can enhance engagement and facilitate learning (Flood et al., 2020). This oversight is particularly critical in early ch ildhood education (ECE), where nonverbal communication is vital to  children’s learning and development. Children devel op their thinking processes and language skills through sensory and motor experiences (Sommer et al., 2009). Their physical expressions through movement and gestures often reflect their mental engagement and active involvement in shared meaning-making processes (Nyland, 2009; Sung & Nathan, 2024). Embo died responsive teaching, encompass ing strategies that support STEM learning by actively responding to and  engaging learners’ embodied ideas  (Flood, Shvarts, & Abrahamson, 2022), has been shown to enhance young children ’s development and engagement across multiple domains, like computational thinking (Flood, Wang, & Sheridan, 2022), and mathematical learning (Flood et al., 2020). Despite the recognized importance of nonverb al communication, current improvisation practices  in ECE often overlook these aspects. For instance, Dahn et  al. (2021) introduce “instructional improv” to und erstand teaching in inquiry-based science classrooms, demonstrating how teacher s can spontaneously blend improvisational principles with teaching practices to support student learning, engagement, and agency. Although their unit of analysis incorporates both teacher and student dialogue and actions, their findings primaril y emphasize responding to and assessing students’ verbal inputs through verbal means, with less focus on nonverbal cues and embodied pedagogy. Given the significant r ole of embodiment in ECE, integrating embodied aspe cts into the improvisational teaching framework is crucial to fully support children’s cognitive and emotional growth. The increasing introduction of AI and IAs into educational settings adds another layer of complexity. As AI technologies continue to evolve with undefined d isciplinary goals (Su et al., 2023) and are increas ingly introduced to young children (Yang, 2022), educators face challenges in making these abstract concepts accessible to early learners. The spontaneity and context matt er significantly when introducing IAs to young children, as both the actions and errors of IAs, along with children’s varied responses, require adaptable scaffolding strategies. Furthermore, young children may struggle to compreh end IAs functionalities and implications through conventional, primarily verbal instruction. Embodie d improvisation offers a promising approach to addr essing these challenges.

Adapting Flood, Shvarts, and Abrahamson’s (2022) embodied responsive teaching and the “Yes, and…” improv’s golden rule (Gesell, 1997), we propose a framework of embodied improvisation that captures the microprocess of teachers’ improvisation in supporting children’s learning about IAs through three interconnected phases: co-construct meanings, scaffolding, and evaluation and reflection, each guided by the “Yes, and…” principle and embedding embodied elements (see Figure 1). We use an unexpected occurrence, which could arise at any given moment in the classroom, as an entry point to analyze a teacher’ improvisation. Guided by the principle of “yes, and…,” which embodies the “Agree, Accept, and Add” approach (Halpern et al., 1994, p. 35; Vera & Crossan, 2005), the teacher could accept and embrace it as a potential learning opportunity. Such an attitude allows the teacher to co-construct meanings with children through verbal and embodied interactions, linking learning content with students’ actions to foster engagement and contextual understanding. Based on the co-constructed meanings, the teacher scaffold s learning by adapting verbal and/or nonverbal cues t o children’s immediate responses, affirming their contributions, and building on their input through “yes, and…”. During evaluation and reflection, the teacher observes and interprets children’s responses to assess understanding, integ rating feedback to refine future teaching strategies. If learning goals are not met, “yes, and…” guides the teacher back to the co-construction of meanings, enabling continuous adaptation and ref inement. This iterative process ensures responsive and embodied teaching, fostering a dynamic learning env ironment that promotes  the co-construction of knowledge. Throughout the process, the “yes, and…” principle also reflects trust among teachers (a leader teacher and a co-teacher are common in ECE classrooms) who collabora te to support  each other’s decision-making.  Guided by this framework, our study investigates the research question: How do teachers use embodied imp rovisation to engage children in learning about intelligent agents?

## Study context and methods

Our present study was part of a pilot program introducing young children to IAs in a university-affiliated preschool in the United States. In the Fall 2023 semester, guided by eight teachers, 33 students from three classrooms— Toddler (ages 2-3), Preschool (ages 3-4), PreK (ages 4-5)—explored three kinds of robots (Go1, Spot, Astro) once weekly for eight weeks. The research team video-recorded these sessions (an average of 18 minutes per session), totaling approximately 8 hours. Although we observed improv interactions across all sessions and age groups, our analysis focused on the first three sessions of interacting with the Go1 robot in the toddler class (two lead teachers, eight children). This choice was guided by (1) Go-1 being the first robot introduced, providing uniqu e insights into children’s initial reactions to IAs, and (2) t he need for greater embodied improvisation  to accommodate toddlers’ shorter attention spans and developmental needs. The Go-1, a small, dog-like robot by Unitree Robotics (see Figure 2), is about the height of a low coffee table and designed for fun, flexible movement, including running, jumping, and dancing. It was introduced as “Spark, the robot dog.” Figure 2 Go1 Robot We began by reviewing the videos of the three sessi ons, focusing on moments that went beyond the initial lesson plans or unfolded in unexpected ways. We then identified and analyzed two representative video excerpts that addressed our research question. The first, from Session 1, captures toddlers’ initial close encounter with Spark, eliciting varied reactions as the teach er encouraged them to pet the robot  dog. The focal teacher, Vivian, was a first-year teacher pursuing a master’s degree in early childhood/childhood (EC/C) education with an undergraduate degree in psychology. The second ex cerpt from Session 3 features a “Hide and Seek with Pumpkin” game with Spark. Alyssa, the focal teacher  for this excerpt,  has a master’s degree in EC/C education and taught preschool for over eight years. Both tea chers had no prior experience with robots or teachi ng about them. The toddler classroom comprised eight children aged 2–3 from diverse racial/ethnic backgrounds, including White, Black, Asian, and Hispanic. Three of them were emergent bilingual learners. We applied interaction analysis  (Jordan & Henderson, 1995)  to the selected two excerpts’ video recordings to examine  teacher improvisation and children’s engagement. We transcribed participants’ speech, vocalizations, gaze, body movements, and gestures, including the actions of Spark and its operator, both of whom were treated as participants in the two selected ex cerpts. Transcriptions followed conventions adapted from conversation analysis (Hepburn & Bolden, 2017): capitals for louder speech; question marks for rising intonation (?); periods (.) for falling intonation; timed pauses appear in parentheses (2.5); colons denote elongated syllables (::); and square brackets show overlapping actions ([). Embodied actions are illustrated, and co-timed speech is outlined with boxes.

Specifically, Vivian varied her approach for each child based on their level of comfort and need  for support. The entry point in this excerpt was child A’s unexpected nonverbal actions—moving backward and looking at Vivian that signals his hesitation and emotional need to feel safe and supported– to pet Spark. This likely prom pted Vivian to pay closer attention to child A’s embodied behaviors, attending to his embodied actions and pe rceptions, which is the foundation of embodied resp onsive teaching (Flood, Shvarts, & Abrahamson, 2022). Recognizing child A’s actions as opportunities for learning and invitation to co-construct meaning s, Vivian shifted the  task from petting individually to petting “together” by asking, “Do you want to pet together?” Her attentive response to the child’s embodied cues exemplified the “yes, and…” principle and informed her improvisational use of scaffolding techniques. To assess and address the children’s emotional needs, Vivian employed joint attention—the shared focus of two or more individuals on the same object, main tained through gaze or verbal cues (Tomasello, 1995; Tomasello & Carpenter, 2007) —as a scaffolding strategy tailored to each child’s responses. By establishing, maintaining, and adapting a shared focus on Spark with each of the children, Vivian provided mental and physical support, promoted engagement, and encouraged children to pet Spark. In child A’s case, Vivian engaged in joint attention by petting Spark with him together while continuing to hold his hand. This created a foundation of safety and confidence that enabled his engagement with Spark. The combination of physical and emotional support not only reassured him but also served as co-regulation, with Vivian’s calm guidance helping child A navigate the unfamiliar experience and ease his uneasiness when approaching IAs. For child B, who naturally approached from behind and sat on Vivian’s lap, this served as a ph ysical cue for Vivian to embrace the physical connection and provide subtle support by gently guiding child B’s hand as they petted Spark together. This physical guidance and shared activity reinforced joint attention by foste ring a mutually focused and collaborative experienc e. Although less directive than her approach with child A, Vivian’s support was equally essential in provid ing the emotional security and joint at tention needed for child B’s engagement. In contrast, Vivian’s interaction with child C demonstrated a different scaffolding approach – withdrawal (Cai & Xing, 2024), where she intentionally stepped back to allow the child greater independence in petting Spark. Initially, Vivian extended her arms forward as an invitation, similar  to her previous embodied scaffolding with c hild B. However, u pon observing child C’s nonverbal cues of readiness to pet Spark —her confidence and eagerness demonstrated by focused  attention on Spark and direct movement  towards it (line  19)—Vivian adjusted her approach  and withdrew her outstretched arms. Nevertheless, she stayed close without physical support, enabling child C to pet Spark independently. These adaptive interactions highlight ed the iterative nature of embodied improvisation, whe re Vivian continually evaluated children’s responses through joint attention and ad apts her actions in alignment with the “yes, and…” principle (accepting and affirming varied cues and responses from different children), fostering autonomy and engagement. Through ongoing evaluation and reflection  on each child’s unique needs based on their nonverb al behaviors, Vivian tailored her scaffolding strategies to support their emotion al readiness and encourage the exploration of IAs.

## Conclusion

This study richly demonstrates the significant role of embodied impro visation in early childhood education, particularly in navigating complex interactions wit h IAs. By focusing on two foundational elements —embodied responsiveness and the “yes, and…” principle —our analysis illustrates how teachers effectively adapt to unexpected occurrence, co-construct meaning s, and scaffold children’s learning experiences. Thr ough the iterative observation, adaptation, and reflection  process, embodied improvisation enable s teachers to foster children’s engagement and agency.

In Excerpt 1, Vivian’s physical and emotional scaff olding tailored to each child’s embodied cues demonstrates how attentiveness to nonverbal actions can inform improvisational teaching. Her ability to recognize hesitation, confidence, or eagerness allowed her to meet the children’s needs and encourage positive interactions with Spark. This highlights the importance of embod ied responsiveness through joint attention, which l everages both verbal and nonverbal communication to create a safe and supportive environment for exploration. Similarly, in Excerpt 2, Alyssa exemplifies the “yes, and…” principle by accepting and building on children’s actions to coconstruct knowledge with the operator ’s support. Her use of gestures, verbal cues, and co ntinuous scaffolding reflects her ability to adapt to the children’s cognitive and engagement levels in real-time. The collaborative efforts of Alyssa, the children, the operator, and the robo t in finding the pumpk in reinforce the concept that “ robots are controlled by humans” and enhance the children’s engagement and agency through active involvement. The implications of these findings extend beyond im mediate classroom interactions. Embodied improvisation offers a flexible framework for teach ing IAs in ECE, highlighting the potential of techn ology to support co-constructed, child-centered learning environments. Teachers’ ability t o embrace unexpected occurrence and respond improvisationally ensures that interact ions with IAs remain dynamic and inclusive, fostering both social-emotional and cognitive development of young children. Ultimately, this study emphasizes the transformative potential of embodied improvisation in enhancing engagement, promoting agency, and creating meaningful, shared experiences in ECE. As technolog y continues to evolve, incorporating embodied improvisational strategies into teacher training pr ograms could empower educators to navigate the  complexities of integrating IAs, ensuring that these tools suppo rt equitable, collaborative, and developmentally ap propriate learning experiences.
