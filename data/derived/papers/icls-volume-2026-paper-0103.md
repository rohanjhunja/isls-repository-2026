# Community-Building in K12 Classrooms with a Collaborative AI Agent and Associated Curriculum Routines

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper examines how curriculum routines can mediate tensions between the convergent tendencies of artificial intelligence (AI) systems and the divergent, equity-oriented affordances of collaborative learning. Drawing on a multi-year partnership with te achers, we analyze the co-design and classroom enactment of a collaborative AI partner, CoBi, and associated “revisiting community agreement” routines. Using video data and thematic analysis, we show how these routines functioned as boundary objects linking design intent with classroom practice. Teachers enacted the routines with high integrity and creatively adapted them to local contexts, leveraging AI errors to foster AI literacy and critical reflection. Students engaged in meta-collaborative discussions that positioned AI as a phenomenon for critique rather than a source of authority. Findings illustrate how curriculum routines can align human and technological agency, sustaining equity-centered and reflective collaboration in AI-embedded classrooms.

The possibilities of artificial intelligence (AI) have long captivated learning scientists and educational researchers, with many promising AI-based proposals that forefront student learning and achievement. Recent youth-engaged critical co-design spaces h ave pushed researchers to consider more expansive metaphors for AI-supported learning, particularly around the issues of resilient and community-focused collaborative learning that position students as transformative agents and world makers of the future (Chang et al., 2022). Yet, in part because of their unfamiliarity and in part because they push the boundaries of the “grammar of schooling” (Tyack & Tobin, 1994), these new metaphors for AI-supported learning raise new tensions as they are taken up by interdisciplinary teams of AI and education researchers.

In this paper, we present a model for how curriculum routines can effectively support expansive youthfocused AI designs becoming classroom reality. To do so, we use a case study of the collaborative AI agent, CoBi that was designed to support students in establishing and revisiting community agreements for collaboration in classroom learning communities. Our analysis looks at how design decisions about AI powered interfaces can support these community agreement routines and how these routines support the i mplementation and fidelity of use of the interface. By curriculum routines, we mean a generalizable (across lessons, units and curricula) and repeatable sequence of teacher moves that promote a curricular student learning goal. While routines have been discussed as a strategy for 1) aligning good teaching practices and classroom technology, 2) guide classroom usage of technology and 3) guide instructional design (DeBarger et al. 2011), their integration with AI technologies has not yet been explored.

AI technologies bring novel threats and affordances to supporting justice and fairness in learning communities. So, the design of educational technologies alsoneeds to attend to equity. While the learning sciences and CSCL communities have been addressing this for decades, AI specific challenges bring new dynamics with how we attend to equity. One of these AI specific challenges centers on how AI tools tend to reinforce dominant and common forms of behavior, termed convergent behavior while justice and equity concerns celebrate and value diversity and uniqueness, termed divergent behavior. Machine learning models inherently both reproduce and mimic patterns in their training data, hence valuing convergence towards those observed patterns and the patterns most represented in the training data. Building on other work that has positioned AI as a thought partner to support divergent (and thus more human) thinking (Simeone et al. 2022), we explore the tension of ideational convergence and diversity in the context of co-designing an AI collaboration support tool for K-12 classroom spaces. We explore how the tension of convergence with divergence can be mediated through curriculum routines and a revised interface designed to support those routines by exploring the following research questions: 908

1. When expansive co-design illuminates the possibilities of collaborative AI agents, how can curriculum

routines mediate the tension between AI’s convergent tendency and the affordances of divergent thinking for equity?

2. When enacted in classrooms, how do curricular routines work in synergy with an AI-powered

collaboration support agent to serve as a boundary object to mediate these tensions, afford students’ crucial agency about AI tools, and their engagement with productive collaborative reflection? By boundary object, we mean artifacts that fulfill a “bridging function” (Akkerman & Bakker, 2011 p. 133) between intersecting practices and agents. We examine how the routines provide dialogical learning mechanisms of identification, coordination, reflection and transformation. Theoretical framing In learning, heterogeneity is fundamental. Heterogeneity is evident in what learners bring to classrooms, the pathways they pursue, and their learning goals (Rosebery et al., 2010). Valuing heterogeneity, is a key aspect of equity-oriented learning that fo sters cultural sustainability and resurgence (Alim et al., 2020; Paris, 2012). Successful groups, moreover, need to be able to value divergent thinking and perspectives, evident in how participants respond to one another’s ideas (Barron, 2003). However, there are cultural variations in models for collaboration that can shape how diverse groups of students function when working in groups (Gu et al., 2017; Ruvalcaba & Rogoff, 2022). As such, learning should value diversity of both ideas and modes of collaboration. In contrast, many AI algorithms are designed to reproduce patterns that they have already seen; thus, by default these tools will converge  towards a set of decisions that appear most commonly. This may seem counterintuitive in the age of “Generative AI” which has the guise of creativity, in some cases even win ing art prizes (Roose, 2023). However, despite these novel productions, AI still relies on training data to determine its generative components and thus reflect a convergence to the available training data. So, AI-supported collaboration tools have the same constraint; by default, the AI algorithm will converge towards the most well represented models of collaboration. Data that provides input into an AI model necessarily relies on patterns of past collaboration, potentially reproducing dominant models at the expense of less common, but potentially productive ones. Many scholars seek to address this problem of conformity and homogeneity by seeking to ensure that the use of models results in a fair distribution of benefits (e.g., Holmes et al., 2021), which in the case of collaboration can mean support that is equally valuable to students of different genders, racial educational systems in ways that may devalue forms of thinking and contributions of members of those same groups (Madaio et al., 2022).

Our work seeks to hold convergence and divergence, and thus homogeneity and heterogeneity, in “productive tension” (Pierson et al., 2023, p. 125). While AI systems designed to describe collaboration currently need to rely on existing data to converge on which moments of collaboration are more or less “successful”, other ideas and artifacts have the potential to mediate and support divergent thinking. For example, valuing equity and respect for heterogeneity of ideas can function as a key expectation for groups (Affolter et al., 2022). Further, we build in opportunities for students and AI to collaborate to promote divergent, versus convergent thinking (Simeone et al., 2022). Our approach recognizes that a classroom’s social context is shaped by larger patterns of inequity in society, and that these patterns give rise to status differences within groups (Langer-Osuna, 2015) Finally, it creates opportunities to improve prototypes by building opportunities for diverse groups of students to “speak back” to AI-generated feedback on collaboration, to ensure that data on which algorithms are trained are broad and heterogeneous (Celik & Muukkonen, 2022).

We contextualize this through a pedagogical approach called community agreements. Community agreements are a central focus of the collaborative AI agent interface; it was envisioned by youth and then designed to support students in co-negotiating and then working together to uphold shared ideas about how to collaborate in class (Breideband, 2024). Community agreements are also discussed in the context of a class contract or community norms, with the distinction that community agreements are co-negotiated by the class and then returned to throughout the year rather than imposed from the top down. Community agreements have affordances for supporting more caring classroom environments and facilitate “dialogic pedagogical values of listening to and honoring student voices and taking their ideas seriously” (Boyd, Vasquez and Monaco, 2023 p. 18). Community agreements are a core part of storylined curricula that connect student interests with community priorities and authentic questioning (Penuel et al., 2023) where attending to collective enterprise and care has been shown to increase student self-reported interest (Singleton et al. 2024). 909

## Participants, data and analysis

The research draws on a multi-year partnership with teachers in multiple suburban and rural U.S. districts. A set of routines, co-designed within a professional learning community focused on computational thinking –infused storylined units (Biddy et al., 2021), were developed to engage small groups of students in rich, collaborative activities (Prieto et al., 2011) that serve as the core “building blocks” of these curricular units, structuring student– AI interactions to promote collaboration. Teachers co-designed and iteratively refined routines centered on creating classroom community agreements, which were integrated into units to ensure coherent use  of the AI partner, CoBi. The co-design and subsequent classroom enactment of these routines informed the ongoing development of CoBi  to facilitate small-group collaboration and community-building discussions. We analyze both the revisiting curriculum routine and teacher interviews to explore how these routines mediate tensions between AI’s convergent tendencies and the affordances of divergent, equity-oriented thinking. Finally, we examine how teachers’ enactments with CoBi demonstrate the practical uptake of these affordances. After this co-design period of the routines (Bush et al., 2025) and AI-partner (Brideband et al., 2025), teachers used the CoBi  in their classrooms in conjunction with the routines. Ten  implemented CoBi during the 2024–2025 school year using one of two curriculum units (“Sensor Immersion” or “AI and Self-Driving Cars”) also co-designed with the research team. Data comes from analysis of  video recordings of classroom enactments of the curriculum routines. Using self-study methods (Pinnegar & Hamilton, 2009) and thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006), we identified moments when the routines mediated students’ meta-collaborative reflectio n, critical agency toward the AI tool’s convergent tendencies, and creative uses of the tool that—though off script— still aligned with design goals around agency and reflection. The Co-Designed Revisiting Community Agreement Routine At the end of individual lessons within the unit, students engage in the revisiting community agreements routine (see Figure 1). Students 1) reflect on agreements in action, celebrate successes, and engage with new ideas to uphold to support our learning; and 2) investigate and discuss the AI concepts used in the agent (See Figure 2). 910 The instructional routines for revisiting community agreements are presented as context for the deployment and study of the collaborative AI agent that it is intended to support. Yet, they exist and function independent of the AI technology and can serve as a routine in any classroom, regardless of AI-based or other classroom technology. They also are curriculum ambivalent, being easily adapted to multiple domains and learning contexts. This feature is intended to support both the classroom implementation and focus the developer vision in the context of a relatable and tangible design space.

AI Agent for Supporting Student Collaboration and Community Agreements The collaborative AI agent, CoBi, supports the enactment of the community agreement routines. Instances of student talk are recognized and automatically categorized into the four community agreement categories by the system. The system presents noticed agreements as flowers on a tree intended to display the data as a simulation and phenomenon, not an evaluation or score. In the curriculum routine, students  are encouraged to reflect on successes, create goals for future collaboration or critique the AI, discussing how it works and what could be improved by navigating through different tabs on the interface. This AI powered interface and the associated metaphor (AI as a Community Building Support) originated from a research team backcasting design from a series of participatory design workshops with youth who envisioned equitable AI futures (Chang et al., 2022). The revisiting tab  (Figure 2) provi des multiple options, visualizations, and supports for facilitating reflection and revisiting the community agreements with three sub-tabs (Celebrate, Notice & Wonder, and Think Critically), each with different visualizations for students to interact with. These tabs are designed as implicit scaffolds to reduce the amount of explicit instruction needed and guide the user through a series of increasingly complex interactions with the tool (P aul et al. 2013). The Celebrate tab shows the full-grown collaboration tree. The Notice & Wonder tab a radar chart representing the number of utterances in each category relative to other classrooms’ averages. The Think Critically tab shows the top three utterances from each category with the AI agent’s categorization confidence and the automated speech recognition confidence  presented as a percentage. Additionally, each of these tabs display student sentence stems for discussion that come from the  teacher guide for the curriculum routines.

Analyzing the curriculum routines as boundary objects to mediate design tensions During the revisiting routine, the AI engages in convergent analysis of student noticings. This part of the routine helps mediate the possible implications of decentering and devaluing diverse ideas by providing opportunities for critiquing the AI and its bias, omitting evaluative use s of the AI agent and having students discuss the prompt, “what was an example of one of these agreements that you heard but think maybe the AI did not hear?” This also mediates design tensions by allowing researchers to focus on novel categorizations of student talk while practitioners can integrate these routines into existing classroom practices. While many of the design components of these routines were present from the beginning, others emerged through iterative co-design with researchers, external evaluators, teachers and youth. To understand these contributions and how they helped the routines mediate design tensions, we compared earlier versions of the 911 routines to the current version in the curriculum that uses the AI powered interface deployed in classrooms. We found three central themes through this analysis.

First, setting up the AI’s logistics and intended design early in the routines helps position it to the students as non-evaluative yet innovative. This helps mediate the tension between surveillance and practicality as well as position the AI as a tool that will not always get things right and that students are expected to critique. Since the AI is positioned as a non-expert, the convergence of its algorithms are de-centered. Second, examples of the AI’s functioning in the context of short activities to review meta-collaboration (Agreement Reflection and Celebration, Engage with Noticings and New Ideas, Recommitting to the Culture We Want to Create in Figure 2) both make the AI powered feedback more accessible and let its innovations be used for supporting conversations about inclusive, just and accessible collaboration. Finally, critiquing the AI and its bias (Exploring “How does the collaborative AI work? in Figure 2) supports divergence and opens opportunities for youth who the AI is under-serving through bias in design or training data. For example, if the AI system did not accurately transcribe or categorize a student’s idea, the routine provides an opportunity for students to be critical of the convergent nature and homogeneous-leaning nature of the AI. By helping students look “under the hood”, the routines help minimize possible harm done by the convergent nature of the AI. Here, the routines position the students as the heterogeneous thinkers with AI as the phenomena of which to be critical. enactment of the curriculum routines Through multiple rounds of thematic researcher analysis, we identified four themes from the teacher data. ## Case study vignettes

Case vignette 1: AI partner as collaboration mirror During a revisiting routine, Ms. Tessa asked students to reflect, saying “So taking a look at our collaboration tree, what is something your group did well with today?”  Students responded by naming examples such as splitting up the work and taking turns with different parts, “my partner was wiring while I was coding”. These reflections were tentative and even ephemeral, easy to affirm in the moment but difficult to carry forward into the community’s ongoing work.

To extend this reflection, the teacher projected the three different CoBi  views (tree, radar and sentence cloud) on class collaboration to help students deepen their reflections and engage with the nature of their collaborative work. On the “think critically” page, they specifically looked at the confidence levels of the Automated Speech Recognition (ASR) and the category classifications, such as “the ASR shows it thinks it got 55% of the speech correct,”  and later, “it’s 64% sure that this sounds like it w as being respectful.”  The teacher framed the display as provisional: “it does kind of show what it thought and [how] accurate [it was],”  implicitly inviting student judgment. This positioning of the display as a “proto-judgement” allowed the students to think together about how and why there could be mistakes; positioning the AI’s classifications not as final judgments but as a dynamic artifact to be questioned.

Students quickly weighed in, questioning the AI’s decision-making. When the system labeled a segment as respectful, one student agreed with the teacher that the phrasing might not fit the category. Another exchange shows the teacher trying to surface categ ories for discussion: “What were the two areas that CoBi  noticed that groups did really well with?”  to which a student answered, “being respectful and committed to the community.” The teacher followed: “It recognized [fewer] things [where] we were moving o ur thinking forward.” Rather than passively accepting the AI’s read, class members compared the tool’s labels against their lived experience. The AI’s classifications and confidence scores acted as a shared referent, a “collaboration mirror” to inspect, contest, and reason about. The result was not immediate consensus but a negotiated sense of what their collaboration looked like and which norms mattered. The later conversation grounded the initial tentative reflections into an ongoing constructive dialog. The AI-generated display surfaced collaborative features that were previously ephemeral. Its presence catalyzed explicit, sustained conversation about what counts as productive group work, transforming momentary reflections into a collective inquiry. Case vignette2: Student ideas in the revisiting routine At the start of the routine, the teacher invited each table group to share how they had met their community agreements. Students responded with short but concrete examples: “giving each other turns,” “helping with errors,” “not letting one person do everything,” and “hyping each other up.” These contributions, though brief, anchored the conversation in the class’s own authored agreements. Each response was acknowledged by the teacher, who paraphrased and added them to the shared conversation, helping ground the class in the agreements. The routine then shifted to the views in the CoBi  interface. Students were asked not only to make observations about the display but also to evaluate “how do you think CoBi is doing?” This reframing positioned their earlier reflections as a reference point for critiquing the AI’s classifications. Students quickly began to spot 914 mismatches: one noted that the AI had categorized “no, yes” as respectful, which they stated, “doesn’t feel respectful.” Another explained that a misclassified phrase came from joking around. This layering of student-authored reflections and AI categorizations created a productive tension, but it also revealed limits in how deeply students engaged. Many of their initial examples, while meaningful, remained surface-level (“taking turns,” “helping with errors”), and the critiques of CoBi  were likewise concise. Still, the sequence suggests a promising opening: the AI artifact did not displace students’ earlier reflections but interacted with them. Their lived experiences of collaboration and the agreements they had authored served as a yardstick against which CoBi’s categorizations were tested.

The teacher’s orchestration was crucial: by first surfacing student accounts and then layering in the AI output, the routine elevated student ideas into a tool for critique. What might have remained ephemeral, brief phrases about “helping with errors” or “taking turns,” was given durability as students used those ideas to question, affirm, or reject the AI’s judgments. The moment points toward the potential for revisiting routines to support deeper engagement. With careful design, these routines could move beyond surface-level checks to richer negotiations of what counts as collaboration and how students want to define it together. Through iterative co-design with teachers and youth, we developed and studied a set of community agreement routines and an AI collaboration partner, CoBi, that together functioned as a boundary object linking design ideals and classroom realities. Our findings show that curriculum routines are not merely delivery mechanisms for technology but vi tal mediators that shape how teachers and students interpret, critique, and appropriate AI tools for meaningful and just learning. Across classroom enactments, teachers used the revisiting routines with high integrity and adapted them in creative ways that remained consistent with the design intent. These findings suggest that the routines anchored practice in stable pedagogical patterns while still allowing for local flexibility and innovation. Teachers also leveraged AI errors as productive learning moments, transforming misclassifications into opportunities for AI literacy and critical reflection. When teachers positioned CoBi as a “non-expert collaborator,” students engaged with the system’s imperfections to question its classifications, discuss bias, and surface their own collaborative values. Such enactments reframed the AI’s convergent analytic processes as shared phenomena to in vestigate rather than fixed judgments to accept.

At the same time, the findings reveal that fostering student critical agency toward AI remains an ongoing challenge. While students frequently engaged in reflective and community-oriented discussions, moments of deep critique about the system’s operations or broader social implications were comparatively rare. Achieving this deeper engagement may require expanding professional learning for teachers to include facilitation strategies that explicitly position AI as an object of collective inquiry. Moreover, r outine structures that balance stability with openness—such as prompts that foreground “thinking critically” about AI—appear to create the conditions for students to move beyond surface-level observations toward more sustained, divergent reasoning. Theoretically, this work contributes to understanding how curriculum routines can serve as boundary objects within the complex sociotechnical ecosystems of AI-supported classrooms. As stable yet flexible structures, routines link the epistemic aims of designers, teachers, and students, providing a shared frame through which tonegotiate meaning and practice. Practically, this insight highlights the importance of designing not only AI systems but also the curricular infrastructures that enable teachers and l earners to engage them productively. By embedding AI tools within recognizable pedagogical rhythms, teachers can better align technological affordances with equity-oriented goals and manage the orchestration challenges of classroom life. More broadly, this research underscores the need to hold convergence and divergence in productive tension within AI-mediated learning. Convergence allows AI to generate usable feedback and recognizable patterns; divergence ensures that the richness of human variability, creativity, and justice-oriented critique remains visible and valued. Curriculum routines provide the temporal and social scaffolds through which these dual aims can coexist. In doing so, they make possible a vision of AI-supported learning that amplifies human agency rather than automating it— a vision where teachers and students act as co-interpreters of data, co-designers of meaning, and co-creators of more equitable futures for learning.

## AI and ethics in the learning sciences: A literature review of the

Santiago Hurtado, Anna Keune s.hurtado@tum.de, anna.keune@tum.de Technical University of Munich Abstract: The rapid rise of artificial intelligence (AI) has increased research at the intersection of AI and education. AI technologies are rapidly embedded in everyday pra ctices, including educational contexts, which raises questions about their ethical implications. This study examined how the learning sciences field has researched AI ethics by systematically reviewing AI-related papers were identified, of which 53 focused on ethics. Findings reveal a recent surge in AI and AI ethics research. An analysis of 53 ethics papers found a tendency to use qualitative frame ethics through learning theories rather than established AI ethics frameworks, highlighting principles like bias, equity, and agency. We point to how the field’s research on AI ethics can inform policy and practice insights and to further research. The development and deployment of artificial intelligence (AI) have become ubiquitous since the commercial release of large language models (LLMs), rapidly embedding AI into everyday digital tools like search engines, social media, and smartphone assistants (OECD, 2023). This has raised concerns about its impact on assessments, homework, and learning outcomes in educational settings, as well as ethical concerns about its use (Holmes & Porayska-Pomsta, 2023).  AI tools provide access to a breadth of information through text, audio, and image outputs, and can generate large quantities of content with small inputs, which has motivated research on AI’s role in learning processes (Holmes & Porayska-Pomsta, 2023). As AI can quickly generate content and provide various rounds of iteration, its role as a co-creator is being considered (Keune et al., under review). While access to AI tools increases and their embeddedness in everyday contexts expands, this raises questions about their ethical implications, including issues of agency in learning (Jobin et al., 2019). Policy frameworks have been developed to address ethical concerns, such as the OECD AI principles (OECD, 2023), the UNESCO Recommendations on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence (Morandín -Ahuerma, 2023), and the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act (Edwards, 2021). However, frameworks fall short of accounting for the complexities of educational environments, as research exploring human-AI processes with LLMs grounded in empirical educational research is still emerging.

Nevertheless, research on AI and learning has a long-standing history within the learning sciences. AI was used to develop models of cognitive processes to learn about learning during the 70s and 80s at the MIT Artificial Intelligence Lab (today the MIT Media Lab), founded by Seymour Papert (Kahn & Winters, 2021). The learning sciences also investigated initial implementations of intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) as precursors for personalized learning opportunities during the following decades (Graesser e t al., 2019; Rau et al., 2009). More recently, learning sciences have investigated learning about machine learning and neural networks (Barnett et al., 2023; Tseng et al., 2021). Given this long-standing history with AI, we argue that the learning sciences community is uniquely positioned to provide pointed educational insights on AI ethics. Therefore, we conducted a systematic 1995-2025, analyzing 7652 papers. We aimed to identify the volume of research on AI and AI ethics, which specific ethical principles are present, how the learning sciences frame discussions of AI ethics principles, and the insights gained. This process identified 53 papers focused specifically on AI ethics. Our analysis revealed an increase in AI submissions over the years and the grounding of ethics in learning theories and concepts. The most prominent research relates to bias, equity, and agency. We point to implications for policy and practice from these insights as well as to opportunities for further research. Research on AI and education, within the learning sciences field, did not begin with the release of LLMs. The field started investigating AI as a way to model cognitive processes and to learn about learning (Doroudi, 2022, 2023; Schank, 1987). These initial approaches did not develop AI as  we know it today, but engaged with AI as a tool for learners, for example, to program, to create artifacts, and to reflect on learning processes (Kahn & Winters, 918 2021). The field continued to slowly engage with AI in different ways, for example, as ITS or pedagogical agents for personalized learning opportunities (Rau et al., 2009). Although ITS were not explicitly called AI, their functionality laid the foundation for many of the features we see in AI today. ITS systems emulated one-on-one educator interactions, personalized instruction, immediate feedback, and learning pathways for learners (Rau et al., 2009). While the interfaces did not look like those we see today, they could track learner progress (e.g., while solving an equation) and offer hints or highlight  mistakes in the learner's approach (Graesser et al., 2019). Other approaches included classroom orchestration tools  that aimed to support teachers in their multitasking  roles, for example, by coordinating digital resources and collaborative student work (Dillenbourg et al., 2019). As computer technology and processing power further developed, AI as we know it today began to take shape, and machine learning technologies became available with early versions of chatbots and  image recognition tools (Kahn & Winters, 2021). These technological developments also powered learning sciences research in computer science fields that engaged learners with computational concepts related to machine learning, for example, by building neural networks, speech and image recognition tools, classification tools, and how to embed them in programming projects in coding websites like Scratch (see Tseng et al., 2021). Now, all of these features are easily accessible with LLMs that combine multimodal inputs and outputs, which are now impacting  research within the learning sciences and CSCL.

The current embeddedness of the digital in everyday life (e.g., smartphones, computers, social media, media consumption) has made the rise of AI surprisingly fast in both implementation and adoption (OECD, 2023). Since the release of LLMs, most, if not all, digital spaces now feature AI functionalities such as chatbots for customer service or smartphone assistants. Anyone with a smartphone or computer will likely interact or be prompted to interact with AI, including children and young people. Thus, the rise of AI has impacted educational settings, as young people can easily access AI and engage with it to  support their learning processes. This raised ethical concerns about how young people’s interactions with AI affect their learning processes, for example, regarding their agency or the fairness of their use of  AI (Holmes & Porayska-Pomsta, 2023). Educational stakeholders have expressed concern that learners’ learning processes are being taken over by AI, enabling learners to answer complex questions in a ma tter of seconds (Mouta et al., 2025). Moreover, AI systems rapidly evolve alongside policies meant to regulate them, while remaining largely opaque  to users (Holmes & PorayskaPomsta, 2023). Several frameworks and policy documents have been developed that address a range of ethical principles to guide the design of ethical AI. One of the first and most prominent frameworks was first published in 2019 by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) which developed five principles: (1) inclusive growth, sustainable development and well-being, (2) human rights and democratic values, including fairness and privacy, (3) transparency and explainability, (4) robustness, security and safety, and (5) accountability (OCED, 2023). The principles cover a wide range of issues related to AI and adopt a human-centered approach that argues for AI systems to safeguard human rights and democratic values (OECD, 2023). The OECD AI principles were updated in 2024 to address new technological developments (OECD, 2023). Several frameworks and policy documents have continued to develop, such as UNESCO’s Recommendation on the Ethics of AI and the EU AI Act, which aim to address the risks and benefits  of AI implementations across different systems (Edwards, 2021; Morandín- Ahuerma, 2023). Other approaches focus on specific principles, such as responsible AI (RAI) or explainable AI (XAI), that value AI systems that demonstrate consideration for stakeholder accountability and the tracing of outcomes (Jobin et al., 2019). As AI systems become embedded in many aspects of everyday life, AI ethics frameworks aim to be generalizable across a wide range of contexts (e.g., medical, government). Currently, AI ethics frameworks that address the complexities of educational environments remain underdeveloped, lacking guidance on how AI ethics should be integrated into education. One way to address this has been through AI literacy. AI literacy argues for supporting educators and learners in critically evaluating whether and how to use AI. In some cases, AI literacy involves learning about AI ethics; for instance, learning about AI’s inner workings can help understand bias-related ethics (see Mills et al., 2024). In the present  study, we focus on AI ethics to investigate which specific ethical principles and concerns AI ethics as supportive of AI literacy, as it involves reflecting on various aspects of AI systems, including technical, social, and educational, and can provide learners with knowledge on whether and how to use AI critically (Keune et al., under review). the Learning Sciences Annual Meeting from 1995 to 2025, following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) methodology (Page et al., 2021). The final data set analyzed included 53 studies, of which nine were symposia. Each symposium included several contributions, totaling 46 studies, so for the codes of research design, participants, and learning environments, we coded each contribution as a separate study. After accounting for symposia's individual contributions, we coded 90 separate studies. Because some of those studies did not include participants, we removed them from the participant analysis, leaving 73 studies.

Research approach. The methodological landscape of ISLS submissions related to AI ethics is mostly qualitative, represented by 53 studies (58%), including case studies and design-based approaches. Case studies and design-based research (DBR) were the most common methodologic al approaches, with 8 (9%) and 7 (8%) studies, respectively, highlighting the field's preference for investigating this topic in authentic s ettings. For example, Yu et al. (2025) employed a DBR approach to co-design AI knowledge assessments with middle school teachers and highlighted the need for equitable (AI) assessments and understanding of accountability of manufacturers and lawmakers. Mixed-methods approaches (e.g., quantitative surveys  with interview analysis) comprised 19 studies (21%). Theoretical, review, or conceptual approaches comprised 14 studies (16%). Finally, five were quantitative (6%). The methodological preference indicates a field-wide approach toward understanding AI ethics in specific social and educational contexts.

Learning environment. Research on AI ethics in the learning sciences was predominantly conducted in school and university educational settings, with 47 (52%) studies conducted in K-12 and higher education contexts. There is also a strong focus on out-of-school learning, with 21 studies (23%) conducted in settings such as summer camps, museums, and community programs. For instance, Zhou et al. (2025) call for  communitycentered approaches to ensure that the  design and ethical evaluation of AI  are rooted in local contexts and concerns. A community and society focus looks to situate AI ethics within the specific learning contexts it can impact, often considering co-design and evaluation of AI. The distribution of the learning environments shows the field’s range of research in AI ethics, not just in theory, but in authentic, lived experiences of learners across diverse environments.

Participants. Of the 73 studies with participants, most (56, 76%) included learners and highlighted the field’s concern for empowering young people in their learning processes while expressing the need to support and protect them as a vulnerable population amid the unknown consequences of emerging technologies. A portion of the 73 studies focused on identifying and mitigating the risks of AI for learners, such as algorithmic bias, negative stereotypes, and impacts on marginalized youth. Another portion of the studies positioned learners as active and critical agents capable of designing and shaping AI technologies. On the other hand, 11 (15%) studies involved educators and positioned them as mediators of learning processes rather than merely  implementers of these technologies. This research showed educators concern for AI’s potential to affect teacher skills and reduce teacher autonomy. Research from these studies explored how AI could augment teacher tasks to support learning (Choi & Han, 2025; Yin et al., 2025).

Guiding theories and frameworks for AI ethics The analysis revealed that although all the studies engaged with AI ethics, they  did not always mention or apply established ethics frameworks from policy, industry, or philosophy. The most prevalent approaches to tackling ethics were those derived from learning theories. For these studies, an AI intervention that considered ethics was related to supporting learning environments (e.g., collaborative, equitable, agentic, or inquiry-based). The ethical framework was then linked to learning outcomes rather than to dedicated frameworks. For instance, Famaye et al

(2023) grounded their design of a machine learning program for elementary and middle school children in the

framework of critical constructionism to blend learning with a critical, justice-oriented approach. In their study, ethical machine learning education was grounded in the  principles of critical constructionism, in which learners connected their lived experiences and reflections on their socio-political contexts to design toward more equitable futures. This example highlights the approach of most studies that address ethics, which frame ethical principles within learning theories, frameworks, or approaches.

Only six studies (11%) explicitly cited or grounded their work in such frameworks (e.g., OECD AI Principles or Responsible AI principles). Some studies engaged directly with established ethics principles, such as Wang et al. (2025), which used moral foundations theory and critical systems heuristics in a role-playing game 922 of AI-controlled killer robots to facilitate understanding of complex ethical issues. Similarly, Yin et al. (2025) used a set of Responsible AI principles as survey items to investigate teachers’ values regarding AI ethics. Here, principles like fairness and autonomy presented values that shifted in importance depending on teachers’ pedagogical beliefs and classroom contexts. The study used a framework to conceptualize the ethical principles to be analyzed, providing an analytical lens that argued for the design of AI systems that incorporate a wide range of pedagogical approaches.

A third approach to investigating ethical issues related to AI in education was to develop field-specific frameworks. Recognizing that frameworks were not developed for specific contexts that require considerations of processes of stakeholders, such as w ith learners and educators in educational contexts. One example is the study by Coelho et al. (2024), which developed a design space for articulating AI risks in educational contexts and sought to create a systematic approach for the research community to debate and justify ethical issues in  AI. Another example is from Raj et al. (2025), who proposed a Source, Medium, Agency, and Transformation (SMART) framework as a critical approach to AI’s implications as an educational technology. They critiqued existing frameworks for assessing educational technologies or AI literacy frameworks, which, according to them, fall short in mapping and addressing socio-technical aspects, and sought to develop a framework that accounts for just that. Thematic landscape of ethics principles The analysis of the dataset identified which ethical principles and concepts ISLS research has thematized to date. Across the board, most studies named ethical issues under investigation but did not consider them ethics principles within particular ethics frameworks. To provide consistency, we call them  principles, but provide examples and definitions grounded in the research of the studies. Definitions and interpretations varied across studies, especially because the ethics principles under investigation were not related to specific frameworks. Overall, the ethical principles within ISLS research were framed as  contextually intertwined with the socio-technical systems in each research. The most common principles we identified were bias, equity, agency, transparency, fairness, accountability, and privacy. Across the 53 studies analyzed, these most common principles also occurred. The average number of ethics principles per study was 2.4. For two-principle co-occurrence, the principles that co-occurred the most were bias-equity (n=23, 43%), bias-fairness (n= 13, 25%), agency-equity (n=13, 25%), and equity-fairness (n=12, 23%). For three-principle co-occurrence, the most common ones were bias-equity-agency (n=10, 19%), bias-equity-fairness (n=9, 17%), and bias-equity-transparency (n=9, 17%). Bias in data sets with a focus on socio-historical justice.  Bias was the most common ethics principle, appearing in 31 studies (58%). Within these studies, bias was treated as more than a technical problem of skewed data sets. The research considered it as  a socio-technical and socio-historical issue. For instance, the study by McClure et al. (2024) taught data bias in high school history classrooms as students built machine learning models using primary source documents. The study connected the technical concept of biased data to concrete historical events of injustice, framing it as a historical issue with long-standing societal implications of inequity. The research fostered an understanding of implications beyond digital systems that contribute to biased data. Another example of bias connected to societal impact was the case study by Edouard (2024), which presented the analysis of  racial bias in image generative AI that hindered a black undergraduate game designer ’s creative processes.

The study explored how image generative tools’ lack of representation of cultural diversity can limit how learners use these tools. Overall, ISLS research identified how bias in  AI tools could impact representation and reinforce stereotypes in a range of contexts.

AI equity as access to and representation in design and systems.  Another ethics principle frequently discussed was AI equity in terms of access and representation, present in 29 studies (55%). AI equity was not always the central focus of the studies we analyzed, yet it was often mentioned as an important consideration when addressing AI in education. AI’s impact in education often intersected with how it might, positively or negatively, affect equity in relation to educational access or representation. The studies that mentioned AI equity often worked toward equitable outcomes for stakeholders involved in AI use. For instance, Mawasi et al. (2022) examined a co-design process with historically underrepresented youth in STEM to create an AI unit. The study’s ethical commitment to AI equity was achieved through disrupting power dynamics and centering youth expertise in the design process. In this study, equitable AI was designed with and by the communities it served. AI equity was enacted in the process and carried forward through the design outcome. Another example was  Kim et al.’s

(2024) study of teachers’ conceptualizations of AI and its impact on  inequity. The research  highlighted the

importance of considering AI’s impact on equity and how teachers can contribute to the design of more equitable AI. Overall, equity was a major theme  in the analyzed studies, highlighting the field's focus and contributions to knowledge about the impact of emerging educational technologies, such as AI, on topics like accessibility, justice, and representation.

923 Agency and autonomy in AI for student-centered learning and critical design. Agency and autonomy were addressed in 20 studies (38%), generating insights into how AI in educational technologies could impact learner and educator agency and autonomy. Several studies investigated how AI systems could constrain or support decision-making. For example, Cheng et al. (2025) evaluated AI-generated lesson plans against a student agency framework and found that AI tools tended to produce teacher-centric plans that limit student choice. The research highlighted how AI-powered approaches could impact learner agency and decision-making. Moreover, research on agency and autonomy contributed to understanding how AI can best support learners in remaining central to their learning process. For example, Ojeda-Ramírez et al.’s (2025) research on AI for speculative design found that AI supported critical design processes that could prepare for future complex challenges. Overall, studies investigating agency focused on designing environments that supported teacher and learner agency and autonomy when using AI. Additionally, some research mentioned agency and autonomy as concerns in working with AI tools, but did not necessarily engage with the principles in their studies. This mirrored our findings on other principles, suggesting that agency and autonomy are emerging ethical aspects of AI that the learning sciences can further explore.

Transparency and explainability to foster accurate mental models. Transparency and explainability, present in 16 studies (30%), were contextualized as a need for sense-making of system logics to build accurate mental models and to establish appropriate levels of trust to use the tools effectively. We combined the two ethical principles because they are closely connected. Across the board, the studies discussed the lack of transparency in AI, referencing the black-box metaphor. Thus, studies that focused on transparency called for AI to be designed with greater transparency built in. For example, Harbarth et al. (2023) investigated transparency  by testing how the elaborateness of explanations provided by AI could affect trust and perceptions of the system. They found that increased perception of the quality of AI explanations affected learners' trust and understanding of AI systems. Additionally, Solyst et al. (2024) highlighted the transparency issue and argued that learners initially overtrust AI because of inaccurate depictions of how AI works. They argued that some AI tools provide information about their reasoning in the responses they generate, presenting it as their thinking process or the technical steps followed to generate those  responses. The research shows that these explanations fostered  participants’ trust in AI. However, AI typically misrepresented how it actually worked, leading to overtrust because the AI's transparency was fake. Overall, transparency and explainability were ethical issues of important concern across the  analyzed research and highlighted insights into how to foster AI explainability and transparency to uphold high-quality learning.

AI fairness AI for assessments and academic integrity. and Fairness was discussed in 15 (28%) studies and was conceptualized in two ways. First, fairness  was discussed in relation to how it intersected with other ethical concerns, such as equity in evaluation and assessment. For example, Yu et al. ’s (2025) research on codesigned AI assessments with teachers, produced equitable assessments that provided learners with fair opportunities. This example highlights fairness as it relat es to the use of AI for assessment. Second, fairness was framed as an issue of classroom rules and academic integrity. For instance, Jung et al. (2025) explored how English language  learners us ed AI and expressed concerns over plagiarism and over-reliance. Fairness was discussed by questioning whether it was fair for one learner to use AI while others did not. The two ways in which fairness was discussed showcase how the field defines and approaches fairness as an ethical principle of AI research, exemplifying that ethical principles might be addressed in various ways on a case-by-case basis. Accountability as understanding responsible stakeholders. Accountability was discussed in 10 studies (19%), and addressed questions related to who is responsible for AI systems failing, producing biased outputs, or causing harm. As highlighted by previous ethical principles, AI systems are often untransparent, and their impact on learning environments is still under explored. This also affects reflections on who is responsible for these impacts. For example, Wang et al. (2025) developed a table-top role-playing game to engage participants in ethical dilemmas of using killer robots. The design of the study asked participants to consider the perspectives of different stakeholders involved within this dilemma (i.e., military, politicians, algorithm developers) and their roles in deploying and using such AI systems to trace responsibility. Overall, accountability calls for conside ration of stakeholders involved in using AI systems.

Data privacy in AI as a topic of teacher training. Finally, privacy was present in 8 studies  (15%) and was often related to concerns about  learner data. For instance, Diederich and Nguyen (2024) explored the use of ChatGPT to facilitate data conversations among graduate students and problematized privacy issues related to conversational data when using commercial AI to process  it. Research by Choi and Han (2025) and Wu et al.

(2025) resonated with this by arguing that teachers’ concerns about privacy also posed barriers to adoption and

emphasized the need for more teacher training with AI toward  weighing data privacy issues to foster  decisionmaking about whether and how to use AI in their settings. Overall, privacy was mentioned across studies but was 924 not necessarily central to the research but rather acknowledged as a risk or as a consideration that needed to be addressed when considering research and implementation of AI tools in educational contexts. This systematic literature review aimed to understand the insights about AI ethics in education that  the learning two distinct moments. First, a constant but small engagement with AI concepts such as ITS and machine learning (1-3% of submissions pre-2021). Second, a surge in research corresponding with the public release of generative AI (up to 43% of submissions in 2025). This shows that the field has responded to current, impactful technological shifts, with increased interest in contributing core insights into AI in education. A key finding of this study is the community’s tendency to define ethics in terms of learning theories rather than AI frameworks (e.g., the OECD AI principles). Learning scientists and CSCL researchers often frame learning as fundamentally relational and socially situated. The learning theories employed can include ethical principles, albeit not necessarily explicitly. Most studies  drew on learning theories to examine ethics from a learning perspective, such as critical constructionism (Famaye et al., 2023) and situated learning (Choi & Han, 2025), and questioned what these  imply for the ethical design of AI. This highlights how the ethics approach to investigating AI is rooted in some of the most present ethical concerns already identified in learning sciences approaches. This aligns with the field’s commitment to context, learning, and social justice. This is supported by our thematic analysis of AI ethics, which found that bias, equity, and agency were the most represented principles. Moreover, the strong co-occurrence of bias-equity and bias-agency indicates the field views these as not isolated technical problems, but as socio-technical issues impacting learners and social justice (see Edouard, 2024; McClure et al., 2024).

The methodological landscape and the participant focus  reinforce this context-driven approach. The prevalence of case studies and design-based research (DBR) shows the field is investigating AI ethics in context, highlighting the lived experiences of learners and educators in authentic settings. These approac hes prioritize understanding how learners and educators perceive and are impacted by AI. This is seen in studies exploring educator autonomy (Choi & Han, 2025) and learner agency (Mawasi et al., 2022).  The strong qualitative prominence is also explained by the field's recent shift into AI and AI ethics, as the field is in the early stages of understanding how educational contexts are impacted by the surge of AI, how people learn with AI, and how what people can learn shifts, which ethical issues are involved, and how to learn about these  issues (alongside other outcomes).

Our findings suggest the learning sciences community is uniquely positioned to contribute a humancentered perspective to the broader AI ethics discourse. However, the current tendency of addressing ethics primarily through learning theory may impact its contributions to wider policy and computer science conversations that rely on a different language. Therefore, learning sciences research can align its AI ethics studies with learning theories  and ethics frameworks to strategically consolidate research fin dings toward robust and strong informing of policy and practice around AI in education. For instance, ISLS research emphasizes how AI bias impacts representation, marking it as a highly consequential factor in learning processes. Furthermore, prioritizing AI equity, specifically in access and representation, generates valuable process knowledge for designing AI ethics-aligned systems through meaningful stakeholder co-creation. The research also highlights the importance of transparency and explainability fe atures to increase learners' and teachers' accurate understanding of AI as a means to maintain human agency and autonomy. However, the research also highlights how such features can increase young people’s trust in AI, underscoring the responsibility of AI  system designers in education to present technically accurate explanations and transparency. Furthermore, the research also calls to consider fairness in AI systems, especially related to the use of AI for assessment for young people’s learning and to consider stakeholders involved in using AI systems to increase understanding of accountability. Finally, the field highlights the need to consider data privacy in AI as a topic in educator training because privacy concerns can be a catalyst for or against the use of AI in education. Ultimately, the field should continue developing education-specific AI ethics frameworks that integrate its rich learning theory with the ethical standards utilized in policy and industry.

Further research into the contributions of learning sciences and CSCL research to AI ethics in education should cast a wider net into other AI education outlets for a more comprehensive map of the landscape. Moreover, using a deductive approach  to AI ethics principles  that accounts for more principles such as sustainability and creativity, could identify further gaps and future directions for ISLS research.
