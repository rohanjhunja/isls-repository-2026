# Computational Thinking Integration as Trojan Horse for Teachers’ Rethinking Science and Mathematics Teaching and Learning

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Mathematics and science teacher educators have worked to help current and pre-service teachers understand “computational thinking” (CT) and integrate it into their lessons. This structured poster session highlights an unexpected phenomenon we have observed across multiple contexts: teacher education efforts aimed at CT integration didn’t achieve their stated goals but somehow led teachers to rethink what counts as good mathematics/science inquiry and learning. We don’t have a well-supported explanation for this phenomenon. The session aims to bring together scholars who study CT, CT integration, and STEM teacher education—the presenters and the session attendees—to explore the nature of this unexpected phenomena and brainstorm potential explanations to explore in future research.

Symposium summary As part of efforts to engage K-12 students in “computational thinking” (CT) in sci ence and mathematics classes, teacher educators have worked to help current and pre-service teachers understand CT—roughly, ways of thinking and problem-solving aligned with those of computer scientists —and integrate CT into their lessons. Other work explores the efficacy of these. By contrast, this s tructured poster session highlights an unexpected p henomenon we ha ve observed across multiple professional learning c ontexts aimed at CT integration:  teacher education efforts aimed at CT integration didn’t achieve their stated goals but somehow led teachers to rethink what counts as good mathematics/science inquiry and learning.

To be clear, we don’t have a happy story to tell about how teachers came to understand CT, integrate CT into their own and their students’ disciplinary inq uiry, and thereby deepened their understandings of their home disciplines and shifted their teaching practices ac cordingly. For the most part, we didn’t observe tea chers to understand CT or take up CT integration in the ways  we intended. Rather, we observed that teacher educ ators’ focus on CT and CT integration led teachers to shif t toward deeper engageme nt in mathematics/science disciplinary practices in their own mathematics/sci ence inquiry (during the professional development) and/or in their lesson plans for their students. These shifts  included exploring multiple possible solution path s, giving students more choice over how to approach a task or probl em, engaging students in more collaborative problem solving, noticing more conceptual as opposed to just procedural aspects of students’ thinking, and trusting students to take more epistemic agency over their engagement  in difficult tasks. We observed these shifts acros s teacher education settings that differed in structure (summ er “camp” vs. ongoing teacher professional developm ent vs. university courses), discipline (mathematics vs. science vs. both), grade level, and teacher experience (pre-service vs. current teachers).

We don’t have a grand unified theory about how these shifts occurred, though in our final poster we pose some possible socio-cognitive mechanisms at play. Do they arise from specific affordances of CT? From introducing teachers to interesting new perspective s about thinking and problem solving —CT or otherwise? We don’t know. Indeed, this session’s objective is bri nging together scholars who study CT integration and STEM teacher education in order to explore the nature of this unexpected p henomena and brains torm potential explanations to explore in future research.

The affordances (intended or otherwise!) of teacher education targeting CT are worth exploring because CT infusion into K-12 curriculum and instruction remains an active, in tellectually rich area of research and development. The unexpected affordances centered in  this session are interesting and generative for le arning scientists wishing to build theory about teacher le arning. They’re also important from a practical per spective; increased understanding of these affordances could help teacher educators inten tionally leverage —rather than accidentally stumble upon! —the learning pathways by which introducing teachers  to CT can help them rethink their home disciplines.

Poster 1: Planning for CT integration led a pre-service teacher to emphasize collaborative engagement in mathematical practices Peter Moon and Janet Walkoe The purpose of this study, undertaken within a pre-service mathematics & science teaching methods cour se that included computational thinking (CT)  framed as practice s useful when problem-solving in computational environments but also in other contexts, was to see what elements pre-service teachers view as important to include in a CT-integrated lesson plan.

## Methods and Perspectives

We conducted three clinical interviews with Georgia, a college senior enrolled in an undergradu ate teacher preparation program in middle school mathematics. S he was teaching eighth grade mathematics as her ful l-time internship. Georgia had previously participated in the course’s five-lesson module on CT in a teaching methods course (Moon et al., 2023).

In the semester following this course, Georgia met with us to plan, execute, and debrief a lesson plan modified to include CT. We invited Georgia to choose a lesson suitable for a CT integration and to decide how to integrate CT, as we thought leaving these choices to her would draw out her conceptions of CT (Fereday & MuirCochrane, 2006; Gibbs, 2007). Inductive coding (Tho mas, 2003) was used to preserve Georgia’s original intentions as much as possible without imposing existing CT frameworks.

## Results

Three major themes emerged, two of which were Doing  CT as a Teacher, associated with planning and lead ing the class through a CT activity; and Doing CT as a Learner, associated with Georgia’s own experience l earning about CT. Poster 2: Attention to computational thinking supports pre-service Mathematics teachers’ noticing of students’ mathematical thinking Margaret Walton and Janet Walkoe Teacher noticing of student thinking is an important skill for teachers, as the moments they see and take up in the classroom have implications for student learning (S herin et al, 2011). Preservice math teachers often have difficulty focusing on student’s conceptual reasoni ng, in algebra, in particular (Stephens, 2008). Pri or work has explored ways to help teachers recognize students engaged in algebraic thinking that goes beyond procedures and symbol manipulations (Walkoe, 2015).

In this study, we introduced computational thinking (CT)—conceptualized as practices such as decomposition and algorithmic thinking—in a middle school math and science teaching methods course with the goal of helping pre-service teachers (called “teachers” from here forward) integrate CT into their domains. We aimed to see what a prompt to attend to “CT” would help them notice.

## Theoretical perspective, methods and data sources

Fourteen PSTs in a middle school math and science m ethods course were asked to watch and tag a video o n an online platform (www.anotemos.com). The 5-minute video clip showed two students programming a Sphero robot on an iPad to complete a measurement task.  We asked the PSTs to tag moments of interesting stu dent thinking and to first identify the type of thinking the student was engaged in as (a) mathematical, (b) computational, or (c) both; and second, explain why they thought the moment the y tagged was an interesting moment of student thinking. The design of this experiment is grounded  in the constructs of “professional vision” (Goodwin, 1 995) and selective attention, the idea that members of a profession come to “see” things in disciplined, learned ways— to pick out elements of their professional environm ent from the myriad of cues availab le and interpret them in certain ways. Understanding pre-service teachers’ emerging professional vision can enable teacher educators to help develop their professional vision.

## Analysis and results

We logged whether the teacher was attending to math ematical thinking, computational thinking, or both.  To explore the way teachers discussed the moments they  noticed, we coded the teachers’ comments and looke d for themes. We found three main types of thinking descr ibed: procedural math, conceptual math, or no math (when teachers talked about programming only).

Teachers tended to notice more moments of students’  procedural thinking when asked to look for students engaged in mathematical thinking. However, when teachers attended to CT or to both CT and math, they tagged no instances of procedural math thinking, and in over half of the noticed instances, students were engaged in conceptual math thinking, even though the tags w ere marked CT or both math and CT by the teachers. Poster 3: Computational thinking integration supports teachers’ making progress amidst uncertainty in self-directed science inquiries Christina Krist, Erin Sohr, and Andrew Elby We examine how elementary school math and science t eachers used “unplugged” computational thinking (CT) (e.g., with no programming or computational medium)  in their adult-level science inquiry during a summer professional development (PD) workshop. We aim to illustrate how teachers’ attempts to integrate C T in their reasoning enhanced the scientific quality of their inquiry, despite the relatively limited version of CT—roughly, conditional logic —that they integrated. Our analysis draws on sociocu ltural theories of learning as situated in practice (Greeno, 1997; Lave & Wenger, 1991) and therefore takes a bottom-up approach to dissecting the nature and in-the-moment purpose of how teachers were collectively ut ilizing CT, framed roughly as ways of thinking central to computer programming but useful in unplugged problem-solving contexts, too.

## Methods

Video data came from a two-week summer PD workshop for teachers in an urbandistrict in the mid-Atlantic US. The PD goal was to introduce teachers to computational thinking and its integration into science inquiry by having them participate in science inquiry themselves and organically identify opportunities for CT integration. We drew on tools from interaction analysis (Jordan & Hender son, 1995) to make visible the disciplinary functio ns of the teachers’ interactions while engaged in these inqui ries. Here, we analyze a science inquiry in which the teachers Lynn, Travis, and Amy explored how and why blind snakes were found in screech owls’ nests in Texas (Gehlbach & Baldridge, 1987).

## Results

The Owls & Snakes inquiry required teachers to grap ple with a complex biological phenomenon, which mea nt teachers’ explorations were riddled with uncertaint y, sometimes to the point of blocking progress. Ear ly in the investigation, a facilitator noticed th at the teachers were stuck but that they had begun to divide up the situation into three distinct “phases,” each driven by a ques tion with multiple possible explanations. The facil itator noted that this move seemed productive and labeled it “pr oblem decomposition,” a CT term the group had learn ed the previous week. In response, the group decided to, i n their words, “do some computational thinking,” and began mapping out decision trees for each phase using the  CT-inspired notion of conditional logic (if/then  statements) to work through the relationships between decision points. This seemed to give the group permission to  explore explanations that they were not certain about and h elped them track the logical relationships between these possibilities. Supported by this structuring move, the group built out several possible scenarios and then generated lots of targeted questions related to both the nest lings’ and snakes’ survival. In short, though the “ CT” never deepened beyond conditional  logic, it supported the teachers’ engagement in exp loring multiple possible explanations about a scenario in which the “correct” explanation was very underdetermined by the available data.

## Significance

From this case study, we argue that (1) explicit, i ntentional use of what the teachers called “computa tional thinking” can benefit learners’ science inquiry, even if that CT wouldn’t be viewed as “deep” by CT experts; and

(2) exploring how teachers take up “CT” and incorporate it into their science inquiry is worthwhile for researchers,

instead of simply evaluating whether teachers “get” CT in the ways the facilitators intended. Poster 4: Teachers’ enthusiasm for computation thinking integration led to reconsideration of science inquiry Christina Krist and Andrew Elby Drawing on framing as a sensitizing concept (Goffma n, 1975), we examine how  elementary school math and science teachers initially deprioritized professional development (PD) framed as deepening the use of sci entific practices such as argumentation and  explanation-building in their “inquiry” lessons, but embraced P D framed as helping them add  “unplugged” computational thinking (CT) (e.g., with  no programming or computational medium) into their  lessons. We examine how PD focused on  CT, introduced as ways of thinking central to computer programming such as problem decomposition and “parallel processing” of the resulting subprobl ems, affected their mathematics and science teaching.

## Methods

Data collection occurred during academic-year professional learning community (PLC) meetings  at “Parkside Elementary,” an arts magnet school in a mid-Atlantic US urbandistrict. During an initial workshop, we introduced the goals of the project as (1) supporting inquiry-style learning in math and science, and (2) thinkin g creatively about how “unplugged” CT might be integrated towards those inquiry goals. Prior to each PLC meeting, we (the authors were the PD facilitators) drafted an agenda, marked up with comments from each other. Meetings were videorecorded, and we wrote reflective debrief notes afterward. Analytically, in the written artifacts, we identifi ed instances addressing CT and/or inquiry as PD goa ls. We then watched the PLC session videorecordings to identify how these conflicting sets of goals and as sociated framings of activity were made visible.

## Results

Initially, different teachers expressed different visions of desirable inquiry, including d ifferentiated labs, take-home labs, science “centers” (lab stations) in class, and “kids coming up with questions and exploring  hand-on.” This divergence, along with the backgrounding of disc ourse-based science practices in most of the visions, lef t us, as facilitators, feeling uncertain about how to best move forward  to pursue our inquiry goals. In contrast, teachers expressed multiple goals around (broadly) integrating technology, CT, and coding. Together, these goals communicated a shared sense of enthusiasm toward CT.

In response, we spent the next PLC meeting doing a CT-focused activity, which was well-received. However, we failed to connect the activity to math and science instruction. In our debrief, we wrestled with how to bring “inquiry” back into the conversation. We u ltimately settled on a strategy repeated throughout  the fall PLCs: show a  classroom illustration (video, vignette) of science inquiry c entered around argumentation and explanation-building, and ask teachers to identify the “unplugg ed” CT they saw. The se illustrations could, we noted in the planning document, “try to highlight the role of CT in talk and ideas, not just organizing activity.” In these sessions, we also asked teachers to identi fy upcoming lessons where a strategy similar to one they had observed (either CT or “inquiry” -focused) might enhance their lesson. Ultimately, ov er the year, to varying degrees, all 6 regularly participating teac hers adapted or modified a lesson to include some e lement of discourse-based inquiry.

## Significance

Although, teachers’ divergent senses of “inquiry” in math and science and enthusiasm to focus on CT rather than math/science inquiry led us to frame our PD as centered on CT integration, their enthusiasm for CT created an opening to work with teachers to introduce more discourse-based inquiry (argumentation, explanation-building) into their instruction.

Poster 5: “I was amazed with the projects”:  Computational thinking integration as a window into student abilities Melissa S. Gresalfi This presentation explores how co-designing for hybrid math and computer science lear ning experiences influenced the ways that teachers came to see new a nd different mathematical possibilities for their s tudents. In the context of rushing through prescribed curricula  and preparing students for testing, it is easy for  teachers to focus primarily on students' mastery of the targete d idea for the day, making it almost impossible not  to fall into deficit thinking (Adiredja & Louie, 2020; Jackson et al., 2017). By contrast, when given opportunities to explore students’ mathematical thinking, they can shift towards an asset-orientation (Bannister, 2015). An interactional view of activity that couples person with context, consistent with a situative perspective on learning (Greeno, 1998; Hand & Gresalfi, 2015; Lave & Wenger, 1991) pushes us to ask not “why are teachers deficit oriented” but instead “what prompts teacher s to focus on student thinking in terms of what the y know (or don’t know)?” Our analysis takes this situative per spective as a starting point to explore a shift in a group of teachers’ perspective on students’ capabilities.

## Methods

Data comes from teachers’ reflections on their expe riences in a free summer camp called Code your Art.  Partner middle-school teachers (n=4) were invited first for a week of professional development focused on programming in NetLogo and co-designing lessons and activities on Coding, Art, and Math. The next week, participating teachers taught groups of 16-18 students. Because teachers recrui ted at their own schools, many campers were students of our partner teachers.  We analyze the full-day reflection session with teachers that took place one week after camp.

## Results

Our analysis suggests that observing students do so mething new and different —something difficult for the teachers themselves—offered an important opportunity to see their stude nts’ capabilities. Teachers noted their excitement at seeing such growth in stud ent learning in just five days. However, although a ll teachers expressed interest in teaching the camp again (and returned t he next year to do so), they were less certain abou t integrating CT—for them, ways of thinking used in coding —into their classroom i nstruction, and none of our teachers independently integrated CT or our lessons the upcoming year. However, all teachers noted that teaching in a way that integrated CT into math allowed them to focus on students themselves, for example “the goal wasn' t to perform on an assessment of specific standards. Instead, I measured my success on what students were capable of doing at the end of the week that they were unable to do at the beginning of camp.” This focus on grow th and interest helped teachers to see th eir students in a new light, and for some, wonder h ow their students might look different to them in class the upcoming fall.

## Significance

Although this is a small sample from a single experience, the findings paint a picture of how disrupting canonical STEM practices by integrating new practices and act ivities involving CT might create opportunities for  teachers to see students in a new, asset-oriented light.
