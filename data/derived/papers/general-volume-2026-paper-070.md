# Multimodal Interactions with AI: Human–AI Partnerships in Learning, Teaching, and Research

**Conference:** ISLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
My research program examines how multimodal interactions with AI shape learning, teaching, and research. I investigate how learners build and express knowledge across verbal, embodied, and digital modalities, and how AI can meaningfully participate in these processes. My work spans three interconnected strands. First, I design AI-based systems that personalize learning by assessing cognitive processes through multimodal data triangulation. Second, I develop human–AI hybrid intelligence approaches for modeling complex, temporally layered learning processes, leveraging methods such as the interleaving approach, epistemic network analysis, and computer vision. Third, I design immersive XR learning environments in which AI agents scaffold embodied STEM learning through interactive multimodal guidance. Across these strands, my goal is to advance theoretically grounded and methodologically rigorous frameworks for understanding and designing human–AI partnerships that enhance multimodal sense-making and meaning-making in learning sciences and CSCL contexts.

My work is driven by a simple but persistent question: How do learners build and express knowledge when their learning unfolds across multiple modes, such as speech, gesture, digital tools, and now, interactions with AI? In today’s technology-rich learning environments, these multimodal interactions shape not only how students make sense of ideas but also how they share and demonstrate what they know. When we overlook these diverse ways of meaning-making and knowledge expression, we risk misrepresenting learners’ abilities and missing opportunities to support them more effectively (Nathan, 2023). Motivated by this concern, my research advocates for multimodal approaches to knowledge construction, expression, and assessment. I study how learning happens across verbal, embodied, and digital channels, and increasingly how these processes evolve when learners collaborate with AI systems. Much of my early work focused on human–human interaction—how people speak, gesture, and use digital tools while learning, collaborating, reasoning, and teaching. Recently, my work has expanded to examine human–AI interaction as a new and important layer of learning, teaching, and research, where AI can shape, scaffold, and even participate in meaning-making processes.

Across these commitments, my research spans three interconnected strands: (1) personalizing and supporting learning through AI systems, where I explore how AI can act as a reflective thinking-partner rather than an answer-giver; (2) partnering with AI as a research collaborator to model complex, temporally layered learning processes; and (3) designing immersive XR-based learning environments that provide AI-mediated multimodal interaction and scaffolding to support embodied STEM learning. Across these strands, my overarching goal is to advance our understanding of human–AI partnership in learning, teaching, and research— specifically, how humans and AI co-construct sense-making and meaning-making. Personalizing and supporting learning through AI systems The first strand of my work focuses on personalizing and supporting learning through AI systems, grounded in my expertise in assessing learners’ cognitive skills and constructs through multimodal data triangulation. By integrating speech, gesture (or body movement), and digital traces collected throughout the learning process, I investigate how observable behaviors can serve as meaningful proxies for internal cognitive processes. For example, in Sung et al. (2024), I led the design and development of an intelligent tutoring system to support learning with visual representations in engineering education. Leveraging multimodal data triangulation, including gesture, log data, and formal and informal assessments, I demonstrated the feasibility of evaluating students’ internal visualization skills. In another study (Sung et al., 2024), I modeled the temporal dynamics of self-regulated learning (SRL) in a technology-mediated biology course by integrating multimodal traces, including digital traces with think-aloud data. This work revealed distinct SRL processing patterns between achievement groups, demonstrating how multimodal traces can surface meaningful differences in learning processes.

Building on this foundation, I am extending this line of work to better understand how learners interact with technology, including AI, and how these interactions shape learning in AI-mediated contexts. Most recently, i n my graduate-level programming course (Fall 2025), I incorporated a constrained LLM-based AI assistant called PingPong (Beta version; Computational Policy Lab, 2025) as a virtual teaching assistant and observed how students and AI co-solved programming problems. At the University of Tennessee, Knoxville, I am now codeveloping implementation guidelines and rubrics to help instructors adopt constrained AI models responsibly and effectively in their courses.

Human–AI collaboration in modeling multimodal learning processes The second strand of my research focuses on human–AI hybrid intelligence in multimodal learning analytics (MMLA). Contemporary learning is deeply interactive and collaborative, requiring analytic methods that preserve the temporal and contextual richness of multimodal interactions to understand how meaning-making unfolds over time. In my prior work, I proposed an analytic approach called interleaving (Sung et al., 2021), which models “temporally entangled” multimodal interactions while retaining the contextual detail of each event. Using epistemic network analysis (ENA; Shaffer, 2017), this approach produces interpretable visualizations that support researchers, instructors, and learners in understanding the dynamics of learning. This work received the 2022 CSCL Naomi Miyake Best Student Paper Award, a recognition of its methodological contribution to the field. Building on this foundation, I recently proposed a framework for human–AI hybrid intelligence in MMLA, in which humans and AI collaborate through interlinked feedforward and feedback processes (Sung, under review). These iterative cycles spandata collection, analysis, and interpretation, positioning AI not just as a tool but as a partner in uncovering patterns and generating insights about learning. Empirically, I have employed computer vision and machine learning to automatically detect and track fine-grained learner behaviors. This work has enabled the interpretation of body movement as an indicator of engagement in collaborative learning (Sung & Nathan, 2024), the examination of verbal and nonverbal contributions to co-constructing embodied mathematical understanding (Sung & Nathan, 2025), and the detection of learners’ physical interactions with objects such as worksheets and keyboards (Lee et al., 2026). Together, these studies demonstrate how human–AI hybrid intelligence candeepen our understanding of complex, temporally layered multimodal learning processes through coordinated feedforward and feedback cycles.

Designing AI-mediated XR environments for embodied STEM learning This strand of my work focuses on designing immersive XR learning environments, such as VR and AR, where AI acts as a collaborative learning partner. My goal is to help learners experience abstract STEM concepts through embodied and tangible interaction, making the learning process more intuitive, engaging, and conceptually grounded. In earlier project, I developed a movement-based simulation for pre-service mathematics teachers to experience and co-design embodied approaches to math learning (Sung & Nathan, 2024). More recently, I codeveloped a motion-based AR geometry game for elementary students (Na & Sung, 2025). Using computer vision and augmented reality, this intervention was implemented in real classrooms and resulted in significant improvements in students’ geometry understanding. Building on this foundation, I am now co-leading a VR science learning project that features an AI agent capable of communicating through speech and gesture. This AI agent provides guidance and feedback in an interactive and embodied manner, enabling learners to engage with scientific ideas through multimodal dialogue and action. Collectively, this line of research advances the design of immersive learning environments that enable embodied knowledge building with AI as an active collaborator. It deepens our understanding of how learners coconstruct meaning with AI through multimodal interaction and informs the development of future educational interventions that leverage emerging immersive technologies. Future directions In future work, I will push each strand forward by design AI systems that serve as responsive and trustworthy thinking partners, creating hybrid intelligence approaches that make complex learning processes visible, and designing XR environments that bring embodied interaction and AI guidance into collaborative STEM learning. Through these efforts, I aim to contribute evidence-based frameworks for how human–AI partnerships can enhance learning, interaction, and sense-making in learning sciences and CSCL contexts.
