# Designing Equitable Partnerships for the Future of Play

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Educators and a university researcher co-designed play-based learning to support children to gain a deep and enduring understanding of scientific concepts, critical consciousness, and practicing joy. We present how reciprocity emerged through our equitabl e partnerships and how these partnerships make research, theory, praxis, and student learning more robust. Play Play is messy; while we wanted to include play in our classrooms as social justice advocates and practitioners, the structures, schedules, and everyday boundaries of typical classrooms make it a challenge. Play allows us to grasp important ideas from multiple angles with empathy and care, both as educators and students. This is a freedom we are slowly losing with the rise of standardized curricula, testing, and the prevalence of unfeeling screen-based learning. Standardized and for-profit curricula can also strip educators of autonomy. Play is learning. Play advocacy and research aren’t just about joy—they’re about justice too. We present four design components from our equitable partnership that made our participatory design-based research successful. Our poster shares how we practiced reciprocity in our researcher-educator partnership. Understanding how future educator-researcher collaborations candesign their partnerships in equitable ways is critical for the future of design-based research. Co-designing play The goal of our partnership was to design an interdisciplinary environmental curriculum that empowered young students to learn through play. Throughout the school year, we designed play lessons to support students in learning about interdependent relationships between people, pollinators, and plants. Cristina and Anna are elementary teachers of mixed-age 1st and 2nd grade students at an elementary school in Los Angeles. Cristina teaches in a dual language bilingual education (DLBE) program with 90% instruction in Spanish and 10% instruction in English. Christine, a university researcher, has been interested in designing a curriculum for environmental education through play and has been collaborating with Cristina and Anna since the Spring of 2022. Throughout the academic school year, our team met weekly to co-plan play-based lessons. Our year-long play-based curriculum had three parts that wove together play and movement: first, we investigated plants and flowers; then, we investigated bees as pollinators; and finally, we investigated how people impact and rely on plants and pollinators. Designing partnerships: Reciprocity While our participatory design-based research study (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) aimed to integrate play into our curriculum, the goal of our poster is to reflect on the design of our equitable partnership that made it possible. The data in this poster are fro m our collective experiences and reflections on educator-researcher relationships in design-based work. Through reflection, we identify and present four components that made our work reciprocal. Our partnership was built on curiosity, trust, humility, and multiple competencies. Instead of focusing on the distinct tasks of rigid roles, we practiced reciprocity through equitable partnerships –sharing responsibilities and making decisions together about how to implement play. Component one: Play Play is a human need and everyone has the right to play (United Nations, 1989). Throughout our work, play was a goal and a means for children, teachers, and researchers. We intentionally designed our curriculum with embedded play, and to accomplish this, we also played with one another and alongside our students. Of course, we played with our lesson designs, with how to collect data on students’ learning, and even played with the design of our classroom spaces by moving furniture to make room for play. More importantly, we made and played with costumes, created art, and pretended to be creatures and plants alongside our students. Play helped us to reflect on and refine our thinking–not only with our own experiences to anticipate challenges, but to revise our lessons, and to make decisions about lesson sequences. This freed us from restrictive, static, and hierarchical ways of learning, designing, and researching. 2907 Component two: Autonomy for all While traditional research methodologies tend to uphold a sense of control in research settings, our participatory work is grounded in the context of our classrooms. We rejected the notion that we could have complete control over research and what happens in the classroom. Instead of controlling how teaching and learning should occur, we embraced unscripted, yet meaningful interactions, discoveries, questions, and misconceptions. For example, children unexpectedly sang during play lessons. When teachers asked children about word choice and how they mixed Spanish and English, the volume, and/or speed of their singing, the children’s responses demonstrated the intentionality of their decisions and their nuanced understanding of big ideas like pollination. If t eachers had simply asked children not to sing during a dance lesson or to follow the instructions with more fidelity and play quietly like a pollinator, these understandings may have never emerged and developed. Play offers opportunities for children to participate in expansive and joyful ways. It gives students space and agency to explore ideas, negotiate meaning, and co-create rules of engagement as they interact with peers. It is impossible to predict all the ways children may engage in a topic, and that should delight educators and researchers. Component three: Support for all Our classrooms have a diverse student population, which includes neurodiverse students, students with disabilities, and emergent multilingual learners. We wanted to design play for all, not just for the majority of our students. Throughout the school year, we noticed some neurodiverse students struggled with noise, lack of explicit directions, and more freedom in moving in classroom spaces. This led us to modify our lesson designs. Some of the design changes included frontloading ideas for where to play, roles to take on with which students might feel most comfortable, creating boundaries in our indoor and outdoor spaces, using noise-reducing headphones for certain children, and including children in decision-making about classroom set up for play. When desi gning classroom spaces or thinking about how to use available outdoor spaces, we ask ourselves if the physical, emotional, and academic environment we’ve created is autonomy-supportive (Amado et al., 2020). Are the materials, furniture, and structures for participation accessible to all children? Are relationships being tended to with care and curiosity? Are researchers and teachers modeling care and curiosity for students? Sometimes it’s not simply the chairs that must be moved; it’s our hearts and minds. Component four: Data sharing Our partnership was designed to share knowledge and resources, which often led us to teach one another new skills. Taking photos or videos for formal research analysis was new for Cristina and Anna. Video recordings allowed us to see and hear movement, con versation, negotiation, discussion, and problem-solving that led to the creation of each movement during play. We could see and hear students' reasoning and decision-making, which demonstrated a clear understanding of the function of each part of a flower. In a busy classroom of students, it is difficult to observe and gain a deep understanding of how each student is making sense of the information. As teachers, video recordings made it possible to follow children’s understanding; we could see how knowledge cross-pollinated between students so they could make deeper connections to the content and each other. More importantly, analyzing and re-watching the video alongside Christine changed how Anna viewed participation structures when students were playing. It changed how Anna listens, observes, and builds on students’ ideas in new ways. Concluding remarks The future we dream of is one of delight. Yet, education and academia are fraught with constraints and seeming barriers to joy-filled work. Educators are taught behavior management strategies. Administrators and other evaluators that walk through classrooms expect to see a level of control that demonstrates that children's behavior is being effectively "managed." Play is the antidote to control. The future is relational. To be in healthy relationships with each other as researchers, teachers, and students is to relate to each other in mutually respectful ways—ways of being that anticipate the beauty and logic of knowledge created in collaboration rather than in isolation. We cannot do reciprocal work without being in equitable partnerships. The future we hope for is one of reciprocity between students and teachers, teachers and researchers, and researchers and children. The democratization of knowledge and expertise in our classrooms and research wasn't accidental. It was dignity-affirming (Espinoza et al., 2020) and humanizing for children, educators, and researchers. 2908 References Amado, D., Molero, P., Del Villar, F., Tapia-Serrano, M.
