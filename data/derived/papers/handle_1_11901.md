# Educating for World-Making in Makerspaces

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This symposium gathers the results from several different research projects conducted in maker education in Finland and Norway. Educational makerspaces, in connection to school premises, offer multiple possibilities for students to take part in world-making-quite concretely. The enthusiastic and participatory approach inherited from the maker-movement meets the more static frames of our formal education systems, shaking them up in a good way. When given open-ended design tasks related to real-world problems in the form of projects-based learning, students are encouraged to acknowledge their role in the preferred “futuring” of their own worlds, their personal beliefs and wishes, their home environment, their school or their local neighborhoods. Research results will be presented and discussed by educational researchers, who have studied how maker-centered practices can educate for personal growth, creativity, innovation, citizenship, equity and participatory democracy – all skills and abilities in the important work of world-making for a better future.

## Introduction: Maker-centered learning as world-making

Making things by hand is considered a fundamental characteristic of human creative activity. Making refers to the various kinds of skilled practices involved in mate rial manipulation. However, makerspaces have extend ed and enhanced the traditional meaning of making toward a  more hybrid combination of traditional and digital technologies, used in the making of artifacts. Maker education has gained worldwide popularity as an educational approach to encourage student engagement in learnin g science, technology, engineering, arts, and mathe matics (STEAM). Maker education has further been developed  into Maker-centered learning (Clapp, 2016) to point at the student active turn. This involves engaging students to collaborate and innovate together by turning their ideas into tangible creations using conceptual ideas, visual representations such as drawings and sketches, and material objects such as prototypes and models (Davies et al., 2023; Mehto & Kangas, 2023). Iivari (2020) has f urther emphasized the potential of digital making as a mea ns for empowering children and adopting a proactive  stance on how design and technology can be used for making the world a better place to live. World-making and knowledge creation World-making relates to the design of preferable worlds, both current and future, but also to personal growth and redefining our shared worlds (Agid, 2012; Wargo & A lvarado, 2020). The practices that unfold in makerspaces give many opportunities for world-making in a concrete way. This can be a process of multiple types, for example fictive ideas and designs that could improve the state of matters to preferred ones in a future far ahead. Or it could involve knowledge creation (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2014) and hands-on making to solve current real-life problems of today. World-making can also mean a process of sense-making and personal growth that is needed for a person to be able to actively participate in their communities, through first making their own w orld more open to change. In either case, such world-making often includes a democratic process between the participants of a project, who will identify a common reason and motivation for changing the existing world or circumstances to preferred ones, together. Making is thus a criti cal aspect of knowledge creation that, beyond knowl edge acquisition and social participation, involves syst ematic collaborative efforts in creating and advanc ing shared epistemic objects by means of externalizing design ideas and constructing various types of tangible artifacts. The knowledge creation process is seen to consist of va rious phases: producing ideas, designing, experimen ting, production, and application as well as documentatio n, reflection and assessment. Making is a  very effective way of engaging students in a design mode that guides them to focus on the usefulness and ade quacy of ideas and, moreover, invest efforts in the continuous improvement of ideas (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 2003). Maker education Maker education is building on a democratic and constructionist understanding of learning as knowledge creation, a process of co-constructing and sharing knowledge in a socio-cultural and socio-material context (Blikstein, 2013; Halverson & Sheridan, 2014;  Seitamaa-Hakkarainen, 2022). The educational value of partic ipating in artifact making was already anticipated by progressive educational researchers, such as John Dewey and Seymour Papert. Learning by making entails, in accordance w ith Papert’s construct ionism, that learners are not only building conceptual knowledge but also creating new  artifacts and cultivating new ways of thinking and  acting, with things (Papert, 1994).

The Scandinavian tradition of craft education provi des ample opportunities to involve elementary and lower secondary school students in creative designi ng, in making, and in invention activities. Scandin avian countries have a strong tradition of pursuing compu lsory craft studies from first, through to seventh grade. It became an independent and compulsory school subject, and it has a stable position in most of the Scandin avian school systems. Craft and the act of making has bee n used as a formative and holi stic education of the whole person and has long traditions, with roots in the p edagogical strand of Bildung. Bildung is a socio-philosophical understanding of education that was common especial ly in North Europe, in Germany and the Nordic count ries. The basic thesis is that knowledge changes a person holistically and that a person grows their character and person through knowledge (Sjöström, et al., 2017). This un derstanding relates to the idea of building both a body of knowledge as well as building oneself and one’s understanding of the world and one’s ca pacity to act in this world. In this way the teacher is not necessarily teaching the students to change the world, but the teacher teaches the student to have confidence, skills and resilience that can be used in changing the world – this indirectly teaches for world-making capacities in their students.

Maker education is thus not simple hands-on learning, likewise, making is not only about mak ing artefacts. The pedagogical approach of maker educat ion emphasizes the importance of engaging pupils in  the holistic craft processes from ideation to iterative  testing and making, as well as reflective evaluation. Design thinking and making are therefore a natural part of  maker education (Bosch et al., 2024) in which coll aborative making and learning involves problem finding throug h empathic processes of investigating users or part icipants pain points and dreams, followed by collaborative i deation in which information is gathered and shared  to form alternatives for problem solving and creation of pr ototypes. The material artifacts work as epistemic objects (Mehto & Kangas, 2023; Peppler, et al., 2023) that forward n ot only the physical design, but also concretize th e learning, making it easier to share meanings and ideas in a group. The maker projects take the students' initiatives as starting points, which are closely linked to wha t the students are concerned with and experience as  a problem they want to change. In such a process of learning by making, taking on real life matters and issues t hat are important to them, students learn how they can be a gents for change  in their lives, in their schools or in their neighborhoods. Their perspectives of their role in society changes from passive to active, and they ca n start to dream about making an impact for the better also beyond the school project. The core of invention pedagogy – the Finnish approach to maker education – (Korhonen et al. 2023) is to engage student teams in CSCL (Stahl & Hakkaraine n, 2021) and knowledge creation (see, e.g., Paavola  & Hakkarainen, 2021) through making and designing in a collaborative setting. Making involves systematic efforts in creating and advancing shared epistemic objects and cultivating creative knowledge practices that e ngage learners in knowledge creation inquiries (epistemic  mediation) by means of externalizin g design ideas and constructing various types of complex tangible arti facts (material mediation) (Davies et al., 2024; M ehto et al., 2020). Further, the multifaceted and iterative nature of designing and making is always future-oriented, aiming at the preferred and imagined future. Designing can be  conceptualized as a process, a quest for something  new, something that does not yet exist. The pedagogical potential of open-ended and authentic problems and material engagement are central elements when educating students for world-making in maker projects. Embodiment in learning Tangible engagement with materials and thinking thr ough making are vital parts of the sense-making process in learning. This is not only a cognitive process but involves the whole person, previous experiences, cu ltures and beliefs as well as embodied knowledge of materials and actions gained through trial and error (Schilhab & Groth, 2024). Meaning is negotiated through the material i nteraction and mediated through the artefact (Nimku lrat & Groth, 2024). Embodied experiences are thus key to making sense of our encounters with the world. Such bodily interactions with materials are seen as especially crucial for young learners (Kiefer & Trumpp, 2012),  but experiential learning is carried on all through lif e and becomes especially important in the meeting w ith new materials, and technology. “Tinkering” is a form of  material and technological exploration in which th ere is not necessarily any meaningful task or goal, but rather students can explore what the material “cando”. Bevan et al.,

(2014) describes this type of exploration as “serio us play”, and similarly to any other exploration, t his type of

making sense of things is a fundamental aspect of making. It is a way of getting the material and processes “under the skin” without any pressure to perform, or to at tain learning goals, but is a fundamental activity that needs to be given time.

Learning environments that encourage a sense of citizenship and participatory democracy The learning environment conditions the learning th at can happen in any given teaching session, this i s the case both for what the physical equipment facilitates or  how the spatial constraint plays a part, as well a s the psychological atmosphere in the learning situation. In this respect, the environment also has a say in possibilities for creativity, when it comes to available materials, and the accepting social construction that allows for mistakes, open ended explorations, taking one’s time, mutual respect, inclusivity and a culture of sharing knowl edge and skills. Collaboration and problem-solving in school makerspaces have the possibility to create a growing ground for a sense of citizenship and participatory democr acy. By engaging in projects with tangible outcomes, students learn the value of their contributions and the importance of working collectively towards common goals (de Roock & Baildon, 2019). Such empowerment fosters a sense of agency, and opens doors for students to be activ e, engaged citizens. When successful, students may attain a sense of  contribution and social recognition in a group as they are invaluable members that have important roles in a shared agenda. The presentations in the symposium In this symposium we will present research in maker education conducted in several different projects in Norway and Finland. The projects stem from very different contexts and methodologies but all relate to world-making as a phenomenon. Four presentations will be discussed, giving each paper 12 min for presentation and 5 min for our discussant professor Anna Keune from the Technical University of Munich to discuss the content, after which we open for questions from the public. After thi s conference we aim to write a theoretical article based on the ideas presented in this symposium. We start the session with a short i ntroduction to the theme of education for world-making and how maker-centered learning and teaching can promote a holist ic view on education that facilitates students' self-efficacy and agency, touching upon many of the themes introduced above. The presentations follow the order of the abstracts presented next.

Presentation 1: World-making through knowledge-creating invention pedagogy in Finnish formal education Sini Davies, Kati Sormunen, Kaiju Kangas, Pirita Seitamaa-Hakkarainen Project description For almost a decade, we have explored new possibilities for engaging students in knowledge creation practices at different levels of formal education. The Finnish e ducational setting is exceptional in that it enable s the implementation of maker education a s a core curricular activity through its compulsory  craft education and sophisticated craft-and science-lab spaces. Our findings have revealed some key fea tures of knowledge creation in the context of the invention projects. Based on the rich and longitud inal data from several projects, we have analyzed student ideation, creation of epistemic ob jects, and engagement in maker practices in connect ion with the supportive roles of the teachers and peer tutor s (Riikonen et al., 2020; Davies et al., 2023, 2024) as well as how children themselves describe and reflect their own invention processes (Sinervo et al., 2021). Lessons learned Our results show how children could recognize diffe rent external constraints involved in their design and the importance of iterative ideation processes and testing the ideas through prototyping (Riikonen et al., 2020; Davies et al., 2023, 2024). We have also demonstrated how idea development and material making processes intertwine in students’ knowledge creation practices. Materials were shown to have an active role in knowledge creation and ideation in open-ended projects, which allowed for thinking toge ther with the materials (Mehto, Riikonen, Hakkarainen, et al., 2020). The task-related physical materials act as a focal point for team collaboration and invite participation (Mehto, Riikonen, Kangas, et al., 202 0). However, the singularity or multiplicity  of the materials potentially affects the opportunities for access and control of the process (Mehto, Riikonen, Kangas, et al., 2020). Suggestions for the field Educating for world-making aims at developing students’ sense of contri bution and social recognition. In maker projects, students need to manage the procedures of  planning and making, integrating representations o f mind to surrounding material, physical and societal conditi ons, reflecting on possibilities, and testing the b oundaries of self-fulfillment. Maker proje cts support students’ active engagement in solving wicked, ill-defined problems by trial and error based on their insight and past exp eriences by educating impending citizens with capab ilities and confidence for actively participating in the surrounding community. Social recognition is important for reinforcing a sense of value and belonging by encouraging stude nts to contribute to world-making. Encountered challenges and frustration develop resilience, adaptability, a nd believing that one can improve. Design is an interpretive practice within which particular kinds of sense-making require abilities for rapid evaluation of co mplex contexts and iterative projection of promising possibilities. Thus, the notion of figured worlds (Holland et al. 1998; Jurow

2005) bridges the world of professional designing as a socially and culturally constructed framework that guides

students to recognize the significance of their mak er activities and provide valuable boundary encount ers that inter-link their maker project with authentic cultural practices). To better support ed ucation for world-making already in formal education, we propose that partic ipatory practices with various communities should b e better implemented in maker projects to induce an awarenes s of people’s various life circumstances and needs.  Future studies should address how these community-based participatory practices, empathy and social design could better engage young students in an activistic stance in world-making. Presentation 2: Norwegian educational makerspaces: A great potential to educate for world-making Camilla Groth, Larysa Kolesnyk, Ingrid Holmboe Høibo, Lovise Søyland, Brynjar Olafsson, Pirita SeitamaaHakkarainen Project description In the Nordic countries, educational makerspaces ha ve been introduced in the school context to enhance technological competences and to promote interdisci plinary and student-led forms of creative and collaborative learning through making. While there is a general c onsensus over what an educational makerspace is, and what type of activities are introduced in them, the actu al learning environments and pedagogical infrastruc tures vary greatly both internationally and nationally depending on the initiator of the makerspace. Both grassroot initiatives and national educational initiatives give rise to educational makerspaces and cultural differences and pedagogical ideals, aims and traditions color the activities in them. In the largest study of Norwegian educational makerspaces to date, the Maker project, we interviewed 30 teachers in 18 makerspaces across the country. In this presentation we show who initiates them and how they are run and  what activities are going on in them and discuss opportunities and challenges they pose in educating for world-making. Lessons learned Theoretically makerspaces are ideal for world-making in a number of ways, but we wanted to find h ow the teachers articulate their pedagogical beliefs and t heir aims for their practices. In our study on the Norwegian makerspace context, we found that almost  all were initiated by teachers in the STEM field, a nd only a few Arts and Craft teachers were involved in the maker activ ities in the schools. The inclusion of all educatio nal levels in our interviews led to some challenges when comparing teachers’ beliefs and pedagogical approaches as they were different depending on their students' age. However, many of the teachers looked at education holistic ally and considered themselves to be educating students towa rd becoming citizens of the future world, regardles s of their ages. We found further that despite the diverse curricular aims, several of the teachers said they aimed at teaching for personal growth in their students. We found that this attitude of a holistic teaching aim was not only related to a maker mindset but could also be traced to the teacher’s general teacher education that in Norway bares traces of the “Bildung” tradition first developed by Wilhelm Von Humboldt (1767–1835) (Sjöström, 2017) and explained in the introduction.

The bildung tradition is especially strong in the a rts and crafts teacher education, that additionally  also draws on other theoretical influences shared with t he maker education, such as Dewey, Piaget and Vygot sky. Even so, we also found that teachers from  different disciplines clashed over values in maker education that were to do with material and technical quality of the ar tifacts, the aesthetics and sustainability issues. We thus found epistemological differences between different subje ct teachers and a  difficulty to work across the disciplinary borders for these reasons. For example, some Arts and crafts teachers found it challenging to join the educational makerspace activities due to the high expectations on digital competence and due to the experienc ed irrelevance of the STEM content in the proposed pedagogical material promoted by national initiatives, such as science actors in Norway.

Suggestions for the field By this study we recognized that arts and crafts processes could be utilized more to vary the content and methods of teaching in Norwegian Makerspaces. We can also s ee an epistemological divide between the STEM and a rt and crafts teachers that is grounded in value differences when it comes to thinking abou t processes, material quality, and skill learning. Theoretically this dif ference seems superficial as they share many of the  general pedagogical aims of a holistic education through ma king and hands-on learning. We see the makerspace as a platform, not only for the students, but also for t eachers to learn from each other in an effort to ov ercome such differences and education for world-making, using the best practices from multiple area s. Teachers need support and recognition from the school leaders and the principal to succeed in their efforts to be successful. Perhaps most importantly, teachers need time for planning and organizing activities as well as time for professional development to catch up in thei r digital competencies. Finally, demands on measura ble results and forms for evaluations in educational makerspaces should be rethought in rela tion to the benefits of opening up the floor for student-led initiative taking and learning from mistakes, as th is will encourage more teachers to embark on maker-centered learning pedagogies that aim larger than limited le arning goals. Thus, the opportunities for world-making and children’s sense of agency and participation in society can be facilitated. Presentation 3: Norwegian educational makerspaces: Facilitating learning for world-making Camilla Groth, Larysa Kolesnyk, Ingrid Holmboe Høibo, Lovise Søyland, Brynjar Olafsson, Pirita SeitamaaHakkarainen Project description As part of a four-year project, investigating how Norwegian makerspac es are and can be used in schools, we conducted several research-practice partnerships (Coburn & Penuel, 2016) in a Educational Design Research methodology (McKenney & Reeves, 2019) with Norwegian teachers and their students. Through engaging in their everyday school life and context, we found situations that spoke of the importance of embodied sense-making and personal growth through meaningful artifact mediate d processes. We also found signs of equitable engag ement and positive identity development in newcomer stude nts' collaborative processes. In this paper we pres ent two learning settings and the practices and understandi ngs that were developed in them. In both settings t he makerspaces offered transformative learning experiences that we re connected to embodied learning and tangible and material experimentation. The two settings showed that there are good opportunities for world-making when paying attention to personal sense-making and embodied learning through material interactions. We further found that the physical and psychological role of the lea rning environment is important for both sense-making and equitable engagement.

Lessons learned Embodied and tangible experiences seem to be import ant when young students are about to learn more complicated uses of technology. Making the tasks si mple and contextualizing the subject matter helps t hem feel connected to the processes they are about to engage in. The sense of agency and opportunities to actively engage in all aspects of technology feels important to the m. Fabrication technologies such as laser cutters o ffer new possibilities for making; however, young pupils may  become more passive when too complicated and dista nt technologies are integrated into their creative wor k compared to when using hand tools. The absence of  deep involvement may lead to pupils feeling detached fro m the process. Deep involvement is alsonecessary w hen utilizing new technology for pupils to become world-makers. Pupils need to be permitted to use the tech nology by themselves, ensuring they are not excluded from technological progress, and merely become users of technology.

For older pupils, Makerspaces present a powerful op portunity to foster equitable engagement and positive identity development (Kajamaa, et al., 2018). Especially in cases where different social or cultural groups meet, such as is becoming the norm in many urban schools. Hands-on activities can help bridge cultural gaps and support developing democratic, inclusive learning e nvironments. The integration of equitable engagemen t principles in education, particularly through the l ens of makerspaces, not only enh ances the learning experience for all students but also fosters a culture of incl usivity and innovation. By leveraging the diverse p erspectives of students and creating environments that encourage c ollaboration and creativity, educators can cultivat e a mor e equitable educational landscape. Further, the artifacts and material interactions become means for communication and knowledge sharing through actions and gestures (Härkki, 2018). This type of learning environment h as the potential to help overcome language and culture bar riers. In our study we could see evidence of equita ble engagement that was brought by the nature of hands-on making and collaborating that took place through makercentered teaching and learning in the setting of a makerspace. Suggestions for the field We suggest that makerspaces offer opportunities for embodied learning that transcends verbal formulations in the way many activities are experienced materially. For  very small children this experience-based type of learning makes the topics more concrete a nd has the possibility to get closer to their lived  experience and everyday life. Thus, they can also see how they themselves can participate in world-making on a concrete level, whether or not they are able to explicate their ideas in verbal fo rm. Similarly, for older children learning through making and hands on showing can help students who due to migra tion are not yet verbally capable of joining collaborations that require complicated verbalizations of ideas and opinions. Even when ideas are formulated, and decisions are taken, drawings and three-dimensional artifacts can mediate their communicati on. To be able to participate in world-making one needs to be able to feel included and respected in the group of peers. The topics need to be felt as important and relevant, and one needs to be able to see one’s own role in the activities and the meaningfulness of them. Makerspaces as learning environments have the potential for offering such arenas if the teachers are able to facilitate embodied aspects and an inclusive atmosphere. Presentation 4: The role of diverse tools and technologies in collaborative etextile projects in lower secondary education: An ordered network analysis Niina Niinimäki, Kati Sormunen, Sini Davies and Kaiju Kangas Project description Technological competence has been brought to the fo refront as a requisite attribute of competent world-makers. Although previous studies (see Rouse & Gillespie Rouse, 2022) indicate that working with maker tools can benefit the learning of 21st-century skills, there is very little research on how the different technologies used in maker spaces affect the technological competence used and gained during maker projects. This study aims to clarify what tools and technolog ies are used in various phases of maker projects and to examine their relation to the learning of techno logical competence. We studied five 7th-grade student teams’ (3–6 students/team, 13 –14-year-olds) discourse from classroom video data, in which  they were engaged in designing and making e-textiles. To discern the technological competence learned during the project, the students’ transcribed discourse was categorized into five dim ensions of technology competence: crafting, designi ng, engineering, programming, and documenting. We furth er categorized the data into four categories accord ing to what tools and technologies the students were using: 1) pen and paper, 2) traditional crafting tools a nd technologies, such as scissors and sewing machines, 3) informatio n and communication technologies (ICT), implying to conventional digital technologies, such as laptops and mobile phones, and 4) fabrication technologies, such as microcontrollers and LEDs. We then used Ordered Network Analysis (ONA) to analyze and compare the learning of technological competence in these four categories. Lessons learned We found that different tools and technologies promoted various types of technological learning. Engineering and programming competencies were emphasized when stude nts used fabrication technologies, while documentin g and designing competencies were promote d when they worked with traditional crafting tools and technologies, ICT, or pen and paper. Similarly to Blikstein et al. (2017), we found that fabrication technologies pr omote different technological competence than ICT. Fabrication technologies were connected to the iterative process of inventing and solving problems by technical design,  engineering, and programming. In addition, circuit ry was learned mainly using fabrication technologies. On the other hand, in these projects, ICT was used mainly as a tool for documenting and reflecting, as well as for design activities and for seeking information that were found to be very similar to competencies promoted when the students were using pen and paper. Our analysis revealed that crafting played a crucia l role in the design process. In students’ discours e, crafting preceded technical design more often than vice versa, indicating that working and experimenting with the materials helped the students push the design proce ss forward. In addition, when using fabrication tec hnologies, engineering preceded technical design as often as t echnical design preceded engineering, indicating th at even though students’ engineering choices were informed by their technical design, their technical design process was equally often informed by engineering experiments. Our findings highlighted how craft and working with concrete materials helped the students to concretize difficult concepts and solve their design challenges. Suggestions for the field Fabrication technologies enable the learning of a unique set of technological competencies that can help students to become competent world-makers. However, the implementation of technology e ducation in school often focuses heavily on general computing and  ICT with little attention to fabrication technologi es (Blikstein et al., 2017). While ICT is so ubiquitous that it is already integrated into children’s everyday lives, schools might be the only place where learning opportunities with fabric ation technologies may arise. The tool-rich environments of formal education maker spaces with an emphasis on f abrication technologies may, therefore, play a key role in teaching 21st-century skills to our future citizens. Furthermore,  if technology education in schools  is conducted only through general computing and ICT, often merel y involving closed textbook problems and digitaliza tion of activities previously done with pen and paper, stud ents’ abilities to participate in world-making or their technological competencie s will never reach their full potential. Therefore,  we suggest that the differences in technological competence furthered with different k inds of tools and technologies should be acknowledg ed, and the usage of diverse fabrication technologies shoul d be promoted in teaching, assessment of learning, and research.

Synthesis The presented research provides a unified understanding of world-making through maker-centered learning, emphasizing the importance of participatory practices and “making” in formal education. By involving end users and various communities in maker project s, students become aware of people's life circumsta nces and needs, which prompts critical thinking and creative proble m solving. This approach also fosters empathy and s ocial design, encouraging young students to adopt an activistic stance in world-making. Teachers play a crucial role in this process, requi ring time for planning, organizing activities, and professional development to improve their digital c ompetencies. Rethinking demands on measurable resul ts and evaluation forms in educational makerspaces c an open up opportunities for student-led initiatives and learning from mistakes. This shift encourages more teachers to adopt maker-centered learning pedagogies that aim beyond limited learning goals, thereby facilitating children's sense of agency and participation in society. For students to effectively participate in world-making, they need to feel included and respected am ong their peers. The topics must be relevant and meanin gful to them, and they should be able to see their own role in the activities. Makerspaces, as learni ng environments, have the potential to offer such i nclusive and embodied experiences if teachers can create an accepting atmosphere. However, if technology education in schools is limited to general computing and ICT, students' abilities t o participate in world-making and their technological competencies will not reach their full potential. O verall, the research presented highlights the need for a holistic and inclusive approach to education that empowers s tudents to engage in world-making through collabora tive, empathetic, and socially relevant practices.
