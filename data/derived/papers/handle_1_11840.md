# Examining Support Moments With a Conversational AI Partner

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
AI systems can facilitate collaborative learning, but few studies examine how conversational AI partners can support collaboration. This study continues our application of the MOSAIC-AI protocol to explore support moments between a collaborative group and an educational expert serving as a Wizard-of-Oz (WoZ) agent. We present our refinement of the protocol, explore the nature of student-AI interactions, and discuss implications for building a dialog policy for informing the design of fully autonomous agents to support collaboration.

## Introduction

A key area of computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) research explores how digital tools can be utilized to support and/or enhance student collaboration (Dillenbourg et al., 2018), with growing interest in incorporating artificial intelligence (AI) in these tools (Rosé & Ferschke, 2016). In both CSCL and related learning analytics (LA) research, AI is more frequently being used to analyze student behaviors and interactions and provide metrics about their collaboration. With advancements in natural language processing (NLP) technologies, research has begun to explore how conversational ag ents can support learning by offering adaptive guid ance or scaffolding on an as-needed basis; however, they primarily focus on indi vidual users and for content lear ning (Ouyang & Zhang, 2023). Few studies have examined h ow conversational agents can support collaboration by engaging in dialog with more than one student at a time. Our study investigates the interactions between a Wizard of Oz (WoZ) conversational agent  and student groups working together. We aim to unde rstand the nature of the interventions, to inform the design of an automated conversational agent for scaffolding collaboration.

## Background

Collaboration is defined as two or more students working together, with goals of building knowledge and solving problems (Dillenbourg, 1999). This co-construction of knowledge is a fundamental aspect of collaboration, but it is not easy. Collaboration research indicates that successful knowledge building involves students dev eloping shared understanding of the problem (Roschelle & Te asley, 1995), and for them to share, build on, and refine each other’s ideas (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2006; S uthers et al., 20 10). However, this is hard to do and sustain over long durations (Järvelä et al., 2010). Varying group dynamics (Woodrich & Fan, 2017) and other constraints (Jarvenoja & Järvelä, 2009) can also add to the challenge. Thus, students need support for effective collaboration. T eachers play a central role in facilitating student  collaboration by monitoring progress and providing contingent support (vande Pol et al., 2010); however, managing these activities across multiple groups in realtime can be challenging (Dillenbourg, 2018). Orches tration technologies can address some of these chal lenges, by using various learning analytics to display information about student participation and learning (e.g., Holstein et al., 2019).  Other technologies leverage advancements in automat ic speech recognition (ASR) and discourse analysis techniques for analyzing student conversations during collabor ation in real-time, enabling systems like conversational agents to provide adaptive, personal ized support. With human-like conversation, conversational agents can simulate aspects of teacher support by i dentifying where students need assistance and provi ding interventions to guide their understanding and lear ning (Graesser et al., 2017). Studies examining the se AI partners often focus on the management of activities across different levels and instructional goals (Aleven et al.,

2023) or examine how they support individual learni ng (Ouyang & Zhang, 2023); few explore AI systems that

provide collaboration support for multiple students. To do this, these agents or AI partners need to r ecognize the groups’ challenges, provide timely and appropriate support, and foster more productive collaborations. We situate our work as part of larger ongoing research to develop AI partners that support collaboration. To inform the design and development of these AI pa rtners, we first aimed to understand the nature of support students typically receive while collabo rating in classrooms. We developed and used the MOS AIC (Moment of Support Analysis in Collaboration) protocol to examine support moments in the classroom while students worked on collaborative activities, and to better understa nd the nature of these suppor t moments, such as who initiates and provides support, what types of support are provided, and student behaviors before and after receiving support (Hoang et al., 2024b). The MOSAIC protocol is groun ded in sociocultural theories that view learning as  a social process, highlighting the role of scaffolding to su pport learners to accomplish what they may not on t heir own (vande Pol, 2010; Vygotsky, 1978). The protocol’s codes were developed both deductively and inductively from watching videos of teacher support during small group work and refining the codes by g rounding them in literature. Building on this foundation, we adapted the protocol into MOSAIC-AI to better capture the interactions between a conversational agent and a small group of students in a preliminary study (Dey et al., 2025). This paper describes our ongoing work to refine the  MOSAIC-AI protocol and its application to a lab study, where an educational expert acted as a Wizard-of-Oz (WoZ) and delivered interventions as a collaborative agent. The WoZ technique is commonly used to prototype and evaluate human-AI interactions before transitioning to fully autonomous systems (Demir & Cooke, 2018). Unlike education experts, a  generic generative AI model may not be capable of determining when and how to i ntervene effectively. By using the WoZ method, we hope to leverage education domain experts’ input to info rm the subsequent design of a conversational agent that effectively simulates expert interventions. We also  demonstrate the MOSAIC-AI protocol’s applicability in examining and analyzing student-AI interactions, i.e., the nature of the support and students’ behaviors before and after to understand how the support is taken up. We discuss our findings in the context of informing the design of a dialog policy, to be used in future autonomous ag ents. To design a conversational agent that takes i n dialog, interprets it, and formulates a coherent and appropriate response, the agent must follow a set of guiding rules or a ‘dialog policy’. To know when best to intervene, we  added several linguistic behaviors, or ‘dialog states’, to the annotation protocol to guide the future policy. The research questions guiding our study were:

1. How can the MOSAIC-AI protocol be refined/applied to explore the nature of the support provided by a

conversational agent to facilitate collaboration?

2. What are strengths and challenges of interactions with a conversational agent supporting collaboration?

## Participants and context

A total of 50 participants, aged 12-17 were recruited in the Western United States thro ugh recruitment flyers for this lab study. Of these participants, 20 had not d one any computer programming, 22 were novice progra mmers (i.e., recently learned programmin g basics), and 8 had experience and applied program ming skills. Students engaged in the tasks in small groups of 2 or 3 members, for a total of 20 groups (10 dyads and 10 triads). Groups were automatically created when participants signed up for a certain time slot. Initially, each participant became an “expert” in one of thr ee sensors (environmental, sound, or soil moisture). They then collaborated in a group jigsaw activity, sharing their sensor knowledge to answer questions and brainstorm a real-world problem solvable with all three sensors. During this activity, participan ts received support from an AI agent via text-based prompts to facilitate their collaboration. The task interface, including the AI agent’s message window, is shown in Figure 1. Although students were informed that they interacted with an AI agent, it was manipulated by a trained researcher; this is known as a Wizard of Oz (WoZ) approach (Demir & Cooke, 2018). After the study, students were debriefed that the agent was controlled by an educational expert. Students completed a pre-survey (with demographic and personal information) before the activity and a post-survey (measuring their perceptions of the AI agent  and their collaboration) afterward. Students also participated in a post-study group interview. The interviews and surveys are outside the scope of this paper. Data and analysis Each group was video-recorded and screen-recorded. Thus, we could examine what students were doing, and how they were interacting with each other and the WoZ agent. We used the MOSAIC-AI protocol (Dey et al., 2025)— adapted from the MOSAIC protocol which identifies p atterns of support and student behaviors in classro oms (Hoang et al., 2024b)—to better understand the nature of the human-AI interactions. As guided by the MOSAICAI protocol, we considered a support moment  as the interaction between the WoZ agen t and the group. Interventions of the same type occurring within 60 seconds of each other, were considered as part of t he same support moment. If the intervention type was differ ent (e.g., a procedural versus collaboration assist), we considered the second  intervention as a separate support. To understand t he context of these support moments, we also examined student behaviors 1 minute before and after the support moment. Thus, our unit of analysis was the support moment, as well as one minute before and after the support. We identified and analyzed 143  support moments across the 20 groups, for approximately 521 mins of video. Table 1 shows a summary of the MOSAICAI protocol codes. Each behavior (before and after the support moment) was rated on a Likert sc ale from 1-5 on how often they occurred.

## Results

We will first report our inter-rater reliability work, followed by the descriptive  statistics of our annotations. We then present two examples where students’ collaboration was facilitated or impeded by the WoZ agent. Reliability work We did inter-rater reliability work on 20% of the data of random ly selected support moments from each group. We had perfect agreement (K-alpha (α) = 1) or good agreement (α ≥ 0.80) for several of the codes related to the support moment, including who initiated the support; intervention type related to validation (α=0.931), help with problem solving  (α=1), direction to task  (α=1), avoiding misdirection (α=1); when collaboration support was about getting everyone to share (α=1); and students’ interaction with the intervention, related to talking about the WoZ agent (α=0.871) and getting distracted by the agent (α=1). There was also perfect agreement or good agreement for student behaviors before and after the support moment, such as when the students: were stuck due to a content-related (αbefore=0.785), procedural (αbefore=1, αafter=1), or collaboration (αafter=1) issue, expressed a need for direction on the task (αbefore=1, αafter=1), advanced ideas (αbefore=0.803), assigned work to themselves or others (αafter=1), built on each others’ ideas (αafter=0.817), laughed (αafter=0.841), or did not collaborate (αafter=1). There was moderate agreement on recognizing when students ignored the WoZ intervention  (α=0.672), and perfect agreement on recognizing certain dialog sta tes such as when the conversation is not moving forward due to a collaboration (α=1), content-related (αbefore=0.66, αafter=1), or procedural (αbefore=1, αafter=1) issue. Since a future AI-based conversational agent will likely be needed mo st when students are stuck, having high reliability in recognizing dialog states related to being stuck, is important. The inter-rater agreement was significantly different for the before support moment codes of identifying whether students were: stuck due to a collaboration issue (α=0), talking about how to work (α=0), and laughing (α=-0.0351); and their dialog states related to non-productive discussions due to a collaboration (α=-0.0172) or other (α=-0.0351) issue. The inter-rater agreement was significantly different for the intervention types related to directions to meet expectations for the assignment  (α=-0.0351), helping students reach consensus  (α=-0.0172), providing a procedural h int (α=-0.0172), or no collaboration (α=-0.0351), and for recognizing the dialog state as being dominated by one individual (α=-0.0172). These codes had a negative value for alpha, indi cating disagreement among the raters. This may have been d ue to a misunderstanding due to the similar-sounding or overlapping nature of some codes, such as providing a hint  and helping students reach consensus. While not statistically significant, most of the before and a fter support moment codes had poor agreement or disagreement, i.e. the alpha was below 0.67 or negative, and the average Krippendorf’s alpha (for only the codes, not the Likert responses) was 0.53, which is considered low (Kripp endorff, 2004). The disagreements or overall low re liability may have been due to the infrequent observations of some of the codes. We resolved these discrepancies through discussion and updated the definitions of the codes for more clarity (e.g., better distinguished between “talk about the AI agent” and “talk about what the AI agent said”). We also combined similar or overlapping codes related to student behaviors, such as walking through answers and sharing information, advancing ideas and building on each other’s ideas, being social and laughing. We also changed the codes having “other” to be on ly an open-ended response, as we noticed most of the time, that they could be captured by one of the existing codes. We kept the other codes as we wanted to capture these different dimensions of before, after, and during the support moment to better understand future student-AI interactions and guide the design of a dialog policy. Descriptive statistics During the support moment We found that the WoZ agent initiated the support (79%), followed by students either pressing the ‘hel p’ button (11.1%) or verbalizing (9.9%) that they needed help. The WoZ agent’s validation or praise of students’  work (37.9%) and asking questions to the group (24.2%) constituted about two-thirds of the support (Figure 2). Support related to collaboration was less than 10% of all t he support moments. When students initiated help, t hey mostly asked for task-related help, such as help with working through an idea (42.3%), asking the agent to validate their idea (34.6%), or directions on what to donext (11.5%); or they asked how much time was left (11.5%). While these codes are important for understanding the nature of the support moment, to build the dialog policy guiding the autonomous agent’s responses, we  wanted to clearly define the intervention type as a Procedural Hint, Content Assist, or Collaboratio n Assist. We further broke down collaboration assis t into assisting: i) for general collaboration (e.g., aski ng students to build on a discussed idea), ii) when  students are silent (e.g., asking students to share about their sensors), and iii) when one individual is doing most of the talking (e.g., asking others to share their ideas). We found that Content Assist (40.9%) and Collaboration Assist-General Collaboration (35.4%) constituted about three-fourths of the intervention types. Nearly one-fifth of th e intervention types were coded as “other” or “none” (16.5%); a closer examination of the open-ended responses led us to realize that the majority were related to the validation of student work. Therefore “Validation or praise” was included in the protocol as an intervention type.

## Discussion

This study discusses MOSAIC-AI, a novel annotation scheme for capturing student-AI interactions before, during, and after support moments, emphasizing a human-centered approach to AI design. We presented the protocol and its refinement in detail, to enable others to apply  it in their research on student-AI interactions during collaboration. Using a Wizard-of-Oz (WoZ) methodology helped us integrate an educati onal expert-driven approach to our development of the AI partner (Doherty et al., 2025), and we highlight a few potential challenges that students might encounter when supported by an AI partner. We found that the WoZ agent primarily initiated support, with a frequency  comparable to that of classroom teachers on a similar task (Hoang et al., 2024b). Similar to our findings on classroom teacher suppor t (Hoang et al., 2024a), the WoZ agent provided more task-related support, despite its goal to foster collaboration. This mirroring of findings suggests students need substantial task-related support, as well as collaboration support. This further supported by observations that student-initiated support mainly involved task-related questions or asking the agent to validate their ideas; they never asked for collaboration support. While our AI partner’s primary goal is to support collaboration, helping students progress on tasks is equally important, as they may  become frustrated if stuck. Attending to lower-level task-related issues, such as asking what to donext, may not efficiently utilize a teacher’s time and resources; delegating such tasks  to an AI partner, could allow students to continue with their tasks and the teacher to provide more targeted high er-level support where needed. Designing adaptive support for specific tasks may require task-specific training data, potentially limiting its ap plicability to a particular task or curriculum. Generalized task support, such as progress checkpoin ts or time reminders, may provide some structure, while allowing teachers to focus on more high-level guidance. Most of the time, the WoZ intervention and its timi ng was appropriate; however, students only verbally acknowledged the intervention about half of the tim e. The agent’s messages did not always prompt the s tudents into further discussion about the task or their ideas, although sometimes students exclaimed surprise that the agent really was “listening” to them and seemed to be dis tracted by the messages that popped up. Chen et al.  (2023) found that students can get distracted without a ce rtain level of “humanness” to the conversation resp onses. It is not likely to be the reason students were distracte d in our study, as the WoZ agent’s responses were c rafted by a human; however, it may be worth consideration when designing how and when an autonomous agent should intervene. Students’ distraction by the agent’s messages was reflected i n the increase in laughter and socializing behaviors after support was provided; however, it c orresponded with increased student on task behavior  and a “flow” state in their dialogue. This behavior was r eflected in our example of Olivia and Emma, who wer e momentarily distracted when the WoZ agent responded to their request for validation, but then got back on track. The excerpt from Emma and Olivia’s interaction with  the WoZ agent highlighted two potential challenges in student-AI interactions, with having a conversational agent at the students’ disposal. Initially when Olivia had put an idea forward, she said it slowly,  as though she was thinking through the idea while sharing it with Emma. But when she realized that the agent was genuinely responding to what they were saying, she quickly put forward her next idea and sought its validation from the agent. While the agent’s message may have prompted Olivia to think more about the problem, her quick r esponse may have indicated she was throwing out an idea without thinking more deeply about it. This behavior was also captured by MOSAIC-AI on our overall data, where we observed that when students initiated help from the agent, over one-third of the time it was to seek validation for their ideas. Stieglitz et al. (2021) termed this type of behavior as “smart loafing”, where students engage with the work more superficially, putting the responsibility of deeper thinking onto the agents, as also observed in other studies (Chen et al., 2023). This highlights a potential challenge with using conversational agents. Although they are designed to provide a particular support, stude nts may game the system to complete tasks, without engaging in deeper thinking or contributing to the collaboration. Another potential challenge of interacting with non-human agents is misdirection or misinterpretation of the support, as illustrated by Alex and Bei’s example. While the agent’s message intended to uplift Alex’s idea and ensure it was considered, it unintentionally led the students to pursue two different ideas rather than building and collaborating on the same idea. This difference in coordination led the students to not collaborate well, as has be en reported in other studies (Barron, 2003). A  human support provider (e.g., teacher) would likely recognize and address such challenges immediately with additional support, but an AI agent may not do so as dynamically from only speech inputs. Therefore, developing AI dialog policies should consider providing multiple types of support for the same issue to better facilitate collaboration. Our work highlights observations that could inform the design of a dialog policy for a conversational AI partner. We demonstrated how using the MOSAIC-AI protocol with a WoZ approach can capture student s’ collaborative behaviors and guide when and how an autonomous AI partner should intervene. The MOSA IC-AI protocol also enables the analysis of student-AI interactions, offering insights into the nature of the support and students' behaviors before and after intervention. Specifically, the data obtained from MOSAIC-AI annotations is being used to deduce students’ collaborative states, the identification of which could inform when and  how in which an autonomous AI partner should intervene. We can identify key dialogue indicators of these ‘dialog states’, using tools in NLP and AI, and design an agent to automat ically detect such states and closely mimic the WoZ interventions (Doherty et al., 2025). A limitation is the low inter-rater reliability for a few codes; however, the protocol remains adaptable for researchers to evaluate AI agents, as it is independent of curriculum or system. Our next steps are to apply the MOSAIC-AI protocol to human-in-the-loop studies on the same activity, where trained-AI models will generate a response, but a human wil l oversee the response before sharing it with students. These understandings will continue to bui ld on the dialog policy that will inform an autonom ous AI partner for supporting collaboration. As the MOSAIC-AI protocol employed a WoZ setup, we anticipated th at further refinements would likely be necessary once the agent is autonomous and deployed in actual clas srooms. However, MOSAIC-AI’s adaptable nature allows researchers to evaluat e student-AI interactions across various curricula and settings. While dialog-based systems are ideal for settings like classroom s where multimodal data collection is not feasible, using only dialog comes with limit ations. Without the context of students’ nonverbal behaviors, it may miss the nuances of the interacti ons and provide incorrect or unhelpful support, or may not provide support at all if students are not verbally communicating (as observed in the examples). Thus, future work should also consider a multimodal approach for designing and developing conversational agents.
