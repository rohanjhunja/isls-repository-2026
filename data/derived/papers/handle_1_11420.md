# Enhancing the Relevance of Science Learning: Exploring the Potential of the Student Experience Improvement Cycle

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To support equitable instruction in science, the Framework envisions implementing science instruction around students’ interests and experiences. In this study, we tested a strategy for using student experience data for data-informed decision-making called the Student Experience Improvement Cycle (SEIC), which engages groups of educators and a coach in adapting evidence-based practices for enhancing the relevance of instruction to students and their communities. This study investigated the effects of the SEIC using a single-group, pretest-posttest design using experience-based exit ticket surveys collected from middle school science students. Analyses from hierarchical linear modeling indicated students’ perceptions of relevance increased significantly over time and that Black/African American students’ perceptions of experience increased more than a reference group of Latinx students.

## Introduction

An enduring concern among educators, curriculum developers, and science education researchers is how to make science education relevant to students. An importan t aspect of relevance is students’ perceptions of t he meaningfulness of science, that is, its connections to their interests and experiences and to matters of importance to students’ communities (NRC, 2012). Most students, however, perceive science as important but say that school science is “not for me”. Especially important is su pporting relevance for students who are owed an “ed ucation debt” (Ladson-Billings, 2006) for their historical and continued marginalization in science, that is, students who are Black, Indigenous, People of Color, as well as students who identify as female, trans, or gender n onbinary, and students with disabilities. In many classrooms,  such students struggle to find meaning within s chool science that is disconnected from their own interests, experiences, and concerns. Science teachers need practical ways to link studen ts’ interests, concerns, and everyday experiences i n science teaching, in ways that center the experienc es of students owed an education debt in our societ y. In this study, we investigated one approach to meeting thes e needs through a collaborative professional learni ng experience for middle school science teachers. A co hort of 2 0 teachers using phenomenon-based instructional materials aligned to the Next Generation Science St andards participated in a professional learning exp erience called the Student Experience Improvement  Cycle (SEIC). In the SEIC, educators adapted and tested s trategies for promoting more equitable science experiences fo r students and monitored the effects of their strat egies using a web-based system for collecting data on student experie nces of lessons and visualizing data disaggregated by race/ethnicity and by gender. We fit hierarchical models to the data on student experience collected in the system to explore overall growth in the pe rceived relevance of science lessons and to investi gate whether students from racially and ethnically minoritized groups and students who identified as female or as gender nonbinary benefited more from their teachers’ participation in the SEIC. Specifically, teachers who participated in the SEIC had students’ perceptions of relevance increased signif icantly over time and those Black/African American students’ perceptions of experience increased significantly.

## Theoretical framework

In this paper, we define relevance as students’ own  perceptions of the meaningfulness of science instr uction for them, as well as to students’ own perceptions of the societal importance of what they are studying. We emphasize student perceptions, because they directly shape th eir engagement in science learning activities (Järv elä & Renninger, 2014). When students perceive a lesson to be relevant, they feel as though they have a good answer to the question, “Why study this?”, which in turn supp orts engagement. While there is evidence that for a ll groups of learners building such connections can help build and sustain interest in subject matter (Albrecht & Karabenick, 2018), paying close attention to the interests, experiences, and community priorities of students owed an education debt can be an important strategy for promoting equ ity. Thus, intentional practices on the part of tea chers are needed that are reparative (Reinholz & Shah, 2021),  that is, that counteract historical and continuing  inequities, in terms of whose interests, experiences, and concerns ar e centered. That is to say, evidence-informed strategies are needed that help students from nondominant grou ps and communities —such as students from Black, Indigenous, and People of Color communities and gir ls and gender nonbinary students —see in science and engineering both a “mirror” to their experience and  a “window” into a broader world connected to their in terests and concerns (cf., Gutiérrez, 2012).  Next, we share few example research strategies that informed the design of our cycle of improvement to help educators promote relevance in science.  These strategies require an explicit focus on equity by centering the experiences of nondominant students. Teaching practices to enhance relevance for students owed an education debt Prior research suggests that educators committed to  these goals can scaffold students’ perceptions of relevance through (1) anchoring instruction in meaningful phe nomena: one family of approaches to instruction that can promote a sense of relevance of science is problem-and project-based learning (Blumenfeld et al., 1991); (2) promoting equitable epistemic agency: epistemic agency involves students taking or being responsible for shaping the knowledge and practices of a classroom community (Stroupe, 2014); (3) attuning to and recognizing students’ diverse sensemaking repertoires: one strategy involves suspending evaluation of students ’ initial and emerging ideas in discussions, inviting students to “say more” in ways that help teachers understand what students are trying to communicate (Bang et al., 2017); and (4) cultiva ting equitable relationships among and with student s: several approaches, such as Complex Instruction  (Cohen et al., 1999), have proven successful in attenuating inequity within small groups when teachers address status and power directly as they manifest in small groups. The current study Despite a body of research on how to enhance relevance and how to attenuate inequities in participation that limit opportunities for interest development, there is little research in the science education on how to support teachers in promoting more equitable experiences among nondominant students. This work builds to (1) support teachers to make changes in everyday participation structure s and (2) engaging teachers in monitoring data on i nequities of student experience. Specifically, our goal was testing a strategy, the SEIC, for attenuating inequities in students’ perceptions of the relevance of instruction to them selves and their communities. In this study of the SEIC, we addressed two primary questions: 1) How did students’ perceptions  of relevance of instruction vary by lesson, student, and teacher? 2) Can a professional learnin g cycle support teachers to use student experience data to increase equity with respect to students’ perceptions of the relevance of science lessons? Description of the intervention: The student experience improvement cycle The SEIC is based on a Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycle (Bryk et al., 2015) that engages teachers in adapting research-based strategies for promoting more equitable small group and whole class knowledge-building activities (Penuel, Raza et al., 2024). As other PDSA cycles do, the SEIC also relies on tools called “practical measures,” instruments seeking to measure progress toward spec ific improvement aims that are easy to use on repea ted occasions in the context of practice (Yeager et al., 2013). In a four-session virtual series, a cohort of participating teachers investigated research-based strategies for improving equity of participat ion in science classrooms that elaborated the strategies presented in the conceptual framework. In the first session, we provided educators with a  list of strategies grouped into different categorie s that aligned with key elements of our conceptual framework. Educators  read about the strategies and annotate documents explaining how they could be integrated within their adopted i nstructional materials. In this session, they also learned about a practical measure we plan ned to use and were oriented to a web-based system that supports in collecting  and visualizing student experience data to reflect on pa tterns (Raza et al., 2024). In the second session, teachers further explored strategies and chose a group to develop a plan to test a cluster of practices from the list provided in their classroom. They cho se groups based on their interest and the specific instructional materials they used. In the third session, teachers reviewed data from their own classrooms on student experience using the visualizations from the system and discus sed the data with their group using a structured pr otocol provided by facilitators. In the fourth session, teachers reviewed and discussed new data with their peers, looking at data for each strategy group. Teachers discussed  refinements they wished to make to their strategie s before summarizing and they also discussed the broader systemic context of thei r data collection and the limits of work at the class level that does not consider societal and school-level oppression as an ongoing challenge for equity.

## Participants and source of data

Participants were middle school teachers using one of two different sets of NGSS-aligned curriculum materials, OpenSciEd and Amplify. Both sets of materials are well aligned to the vision of equitable teaching and learning outlined in A Framework for K-12 Science Education (National Research Council, 2012). We had 25 started and 20 completed the SEIC series. Of these, 14 were using the OpenSciEd instr uctional materials and six were using Amplify materials. The demographic background of the teacher cohort th at participated in the series involves 16 females and 4 males. Of the teachers, 20 identified as white, Asian American (1), Latinx/Hispanic (1). The source of data for the study was student experi ence data from “Student Electronic Exit Tickets” or SEETs. The data come from a short survey administered at the end of each lesson. The SEET was used both as a practical measure to inform educators about their own practice and as the primary research inst rument in this study. Validity evidence gathered as part of earlie r studies found that the SEET items were readily interpretable by students and responses correlated with performance on a three-dimensional assessment of student learning (Penuel et al., 2018). When registering in the SEET system  to attempt survey measure, students receive an anonymous identifier to use to log in. The exit ticket, includes a set of items focused on a dimension of experience: relevance. The three items of relevance that were used in the S EIC cycle are: 1) Today’s science lesson was personally meaningful; 2) I found today’s lesson interesting; 3) If people in my city or town understood the science we learned in today’s lesson, they would do something that could help make our city or town a better place. Approach to analysis The main outcome was growth in students’ reported r elevance of the day’s lesson, measured by the average of three relevance items. Hierarchical linear modeling (Raudenbush & Bryk, 200 2) was applied to account for the nested structure: daily student observations (exit ticket) within stu dents and students within teachers. To capture growth over time, we included DAY as a temporal predictor, defined as the number of days since each teacher’s first observation  and recentered around a common  baseline (DAY 298; O ctober 25)  for interpretability. We intentionally selected female and Latinx students as reference categories to focus on historically underrepresented groups and avoid centering dominant perspectives.  Gender and race/ethnicity variables were included a s the student-level predictors to examine equity in initial relev ance scores and growth over time. All models were fit using HLM 8.2 software with full maximum likelihood estimation.

## Results

How student experience varied by lesson, student, and classroom The final dataset contained 2,572 observations from  1,094 students nested within 48 classrooms. The av erage score of relevance was.38 (SD =.34). The unconditional means models revealed more than half of the variance in perceived relevance (53%) was at the lesson leve l. Differences in student experience of individual lessons – rather thandifferences among individuals or across classrooms – accounted for the biggest percentage of variation in students’ classroom experiences. That said, 33% of the variance in reported experience was attribut ed to the student level, while approximately 14% was due to the teacher. The variation at the student and teacher levels was of particular interest, these levels relate to stud ents’ social identities and teachers’ capacity to interve ne in ways to improve the quality of student experiences. A  consideration for equity is whether the rates of ch ange are different for individuals and classrooms that, at b aseline, have low or high levels of perceived relev ance. The estimated correlation between the baseline and grow th rates was negative at the classroom level (r = -.547) but positive at the student level (r =.226). This suggests that students who started higher grew faster over time, while classrooms that started lower may have grown at  a faster rate. This pattern shows that for individual s, those with a greater interest in lessons grew in perception of  how relevant they were to themselves and their com munities, following the pattern of a “Matthew Effect” (Merton, 1968) whereby individuals with higher quality experiences at the beginning had even better experiences after their teachers participated in  SEIC. By contrast, classrooms became more equal across the sample: there was less  variability across classrooms after participating in SEIC in students’ average quality of experience.

Modeling equity in growth in relevance To answer our second research question, we modeled growth by examining whether DAY significantly predicted student experience (see Table 1). Overall, student ratings of relevance increased significantl y over the course of the intervention (𝛽 =.001, p <.05; Model 1), equivalent to students saying “yes” for one additional relevance item (out of three) over a school year. When gender was added (Model 2), male students tended to score slightly higher at baseline (𝛽 = -.041, p =.073), while gender nonbinary students exhibited a borderline significant negativ e growth rate (𝛽 = -.003, p =.088), suggesting slower progress compared to female students. In the growth models with race/ethnicity as predictors (Model 3), there were no significant differences at baseline, except that white students tended to score slightly lower (𝛽 = -.056, p =.072). Black/African American students demonstrated significantly greater growth in relevance compared to Latinx students (𝛽 =.004, p =.008), corresponding to one more positive response over a year.  Consistent with earlier models, the full  model (Model 4), including both gender and race/ethnicity, showed that male students started slightly higher (𝛽 =.039, p =.093) than the reference group, while white students started marginally lower (𝛽 = -.053, p =.093). In terms of growth, being Black/African American was the only characteristic significantly associated with a positive individual growth rate in perceived lesson relevance (𝛽 =.004, p =.009).

## Discussion and conclusion

In this study, we investigated the potential of a collaborative teacher inquiry, the SEIC, for enhancing the relevance of science teaching for students, particularly thos e owed an education debt. We found that students’ p erceptions of relevance varied widely by lesson. We also saw growth over the course of the interventi on in students’ perceptions of relevance overall. The pattern of results was mixed with respect to the potential of the intervention to promote equity, however. While classrooms became  more equitable, in terms of students’ perceptions of relevance, individual students with greater initial p erceptions of relevance grew more than those with l ower perceptions of relevance. Black/African American students grew more than Latinx students, but the qual ity of gender nonbinary students’ experience wa s consistently lower —if not significantly so —across the cohort. The finding that students’ perceptions of relevance var ied significantly at the lesson level is consistent  with past studies’ findings that there is typically great variation in instruction and student experience from lesson to lesson. Scholars who have investigated data from observational studies have shown that there is significant variation from lesson to lesson in the quality of instruction (Praetorius et al., 2014). What is novel in our findings, it is possible to improve the quality of students’ experience, specifically their perceptions of relevance of science lessons t o them – that is, how meaningful and interesting to them the lesson was and how important they perceived the les son’s content was  to their community. Past research has shown the power of interventions targeting secondary students’ perceptions of the relevance of science (Hulleman & Harackiewicz, 2009), but no interventions have targeted teachers of students. The study also shows how elusive the goal of attenuating inequity is for all student groups. We did not observe greater gains in perceptions of relevance for Latinx students, for example, or for students who identify as female. In addition, there was evidence that the intervention may have amplified inequities  for gender nonbinary students. Also, the possibili ty that interactions in a classroom amplify inequities is ever present (Shah & Lewis, 2019). Our results highlight the promise of a cycle of imp rovement for improving the relevance of science instruction for students, especially for Black/Afri can American students. At the same time, we continu ed to observe differences in perceptions of relevance across groups. More work is needed to develop the SEIC to address the particularities of experiences of nonbinary students, as well as those of racially minoritized students. We also conjecture that more time is needed to see significant gains in perceptions of learning, and so improvement work in SEIC is unlikely to prove beneficial without deep work of forging long-term partnerships with teachers.
