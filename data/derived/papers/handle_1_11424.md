# My Beliefs Matter: Role of Teachers’ Beliefs in their Use of Analytics

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
The use of analytics to inform pedagogical decision-making is a complex socio-technical process. While research has explored how analytic data is interpreted and applied to guide instructional actions, the influence of teachers’ beliefs-a critical social factor-has received limited attention. This study examines how four instructors engage with analytics and how their beliefs shape pedagogical decision-making. Using Wise and Jung’s (2019) model of instructors’ use of analytics, we perform content analysis of interviews with the teachers to reveal their beliefs about teaching and learning. We found that beliefs shape pedagogical actions quite considerably. Based on these insights, we propose simple modifications to Wise & Jung's (2019) model to account for the critical role of teacher beliefs in analytics-informed pedagogical decision-making.

## Introduction

Learning Analytics (LA) is “the measurement, collection, analysis and reportin g of data about learners and their contexts, for purposes of understanding and o ptimizing learning and the environments in which it  occurs” (Siemens & Long, 2011). Alongside the development o f advanced methods and suite of analytics for diffe rent stakeholders-teachers being key among them-the ‘intent to use data traces from the learning contex ts to design interventions with the goal of improving the  learning’  has been recognized as equally critical (Clow, 2012). However, the ‘process’ of teachers’ use of analytics and factors affecting  it, though critical, remain relatively underexplored in LA research. Recent studies have m ade notable progress in examining how teachers use analytics (e.g., Amarasinghe et al., 2020; Mole naar & Knoop-van Campen, 2018; Wise & Jung, 2019). For instance, Wise & Jung (2019) synthesized a comprehe nsive model outlining stages of analytics use: aski ng questions, interpreting data, explaining patterns, taking action, and checking the impact. A common thread in these studies is the focus on un derstanding how teachers make sense of analytics and take pedagogical decisions. However, analytics “exist as part of a socio-technical system where humandecision making and consequent actions are as much a part of any successful analytics solution as the technical components” (Van Harmelen & Workman, 2012, p.4). Decision-making is influenced not only by technology (e.g., perceived usefulness of LA tools, Mavroudi e t al., 2021) but also by social factors such as tea cher beliefs, values, and culture. These social factors are likel y to influence how analytics translates into locall y meaningful knowledge and decisions (Kitto et al., 2018; Wise & Vytasek, 2017). Despite acknowledging the socio-technical nature of analytics (Van Harmelen & Workman, 2012) and the well-established role of teacher beliefs in decision-making (e.g., Fives et al., 2014; Pajares, 1992), their role in analytics use remains underexplored. This study explores the role of teachers’ beliefs in the use of analytics a nd pedagogical decision making within the context of Tcherly, a feedback system for video lectures. Tcherly cap tures voluntary, real-time, and anonymous student self-reports of cognitive-affective states (Difficult, Easy, Boring, and Enga ging-DEBE) (Chavan et a l., 2018; Mitra & Chavan, 2019) and  reasons, while watching a video lecture (Figure 1a). The aggregated feedback is presented on a teacher-facing dashboard (Figure 1b) (Chavan & Mitra, 2022). Using Wise & Jung's (2019) model  as an analytical framework, we explore teachers’ us e of Tcherly analytics and pedagogical decision making process. Our findings reveal how teac hers’ beliefs about teaching,  learning, and students emerge through their engagement with analytics and,  in turn, influence their pedagogical responses. Exploring this intersection of teacher beliefs and learning analytics deepens understanding of analytics-informed pedagogical decision making, and also results in extension of Wise & Jung's (2019) model. Analytical framework: Wise and Jung’s (2019) model of instructors’ process of analytics use The model of instructors’ analytics use (see Figure  2, left) proposed by Wise & Jung (2019) outlines a ctivities structured within the two-part framework of sense-making and pedagogical response. It provides a comprehensive framework for analyzing instructors’ analytics use in detail. In contrast to the general  teacher inquiry model, which starts with problem identifica tion leading to the inquiry questions, the model su ggests multiple possible starting points, such as pre-defined question(s), areas of curiosity, and interactions with data. Sense-making consists of two components: reading data and explaining patterns. Reading data is about how the instructor makes sense of the data-whether they start with a broad orientation or atte nd to a specific piece of data to understand the bigger picture-and with ‘what’ the data are compared (reference points). The second component is explaining patterns. Instructor s often triangulate the meaning behind a datum and connect observations in the data to their context. The mode l also highlights the affective processes (e.g., triggering disappointment, joy, etc.). In the model, pedagogical response  follows sense-making and includes three components: take action, wait-and-see approach, and reflect on pedagogy. The ‘take action’ component shows three actions prevalent in the literature, illustrating how instructors react to learning anal ytics. The ‘wait and see,’ arises from uncertainty when data is inconclu sive, leading instructors to delay action until fur ther confirmation. ‘Reflection on pedagogy’ goes beyond using analytics to just tweak small parts of a course, where instructors think much more globally about their teaching practice, course design, or student learning. ## Methods

The study was conducted with four instructors (thre e males, one female) in authentic teaching-learning settings. Three taught UG mechanical engineering courses at a private enginee ring institute in India (face-to-face), and one taught an online UG environmental science cours e (online) as adjunct faculty at a public instituti on in India while serving as full-time faculty in the United States. To familiarize p articipants with Tcherly, demonstrations of the student feedback interface (Figure 1a) and t he teacher-facing dashboard (Figure 1b) were provided. Instructors designed flipped lessons, including cre ating or curating video lectures for pre-class activities and designing quizzes or classroom activities for face-to-face or live sessions. Videos were uploaded to Tcherly and shared with students (viewing window: 1 –3 days). Instructors analyzed dashboard feedback a day before the classroom session or on the morning of an afternoon  session and decided on refinements to in-class activities or future course offerings. None had experience using dashboard analytics. Informed consent was obtained. Two weeks later, retrospective semi-structured interviews were conducted, focusing on instructors’ interpretations of analytics, observations, and ins tructional decisions (they did a walkthrough of the ir process of using dashboard). Interview data were coded using d eductive and inductive content analysis. Themes fro m Wise & Jung (2019, p.59) guided deductive coding, while new codes were generated inductively. All interview s were manually transcribed and read several times. Inter-coder reliability (Cohen’s kappa = 0.76) was establ ished through practice and pilot coding (20% data) with a nother researcher, followed by independent coding o f one interview by both primary and secondary coder. The primary coder then coded the remaining interviews.

## Discussion and conclusion

This study reveals the critical role instructors’ beliefs play in shaping pedagogical responses to analytics. We propose two additions to Wise & Jung's (2019) model: 'no action' as a pedagogical response and ‘instru ctor beliefs’ as an overarching factor influencing all p edagogical responses (Figure 2).  Previous research (Molenaar & Knoop-van Campen, 2017) has also considered 'no action' (defined as ‘teache r not acting after consulting the dashboard’) as a valid pedagogical response. The need for incorporation of ‘no action’ is far fr om trivial as making such choices explicit in the model would nud ge future researchers to consider questions related  to why it occurs and how it might be addressed. Furthermor e, our results indicate that both ‘no action’ and other pedagogical actions (e.g., simplifying complex term s, re-explaining concepts, or designing new activities) w ere influenced by instructors’ beliefs about teaching a nd learning (Fives et al., 2014) and  students’ cap abilities and needs (Fives & Buehl, 2016). We therefore propose a dding ‘Instructor Beliefs’ as a bounding box within  which all pedagogical responses play out (Figure 2). This  demonstrates that social factors like teacher beli efs could play a critical role in their use of analytics, and  hence in supporting or improving student learning.  Wise and Jung’s (2019) model does not explicitly account for  the role of beliefs. These modifications serve a purpose beyond mere accuracy or completeness, as they contr ibute to a more nuanced understanding of how teache r beliefs mediate analytics-informed instruction and its broader implications for research and practice. Finally, despite the evidence presented in support of our primary as sertions, the small sample size and scope of the study limit  the generalizability of these results. Future research with larger and more diverse samples is necessary to validat e these findings and  uncover additional factors. Such studies would be highly valuable to the community as not only would they va lidate what we have proposed but they could also re veal additional factors relevant to the premise of such studies.
