## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper demonstrates how pedagogical practices that are deemed to be equitable may lead to both inclusion and exclusion effects. We present an illustrative vignette of a mathematics lesson. Problems and objectives There is widespread agreement that equity issues should be centered in recent educational reform efforts. Despite many discussions around “equitable sensemaking” and “equitable practices,” however, scholarships around equity in mathematics education have scrutinized that peda gogical practices that claim to be equitable may in stead reinforce systemic exclusion of certain kinds of students. For example, pedagogical practices that are designed to enable student intellectual agency may operate in w ays that perpetuate the assumption of ability hiera rchies in mathematics. Such exclusion is tied to larger discourse that is historically and systemically shaped—what Louie

(2017) has termed “the culture of exclusion in math ematics education.” This calls into question what c ounts as

“equitable” in mathematics learning spaces.

In this paper, we examine how pedagogical practices  that are deemed to be advancing equity may inadvertently lead to both inclusion and exclusion effects. The following question guides this study: What are the tensions of supporting equity during sense-making activities in the classroom? In particular, we demonstrate how practices that are claimed to be supporting epistem ic agency can function in ways that reinscribe hist oric and systemic assumptions of what counts as mathematical thinking and ideal students.

## Theoretical Framework

We adopt the theoretical construct “inscription pra ctice” to elucidate how inclusion and exclusion can paradoxically occur during mathematics learning. Inscription refers to the process that a set of taken-for-granted values and orders are constantly enacted and reinfo rced, and the boundaries that define these values a nd orders are historically resembled (Popkewitz, 2004). Inscription is built upon a historicizing approach that juxtapose past and present (Foucault, 1977) to examine the set of assumptions that fabricate what is desired and make  up particular kinds of people as ideal citizens. Mathe matics classrooms are fundamental sites where inscr iption practices operate due to the assumptions of what counts as legitimate knowledge and sense-making in mathematics (Yolcu & Popkewitz, 2018). While valuing intellectual resources that students bring into classrooms and treating students as agents who are capable of making sense of mathematics is important, what remains at issue is the set of taken-for-granted assumptions that is constantly enacted and paradoxically denied students who act outside of the boundaries of these assumptions.

## Methods

The study was conducted at a public elementary school located in a town in the United States Midwest. Slightly more than half of students were identified as economically disadvantaged, 40% as English learners, and 15% as students with disabilities. Fieldnotes were collected in Ms. Zoe’s fourth-grade classroom (9-and 10-year-olds). A total of 17 weeks of data was collected To explore how inscription practices occur, we adopt a historically-comparative approach (Stinson, 2017) to analyze the sense-making activities taking places in mathematics lessons. Classroom vignettes were analyzed with two different lenses. First, we identified the vignettes in which Ms. Zoe demonstrated substantive support of student epistemic agency. Second, we investigated t he historically-resembled assumptions about mathema tics abilities and learning manifested in these vignette s through building on the existing historicizing st udies in mathematics education.

## Findings and Discussion

The vignette records interaction between Ms. Zoe and four students: Elli ot and Ricky (African American boys), Janet (an Afr ican American girl), and Taevy (a Cambodian girl).

Valuing student epistemic resources and sharing epistemic agency Ms. Zoe set up a context for the students to make sense of skip-counting. She showed the students a box of 24 crayons. She guided the students starting wit h counting one row of crayons in the box (8 crayons) and proceeded to counting two rows (8x2 crayons). Then, she asked the group how they counted all of the crayons in the box. The students came up with different strate gies of skip-counting. Elliot and Ricky counted it by 8, and Taevy counted it by 3. Ms. Zoe affirmed each of the ir ideas and ensured that their ideas were made pub lic. She repeated, clarified the ideas, and wrote them down on her white board (e.g., She asked Janet, “So you kind of use the same method like Elliot, right? Did you count 1 7, 18, 19, … after 16?”). Different skip-counting s trategies were affirmed. Then, Ms. Zoe introduced number-lines as a strategy to make sense of skip-counting. She handed Elliot her marker, asked him to draw his strategy of counting by 8. Then, Ms. Zoe drew Janet’ strategy, and asked Elliot to draw Taevy’s strategy of counting by 3. Before the end of the lesson, Ms. Zoe asked the students to draw their own strategies in their notebooks. Ms. Zoe po sitioned her students as sense-makers rather than knowledge absorbers; she not only invited students’ ideas but  also allowed them to keep the ownership of their i deas. The ways in which Ms. Zoe supported her students are co nsistent with scholarship that suggests fostering s tudents’ epistemic agency as essential in supporting equitab le sense-making because it disrupts the conventiona l assumption that students’ ideas are inferior.

Inscribing what counts as mathematics abilities Elliot was the only one given the authority to hold the marker and show his idea. This shift of epistemic authority from Ms. Zoe to Elliot explicitly positioned him as intellectually capable. The history of the class suggests ongoing appreciation of Elliot’s mathematical strengths. Elliot always finished his tasks accurately and faste r than other students, and Ms. Zoe would push him to work on extra problems that were more challenging. Elliot fit in with conventional conceptions of a go od sense-maker in school mathematics. He not only solved problems quickly and accurately but was also good at asserting his ideas in public. He was always able to follow the problem contexts that Ms. Zoe set up for  students to learn mathematical strategies, just li ke what occurred in this vignette. The other students in th is group, however, did not quite fit in with such f abrication of good sense-makers. Both Janet and Taevy worked at a  more deliberate pace, and Ricky often stopped work ing and got frustrated when encountering a difficult problem. The values and expectations of mathematics sense-making that were manifested in this vignette do not simply function locally. These values resemble historic assumptions around to how students should participate in mathematics learning and what mathematical thinking is desired. Students whose ways of knowing and learning fit in with these assumptions have more opportunities to be positioned as good sense-makers, while students who act outside of these assumptions are frequently described as “at risk,” “disabled,” “unmotivated,” “disadvantage,” “not producing” (Kirchgasler, 2017). We call into q uestion the assumptions regarding what counts as va luable thinking in mathematics classrooms along with preva iling discourse of epistemic agency. Our analysis o f inscription practice suggests a need of complicating understanding of supporting equity in mathematics education.
