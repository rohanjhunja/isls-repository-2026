# A First Grader Making Sense of Graphs: A Knowledge-in-Pieces Account of Functional Thinking

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We present the case of Lucca (pseudonym), a six-year-old who participated in three interviews before, during, and after a classroom teaching experiment on function graphs. We analyzed his reasoning by identifying the resources he drew on: seeds of algebraic thinking and resources tied to the graph. Lucca’s functional thinking can be understood through the lens of Knowledge in Pieces: recursive and functional reasoning was supported by his coordination of resources. As his functional thinking developed, the seeds he used remained consistent, but the resources tied to the graph he coordinated were different.

A central goal of early algebra is to support students in reasoning about relationships between quantities (Blanton et al., 2015; Kaput, 2008). Functional thinking, understood as generalizing correspondences between co-varying quantities, is foundational to early algebra (Stephens et al., 2017). Function graphs provide a representational space where students can reason about relationships and make generalizations (Radford, 2025), yet little research has examined how young children construct and interpret function graphs. In this paper, we analyze the case of Lucca (pseudonym), a first-grade student who participated in a Classroom Teaching Experiment (CTE). We draw on the Knowledge in Pieces (KiP) perspective (diSessa, 2018) to analyze the resources that supported Lucca’s functional thinking while working with function graphs. From a KiP perspective, knowledge consists of small, intuitive elements that are context-sensitive (diSessa, 2018). These pieces can be reorganized into more stable systems over time, allowing continuity between novice and expert reasoning. By resources, w e mean fine-grained ideas or actions that students draw on as they work (diSessa, 1993). Most research on resources has examined older students with extensive experience in a domain (e.g., Jones & Rodríguez, 2023; Rodríguez & Jones, 2024; Sherin, 2001). At the age of Lucca (six years old), we expect resources to be more varied and idiosyncratic than in older students. We use the term resources to describe Lucca’s ideas or actions that he used across moments in the interviews, and had a plausible genesis in prior experience. Some of the resources we identified are seeds of algebraic thinking (Levin & Walkoe, 2022; López et al., 2025), small-scale intuitive actions such as classifying, pairing, replacing, and transforming that students develop through early experiences inside and outside of school. Consistent with Levin and Walkoe (2022), we treat seeds not as misconceptions to be replaced, but as beginnings of algebraic ideas that can reappear across contexts. Other resources emerged as Lucca engaged with the graph itself. Sherin (2001) showed that students learn to see meaning in representational structure by pairing features they notice with ideas about how the world works. Jones and Rodríguez (2023) extended this insight to graphs, proposing graphical resources as knowledge elements students use when graphing. We take inspiration from this work: the resources Lucca drew on when engaging with the graph can be understood as pairings between features he noticed (e.g., grid lines, intersections, intervals) and everyday ideas (e.g., following a path, things meeting at a corner, skipping along a line). Seeds are the actions Lucca performed with these pairings: he used pairing to connect the quantities the graph features made visible, transforming to reason about how  those quantities changed, and classifying  to identify what each axis represented. Since the goal of the CTE was to support functional thinking, we relied on Blanton et al.’s (2015) learning trajectory. At the recursive-general level, students articulate a rule for successive growth across values. At the condensed functional-general level, they explicitly identify the generalized transformation connecting all input-output values. Blanton et al. (2015) conceptualize their trajectory not as rigid levels but a s flexible, bidirectional descriptions of reasoning, a perspective we see as consistent with KiP. We aimed to answer the following research questions: (1) What resources does a first-grade student draw on across three interviews with function graphs? (2) How do those resources interact to support different levels of functional thinking?

## Method

2833 This study draws from a CTE conducted in an elementary school in the Northeastern United States. First-grade students participated in 16 lessons on functional relationships through manipulatives, tables, and graphs. We structured the CTE around relationships such as y=x+x, embedded in contexts meaningful to students (e.g., birds & wings, sandwiches & bread). Lucca participated in three individual interviews (20-25 minutes each): before, during, and after the CTE. We selected Lucca because his interviews offered rich moments where resources were reorganized in relation to functional thinking. In each interview, a researcher asked Lucca to reason about the relationship between the number of birds and wings (y=x+x), record his responses in a table, and interpre t or construct a function graph. We documented Lucca’s verbal explanations, written work, and gestures, and worked through each interview asking: What is Lucca attending to right now? What idea does he appear to draw on? We considered an idea to be a resou rce when it recurred across moments, did specific work in the task, and had a plausible genesis in prior experience. We also coded moments when Lucca’s reasoning was consistent with a functional thinking level as described in Blanton et al.’s (2015) learning trajectory. Across the three interviews, we identified eight resources that Lucca drew on while reasoning with function graphs. Five align with seeds of algebraic thinking as defined by Levin and Walkoe (2022): classifying, pairing, replacing, transforming, and comparing. Three additional resources emerged from Lucca’s engagement with the graph. These are pairings between features of the graph that Lucca noticed and everyday ideas he connected to them: guidelines to connect quantities, in which Lucca paired grid lines with the idea of following a path from one quantity to another; point as intersection, in which he paired the crossing of horizontal and vertical lines with the idea that two things meet at a corner; and spaces mean skip, in which he paired intervals on the axis with the idea of skipping along a number line.  In the first interview, Lucca used these resources to interpret a preconstructed graph, but his reasoning did not yet reach a level of functional thinking as described by Blanton et al. (2015). We observed functional thinking in the second and third interviews, which we describe below. In the second interview, Lucca reasoned about a preconstructed graph using classifying, pairing, replacing, and transforming. He was using guidelines to connect quantities  when tracing lines from one axis to another, point as intersection  when he explained that a point lies where horizontal and vertical lines cross, and spaces mean skip when he attended to the space between two and four on the y-axis, noting that three wings could not be represented. Lucca demonstrated recursive-general thinking when he answered, “You get plus two more,” articulating a general rule that applied across all cases. His transforming expressed the recursive growth, pairing coordinated each input with its output, and spaces mean skip  provided a spatial strategy for tracking how each value increased.

In the third interview, Lucca used classifying, pairing, replacing, and transforming as he constructed his own graph. He was using guidelines to connect quantities  when he drew vertical and horizontal lines and placed a point at each intersection, and point as intersection  when he justified a placement with “Because it should go on the corner.” Lucca demonstrated condensed functional-general reasoning when he responded, “I double the number of wings,” and indicated this on his graph by gesturing along drawn lines and locating points at their intersections. Here, transforming expressed the functional relationship, while guidelines to connect quantities reinforced the connection between the two variables.

Across both interviews, the seeds Lucca used remained consistent. What changed was how he interacted with the graph. In the second interview, spaces mean skip  directed Lucca’s attention to the spatial intervals between successive values, which focused transforming on how each value increased. In the third, guidelines to connect quantities directed his attention to the correspondence between values on the two axes, which supported pairing and transforming in ways that produced a general functional description. The features of the graph that Lucca attended to shaped which pairings became active, and those pairings in turn supported different levels of functional thinking. Seeds do not disappear or get replaced; their function depends on how they interact with the context. Consistent with Levin and Walkoe (2022), progress reflects a reorganization of how knowledge elements interact rather than their replacement. We see functional thinking as supported in the moment by the interaction of resources. A KiP account explains the flexibility described by Blanton et al. (2015) as the coordination of resources. Recursive and functional ways of reasoning were not mutually exclusive levels but different ways of coordinating resources. Lucca’s case shows that a resources-based analysis is productive even at the earliest grades: it makes visible the specific ideas that supported his reasoning and explains how those ideas interacted to produce different levels of functional thinking. With repeated shared experiences, the resources we observed may converge into more recognizable categories across individuals.
