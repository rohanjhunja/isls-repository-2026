# Learning to See Beyond Anthropocentric Views on Bodies Through Transdisciplinary Soil-Centered Pedagogy

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
To examine how bodies were conceptualized by children and teachers who were co-learning, we analyzed videos, photos, and drawings that children, teachers, and researchers collectively created during a program that we designed with a soil-centered pedagogy. In our transdisciplinary program design, we centered intergenerational land-based wisdoms of communities with experiences of forced displacement in relation to soil, fungi, animals, and plants. Our findings depicted the ways in which the notion of bodies was expanded through the participants’ interactions on the land. As previously hidden connections became visible, children and teachers both came to see the interconnectedness between human bodies and the bodies of nonhumans, such as mushrooms and soil. This paper calls for our field to further amplify its learning to see the interconnectedness between more-than-human bodies and human bodies, thus moving beyond anthropocentrism.

Extending the conceptualization of “bodies” in the learning sciences In the field of learning sciences, scholarship on embodied learning  has collectively challenged the separation of body and mind assumed in Cartesiandualism. Critical studies on bodies/embodiment in learning have more fully considered embodied experiences of racial subjugation  (Gholson & Martin, 2019), the geopolitical contexts surrounding bodies (Krishnamoorthy & Ma, 202; Takeuchi & Aquino Ishiha ra, 2021), trails of ethics and care through embodied interactions (Vossoughi et al., 20 20), and listening to the lands that historicize ou r bodily movement (Marin & Bang, 2018). In embodied intergenerational movement on the land, fr om an Indigenous epistemology/ontology, Marin and Bang (2018) demonstrated that human bodies are considered to always be in relationship with lands/water and more-than-humans. Settler colonialism and violence, which continue to harm subjugated human bodies, also equally damage land, water, and more-than-humans (Betasamosake Simpson, 2017). The healing of sickened humans can never be complete without healing the damaged planetary health (Redvers et al., 2022). This paper calls for our field to further amplify the act of learning as seeing and caring for the interconnectedness between nonhuman bodies and huma n bodies, thereby enabling us to  inch toward socioenvironmental justices.

In this Short Paper, we provide a snapshot of the learning that we  immersed ourselves in through our ongoing project, which we call “Soil Camp”  (https://www.soilcamp.ca). Soil Camp started as a summer camp research–design partnership on a community urban farm during  the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 in response to an invitation from our community partner at a local  refugee resettlement agency. Our soil-centered pedagogy draws from the intergenerational land-based wisdoms of families with farming backgrounds, focusing on f ood, plants, and soil. Socioenvironmental visions for reconnection to the land, soil, plants, animals, and each other are at the root of our network of teachers, community workers, researchers, youth, and children who have experienced forced displacement. Aligned with the Calls to Acti on proposed by the Truth and Reconciliation Commiss ion of Canada, we facilitate the creation of space for newcomers in Canada to learn about settler-colonial histories while also learning the worldviews rooted in local Indigenous communities, particularly those related to the soil. Instead of reinforcing hierarchical relationships between a teacher and students, we aim to foster spaces where adults and children learn together, moving  toward something unknown to us all. We challenge the institutional dichotomy imposed between teachers and students as well as that between researchers and the researched. Our programming strives for transdisicplinarity as an act of liberating disciplinary norms and colo nial knowledge prod uction, committing to questions greater than the discipline itself, and aiming for the emergence of new epistemological and ontological realities by embracing historically  silenced voices (Gordon, 2006; Takeuchi & Marin, 2022). In the face of the unknown, which is larger than us and continually emerging, we are all co-learners learning together. In our vision for design, our goal in this paper is to document how we collectively extended the notion of the body beyond anthropocentric views that center and prioritize human beings (McDaid Barry et al., 2023; Tsing, 2015).

## Methodology

We draw from social design research methodology (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016), which centers historicity, diversity, equity, and ecological resilience as design principles. Social design research also aims to co-design just practices and futures in partnership with a range of communities. Children and youth were positioned as active contributors to the documentation of learning alongside the research te am across three iterations of S oil Camp between 2021 and 2023. We explained what we were documenting with various cameras and notebooks and why to child and youth participants and then invited them to document what they came to see and feel on the land. We used multiple modes of documentation: Handycam  video cameras, Go Pro cameras, still cameras, and notebooks. The  video data were collected using three modes (Go  Pros worn by both the participants and researchers, Hand ycams held by the participants and researchers, and still cameras on tripods in the primary spaces of i nteraction). Over the past 3 years, a total of 94 children (aged 5–15 years) and 33 facilitators, including 20 teachers/teacher candidates, joined our program.  A total of 157 hours and 19 minutes of video data were collected. For the analysis,  we completed content logging of the video data collectively. Then, we identified key themes for analysis, reflecting on both our lived experiences of learning on the ground and the video data that we reviewed. We treated photos, drawings, and videos that documented land-based interactions as gifts given to us, in trust of our care (Marin, 2020). For our analyses, we focused on how children’s ways of seeing “bod ies” and “embodiment” came to disrupt the dichotomy of life and nonlife or human and nonhuman (TallBear, 2015). Our attention was drawn to the embodied stories that foreground the vibrancies of more-than-human worlds and their entanglements with children (Nxumalo & Rubin, 2018). In the analysis  process, we were drawn to how the children communicated about their expansive views on “bodies” to us with the videos, photos, and drawings they shared. We analyzed relevant data that were marked by the participants’ use of the key term “body” or any mention of body parts to understand land-based interactions that had influenced their views.

## Snapshots of findings

As this is a Short Paper, we provide some snapshots to illuminate selected stories. Before we present these snapshots, we provide a sketch of the meanings of the land for the children with whom we worked to contextualize the experiences of the learning that we shared.

Meaning of the land for children Our collaborators, who are social workers and work closely with children in the resettlement process, described the meaning of the land for these children as follo ws: “There are so many parallels we candraw in terms of  the impacts of colonialism between Indigenous communities and refugee communities—displacement and a sense of belonging. For example, some Yazidi refugees are Indigenous to their land in their country of origin.  […] It was so nice to see how these refugee children expressed a sense of belonging and reconnection to the land through the program.” As we welcomed these children into our program, we observed their embodied connection to the land, soil, and plants. On a spring day, as we prepared t o plant seeds and seedlings, many children brought excitement and joy to touching the soil, earthworms, and larvae while naming the plants they would like to grow. The plants named by the children were the ones that their fami lies used to grow in their homeland. Tela and Naza (all children’s names are pseudonyms) told us that the vastness of the community urban farm reminded them of home, though the land they used to reside on was much big ger. As we touched the soil together, they shared w hat the land they hailed from looked like: “Back home, we used to have a big land. […] We owned the land; we used to own the land. There were almond trees, you know, th ese green almond trees… we had cherry trees, lots o f other things. We saw lots of snakes and wild animals.” They also said that while their family used to “grow everything,” corn was one of the major plants in their community. As Author 1 shared corn seeds, Tela, Naza, and ot her children became excited, started to talk among themselves in the Kurmanji language, and told the facilitators that they would like to plant corn.

Seeing a mushroom’s body During our shared walk, m ushrooms were one of the most frequently photographed objects by children on the land (Figure 1). The mystery of mushrooms popping up on the land seemed to evoke curiosity among the children about w here the mushrooms c ame from. Children walked around the land and looked for mush rooms. The facilitators and children alike became curious to kn ow where the mushrooms came from. Extending further on this curiosity, Tatenda, who had been growing mushrooms using used coffee grounds, facilitated a workshop on mushroom bag making  through the lens of sustainability studies. The workshop raised various questions from both teachers and children. They asked whether mushrooms are plants, if mushroom s have seeds, and what mushrooms eat to grow. Seeing the process of mushroom bag making led to a conversation between Aafia, Bahisha, and Miwa on decomposition. Together, we looked closely at the mushroom bag w here mycelium was already growing in used  coffee ground s and wood chips as a substrate. Both Aafia and Bahisha took out magnifying glasses and observed the mycelium closely. After closely examining the mushroom bag (far-left panel in Figure 1), Aafia took out her notebook and drew an image (far-right panel in Figure 1) and explained the graphic symbol that she had depicted as follows: “She eats dead bodies when we are dead, and other things when they are dead.” Aafia’s drawing and graphic symbol communicated her story of the mushroom’s body. Noteworthily, she depicted the mushroom as having a body part that was invisible when we initially found mushrooms on the land. The body of the mushroom (mycelium) became visible through our conversations and a workshop. The connection between human and mushroom bodies went beyond anthropocentric, capitalistic relations (Tsing, 2015): humans were not depicted as subject s to consume commercially circulated mushroom s. Aafia’s image of the mushroom body accurately highlighted the fact that fungi candecompose human bodies as well as other bodies. As seen in Aafia’s choice of pronoun (i.e., “she”), the mushroom came to be seen as an agentive body (McDaid Barry et al., 2023).

Wh en we started our program, the most frequent response that we heard from children and facilitators to the question “What is soil?” was “dirt.” The ways of seeing the soil as discusse d in the following story significantly differ from such a disembodied understanding of the soil.  In an activity titled Light of Plants and Soil, we explored questions such as “Can soil communicate?” Nicole and Janice, teachers specialized in mathematics, invited children to exp lore this question with a robot that Nicole had been programming for over 2 years with Arduino. The robot converts electricity detected through electrodes into light (far-left panel in Figure 2). Both the children and facilitators gathered around the robot and attached electrodes to leaves, soil, and their own hands. In one interaction, a group of young children (aged 4–9 years) decided to place the electrodes in the soil. Noticing how moist soil can get as they dig deeper, the children placed the electrodes deeper into the soil. As Sarya placed the electrodes at the roots of plants, where they touch the soil, the light bulbs on the robot  lit up. This caused both the children and the teacher facilitators  to get excited. Sarya decided to open her notebook and started to  draw while posing the following question: “If the soil is alive, how does it have the heart... it’s all soil?” In response, a teacher facilitator affirmed that humans, plants, and soil all have “st uff inside keeping us alive.” While continuing to d raw the soil (far-right panel in Figure 2), Sarya made the following prediction: “I think I know, there is a heart inside, its tiny, so there is a little part of the soil if its cut in  half, actually the heart is in there —really tiny like so tiny.” By “seeing” the invisible connectors (in this case ele ctrons) through our pedagogical design, the children and facilitators alike came to see how our bodies, plan ts, and the soil share commonalities. ## Discussion

A glimpse of our learning, documented in this paper, indicates how both the children and facilitators came to see the interconnectedness between human bodies and the  bodies of nonhumans, such as mushrooms and soil. O ur pedagogical design was guided by our vision of making visible what was previously invisible to many of us—the mycelium network that grows under the mushroom or electrons traveling through human bodies, soil, and plants. In the process of learning and walking together on the land (Marin & Bang, 2018), we collectively chal lenged Eurocentric epistemology and ontology that have for long assumed the dichotomy of human and nonhumans as well as human superiority over nonhumans (Betasamosake Simpson, 2017; Tallbear, 2017). As one child vividly highlighted by saying “she [the mushroom] eats dead bodies when we are dead,” the relationships between humans and nonhumans/more-than-humans are tangled and interconnected, where the su periority of humans cannot be assumed. Facing ongoing human-caused environmental damages and ongoing violence, we need to fundamentally shift colonial and anthropocentric epistemology and  ontology toward our collective visions for healing  (Redvers et al., 2022). Through walking on the land, we have learned and to continue to learn with children how the healing of human bodies and the healing of more-than-human bodies are intertwined.
