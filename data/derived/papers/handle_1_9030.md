# How Young Children Pursue Their Interests With Questions

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This case study is an investigation of how children pursue their interests and concerns in interaction with adults. This processual focus led us to study children’s spontaneous questions, which have mostly been at the margins of research on children’s learning and development. Our analysis found that questions are important interactional tools that allow children to simultaneously pursue their interests and concerns while also working within the familiar asymmetries of adult caregiver and child.

## Introduction

One of Piaget’s insightful ideas was that “There is  no better introduction to child logic than the stu dy of spontaneous questions” (Piaget, 1923, p. 162). Piaget himself did not really study children’s spon taneous questions. Instead, he favored making inferences about children from their spontaneous answers to his questions and the tasks he posed for children. Mostly, developmental psychology followed Piaget in giving limited attention to naturalistic observation, favoring Piagetian style clinical interviews or experimental tasks as preferred methods. As a result, the topic of children’s spontaneous questions has been largely at the margins of the study of children. This study seeks to make children’s spontaneous que stions and their role in children’s lives and their  learning more central.

More broadly, this study seeks to contribute to a line of research that establishes children’s interests and concerns as a core topic for the learning sciences (Stevens, 2020). Our goal is to understand what children’s interests and concerns are and how children pursue these interests and concerns. This processual focus has led us to children’s questions. In this paper we build on an original in sight of one of the founders of conversation analys is, Harvey Sacks, who identified a type of question children u se that a) indicates an understanding of some of th e basic machinery of conversation (i.e., adjacency pairs), b) recognizes their unequal rights in interaction with adults, and

c) pursues their interests (e.g., getting ‘the floo r’ in conversation with adults) in the context of t his recognition.

When children want the floor, they will often pose the question (to a parent or other adult caregiver), “Guess what?” That question invites an adjacent ‘answer’ in the form of another question from the adult, “What?”. This responding question from the adult is then a first part of a new adjacency pair and the second part an swer from the child is expected and gives them an open floor to share what they want to share (Sacks, 1972). We wondered: what are some of the other ways that children use spontaneous questions? What occasions spontaneous questions, what work do they do in interaction, and what do they tell us about children, particularly when embedded in the context of extended interactional sequences that in volve joint projects and joint inquiry with adults (Goodwin, 2007; Keifert & Stevens, 2019; Stevens, Keifert, Davis, & Mehus, 2012)? We analyzed four cases from one family, centered around a 4-year-old boy named Ben, demonstrating how Ben uses questions to initiate, secure, and pursue his learning and project interests with his adult c aregivers. In our discussion, we explore how these cases may generalize to other children and in differing family arrangements.

## Data collection

The case study of Ben and his family is drawn from a large corpus of recordings collected with pre-school aged focal participants in their homes and at their preschools. The goal of this larger study was to directly investigate, rather than speculate about, how different home and school contexts organized learning and participation and how children responded. The methods that we used to analyze four segments selected from the recordings made with Ben’s family involve interaction analysis of ethnographically collected video recordings (Hall & Stevens, 2015; Stevens, 2020). To select children’s questions out of the much larger data corpus, we used previously coded content logs that identified children’s questions. We then created a computer program to extract the p ortions of the content logs that identified children’s questio ns and then looked at the frequency and density of questions within families and across families. This allowed us to identify extended segments of the recordings to transcribe and from these transcripts we selected the segments considered here. ## Discussion

In academic psychology, children’s questions have received little attention despite Piaget’s endorsement of what they might reveal. When they have been considered, they are almost always framed in narrowly cognitive terms, as information seeking utterances (Chouinard, Harris, & Maratsos, 2007; Davis, 1932). While we don’t d ispute this characterization, this analysis and others (Stevens, 2020; Keifert & Stevens, 2019; Stevens, Penney, Keifert, Davis, & Mehus, 2012) enlarge it significantly, suggesting that children are using request-centered questions as “social action” (Goodwin, 1990)—to organize and direct activities in their social and material worlds. Questions were interactional tools that allowed Ben to work around and through the familiar asymmetries of adult caregiver and child to pursue his interests and concerns. Of course, these examples are drawn from only one home and we expect important variations within families (Keifer t, 2020), across families with different cultural b ackgrounds and families with different kin relations. Directed  towards understanding what young peoples’ interests and concerns are and how they pursue them in their everyday lives, this stu dy contributes to a line of research that establishes children’s’ interests and concerns as a core topic for the learning sciences (Stevens, 2020).
