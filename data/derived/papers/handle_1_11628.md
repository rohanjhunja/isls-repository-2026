# Exploring Teachers’ Value Beliefs about AI in K-12 Education

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As artificial intelligence (AI) technology advances rapidly, teachers are required to critically assess and incorporate it into their classrooms. However, since teachers have concerns and biases about AI’s role in education, they hesitate to adopt AI. Teachers’ value beliefs are thought to have a positive influence on their technology integration. Exploring in-service teachers’ value beliefs about AI in K-12 education, this study identified how they evaluate AI and how they want to use AI pedagogically.

## Introduction

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) technology calls for thoughtful and critical approaches to its integration to realize effective but critical ways of using it  in K-12 education, and teachers are at the core of this practice. It was reported that teachers recognize both the benefits and limitations of AI and hesitate to adopt it due to the concerns that AI can cause in the classroom, such as cheating (Bae et al., 2024). Teachers’ value beliefs, which are defined as their tacit assumptions about how valuable a task is, are noticeable since value beliefs indicate teachers’ perception about how technology can help them achieve instructional goals (Cheng & Xie, 2018; Ottenbreit-Leftwich et al., 2010; Vongkulluksn et al., 2018). Value beliefs, such as intrinsic, utility (or extrinsic), and attainment values,  moderate external barriers that teachers perceive a nd can positively influence t heir technology integration (Cheng et al., 2020; Cheng &  Xie, 2018; Vongkulluksn et al., 2018). Value beliefs can also encourage teachers to sophisticate how to meaningfully teach students with technology  (Bowman et al., 2022). Therefore, investigating teachers’ value beliefs can help teacher educators to understand and identify what features of AI motivate teachers to incorporate it into their classrooms and how they would like  to implement it for their classroom teaching.  In this context, this study explored the value beliefs that in-service K-12 teachers developed through a graduate-level course where they studied the technological, social, and ethical aspects of AI.

## Methods

In this study, the participants were 27 in-service teachers enrolled in a graduate-level class called “Intro to AI in Education.” This course provided an overview of  fundamental concepts of AI technology, its applicat ions, and ethical considerations in the educational context, with a focus on how to incorporate AI into their cl assrooms by critically evaluating AI’s potential for K-12 education. Teachers engaged in education-related AI applications and participated in group discussions about the technological, social, and ethical aspects of AI. At the end of the class, they were asked to submit reflection papers on their learning experiences by answering questions, such as “What key insights or concepts did you gain from the course material, discussions, and activities?” and “How have these insights influenced your understanding of AI’s role in education?”. The primary data was a total of 27 reflection papers. After the first author read and analyzed all reflection papers, highlighting key excerpts related to teache rs’ value beliefs about AI, these excerpts were discussed at research team meetings where the team collaborative ly developed a coding scheme. The first author then conducted a thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006)  by applying this coding scheme to the entire dataset.  To ensure validity, the team reviewed a subset of the coded data, addressing discrepancies and refining interpretations to align with the coding scheme. Guided by previous studies on the expectancy-value theory (Cheng et al., 2020; Cheng & Xie, 2018; Ottenbreit-Leftwich et al., 2010; Torsney et al., 2017), we derived five codes consisting of four utility values and one intrinsic value from teachers’ reflection papers: Professional utility value, personal utility value, student utility value, social utility value, and epistemic value. Professi onal utility value refers to a teacher’s understanding of AI’s usefulness  and effectiveness  in their teaching context s. Personal utility value refers to a teacher’s appreciation of AI’s usefulne ss and effectiveness for their personal  learning, not for  K-12 teaching contexts.  Student utility value reflects a teacher ’s recognition, from students’ perspectives, of AI’s usefulness and effectiveness in student learning. Social utility value refers to a teacher’s acknowledgment of AI’s broader application to enhance justice and equity of our so ciety as well as of K-12 classrooms. Lastly, epistemic value, as intrinsic value, highlights a teacher’s interest in learning AI-related knowledge.

## Findings

Teachers reported that, before taking the course, they had not thought about or been skeptical of using AI for pedagogical purposes. However, through the course, they were able to explore diverse aspects of AI and developed clear perspectives and insights about using AI in K-12 classrooms. By analyzing data, we identified the following three themes regarding how teachers are willing to adopt AI. Positioning teachers as pedagogical decision-makers who adopt AI Teachers perceived AI as an assistant to make their work efficient. Teachers said they experienced efficiencies in using AI  tools and found effective strategies for their teaching practices. They understood that  A I c ould be effective for lesson planning and designing classroom activities. They also identified its effectiveness for student assessment, such as creating rubric, generating individualized assessments, and gradin g students’ work. Nevertheless, they wanted to remain responsible for their classroom practices. One teacher highlighted this point: “Rather than replacing educators, AI can increase their capabilities by handling routine tasks, allowing teachers to focus more on personalized instruction and mento rship.” Similarly, teachers’ willingness to learn more about AI literacy implied that they should evaluate AI-generated information before using it for educational purposes. Pursuing a balanced and critical approach to AI Teachers wanted to critically assess the potential and limitations of AI for K-12 education. While teachers believed that they should navigate and maximize the potential of AI for K-12 teaching, they maintained that teachers should critically evaluate AI to use it safely and ethically in the classroom, noting that “ a commitment to fairness and equity” and “ prioritizing transparency and accountability in the  developmental and deployment of AI systems ” are essential. In a similar vein, a few teachers argued that teachers should encourage students to use AI tools by being mindful of and critically analyzing potential  biases in AI-generated information. One teacher, who tested several AI tools, reported that AI tools provided flawed and inaccurate responses as well as inappropriate materials for the K-12 educational context which made him emphasize that AI literacy should be taught to students. Leveraging AI to promote diverse pathways of social and classroom equity Teachers considered that AI could enhance equity in the K-12 classroom and our society. One teacher noted, “AI has the potential to bridge gaps in educational acc ess and support marginalized or underrepresented gr oups.” Teachers also highly appraised AI’s potential to provide personal ized and individualized learning for students, such as presenting relevant images instead of texts for students’ better understanding. Similarly, one teacher found that AI can support English language learners. Furthermore, teachers believed that students’ critical thinking skills as well as their adaptability to the future society  could be developed by making them familiar with  AI in the classroom. One teacher thought that teaching AI literacy could cultivate students’ accountability skills to become well-educated members of society.

## Discussion and implications

Five types of values regarding AI integration into the classroom enabled us to investigate how teachers understand AI. In terms of utility values, teachers thought AI could make teaching and learning more effective, while it could help advance social and classroom equity. T eachers found intrinsic value in building up their knowledge about AI. Furthermore, those five values indicated how teachers envision incorporating AI into their teaching practices. Teachers recognized that they, the designers and practitioner s of curriculum who interact with  and make pedagogical decisions based on  their students, content knowledge, and educational contex t (Rodgers, 2020), should be responsible for when and how to use AI for their teaching practices. Believing that AI not only has potential for K-12 education but also can cause ethical problems  which echoes Bae and colleagues’ (2024) argument, teachers insisted that t hey should critically evaluate whether AI applications guarantee safe use and provide meaningful learning opportunities for students. Teachers also noted that they could use AI to address social and classroom in justice related to student learning. These points indicate that K-12 teachers broadly understand technological, social, and ethical aspects of AI and consider these aspects for their teaching practices, highlighting their agency as curriculum designers and practitioners. The result of this study implies that teachers’ value beliefs can be developed as they learn about AI. A qualitative approach to  investigating teachers’ value beliefs, as this study explored, can inform how teachers conceptualize AI as well as how they interpret their roles as educators in terms of using AI in K-12 education. Future research candelve into how teachers  broadly find intrinsic, utility, and attainment values regarding AI integration and how this shapes their professional identity.
