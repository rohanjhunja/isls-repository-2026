# Routine Care to Build Trust in Co-Research with Young Children

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Counter to pejorative representations of children’s competencies, we show young children can lead co-research when they trust adults will follow. Our research team—child, caregiver, university-based members—present interactional, multimodal analysis of video data in a relational learning ecology. We examine the role of talk, gaze, touch, body orientation, and facial expressions in communicating care. We then link patterns of care to the establishment of trust; namely children’s trust that adults will follow. We begin with an opening moment of our seventh visit (roughly 6 months into our partnership) to illustrate trust in action, and then detail a focal child’s showing of a valued artifact as a coordinated display of care and affirmation of the child’s dignity as learner and leader.

## Introduction

Children are often poorly represented in research which conceptualizes them as not-yet-persons, shares pejorative representations of their capabilities, and assumes universal childhoods (Cannella, 1999; Lee et al., 2 020; Vossoughi & Gutierrez, 2014). This harms researchers and educators’ ability to recogni ze children’s role in shaping community and culture. Moreover, as Templeton articulates (2021): “many adults are unable, and perhaps even unwilling, to conceive of children’s desires a nd capacities to have a say over their own representations” (p. 3). These biases shape research approaches that ass ume adult researchers must solely direct studies of  children’s learning. In the Learning, Being, and Doing (LBD) i n Families project, we centered children in a st udy of their own interests and practice. Here we examine how car e and trust supported children’s leadership and elu cidate relational learning ecologies co-developed by a COVID-competent child + caregiver + university (uni) team. Families are a fundamental setting for early learni ng (e.g., Keifert, 2021; Rogoff, 2003). Moreover, families are a critical site for intergenerational learning (González et al., 2006), though intergenerational learning also occurs outside of family contex ts (Newman & Hatton-Yeo, 2008). We recognize intergenerational learning as multi-directional (Ho, 2010). We were particularly intere sted in documenting, interpreting, and sharing understandings of existing practices for family-based learning and studying processes for co-developing emergent practices for intergenerational learning within research teams of children, caregivers, and uni team members. Given the geopolitical context of COVID, the lack o f vaccines for children under 5 at the time or recruitment, and the PI’s immunocompromised status, the uni team made the decision to form COVID-competent learning communities as the context for this work. COVID-competent communities are a new phenomenon co-occurring with the emergence of the COVID-19 virus in 2019/2020. Despite reports to the contrary (e.g., Nirappil et al., 2023), many people continue COVID-competent behaviors after the U.S. government’s COVID ended t he public health emergency (May 2023). Prior research documented the impact of COVID on school enrollment (e.g., Bauman & Cranney, 2020), and COVID “learning  loss” (Engzell et al., 2021; Donnelly & Patrinos, 2022) including in popular media (e.g., Callimachi, 2021). Developing counter narratives to deficit narrativ es about COVID-competent family learning drove our work, as well as a desire  to develop safe social alternatives for children to engage with non-family, safe adults.

Framework Research demonstrates multimodal analysis is necess ary for understanding human sensemaking (e.g., Good win, 2018), particularly in coming to understand the aff ective dimensions of interactions (e.g., Goodwin & Cekaite, 2018). Moreover, research with childre n has long depended upon analyses of multiple modal ities to ensure that children’s ideas, perspectives, and contributions a re recognized, particularly for young children who are in the process of developing their communicative repertoir es (Ochs, 1979). In the present work, multimodality  sheds light on the interactional work of care and trust as necessary for developing and sustaining children’s leadership. Multimodality is critical in understanding affectiv e dimensions of interaction between child and caregivers, and the developing relationships betwee n families and uni team members. This requires comi ng to understand the “cultural logics of publicly witness able affect display” (Goodwin & Cekaite, 2018, p. 20). These logics help to identify caring—“acting with respect to affection and regard for an other to enhance the welfare or well-being of the party cared for” (p. 121)—in the interactional record. Like Goodwin & Cekaite, we see care as multi-directional within intergenerational learning ecologies. We attend in particular to forms of care that support children’s leadership in relational learning ecologies (Korczynski, 2012); we examine care in shared showing and the development of trust. We focus on showing as a key mechanism by which children share their perspec tives, experience, practice, and interests. A showi ng, a common practice in many families (e.g., Keifert & S tevens, 2019), occurs when something is shared and contextualized. Contextualization may include telling of the artifact’s history (e.g., when it was made, how it was made/remade), how it operates (e.g., how it works, what parts do what), and its purpose (e.g., its job, how it relates to other artifacts, people, or beings). For instanc e, as Jamie (age 4) was showing how to move with fo ur feet, his mom said he looked like a frog (Keifert & Marin, 2018). Jamie’s response was to shift to embodying a frog saying, “Ribbit Ribbit” and finally exclaiming “Yeah! I AM a frog!” Like all showings, this was an interactional activity; Jamie and Mom both shaped this inquiry. At the root  of showing is shared attention as a form of care; when a child makes an interactional bid for attending to a  particular idea, object, or practice, care is demo nstrated when adults share that focus of attention.  Engaging in collective showings are acts of care in that the se interactions serve as a means for demonstrating “regard for anot her to enhance the welfare or well-being of the party cared for” (Goodwin & Ceakite, 2018, p. 121). In our lear ning ecologies, care included the cultivation of ed ucational dignity—"the multifaceted sense of a person’s value generat ed via meaningful participation in substantive intr aand inter-personal learning experiences that recognize and cu ltivate one’s mind, humanity, and potential” (Espinoza et al., 2020, p. 19). We argue further that, “It is not enough to state someone has dignity; i t must be enacted and affirmed” (Keifert et al., 2021, p. 796). Thus, recognizing care through attending to show ing is an accomplishment that can be understood through analysis of multimodal interaction. We conceptualize trust drawing on studies of commun ities of equals (specifically communities focused on teaching and learning). Trust includes care, com passion, and valuing of each other’s contributions,  ideas, and experience (Cranston, 2011; Fullan, 1999;  Hargreaves, 2007; see also relational equity; Boale r, 2008). Bryk and Schneider (2002) conceptualize relational trust as centered on relationships between people, in our case, relations between family/uni, adults/child, caregiver/child, and uni/child. Thr ough these relationships, trust develops as there is a shared understanding about each members’  expectations and obligations —especially children’s trust that adults will follow their leadership. Moreover,  trust becomes visible in the interactional record as obligations are fulfilled. Thus, through the interactional work of care, norms of interaction candevelop that lead to trust. That trust is demonstrable in the record as children are affirmed as leaders as they direct the collective teams’ attention through arrangements like showings. Taken together, care and trust form the interactional basis upon which LBD teams organized to center children’s experience, interests, and practices as they lead learning ecologies. As we analyze touch along with other modalities tha t communicate care in interactions, we will need to consider both idiocultures—local cultural practices within a small community or what Goodwin & Cekaite (2018) name as “haptic cultural repertoires” (p. 1 28)—as well as broader cultural norms. In LBD, our enga gement as a research teams occurred across virtual spaces with child and caregiver in the same physical space and uni members each in different physical spaces. This contextual detail meant that touch between uni and family team members was not possible, and thus care depended on multipl e other modalities. For instance, care was demonstr ated in taking up children’s suggestions (e.g., purchasing the same version of Guess Who to play with kids), listening to their stories, responding to their questions, and attending to the things in which they were intereste d. Using this framework as a guide, we ask: How does routine demonstrations of care support chi ldren’s trust that adults will follow their lead in relational learning ecologies?

## Methods

The Learning, Being, and Doing (LBD) in Families Study was designed to provide counter-examples to narratives of learning-loss popular in the media (Callimachi, 2021) through research-partnerships with children and families to shape anti-deficit narratives about intergenerational learning, being, and doing (Vossoughi & Booker, 2020; González et al., 2006) in COVID-competent families. The focus on COVID-competent families emerged out of the needs in the fall of 2021/spring of 2022 as vac cine rollout demonstrated a societal dropping of CO VID precautions, leading to the marginalization of COVID-competent people. Our Principal Investigator, Teo, is a member of the COVID-competent community as a disabled and immunocompromised human. As a result, Teo was interested in understanding COVID-competent families as a context for learning, being, and doing. LBD draws on co-observation, an emergent method (Keifert et al., 20 24). LBD co-observation is a syncretic method—merging multiple research traditions with everyday practices (Gutiérrez & Jurow, 2016) —to examine children’s lived experience (Rogoff et al.,  2018). This centered families’ existing storying pra ctices— documenting, reflecting, theorizing (Ochs et al., 1 992)—with traditions of video-based research (Jordan & Henderson, 1995; DeLiema et al., 2023). Thus, families were involved in research agenda-setting, data production and collection, and data interpretation. By partnering with children to “tell their story on their terms” (Gutiérrez et al., 2019, p. 9), LBD supported ethically valid res earch—attending to the distribution of research materials, resources, and activities—to produce representations of learning embedded in participants’ understanding of their family culture.

## Data collection

This analysis focuses on one LBD team: Team Poof. Team Poof included Little Poof (5yrs, they/them), Big Book (Mom, she/her), Teo (they/them), and Andria (she/he r). Data were collected over 15 months during the c ore data collection (June 2022-August 2023) and supplemented by ongoing reflection  and analysis by research teams over an additional 12+ months (ongoing at the time of submission, Fall 2023-Fall 2024). Virtual sessions included team members in synchronous engagement via Zoom, recorde d to the cloud to  capture multiple views (i.e., gallery, speaker, shared screen) and generate auto-transcriptions. We created content logs (e.g., Erickson & Schultz, 1997; Jordan & Henderson, 1995) during our sessions using Zoom chat, which we call Live Logs as they were generated synchronously during sessions. In the core data collection, Team Poof gathered 26.37 hours of virtual sessions, and 133 additional photos/videos shared via text and/or Google Photos from asynchronous activities. After consenting, we engaged in a preliminary interview to determine shared interests. The present data come from a transition from the first phase of the study (Playdates) to the sec ond (Show & Tell). Playdates focused on seeding rel ationships, demonstrating care, growing trust, and developing local showing practice. Show & Tell Dates focused on seeding & grow ing shared practice for making and storying (data inter pretation) during showings. However, the focal moment occurred at the start of the session, so it followed the norms we had established in Playdates.

## Data analysis

Methods of analysis were based on a syncretic approach blending children and families’ everyday interp retive practices with traditional-research forms of analysis. Everyday analysis took the form of storying artifacts and practices and reflection on videos and images of prior LBD visits. Traditional forms included Interaction Analysis or IA (Jordan & Henderson, 1995) and analysis of Co-Operative Action (Goodwin, 2018; Goodwin & Cekaite, 2018). We also drew on Och’s (1979) commitments to foreground children’s contributions as we developed representations of our findings. Teo engaged in iterative refinement of hypotheses (Engle et al., 2014) by examining related records of practice such as data from non-synchronous sources, primarily Google Photos captur ed and shared by both the Poof Family and Teo.  They then brought a selection of moments first to t wo IA sessions, and then to Team Poof for collaborative reflection alon g with Jefferson (2004) transcriptions and comic-like representations of key moments. Teo shared the developing argument with the Poof Fa mily to gain their perspectives using this package of materials. Additionally, Teo shared the drafted and revised manuscript with BB. BB was encouraged to share that draft in meaningful ways with LP.

Through repeated acts of care in attending to Little Poof’s showings, Team Poof cultivated LP’s dignity as a learner and leader and developed LP’s trust that if they led, adults would  follow. Our discussion reflects on how we affirmed  Little Poof’s dignity as a learner through interactional w ork to center LP’s interests and practices and how adults demonstrated routine care in ways that led to LP’s trust that we would value their showings; that is, how routine care led to trust. We (re)present this data to highlight a few things. First, multiple modalities served as witnessable affect displays as individuals attended to the welfare of others. F or instance, BB kissing LP’s head, BB asking questi ons about LP’s water truck, and Teo’s gaze and grin all demon strate care. Similarly, trust was evidenced through  LP’s demonstration of an expectation that Teo would list en to their showing as they moved physically into f rame and began to talk about the Water Truck as well as their body orienta tion and gaze towards the camera. Trust was fulfilled by BB’s shift in body to the side and back to make room for LP to take center-frame and Teo’s listening to their showing as well as multiple displays of excitement through talk and facial expression. Trust was expected and fulfilled through demonstrations of care via attention on LP’s Water Truck. We also note touch was not a part of Teo or Andria’ s displays of care for LP and BB, nor vice versa, given the physical constraints of the virtual space. While this design was a result of meeting the needs of COVIDcompetent families and researchers, our time together demonst rated that virtual relational care and trust are possible. We do not diminish the importance of in-person intercorporeality, but rather recognize care  and trust can be established in the absence of touch. Ensuring everyone could see the arti fact of interest takes effort in any interaction, but LP’s growing competence in navigating showing in our vir tual space demonstrated ways they had and continued to take on leadership in our learning ecology. The result of this work was that Team Poof centered  LP’s making practice via routine care that led to LP’s trust that adults would follow their lead. Sha h and Lewis (2019) describe this as participatory e quity, or “a condition where opportunities to participate —and participation itself —are fairly distributed among all [people] involved in a learning interaction ” (p. 428). LP was the team’s resource for choosing  attentional directions, affirming their dignity as a learner and leader, and developing trust that as they led, adults would follow. Children’s leadership in co-observation Co-Observation created a context for relational learni ng ecologies centered on children’s practice and le d by children’s interests. More specifically, Little Poo f, Big Book, Teo, and Andria collaborated to intera ctionally achieve a centering on LP’s practice and support LP’s leadership. For instance, our understanding of LP’s making practice includes many instances of watching LP mak e things, engaging in showings of made artifacts, l istening to the stories that contextualize makings and artifacts, and hearing explicit reflection from both LP and BB about LP’s making practice. In future work, we hope to de velop more forms of co-analysis, including tools that might support creating visual representations together wi th children. The goal of this work is to further pu sh the boundaries of co-observation, which currently includes active methods of agenda-setting, data collection, and data analysis, but relatively limited forms of co-authorship. Our analysis illustrated the importance of care and  trust in co-observation relational learning ecologies, as demonstrated through witnessable displays of aff ection and expectation and cultivating LP’s dignity  as a learner, leader, and author of “their story on their terms” (Gutiérrez et al., 2019, p. 9). Moreover, these ecologies were spread across virtual and physical spaces. In an era when hostilities towards immigrants and tran s humans at cites of U.S. immigration and even travel within the United Sttaes(McNeill, 2025; Watson, 2025) and ongoing quad-demics (Kekatos, 2025) threaten health and livelihoods, traveling to visit families within and across borders is increasingly difficult meaning that research on the possibilities of seeding and growin g relational intergenerational learning ecologies is increasingly needed. Moreover, technology-mediated relationships are a common feature of many diasporic communities, including one of the LBD families, Family Wonderland, as they seek to maintain relationships and belonging across borders (see Madianou, 2019). While LBD learning ecologies spanned virtual spaces, there is good reason to expect that similar possibilities exist in face-to-face settings. Others trying to adopt similar methods should keep in mind  that COVID-competent families expressed a need to have COVID-competent friendly adults in their lives of their c hildren, meaning that our uni team members offer ed a valued opportunity. Researchers should ensure they, too are offering opportunities that families need/desire. Regardless of the context of future relational lear ning ecologies with young children, there are a few features of the LBD study that will continue to be central to success in this form of co-research. Gathering video data was critical as it provided evidence of the multiple modalities through which care and trust were demonstrated as well as supporting attending to the various showings that led to LP shaping Team Poof’s research. So too was reflecting on preliminary data representations and reflective storytelling central to our following LP’s experience, interests, and practices. This allowed us to move f rom a surveillance model of video data collection t owards collaborative, relational work within a learning ecology (see Vossoughi & Escudé, 2016). In face-to-face settings, there is the additional possibility (Vossoughi & Es cudé, 2016; Gutiérrez et al., 2019; Greenberg, 2019) that children can help position cameras and other record ing devices for themselves. This work was also a move from mere assemblages of collections of moments by resea rchers alone (e.g., Keifert & Stevens, 2019; Keifer t, 2021) towards weaving of histories, perspectives, and kno wledge systems (Keifert et al., 2022). It is a meth od that supported direct push-back on notions of learning loss within families, particularly COVID-competent families as well as pejorative representations of children’s capabilities or universal childhoods.

## Conclusion

Team Poof provided evidence that children can lead co-research when they have trust that adults will follow. That trust was built through displays of care, expectati ons that were fulfilled, and creative co-exploration of the very practice that children cen ter in their own lives. That trust was maintained t o collaborative interaction to center LP’s interests and practices and keep those centered. Children led our research in ways that drew on the multiple contexts in which they lived and learned including our shared virtual space, informal learning spaces that focused on their interests, and even subsequent trips and l ife experience that reminded them of our time toget her.
