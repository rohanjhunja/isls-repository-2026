# Data Reels: An Analytic Approach to Creating Data Stories in Video Analysis

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This paper introduces the data reel as an analytic approach to working with video data. Like social media reels in which users curate and layer together multimodal content, data reels are short, annotated texts that stitch together video and other multimodal clips to ‘story’ the data. This paper describes how data reels emerged as an analytic approach in a design-research study about youth writing with generative AI. In illustrating how youth writers developed strategies to ensure their writerly voices were not overpowered by AI, we sought to use the methods of the medium to create data stories with screen capture video and other multimodal data collected at different points in time. We found that data reels served as a form of multimodal analysis that made researchers' interpretive work visible and opened space for sense-making with significant (often overwhelming) amounts of video data.

## Introduction

As young people learn with emerging digital technol ogies across an evolving media landscape, education al researchers have turned to the ‘methods of the medi um’ to study changing social and cultural practices  using methods that reflect those technological t ools and practices (Rogers, 2013). Often drawing on  audio-visual technologies to record how people now move across space and time multimodally (e.g., Møller & Robards, 2019; Pink et al., 2016; Reeves et al., 2021), researcher s can face myriad challenges, from the ethical (e.g., navigating privacy and surveillance concerns) to the practical (e.g., managing copious amounts of data). One major challenge has been making researchers’ interpretive work visible and a vailable for critical and collaborative inquiry, particularly when processes of platformization work  to mask how technologies shape everyday practices (vandijck et al., 2018) – especially in educational research (e.g., Nichols & Garcia, 2022). This paper responds to these challenges by proposin g an analytic method that draws on contemporary ways of working with multimodal content on social m edia: reels. Social media reels are short-form videos that communicate a story to viewers by curating, layerin g, and annotating multimodal content. We have been experimenting with creating what we call ‘data reels’ that operate similarly: data reels are short, annotated videos that stitch together multimodal content to ‘story’ data in ways that make visible researchers’ interpr etive work. We found that social annotation extended the narrat ive potential of data reels by making them availabl e for coconstructing knowledge on collaborative teams, particularly for projects that include participatory methods of codesign. The paper explains how data reels emerged as an analytic approach in a design-research study (Hoadley & Campos, 2022) about writing with generative AI. As the intergenerational research team engaged in co-design to study youth writers’ practices with AI, we created and annotated data reels to make visible different dimensions of the digital writing process that can be challenging to capture, particularly writers’ strategic decision-making in the moment with technological tools (Takayoshi, 2016). This paper is guided by the question: how candata reels support researchers’ understandings of how youth wr iters use rhetorical and ethical strategies when wr iting with generative AI? We found that data reels served as a  form of multimodal analysis that made researc hers’ interpretive work visible and opened space for sens e-making with significant (often overwhelming) amount s of video data.

## Background

Educational researchers have long turned to video technologies to capture the complexities of moment-to-moment social interaction that can be challenging to study  otherwise, developing a range of qualitative analy tic methods to make visible the significant interpretive work involved in theorizing from video data (Erickson, 2012). Screen capture and other video-based methods have been particularly helpful for tr acing people’s processes of writing with digital technologies, as they provide a window into writers’ emergent rhetorical decision-making that can be particularly hard to ‘see’ (Takayoshi, 2016). Howev er, the ethical, interpretive, and practical challe nges of analyzing video data are well established, particularly as researchers navigate an abundance of data that can seem to transparently represent people’s experiences whi le obscuring the interpretive work (and power dynam ics) involved in analysis (Mees & Murray, 2019). Critica l educational researchers have detailed how video r esearch can easily be turned to more surveillant or extract ive ends if researchers are not careful to develop ethical, relational, and humanizing methods (Vossoughi & Esc udé, 2016). Expansive methods of digital video rese arch can make visible different epistemic and analytic viewpoints, opening space for the co-construction of knowledge (de Freitas, 2016). In particular, participatory approaches to video research have been shown to provide powerful learning opportunities for research teams to co-construct knowledge through collaborative analysis of video data (Gomoll et al., 2022). Layering such collaborative video analysis with social annotation practices – which involve people adding notes, comments, and ideas to texts to make their thinking visible to others and co-create knowledge in learning contexts (Zhu et al., 2024) – holds promise for further developing expansive video research methods. One challenge in developing such expansive methods with video data has involved the ways that platforms often mask their sociotechnical dimension s, especially in framing data as neutral measures t hat can be interpreted objectively (vandijck et al., 2018; Nichols & Garcia, 2022). As people increasingly tur n to data to narrate and communicate ideas in media and journali stic settings (Segel & Heer, 2010), learning scient ists have explored how data storytelling can be used in educa tional contexts, particularly to unsettle those often-invisible data storying practices. Kemble and Wilkerson (2024), for example, found that young people told storie s about data and with data in different ways, with implications for educators who want to develop more critical approaches to data literacy. Vacca et al. (2024) define data s tories as “evidence-based arguments contextualized in human experience” that bring together data with personal experiences and knowledge as “a syncretic form of t ext” (p. 1786). Data stories represent one way that individu als can make their interpretive moves with data vis ible (Stornaiuolo, 2020). For researchers, Mosconi et al. (2022) off er a compelling argument for how data stories represent an innovative way of curating and sharing qualitative data, particularly as open data initiatives encourage researchers to make qualitative data available. We found the possibilities of combining expansive forms of video research with data storytelling to be a fruitful di rection for exploring youth’s contemporary digital composing practices with ‘the methods of the medium’: short form data stories with video.

## Methodology

We developed data reels as part of a design-based research study (Hoadley & Campos, 2022) to ex amine how high school writers were using generative AI in the ir everyday writing lives. The Writing with AI (WAi) project included 12 members: five high school students, five graduate students, one undergraduate, and one university researcher. We received institutional IRB approval for the stud y and collected informed consent from all participants and guardians (all names are pseudonyms).

We report here on analytic methods developed in the  second phase of the project. In the first phase, w e held a contest with high school writers who used ge nerative AI (hereafter, AI) creatively in their wri ting (n=19), collecting submitted written artifacts, artist statements, and demographic information. In the second phase of the project, we recruited 10 case study participants who identified as high school students, writers, and frequent users of AI, beginning with the three contest winners fro m Phase 1 of the project and using snowball samplin g to identify additional participants (Merriam, 1998). We conducted two 60-minute screen capture interviews on Zoom with each participant: one informational interview and one walkthrough interview (Light et al., 2018) in which participants walked interviewers through an in-progress piece they were writing with AI, thinking aloud as they composed in the moment (Beck, 2018). Nine participants completed both interviews (see Table 1). We began analysis by building individual case study  profiles of each writer, transcribing all intervie ws, and creating content logs of the walkthrough interv iews. We engaged in first round descriptive coding of informational interview transcripts (Miles et  al., 2018), with two members of the research team c oding each transcript in the online Atlas.ti platform. We coll ectively read and discussed coded excerpts and coll apsed them into 12 inductive codes, which we then grouped into  five thematic clusters (Braun & Clark, 2021): voic e, ethics, purpose, platforms, and writing process. We focus h ere on the ‘voice’ cluster (including codes of control, authenticity, identity, affect, and human/AI interaction) as a predominant theme across all case study participants. ## Findings

Across the corpus, we were struck by the ways that every youth writer described AI as an unruly co-author who had to be managed. While writers found AI to be a useful tool, they expressed the idea that if they were not careful, AI would overwrite their text, overwhelm their voic e, and “insert its own knowledge that it's learned from all its data sets” (Arav, walkthrough interview). As a r esult, all writers we interviewed displayed an arra y of rhetorical and ethical strategies for managing AI and keeping it under control. We learned that writers drew on a felt sense of ‘what AI sounded like’ to guide their composing processes as they wrote with AI. However, it was challenging to ‘see’ how those strategies played out across the  corpus in a way that brought together the multimod al data across different sources over time (artifacts, interviews, vide o data), particularly given our 20+ hours of video data. The 12-person team worked individually and in pairs to cre ate data stories to narrate how participants used different strategies to control what their writing ‘sounded like’ and ward off AI’s “unnatural” or “ro botic” voice intruding into their writing. We present two findings about 1) the interpretive practices involved in creating data stories; and 2) the ways data stories made visible the interpretive practices of both participants and researchers. To make the reel, we storied the data by engaging in three practices com mon to making reels in social media: curating, laye ring, and annotating data. Our second step involved  layering together the walkthrough screen capture da ta with other multimodal data. We then worked i n iMovie to begin layering those materials together, attending to the ways the different modes played together (se e Table 2). We used this layering process to bring in data t o complement or extend what youth were showing in the ir walkthrough videos, but we quickly realized that  that would make the reel too lengthy and less legible to an audience unfamiliar with the full data corpus. We decided to stick closely to one data source (the walkthroug h screen capture interviews) to make the data reel more easily understandable – not least because so many of the youth writers spok e about AI in similar ways. We highlighted the idea of what sounds “natural” because that was most prevalent in the data set, choosing clips that were succinct and clear in the framing of the concerns about AI’s voice. In this stage of the process, we spent time making sure all materials were blinded and that no ‘unintended participants’ were visible in the data we were layering together (Takayoshi, 2016).

Our final step was to annotate the data, and we wer e challenged to find a social annotation tool that worked well with multimodal data. We found the inte rfaces and social annotation tools of social media sites like Instagram and TikTok better than any other tool we explored, but we could not upload data on t hose sites and realized they would not afford the collaborative ex perience we sought. We found Canva’s annotation too ls to be easier than others we experimented with, so we uploaded the ree l from iMovie and were able to add effects like animated screen transcription and our personal annotations that highlighted particular elements (see Table 2). For example, we debated which parts of the audio to transcribe and which to just highlight, and we decided to add the words “human would have said it” in blue to make that analytical point jump out at viewers (given the number of times the word ‘human’ appeared when speaking about AI and voice; n=41). When we watched the data reel as a team, we were st ruck by several insights. First, we found the short form video opened space for critical discussion, not only about the participants’ sensemaking with AI but our own as researchers. Our discussion helped t o clarify our focus on keeping participants’ voices  and perspectives at the center of our inquiry, which drove our decisions about what data would highlight their authorial agenc y (e.g., “It lets us see how the writers are processing in real time”). Second, we learned that the younger people on the team were more adept at critiquing the genre, offering s pecific guidance on how to fluidly move across the curating/annotating/layering process (e.g., “We should layer the quote across both screens for more impact and to smooth the transitions across the data”). Finally, we found that data reels, as short form videos, all owed for multiple viewings (“they were succinct and eye-catching”), which allowed for deeper conversations about data (e.g., “I love the power of multiple voices and juxtaposing their perspectives”). This first finding focuses on the ways that new int erpretive insights were made possible by engaging i n the methods of the medium – the curating, layering, and annotating practices th at are key to creating short form video stories. The constraints of the medium (e.g.,  length, synchronized text/sound/images) required c lose microanalytic work to reduce data in order to narrativize it. To decide what data would be curated and then layered together involved complex interpretive decisions, r equiring team members  to engage in both individual and collaborative sense-making across different forms of complex multimodal  data. Socially annotating the data – seeing what was salient to different members of the  team in the process of reading each other’s annota tions – offered insights about what was important to highlight  when storying our data (e.g., how writers echoed e ach other when describing what AI ‘sounds like’).

## Discussion

This paper examined how we developed a new analytic  method for working with video data: data reels. For researchers interested in new forms of collaborative analysis and co-design methodologies, the process of building data reels (through curating, layering, and annotating data separately and together) can provide a readily accessible genre with multiple  interpretive pathways and opportunities for collabo rative sense-making. We highlight here four affordances from our study of data reels, with implications for researchers.

• Moving across data:  One powerful affordance of the data reel is that it s structure as a short-form

video offers a close and quick look at small bits of data juxtaposed to one another. This brevity draws attention to how data relates to one another across  time and space. Engaging with this form required us to move both non-linearly and relationally across the various data t hat we collected – screen capture, informational interviews, contest submissi ons, and other writing artifacts. We engaged in this close mi croanalytic work both individually and collaborativ ely, as the intergenerational team members took different interpretive paths when cura ting data across more than 20 hours of video footage. The process of collaboratively annotating the data functioned both as an analytical tool and as an invitation to see differently. Layering became a method for holding complexity. Looking across the data reels allowed us to zoom in and out of the  data, offering new interpretive lenses for collaborative sensemaking across space and time (see Monea et al., 2023).

• Storying data: We highlighted how the process of storying data often used in social media platforms

– curating, annotating, and layering – were powerful practices for research. We found these practices

helpful for narrating how participants engaged in s imilar kinds of s trategic and agentive decision-making when composing with AI. Because we were able  to layer together short snippets of video from participants at similar moments in their compo sing processes in Data Reel 1, for example, we could ‘hear’ how AI sounded to the writers in our corpus – bad, robotic, unnatural, stilted – and also ‘see’ how youth writers revised their prose based o n those judgments. The data reels allowed us to juxtapose their strategies in new configurations, h elping to clarify when  youth’s authorial decisions were driven by their sense that AI’s voice was overpowering their own. This form of storying data in smaller, more visible forms highlights the interpretive work involved in data analysis.

• Critical and collaborative sensemaking: For researchers working across large teams with mul tiple

partners or using co-design methods, one challenge is how to make sense of data together. Using a form that is widely taken up in everyday practice i s one way of making practices of data analysis accessible and understandable. Part of what we found powerful about these short-form videos is that they made our interpretive practices as researchers visible as we constructed data stories – we had to articulate to on e another why we chose one clip over another or ann otated a piece of data in a particular way. As we worked together to analyze the video data or looked at each other’s data reels, we found it easier to ‘see’ across time scales, dat a sources, and platforms. Such visibility is particularly important when examining processes tha t can be hard to capture (e.g., composing with technologies) or that are masked by platforms (e.g., how AI interfaces required writers to develop workarounds like copying only one paragraph at a ti me to control how AI responded to their work). The data reels made those platform practices legibl e to the research team, opening space for critical conversations about how platforms shaped writers’ c omposing practices and our own interpretive ones and how power and epistemologies were negotiated.

• Making data visible publicly: An important form of visibility that data reels offer is to make a team’s

data practices available not only to each other but  to wider audiences: data reels are a quick and accessible way of sharing qualitative data with the  public (see Mosconi et al., 20 22 for more about how data stories can be a powerful means of open data sharing). This public sharing of data in a form that is accessible can foster transparency, invite dialogue, and broaden participation in meaning-making beyond traditional academic or institutional boundaries. One current limitation of the data reel approach is the time-intensive nature of building them. We found no single technological tool or platform that made such work straightforward. Instead, we had to work across multiple platforms to do everything we wan ted, and even then we were dissatisfied with the ae sthetic nature of the results (noting that social media platforms lik e Instagram and TikTok have built in interfaces tha t made such layering work straightforward and aesthetically ple asing). One implicatio n is for the development of platforms and tools that allow for such storying of data in intuitive and fluid ways, inviting remix, collaborative annotations, and collective viewing opportunities (a future step  in our own work). We see promise for digital metho ds to continue refining the data reel – not only for researchers but also for young people to work with data in ways more familiar.

Creating data reels requires close analysis of diff erent forms of data but makes them accessible tone w audiences. By leveraging newer media formats and pl atforms, data reels also contribute to multimodal r esearch that prioritizes non-text-based sense-making, analysis, and other ways of knowing. Intentionally designed as short, single-strip videos that integrate multiple data sources, data reels make research accessible to participants  while fostering collaborative interpretive work —both during their creat ion, curation, and through subsequent annotation, allowing researchers to ‘see’ data in relationship to one another.
