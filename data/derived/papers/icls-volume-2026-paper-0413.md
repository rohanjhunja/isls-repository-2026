# Consequential Intergenerational Learning through Collaborative Video Game Design

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how collaborative video game design between graduate and elementary school students facilitates intergenerational learning. Through semester-long action research, three graduate game design students and thirteen 4th and 5th graders co-d esigned a video game addressing local ecological issues. Using communities of learners, constructionist, and sociocultural frameworks and qualitative analysis, we identified how player-centered design, structured playtesting, and productive communication differences facilitated reciprocal learning while disrupting traditional power dynamics across age and institutional boundaries.

Educational systems segregate learners by age and institution, limiting authentic intergenerational collaboration. In classrooms, young people are typically positioned as passive recipients of adult expertise rather than as active contributors, missing opportunities for reciprocal learning that prepare all participants for complex societal challenges. These socially-constructed boundaries become particularly problematic when considering the nature of 21st-century challenges including ecological disconnection and injustice; economic, educational, and technological inequities; and civic distrust, all of which require innovative and sustained collaboration across age groups, backgrounds, experiences, disciplines, and institutional boundaries. Yet educational systems often fail to provide authentic experiences in such collaboration, instead maintaining artificial separations that limit learning potential and civic preparation.

Specifically, this study addresses three critical gaps in learning sciences research and practice: (1) most educational partnerships rely on traditional service learning or mentorship models where knowledge flows primarily from older to younger participants; (2) little research examines how collaborative game design can serve as both medium and method for developing collaborative competencies across age groups; and (3) existing intergenerational learning research often lacks theoretical frameworks explaining how participants from different communities of practice negotiate shared understanding and develop collaborative learning. This research extends the communities of learners (Brown & Campione, 1994; Rogoff, 1994), constructionist (Papert, 1980; Papert & Harel, 1991), and sociocultural learning theories (Vygotsky, 1986) by demonstrating how participants from different age groups  and institutional contexts can successfully negotiate shared practices and develop mutual understanding through structured collaborative artifact-creation activities. Our project yielded design principles for intergenerational learning that develop domain-specific knowledge and collaborative meaning-making through iterative, evidence-based pedagogical adjustments. The research has particular significance for understanding how game design can serve as a powerful context for intergenerational learning. By engaging graduate students and upper elementary school students in the shared task of designing games, the project demonstrates how collaboration across age groups can foster reciprocal learning and challenge traditional hierarchies of expertise. It also off ers a model for educational practice that not only advances gamebased learning but also prepares learners of different ages to participate meaningfully in democratic and community life.

## Theoretical background

We employ a community of learners approach emphasizing shared responsibilities and distributed expertise through joint inquiry (Rogoff, 1994), complemented by informant design (Scaife et al., 1997), where children contribute as experts on their own play experiences at targeted stages of development. Constructionism positions game design as a powerful form of learning through creating shareable artifacts that can be critiqued and refined (Papert, 1980; Papert & Harel, 1991), while sociocultural theory frames  age differences not as obstacles but as resources that enrich collaborative learning (Vygotsky, 1986). Research-practice partnerships (Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Penuel & Gallagher, 2017) structure the sustained collaboration, emphasizing mutualism and iterat ive improvement. Recent work in intergenerational game design (Mansilla et al., 2019; Cucinelli et al., 2018; MacCallum et al., 2006; Mannion, 2012) highlights how shared creative production enhances empathy, digital literacy, and narrative expression while bridging generational divides through mutual apprenticeship. 2668 This study uses action research (Kemmis & McTaggart, 2000) within a research-practice partnership framework involving three graduate game design students (ages 24– 26) and thirteen 4th and 5th graders (ages 9– 11) from a university laboratory school over three months. Grounded in collaborative inquiry principles (Cochran-Smith & Lytle, 1999), the research process paralleled students' own design activities, positioning the study as shared knowledge construction through authentic collaboration. The course is co-facilitated by a university professor and an elementary teacher and introduces transformational game design (Culyba, 2018) through the theme of ecological justice and local watersheds. After two initial gameplay sessions, graduate students created digital games with multiple playtest sessions while elementary students developed board games on the same topic. Over the course of the semester, both sets of students collaborated across ten sessions for approximately an hour at a time. Data were collected through pre/post surveys, researcher observations, participants’ audio-recorded weekly reflections, and semi-structured focus-group interviews, then analyzed through iterative descriptive and analytical coding in ATLAS.ti with collaborative team calibration.

## Preliminary findings and discussion

Early analyses suggest three interconnected mechanisms through which intergenerational game design facilitates reciprocal learning. First, the requirement to create games for elementary partners established genuine interdependence that challenged traditional mentorship models. Because graduate students' evaluations depended partly on game effectiveness with the intended audience, elementary students' feedback became essential rather than tokenistic. When elementary students successfully engaged with a precision platformer that graduate students had deemed developmentally inappropriate, it forced fundamental reconsideration of assumptions about children's capabilities, reflecting informant design principles that position children as experts on their own play experiences (Druin, 1999). Over time, elementary students moved beyond simple evaluative responses to offer specific feedback on mechanics, visuals, and difficulty progression, while applying design thinking to their own board game development, transforming traditional teacher-learner dynamics into a co-design relationship grounded in mutual expertise.

Second, structured playtesting protocols functioned not merely as a design tool but as a pedagogical mechanism enabling reciprocal learning and metacognitive development. Both groups practiced systematic observation, constructive feedback, and reflective analysis, with elementary students developing awareness of how complex projects evolve through iterative feedback cycles and graduate students strengthening professional skills in user research and feedback interpretation.

Third, communication differences became generative learning resources. Graduate students developed multimodal interpretation strategies, learning to read gameplay behavior, facial expressions, and body language alongside verbal feedback. Initially literal suggestions were reinterpreted as expressions of deeper player preferences, such as recognizing a child's narrative request as a desire for more world-building and tutorial support. Post-project surveys confirmed that both groups increased comfort with cro ss-age collaboration, suggesting these competencies developed through scaffolded practice rather than explicit instruction alone. These findings collectively demonstrate how intergenerational game design can operate as a community of learners (Rogoff, 1994), where knowledge is co-produced across participants, tools, and contexts. The partnership disrupted traditional hierarchies by situating each participant as both expert and learner, modeling how educators and researchers candesign learning environments that value mutual expertise, iterative improvement, and shared ownership of outcomes.
