# Exploring the Potential of Design in Early Childhood Education for Sustainability

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Kindergarteners play a crucial role in driving sustainable changes, and there is a growing interest in combining design approaches with sustainable education. This paper presents empirical research on Early Childhood Education for Sustainability (ECEfS) conducted in China. It aims to examine the current situation of ECEfS implementation in Chinese kindergartens and investigate the role of design in promoting its objectives. Qualitative research techniques were adopted in this study, and data collected from a kindergarten were analyzed. Our findings reveal the integration of relevant practices into their nature-based learning curricula. In summary, their ECEfS-related practices fall into three design-oriented categories: a) project-based activities to foster sustainable awareness, b) environmental design to create sustainable learning spaces, and c) co-design approaches for exploring sustainable materials.

## Introduction

Achieving sustainable development is the shared vis ion of humanity in the 21st century (UN, 2015). However, the emergence of COVID-19 in late 2019 has profoundly impacted the sustainable transformation process. Since then, it has been crucial to prioritize the constru ction of a more resilient, inclusive, and sustainab le world, and education is regarded as a powerful instrument for driving positive trans formations toward sustainability (UNESCO, 2005). In 1992, the Education for Sustaina ble Development (ESD) was first introduced (UNCED, 1992). Progressing through the years, by 2007, a st rong consensus emer ged among researchers, and it is recommended that the initiation of ESD practices be gin at the kindergarten level (UNESCO, 2008). Young children are regarded as significant contributors to sustainable transformation, and their thinking patterns, as well as skill sets,  will significantly shape our future (Hedefalk et al., 2015). Considering this significance, the framework of Early Childhood Education for Sustainabi lity (ECEfS) was proposed (Davis, 2010), leading to a notable surge in research within this do main in recent years  (Bascopé et al., 2019). Current studies have shown that kindergarteners were able to discuss environmental problems (Melis et al., 2020), understand basic economic concepts (Borg, 2019), and know indigenous cultures  (Bascopé et al., 2019). It is also suggested that fostering sustainable attitudes, knowledge, and behaviors fro m an early age confers lifelong advantages for chil dren growing up in the 21st century (UNESCO, 2012a). Moreover, the pedagogical frameworks currently employed in k indergartens lay the groundwork for the implementation of ECEfS. Specifically, some widely  used approaches, such as  project-based learning, interdisciplinary learning, nature education, and community engagement, are identified as fitting foundations for the development of ECEfS practices (Siraj-Blatchford et al., 2010; UNESCO, 2012b).

## Related work

This session discusses related work in three main a reas: 1) a preliminary investigation of ECEfS in Ch ina, 2) child-centered design, and 3) co-design with children in ECEfS.

## ECEfS in the Chinese context

China has the world's largest early childhood educa tion system, serving 3.24 million teachers and 46 m illion children aged 3 to 6 years old (Ministry of Education, 2012, 2023). This system is guided by key policy documents: the Guidelines for Kindergarten Education (Trial)  (Ministry of Education, 2001) and the Early Learning and Development Guidelines for Children Aged 3-6 (Ministry of Education, 2012), which together establish a national framework prioritizing children's development acros s five domains: health, language, science, society,  and arts. Reviewing the available research on ECEfS in the Chinese context, the intro duction of the concept of ECEfS in China could be traced back to 2007 (Liu, Y., & Liu, F., 2007). Subsequently, China participated in the OMEP research to explore young children’s views on environmental issues (OMEP China Committee,2010). Regarding empirical studies on ECEfS within the Chinese context, Wang et al. (2019) adopted an action research method and demonstrated the feasibility of integrating ECE fS into kindergartens. In order to assess practices among Chinese early childhood practitioners, Cheng and Yu (2022) developed and validated the Early Childhood Educators’ Education for Sustainable Development Practices Scale (ECEESDPS). Child-centered design The design community has a longstanding tradition of conductin g child-centered design practices and research, emphasizing the importance of integrating children’ s needs and perspectives (Hussain, 2010; Derr, 2015). Children’s involvement has been studied in differen t design projects, such as creating  indoor and outdoor environments (Huang & Liu, 2022), developing design-inspired innovative teaching and learnin g (Garreta‐ Domingo et al., 2018), and enhancing children's design thinking capabilities (Forbes et al., 2021). In addition, the child-centered design tradition seems to align with the current trend in early childhood pedagogy. In these design projects, children’s roles are generally classified into four types: a) users, b) testers, c) informants, and d) design partners, as delineated by Druin (1999).

Around these roles, two design approaches are often discussed: a) des ign for children (DfC), where young children benefit from design as users (Spyrou, 2022), and b) design with children (DwC), which involves them more actively in the design process  (Brinck et al., 2022). Specifically, the DfC approach emphasizes children’s needs and preferences in the design proj ects of products (e.g., toys and video games) (Vale nza et al., 2019). In comparison, the DwC approach underlines the active participation of children during the design process (Parnell et al., 2008). This approach is also seen as a form of radical co-design, which aims to foster innovative solutions by facilitating collaboration across mult iple roles and promoting equal communication among all

## participants (Sanders & Stappers, 2008)

Co-design with children in ECEfS The tradition of child-centered design essentially aligns with contemporary trends in early childhood p edagogy, where the active participation of children in their learning and teaching processes is highly valued (Dewey, 2004; Lofdahl & Hagglund, 2006). Moreover, with a growing interest in combining design with su stainable education (Brinck et al., 2022), the co-design approach has become particularly popular in recent years. For example, there is a study conducted in a Nepalese school in which the researcher developed a framework for creating sustainable learning environments by analyzing children’s drawings of their ideal learning spaces (Shrestha & Keitsch, 2020). Another typical example is Wake’s research (2015) in New Zealand, which provides insight into the feasibility of co-design in ESD projects; this study highlighted a collaborative effort between children and landscape architects to green school grounds.

In summary, the current body of literature highlights the considerable potential of design in driving early education for sustainability. Given an emphasis on children’s involvement, different co-design approaches – including designing with children and designing for children – offer opportunities for creating innovative projects. However, as discussed before, research gaps have been identified in the literature. First of all, there are a limited number of empirical studies of ECEfS within the Chinese context, indicating that its development is still at an early stage (Zhou et al., 2016). Secondly, regarding the role o f design in facilitating children’s participation i n sustainability-related projects, there is still a lot of potential  that has not been explored yet. Therefore, this study aims to a) understand the current situation of implementing ECEfS in Chinese kindergartens and b) identify how design can enhance ESD practices, particularly through a child-centered approach.

## Methods

This study adopted a qualitative form of research due to its fundamentally exploratory nature, and qualitative data was collected from a kindergarten to understand the  meaning and implications behind their ECEfS-related practices and activities (Gray, 2018). Kindergarten B, located in a southern city in mainland China, is highlighted as an international bilingual kindergarten with a focus on nature education. The choice of Kindergarten B for the study was driven by the convenience sampling techni que, capitalizing on the dual advantages of an auth or’s previous employment at the kindergarten and its str ong interest in collaborating with the research tea m. These factors combined make it an apt option for the exploratory stage of this ongoing project (Etikan et al., 2016). To achieve the research objectives, this study implemented three comprehensive strategies for data collection:

• Observation. The study started with direct observation of both the outdoor  and indoor environments of

Kindergarten B, focusing not on individuals  but on the presence of design elements indicative o f sustainability. This involved detailed observations of playgrounds, gardens, classrooms, public learning areas in corridors, and wall decorations.

• Informal conversation. This was then followed by an unstructured, one-hour conversation held with the

principal and the headteacher of kindergarten B. This dialogue aimed to have a deeper understanding of their educational philosophy, curriculum framework,  and the everyday dynamics of teaching and learning within the institution. Such an open-ended conversational approach provided rich insight s into the educational ethos and practices at Kindergarten B (Creswell, 2018).

• Documentation review. The final step involved examining records of teachi ng and learning activities

over the past year, as documented on Kindergarten B’s WeChat Official Account Platform. This platform is highly popular in China for social sharing, allowin g individuals and organizations to showcase their experiences for promotional purposes (equal to a Fa cebook Page). Reviewing these records offered a better understanding of the information discussed in the conversations. In addition, this process enabled the discovery and inclusion of additional related ma terials as supplementary data, enhancing the depth and breadth of the research findings.

The three data collection approaches in this study are regarded as effective ways to gather information in the case study of Kindergarten B (Wen & Jiang, 2010). Together, they create a triangulation process that enhances the study’s credibility (Hammarburg et al., 2016). To interpret the gathered data, an i nductive approach was used to process the data collected in this study, which aims to extract meaning, explanations, or hypotheses directly from the data rather than evaluating pre-established hypotheses (Lacey & Luff, 2001, p.39). This technique is widely used in qualitative research to analyze open-ended responses (Bilgin, 2006).

## Findings

After analyzing data collected from Kindergarten B,  their ECEfS-related practices fall into three design-oriented categories:

• Project-based activities to foster sustainable awareness.

• Environmental design to create sustainable learning spaces.

• Co-design approaches for exploring sustainable materials.

Project-based activities to foster sustainable awareness During discussions with the principal and headteacher, we knew that teachers here were motivated to use projectbased learning as a key pedagogical strategy. This approach allows both teachers and children to engage in depth with specific topics. Notably, several of the previous project themes were directly linked to sustainability, including ocean conservation, food preservation, and waste sorting. Within the framework of project-based learning, activities were shaped by the children's interests and involved diverse strategies designed by the teachers. A notable example is the "recycling waste for a little yellow hat" activity within the "waste sorting" project. Here, children were encouraged to collect recyclable materials from the ir homes. To enhance engagement, teachers allocated points for different types of recyclable items, wit h a reward system where children earning 10 points received a hat. In addition, to extend learning beyond the classroom, monthly field trips were organized to places such as zoos, farms, exhibitions, and fire stations, which introduced the children to c oncepts related to environmental, social, and economic sustainability.

Special activities were also designed around important festivals, such as t he Spring Festival and Christmas, fostering social identity and broadening cultural understanding among the children. Th ese festival celebrations and field trips, importantly, see sign ificant involvement from parents, both in their des ign and implementation, highlighting the collaborative nature of the educational approach. This method not only enriched the children's learning experience but also activel y involved the community, thereby broadening the impact and relevance of sustainability education.

Despite engaging in practices that align with ECEfS, Kindergarten B faced significant gaps in fully embracing this approach. A key problem was the teachers’ limited knowledge of sustainable deve lopment. The headteacher mentioned the absence of professional d evelopment programs focused on ECEfS for the facult y, highlighting a need for  such training. Also, she pointed out the necessity of equipping teachers with specific strategies for integrating ECEfS into their curricu lum. This is crucial because, when  dealing with unfamiliar content, teachers might default to a more directive style of instruction, potentially undermining the child-centered approach that is central to effective ECEfS implementation. Environmental design to create sustainable learning spaces The environment design at Kindergarten B plays an important role in creating sustainable learning environments, emphasizing the use of outdoor functional areas and indoor decorations  (see Figure 1). Guided by the principles of nature education, teachers designed and built a planted garden, a mud kitchen, and a construction site outdoors, each using natural and recycled materials. For exam ple, the mud kitchen was equipped with water, mud, leaves, flowers, and cookware sourced from children's homes, while the construction site feature d leftover bricks from the kindergarten’s own building process.

Teachers at Kindergarten B were good at repurposing waste and transforming discarded painting brushes and collage materials into decorative and educational art pieces  for classrooms and corridors. I t is notable that some of these recycled designs served dual purposes of decoration and education. A typical example of this is an outdoor role-play stage, which was constructed using trees, strings, and fabric sc raps, creating a designated area for children’s performance and role-playing activities. Another example is a water pipe  installation o n the playground, which encouraged children  to explore various mathematical and scientific conc epts through play, such as fluid dynamics, gravity, and geometry, by allowing them to rearrange the pipes and observe the movement of water and spheres.

The environmental designs within Kindergarten B emphasized sustainability in both environmental and economic dimensions, which  was important in fostering  children's sustainable behaviors and attitudes. The se designs incorporated both outdoor and indoor elements, crafted from natu ral and recycled materials that children interacted with daily. Recognizing the importance of including children's perspectives in the design process, the headteacher planned to involve them more directly in future renovation of the outdoor environment. A proposed initiative was the development of  a child-friendly recording sheet that allow ed children to express their observations and preferences regarding their surroundings. T his approach aimed to empower children by considering their favorite locations, least preferred spots, and their visions for new spaces. Co-design approaches for exploring sustainable materials Beyond participating in the project-based learning activities, children at Kindergarten  B were offered opportunities for self-directed inquiry as part of their daily routines. T his approach allow ed them not only to interact with their surroundings but also to learn through hands-on experience with various materials. The collaboration between the teachers and the children in material manipulation reflected a co-design approach within Kindergarten B. Teachers initiated the process by designing, creating, and placing semi-finished materials with educational purposes in functional areas both inside and outside the classroom. These semi-finished items, such as the cardboard mountain in the indoor art area and the glass tire graffiti table in the o utdoor pool, served as canvases for children’s secondary design and cre ativity. For example, children added brick patterns to the cardboard mountain, transforming it into an Egyptian pyramid. In addition, the headteacher mentioned that each class had a basket for collecting "loose parts" found in nature or at home-items such as branches, stones, cans, and plastic bottles. These materials were then sorted and organized by teachers into a cabinet, as shown in Figure 2, for children to engage in future creative activities. The headteacher explained that the idea originated from the childre n’s wish to have a dinosaur in their classroom. To bring this vision t o life, teachers outlined a dinosaur sketch and enc ouraged the children to collect materials from the kindergarten ’s unused toy wareho use. This initiative exemplified the collaborative spirit between teachers and children,  leading to the successful completion of the dinosaur project, which now stands as a testament to their collective creativity and teamwork.

## Discussion and conclusion

Using Kindergarten B as a case study, this study in vestigated the current implementation of ECEfS in China. Similar to the view of Zhou et al. (2016), Kindergarten B has not formally integrated ECEfS  into its curriculum systematically. Despite this, there is evidence to suggest that the kindergarten teachers here have already begun to weave sustainability-related attitudes and behaviors into their everyday  teaching practices. This inclination towards sustainability could be attributed to vario us factors, including the influence of nature education  – a prominent approach deemed an effective foundation for ECEfS in early childhood education (Siraj-Blatchford et al., 2010), and the impact of their social events a nd activities, such as the promotion of waste sorting policies in recent years in China. These factors likely contribute to the focus on environmental and  economic sustainability observed in Kindergarten B’s practices. In addition, considering that nature education is a core educa tional philosophy at Kindergarten B, it is recommended tha t educators seeking  to promote ECEfS should examine existing practices to identify suitable starting points for integration (Davis, 2015). From a design perspective, this study identified a child-centered approach in kindergarten that is similar to the user-centered method prevalent in design disciplines. It acknowledged three key roles of design in ECEfS:

a) project-based activities to foster sustainable awareness, b) environmental design to create sustainable learni ng

spaces, and c) co-design approaches for sustainable material exploration. These findings supported the fusion of design and education for sustainability (Brinck et al., 2022), offering a practical basis for  merging design with ECEfS to enrich curricula. However, the study also noted the challenges teachers face in maintaining a child-centered approach when teaching unfamiliar topics, such as sustainability. Addressing this, it recommends sustainable knowledge training for teachers, including strategies for comfortably integrating ECEfS.

The study, while impactful, has acknowledged its limitations. First, Kindergarten B is situated in a developed urban area with substantial resources and  advanced educational philosophies, which may limit  the applicability of its findings in less developed contexts. Second, the small sample size – centered on a small group of teachers – calls for broader research to validate the practices observed. In our future work, we plan to extend our observations to a wider array of kindergartens.  Also, we will collabo rate closely with Kindergarten B to develop project-based learning initiatives, using co-design as a strategy to enhance children’s engageme nt with different sustainable topics.
