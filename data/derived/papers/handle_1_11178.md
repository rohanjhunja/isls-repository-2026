# Newcomer Youths’ Ethical Stances in Representing War And Forced Migration

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
We illustrate how a group of newcomer (refugee) young women of color engaged in filmmaking using stop-motion animation to represent a story of a family becoming refugees in the face of war. We ask the following research question: How did the youth adopt, negotiate, and represent their ethical stances in telling stories of forced migration using stop-motion animation? Our analysis shows that through designing their narratives, and figural and gestural representations of marginal lives, bodies, and interactions, the youth centered axiological dimensions of representing violence, and amplified the ethical complexities experienced by families facing war and violent displacement. The youths’ work offers a necessary counter-imaginary for public education in which representational work through creating animations can become a context for making visible and centering ethical and affective dimensions of their experiences of forced migration that are otherwise silenced and invisibilized by the procedurality of refugee resettlement, and in their schools in Canada.

## Introduction

For migrants of color to the Global North, their “present” is often concomitant with loss of dignity (Guha, 1999), for example, through exploitative visa regimes and workplace strictures (Banerjee, 2022), deskilling t hrough systemic devaluing of their prior education (Guo, 2019), and ongoing humiliation through racial microaggression and othering in their daily lives (El-Bialy & Mulay, 2020). In particular, for refugee le arners, their dignity and participation in formal educational contexts is hin dered as t hey are “ caught between the global promise of universal human rights, the definition of citizenship rights within nation-states, and the realization of these sets of rights in everyday practices” (Dryden-Peterson, 2016, p. 473). To resist such systemic marginalization of migrant and refugee learners, learning scientists have illustrated how dignity-affirming approaches (Espinoza et al., 2020) can be centered in informal learning spaces to supp ort refugee learners’ disciplinary engagement throu gh centering mobility (Rahm, 2018), linguistic and discursive he terogeneity (Rosebery et al., 2010; Kayumova & Sengupta, 2022), sociopolitical commitments of the community (Tan & Faircloth, 2023), and eco-social connections and responsibilities (Takeuchi et al., 2021).

In this paper, we further expand and deepen this bo dy of scholarship by illustrating how the creation of multimodal narratives by refugee youth of color can reveal marginal and silenced experiences and feelings of loss, pain and hope that underlie their experiences of wa r and forced migration. Our work arises fr om a particular concern that educational experiences of refugee learners must enable them “to connect their pasts, their presents, and their futures” (Dryden-Peterson, 2022, p. 5), which necessitates recognizi ng and centering their stories of migration (Ryu & Tuvilla, 2018). We illustrate how three newcomers, who are young women of color in their late teens, collaboratively created a stop-motion animation of a fictional account of a family  becoming refugees. We ask the following research question: How did the yo uth adopt, negotiate, and represent their ethical s tances in telling stories of forced migration using stop-motion animation? Our analysis reveals the micro-interactional, material, and discursive moves through which the yo uth re-designed their narrat ives and figural representations of marginal lives, bodies, and interactions in ways  that amplified the ethical complexities experience d by themselves and families facing war and violent displacement.

## Theoretical background

Our work amplifies resonances between scholarship i n refugee education, learning sciences, and critica l gender and migration studies. In the field of refugee educ ation, given that refugee children and youth of col or often migrate to European and North American countries where they are positioned as academically deficient, scholars have explored the design and evaluation of hybrid s paces and afterschool programs in literacy (Kennedy  et al.,

2019) and STEM disciplines (Ryu et al., 2019). To r esist and challenge such deficit framing, scholars have also

argued for centering newcomers’ self-authored counter-narratives as told by themselves (Ryu & Tuvilla, 20 18). Scholars have also argued that the reification of n arratives of victimhood by researchers can be avoid ed if “researchers could co-construct narrative with forced migrants positioned  as the tellers of their own stories” (Saltsman & Majidi, 2021, p.  2526). Synergistic scholarship in the Learning Scie nces scholars have argued that filmmaking by marginalized youth of color can cente r affective, political, and ethical voices of immig rant and marginalized youth of color. Vakil and McKinney de Royston (2022) explored youths’ sensemaking about ethical complexities of technology as the yo uth made a documentary on tech-mediated surveillance of immigrant communities of color. Our work is also inspired by scholarship that highlights the importance of attending to, and centering heteroglossia (Rosebery et al, 2010; Seng upta et al., 2021), affe ctive and ethical trails of micro interactions between learners (Kayumova & Sengupta, 2022; Vossoughi et al., 2020), while also highlighting the importance of facilitation (Hladik et al., 2023) in  centering multilingual and immigrant learners of c olor in informal settings.

Critical race and intersectional scholarship have l ong emphasized the power of storytelling to counter dominant and master narratives with experiential kn owledge (Crenshaw, 1991). Unsilencing stories is an  act of “deep care” (Banerjee et al., 2022) that is embedded in the intersectional feminist liberatory practice of centering dignity in forging solidarity with marginalized peo ple. Banerjee et al. (2022) positioned deep care as work in which the ethic of care is inextricably interwoven with practice. Liste ning contrapuntally to voices of women reveals essential tensions within the speakers’ voices, which in turn might reveal distinctions between an ethic of care on one hand, and an institutionalized and indi vidualistic moral language of justice and rights (see also: Gilligan, 2015, p. 72). In our context, stories sha red by newcomer youth and women of color foreground ed an ethic of care rooted in Southern solidarity (Banerj ee & Connell, 2018) that resisted deference to the metropole through making visible feelings of pain, loss, separation, nostalgia, hope and solidarity. This stands in contrast to adopting the institutionalized language of refugee and immigrant “rights” as mandated by governments a nd resettlement agencies, which invisiblizes the ethic al and a ffective dimensions of their experiences of forced migration (YARI Collective, 2024). The rest of this  paper focuses on illustrating how newcomer young women of color crafted counter-stories of violence and displacement through collaboratively telling stor ies that affirm dignity and humanity of the affected. Our focus on the axiological dimensions of the technical-artistic representational practice of creating stop-motion animations foregrounds solidarity and deep c are as ethical stances adopted by the youth in their counter-storytelling.

## Study context, method, and analytic approach

We share insights from a two-year long research collaboration with newcomer (ref ugee and immigrant) youth to center their stories of migration through multimoda l forms of storytelling, and to offer counter-imaginations for Canada’s immigration and resettle ment processes (Banerjee & Sengupta, 2023; YARI Col lective, 2023). In weekly meetings spanning a year, 26 newcomer youth engaged in a range of activities such as designing computer simulations, filmmaking, animation, storytelling, and performance art, held in the form of multi-hour long, weekly sessions on the University campus. The youth participants were recruited in partnership with four of t he city’s largest organizations that provide resettlement services. In our analysis, we focus on the storytelling work of three friends —Sarah, Afsoon, and Daisy (pseudonyms)—whose families obtained refugee status less than a year back in Canada, after nearly a decade of fleeing violence. Religiously, they identify as Muslims and have homelands in the Middle East and Central Asia, in countries that have been affected by imperialist ic invasion, political turmoil, and America’s “war on terror” over decades. The youth are close friends who met i n summer camp for newcomer youth organized by a resettlement agency and joined our project because they wanted to share their stories of forced migrat ion that in their experience, were silenced and invisibilized by the procedurality of refugee resettlement, and in their schools in Canada (YARI Collective, 2023). Over a period of eight weeks, they engaged in making a stop-motion animated film with the first author who served as a facilitator-researcher and is experienced in this form of filmmaking. The data for our analysis is in the form of video and a udio recordings of the participants’ group work, fo cus group conversations and follow-up interviews with them throughout the process. As the youth engaged in animation filmmaking using wooden mannequins and playdough figures, a rich material and discursive space emerged through the interweaving of creating multimodal representations and their dialogic interactions. While planning and creating the animation, the youth enacted to each other the possible gestural/postural moves, monologues and dialogues of the characters in their narratives, and feelings of the filmed characters, and molded the wooden mannequins to emb ody physical stances. T he enactments, as performed by the youth filmmakers and as embodied by the mannequ ins, created courses of possible actions or ‘ethica l trails’ (Vossoughi et al., 2020) among the youth to model the micro-ethics of the experience being portrayed. The youth also replayed the captured video of animated scenes on their recording devices to discuss, refine and advance the plotline and the animation.

We used the constant comparative method nested with in a constructivist grounded theoretical approach to analyze instances of collaborative work, to enter “participants’ liminal world of meaning and action” (Charmaz, 2014, p. 241), while also dialogically n egotiating our theoretical commitments to centering  ethical and affective stances of the youth in doing this representational  work. This meant that rather than focusing on the “mangle of practice” (Pickering, 1995) of intertwined dimensio ns of epistemic and representational work, we focus  on how the feelings of war and migration  are adopted and expressed in negotiating the plotli ne and the character stories of the film. The theoretical constructs of deep car e (Banerjee et al., 2022) and Southern solidarity (Banerjee & Connell, 2019) emerged as a lens that was resonant with our an alysis. We used theoretical sampling (Glaser & Strauss, 2017) identify key episodes where major sh ifts in the film’s narrative took shape, mediated t hrough dialogical and material interactions and facilitato rs’ co-designing scaffolds, rather than other epi sodes where participants worked independently to execute indivi dual tasks (e.g., drawing a backdrop) that were alr eady discussed and negotiated among themselves. The episodes presented make visible who they are in solidarity with, as well as the material and discursive work of representing feelings of loss in the face of war, revealing how they came to represent loss of home (Episode 1) and loss of family (Episode 2).

## Findings

At the broadest level, the youths’ animation reveal s who they are in solidarity with. Their choice to depict the characters as Muslim, and their choice of the openi ng music in Arabic (Daisy’s primary language at hom e) and closing music in Dari (Sarah’s primary language at home) reveal that the story is rooted in the lived experiences of the youth. At the same time, in follow-up interviews after the animation was completed, th e youth noted that these choices should also orient the viewers’ atten tion to the ongoing violence and sufferings in Pale stine and other countries in the Middle East and Central Asia  (YARI Collective, 2024). Next, we present episodes  that illustrate how these solidarities became embodied in their creation through deep care (Banerjee et al., 2022). In talking about the portrayal of the ‘daughter’s’ articulations of disbelief to her parents, Sarah wa nted to clarify with Afsoon if they are going to “add dialogs” in their film (turn  4) and how they are going to be represented (turn 6). In other words, Sarah wished to understand the form of representation, visual or aural, using which they, the filmmakers, would represent the predicament. In these turns, Sarah reflected on the pragmatics of the representational work which was proposed by Afsoon in turn 1 —how they are going to represent what has been decided to be important to represent. Further in their conversation, as they are discussing wheth er to show the family escaping in a car (turn 13), Afsoon argued that it might be “hard to show the things like inside the car” whereas showing characters “walking is easier” (tur n 14). The reasoning which Afsoon proposed was that  the civilians moving to safety amid violence often take  arduous and traumatic journeys, that is, being out  in the sun or in the deserts “happens for many people” (turn 11). For their animat ed film, the pervasive experiences of war refugees’ suffering can be highlighted as the chara cters are shown walking, as compared to escaping in  a car, which might be difficult to depict and animate. We observe in these instances that the youth are reaso ning what experiences of displacement and refugeehood can the y possibly visualize and therefore pragmatically re-present with the representational tools and techniques of a nimation at disposal. In thinking a bout the experiences of the suddenly dispossessed, the youth are grappling with  the details and the modality of re-presentation to possibly argue: how should we depict the suffering that is important to show? Their discursive negotiation with each other regarding how to represent the feelings of the characters (the ‘daughter’s’ plea to her ‘father’) or the extraordinary conditions of escape (families forced to walk in ha rsh weather and geography) centers experiences of dispossession and suffering that fundamentally mark  the beginning of displacement and forced migration. This conversation foregrounds the importance of cen tering familial bond and mourning that entwines corporeal dignity. Rather than giving into the mora l language of human rights, the youth made visible how the right to grieve was denied to them by the violence of an imperial war. The juxtaposition of the youths ’ humor while discussing the ethical decision of representi ng the fallen child’s dignity is also salient, beca use it reveals that surviving war and refugeehood can become part banter among these three very close friends. Their banter, however, should not be interpreted as a frivolous take on the grim realities of war. Instead, the youths’ engagement in such playful banter while discussing the mechani cs of carrying the boy’s body can be seen as a form  of deep care toward each other, while engaging in attempts to foreground marginal experiences of war. Reflective summary The youths’ animation represented the first few mom ents, hours and days experienced by a family in the  face of war, thus portraying an intimate depiction of the h uman impact of militaristic violence that underlies  the very beginnings of a family becoming refugees. The child ’s disbelief of the sudden violence and her despera te cries, the family’s mourning, and the father’s decision to continue moving despite their younger child’s death embodied ethical and affective stances of the youth, which in turn were represented in the form of specific figurations of the mannequins, the youths’ embodied enactments and utt erances. This is evidence of what Banerjee et al. (2022) termed deep care, that is, work in which the ethic of care is inext ricably interwoven with practice, which in our case includes the material, representational and di scursive work of creating stop-motion animations, as well as caring for each other during the process of collaboration. The youths’ work offers a necessary dis/orientation  (Ahmed, 2006) to public education in the form of a counter-imaginary of a dialogic, representational activity that can make visible newcomer youths’ feelings of loss, pain and hope during forced migration that otherwise remain silenced in their classrooms and in their resettlement process (YARI Collective, 2024). Creating this animation can be seen as a contrapuntal (Philip & Sengupta, 2021) move on the youths’ part, as they foreground the st ory of a Muslim family becoming refugees due to imp erial violence, thus also bringing to light who they are also in solidarity with. In this sense, their animation can be seen as a counter-model (Sengupta et al., 2022) of forced migration that they authored in their voices, which stands in contrast to the rigid procedurality of immigration to Canada. In conclusion, our paper shows how engagement in the technical-artistic practice of animated filmmaking can create  a space for engaging with expansive narrative possibilities while critically attending to issues of imperial violence, global displacement, and refugee crisis t hat forcibly structure the lives of racialized and marginalized immigrant and refugee learners.
