# Unpacking Flexibility in Teacher Professional Learning on WhatsApp Messaging Network Platform

**Conference:** CSCL 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This study examines how flexibility manifests as a lived practice within a voluntary WhatsApp-based teachers’ professional community. While flexibility is a recognized affordance of online learning, its role in teacher professional networks remains undertheorized. Drawing on 2,307 posts across 349 conversations among 146 teachers, we conceptualized and measured flexibility through three dimensions: temporal, concerning engagement during discretionary time; sequential, involving the recurrence of conversations; and structural, which maps network connections across discussions. The analysis reveals profound temporal flexibility, with nearly two-thirds of posts occurring outside school hours and over a 40% during holidays and summer break. Although recurring conversations constituted 11% of discussions, they generated 25.4% of posts and were longer with statistically significant differences. These recurring discussions formed the network’s core, effectively integrating teachers and sustaining dialogue. These findings demonstrate that flexibility is not a static design feature but a dynamic, socially constructed practice fundamental to professional growth.

As teacher professional development migrates to online spaces, a critical need exists to understand the mechanisms that sustain learning in these environments. While teacher communities are celebrated for fostering collaboration (Hadar & Brody, 2017; Vangrieken et al., 2017), the integration of popular platforms like WhatsApp introduces under-examined dimensions of professional learning. This paper, part of a larger project, presents a focused investigation into one aspect: the role of flexibility in time, pace, and format. Building on Collis and Moonen’s (2012) framework, this study adapts two of their dimensions, temporal and sequential, to the social network context and extends the model by introducing a structural dimension that captures the connective nature of conversations across the network. This initial focus allows for a deep analysis, while future stages may incorporate additional dimensions tailored to the community's dynamics. We argue that flexibility is not merely a logistical feature of online learning, but a central element that shapes participation and sustains teacher learning in the community.

## Theoretical background

Teacher learning in professional communities The pursuit of educational quality improvement depends on recruiting effective teachers and providing them with continuous, meaningful professional development (Barber & Mourshed, 2007). Teacher quality, however, is not a static attribute acquired in forma l preparation; it is a dynamic capacity cultivated through continuous learning, a process best supported by collaborative practices (Author 2 & colleague, 2010). Sustained collaboration is essential for professional growth, reshaping both teaching methods and teachers’ core conceptions of their practice (Shulman, 2005; Mourshed et al., 2011). Therefore, investigating effective methods for developing their knowledge is critical for strengthening educational systems. This perspective resonates with Lave and Wenger’s (1991) theory of communities of practice, which reframes learning as a social process situated within authentic interactions, where participation and mutual support are central. The core characteristics of teacher communities, including shared vision, collective responsibility, reflective dialogue, and trust, underscore the importance of non-hierarchical and sustained collaboration (Bolam et al., 2005; Vescio et al., 2008; Scherz et al., 2021). When supported by flexibility, the very conditions that enable and sustain teacher learning are amplified, allowing these collaborative features to manifest more dynamically. 294 Online learning and virtual learning communities The digital landscape offers significant opportunities for teacher development, including access to resources, flexibility, autonomy in regulating learning, and rich potential for interaction (Lay et al., 2020; Bragg et al., 2021). Yet, research consistently highlights the difficulty of cultivating authentic collaboration in online environments, a challenge contributing to high attrition rates in online programs (Bragg et al., 2021). Virtual learning communities can address this gap by offering spaces that are both structured and flexible. In these spaces, professional dialogue, trust, and mutual support are actively cultivated. Their unique advantage lies in fostering collaborative interactions while leveraging the distinct benefits of online learning, notably the affordance of flexibility.

Flexibility in online professional learning Within online learning, flexibility is a defining affordance, commonly described as the provision of self-directed learning opportunities, enabling teachers to select content, regulate pace, and adapt the depth of engagement (Veletsianos & Houlden, 2019; Bragg et al., 2021). Flexible learning encompasses the ability to learn at any time, location, and to integrate different modalities (Loon, 2022; Li & Wong, 2018). This allows teachers to weave professional learning into their daily routines (Ulanday et al., 2021). However, established frameworks for flexibility were mostly developed for formal, institution-based systems and are less applicable to dynamic social platforms (Collis & Moonen, 2012). Collis and Moonen conceptualized flexibility across five dimensions: (1) time, referring to when learning takes place and the pace at which learners progress; (2) location, where learning occurs, whether at home, school, or workplace; (3) content, what subject matter is studied and to what depth; (4) instructional approach, how learning activities are designed and delivered; and (5) delivery and assessment, how learning outcomes are evaluated and recognized. While time and location transfer readily to social networks, the other dimensions require significant adaptation. In WhatsAppbased communities, for example, content emerges organically from participant s rather than from a predefined syllabus, instructional methods are socially negotiated rather than instructor-driven, and formal assessment is replaced by social recogn ition and continued participation. Applying this framework to informal, networked contexts highlights both its enduring relevance and the need for conceptual extensions. This theoretical gap is mirrored in empirical work. Studies measuring flexibility (e.g., Soffer et al., 2019) have largely been situated in higher education, conceptualizing it as a technical attribute of learning management systems rather than as a socially constructed practice. Most studies frame flexibility in relation to efficiency, acc ess, or user satisfaction (Huang et al., 2024), leaving its role in the learning process itself underexplored. This underscores the need to investigate how flexibility is enacted in practice. The present study addresses this gap by examining a WhatsAppbased professional community as a site where flexibility is continuously constructed. The analysis is part of a larger ongoing project.

## Research questions

The primary question guiding this study is: How is flexibility in teacher learning enacted as a lived practice within a WhatsApp-based professional community? Specifically, we ask three research questions: RQ1:  At what times do teachers engage in the community, and how does this reflect their use of flexibility (temporal dimension)? RQ2: How does the recurrence of conversations manifest over time, and what is its significance for learning (sequential dimension)? RQ3: How are network connections across conversations shaped by teachers participating in multiple discussions, and what does this indicate about patterns of continuity in the community (structural dimension)?

## Context and participants

The study examines a voluntary virtual professional learning community for middle school teachers of JewishIsraeli Culture Studies, a newly introduced mandatory subject for which most teachers had no prior training. Established in 2017 by the Center for Educational Technology (CET), the community operates on WhatsApp and is facilitated by two facilitators, who combine structured routines, synchronous discussions, and ongoing peer interaction. The analysis covered one school year (2021-2022), during which 149 teachers in the community actively contributed at least one post.

Data sources and units of analysis 295 We analyzed 2,307 posts (containing an identifier, timestamp, teacher identity, and message content) organized into 349 conversations. Drawing on Hymes’s (1972) ethnography of communication, we treat conversations as bounded speech events defined by contin uity in time, content, and participation. Building on discourse research highlighting the role of sequences in shaping meaning (Lefstein et al., 2015), we defined a conversation by its thematic continuity, classifying messages as part of the same conversation if teachers returned to a previous point, after extended pauses, sometimes with new participants. This extends the definition of conversation boundaries to include thematic continuity in addition to temporal and sequential criteria. Modes of analysis To capture flexibility, we adapted existing frameworks (e.g., Collis & Moonen, 2012) to this social network context, examining three dimensions: (1) Temporal flexibility: This dimension captures the ability to participate at any given time. We employed descriptive statistics to analyze post timestamps, tracing teachers’ activity beyond school hours, including different days, time segments, holidays, and summer break. (2) Sequential flexibility: This refers to the capacity to sustain dialogue over time. It was measured by identifying conversation recurrence and by comparing post averages in recurring versus non-recurring discussions. Due to non-normal data distribution, a Mann–Whitney U test was applied to assess statistical significance. (3) Structural flexibility: This refers to the way overlapping participation links discussions across the network. We used social network analysis (SNA) to map connections, visualizing conversations as nodes (sized by number of posts and colored by recurrence). Iterative filtering retained first links with ≥3 and then ≥5 shared teachers, revealing the network’s core, highlighting which conversations were central to the community's structure. Temporal flexibility: Teacher activity was not confined to institutional schedules. Nearly 71% of posts occurred during afternoon, evening, and night hours, demonstrating consistent engagement during personal time (Table 1). Active teachers contributed across all parts of the day, with only the late-night hours (after 21:59 until 6:59) showing lower activity, reflecting a natural boundary aligned with rest time. Some teachers used night hours, demonstrating how flexibility allowed engagement whenever individual schedules permitt ed. Structural flexibility:  The social network analysis reveals the structural importance of recurring conversations. Out of 349 conversations, only 25 remained in the filtered network (edges ≥ 5 shared teachers), connected by 42 links. This indicates that only a small subset of discussions attracted broad participation across multiple teachers. The recurring conversations (red nodes) dominate the network’s core, showing both larger node sizes (more posts) and denser connections (more teachers).  Non-recurring conversations (blue nodes) are smaller and peripheral. Although recurring conversations accounted for only a small fraction of the total discussions, their nodes displayed disproportionately high betweenness centrality, functioning as bridging hubs that connected otherwise separat e clusters in the network. This positioning highlights their structural role in sustaining the community’s cohesion. This pattern suggests that recurring conversations are critical for integrating the community, linking different groups of teachers, and creating a cohesive space for professional dialogue (Figure 1).

This study demonstrates that flexibility in this WhatsApp-based community is a multifaceted practice manifested through temporal, sequential, and structural dimensions. Teachers consistently engaged beyond institutional schedules and revisited topics in recurring conversations that formed the core structure of the community network. Unlike flexibility in formal online courses-typically a predefined feature, the flexibility observed in the WhatsApp community was socially constructed, emerging from teachers’ ongoing choices to initiate, sustain, and renew professional dialogue during their free time. Rather than viewing flexibility solely as a logistical feature, our findings highlight its enactment as a pedagogical practice, resonating with theoretical pers pectives that emphasize its learning value. This study is an initial stage of a broader project. Subsequent analysis will expand on these findings, extend the categorization of message functions and content, and examine the relationship between voluntariness and flexibility.
