# Gamification and Evolution of KB Metacognitive Practice to Promote Agentic Learning and Facilitate Differentiated Learning in the Singapore Primary Classroom

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
This practice-oriented paper illustrates the evolution of a knowledge-building (KB) approach in a fast-paced, content-driven educational setting. The practitioner’s 13 years of teaching experience with 9 years of KB practice, highlights a change in knowledge creation. Over four iterations, the practitioner refined a methodology emphasizing student agency through inquiry-based, gamified approach that fosters continuous metacognitive discourse. The latest iteration showcases the efficiency of the differentiated KB cube (KB3) for autonomous team discourse, a catalyst designed to drive inquiry across subjects while accommodating diverse learner profiles. Differentiation ensures meaningful participation for all students, while gamification promotes motivation and inclusivity. This shifts the teacher's role from a knowledge provider to a facilitator, fostering a student-idea-centric environment (Hong & Sullivan, 2009) where learners lead co-constructive discourse. By integrating Bloom’s revised taxonomy into theory-building questions, a shift in pedagogy is evident, whereby knowledge is created and improvised by students than teachers.

## Introduction

This paper focuses on the evolution of the practitioner’s knowledge-building (KB) practice that has transformed students learning through  active metacognitive discourse, differentiated approaches and gamified learning. The practitioner’s initial KB inquiry cycle started with generating idea diversity from students and gradually shifted the way students learnt which resulted in improvable ideas, rise above and epistemic agency (see Figure 1). The practitioner’s motivation behind the shift stemmed from the identification of consolidated group perspectiv es in earlier iterations, which sustained long diverse  discursive discussion s that were generated by students. The practitioner makes a consistent effort to use students’ collective advancement of ideas (Tan et al., 2014) to move discussions in the classroom which improves student  motivation and participation. This paper addresses the benefits, challenges, improv ised strategies and innovative approaches to promote student agency. Central to the practitioner's approach is the belief that every student’s idea is important and improvable, which fos ters a sense of self-worth and confidence in students. These strategies have also been adopted and scaled by a different primary school after an action research collaboration in 2022. The schools’ successful interdisciplinary implementation of the practitioner’s KB via Iteration 3 was shared at the International Knowledge Building Network Learning Community (KBNLC)  2024 in Singapore, further validating the transform ative potential of this approach in promoting student agency and collaborative learning.

T his innovative approach  supports the principle of "community knowledge, collective responsibility " by fostering collaboration, where students contribu te to group understanding. This process moves students through vari ous levels of Bloom's Taxonomy, from understanding and applying ideas to more complex tasks like  refining ideas,  analyzing and evaluating group contributions. As students critica lly and creatively engage with these diverse viewpoints, they can generate "improvable ideas," as they refine and express their ideas freely without feeling judged by their peers or teacher.

KB3’s game point sheet (Annex E), rewards varied thinking and promotes "epistemic agency," by motivating students to engage in more complex group  discussions. Points range from 1 for individual ideas to 6 for group reflections, with a bonus for promising i deas. Teachers can use  the group point sheet as feedback to assess contributions, aligning with the principle o f " constructive use of authoritative sources," encouraging students to critically evaluate the groups strength in coming up with different types of ideas. In an innovative approach to boost a team’s discussion efficacy, points are awarded based on the depth of discussions rather than the presentation of correct answers. This discussion-based points reward system shifts the focus from answer accuracy to meaningful and co llaborative dialogue  between team members for idea development and growth. Also, any unanswered ideas from KB cloud  (Annex A) discussions are revisited when time permits, and teachers would intervene for alignment to the subject’s syllabus instructional objectives. ## Methodology

This short practice paper is written based on the evolving practices and classroom observations of the practitioner over 8 years. This allowed for continuous reflectio n and method refinement which developed the practit ioner’s KB pedagogical cycle for School A. Also, the improved KB cards, KB cloud and KB inquiry cycles (Arumugam,

2024) were shared with School B via an action research project in 2022.Teacher reflections were also gathered to

validate the effectiveness of the improved KB produ cts towards stud ents learning. Teachers at School B have enacted the lessons successfully and have scaled up  KB to different teaching levels and subjects in th eir school. This reaffirms the effectiveness of the KB pedagogi cal cycle improvised by (Arumugam, 2024) as it had a multidisciplinary outreach. Teachers in the project strongly believe that these structured tools promoted collective responsibility. Teacher from School B reflected: “I really like the idea of using the symbols and the  sentence starters that my students can use as they comment on their friend’s KB clouds during the KB Walk.  The sentence starters also gave them the responsibility on the need to provide  constructive feedback which requires them to think further as they commented.” Sustaining idea-centric pedagogical cycles through gamification The latest KB Cube developed by (Arumugam, 2024) fosters a Kno wledge Building culture through a gamified approach, encouraging idea improvement, community knowledge, and epistemic agency. By incorporating varied points for different question prompts, the cube engages students by creating an element of chance and competition, which motivates them to participate actively to develop better questions (Annex C). This cycle sustains interest among young diverse learners, addressing challenges like idea fatigue which is common when students are unable to produce promising ideas or new knowledge towards the end of a KB inquiry cycle in class. Thus, this approach ensures a fair  and equal opportunity for all students to independently  track their team game point sheet  (Annex E). This provides visual feedback for the teacher to mo nitor the types of questions. Data from student’s survey were used to guide and improve the KB inquiry cycle to meet the diverse learner profiles. The KB Cube promotes higher-order thinking skills from Bloom’s Taxonomy, such a s analysis, evaluation, and creation, encouraging students to e ngage in deeper inquiry across subjects. It support s critical thinking, collaborative knowledge construction, and  metacognitive reflection with minimal teacher promp ting, fostering a classroom culture where knowledge is sh ared as a community asset. The KB Cube fosters equi table learning experiences by transforming classrooms into dynamic, collaborat ive knowledge-building spaces that enhances student engagement.

In Figure 9, students were asked to explain how KB3 provided randomness as their cube had different prompts on e ach side. Students highlighted that it boost ed the team’s question bank of ideas, reduced any conflict due to poorly written expressions and they  learnt that besides a clarifying question, there we re other types of questions to ask their peers.

Based on a class of 53 Primary 4 students  in Figure 10, 15 students reflected that the (KB 3) had deepened their learning of science concepts better. 11 students felt that it a llowed them to gain new insights from their friends’ idea. 12 stud ents informed that they were allowed to contribute more ideas individually than they previously could. In all, t his provides great confidence towards the practitioner’s approach  and pedagogical principals. This continually encourages the practitioner  to evaluate the teaching matrix for continuous growth and improvement.
