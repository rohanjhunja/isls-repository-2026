# Interdisciplinary Contextualizing in Data Storytelling about Immigration Topics

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Case studies illuminate interdisciplinary contextualization in data storytelling, within the context of curriculum modules engaging middle school students in data-infused, PBL drawing from disciplines of social studies, English Language Arts, and data science. We address the questions: How do students tell stories with data, and how candata storytelling contribute to students’ data agency and identity? We discuss the potential of telling empirical stories with data and integrating rigorous data analysis with humanistic learning.

As data science has become a vital interdisciplinary endeavor, r esearch posits the significance of facilitating authentic and deep student engagement with data (Lee et al., 2021; Lanouette, 2024; Miller et al., 2024a, 2024b; Wilkerson & Polman, 2020), especially in providing opportunities for K-12 students to apply data-related understanding and practices to relevant societal issues, which can help them develop acumen with data and agency as learners. Ms. Eileen, teaches at Oak Park Expeditionary, a public K-12 school located in the northeastern U.S., serving predominantly Latine American students and students from immigrant families. Aiming to spark interest in reading materials and examining immigration trends throughout U.S. history, Ms. Eileen organized lessons that explore U.S. immigration patterns in the 20th century (Unit 1), the role immigration and citizenship played in the experience of Japanese American incarceration (JAI) during World War II (Unit 2), and current day immigration, migration, and detention (Unit 3). This poster draws data from engagement in two out of the three units, examining how students tell stories with data, and how data storytelling contribute to data agency and identity.

## Conceptual framework

We developed a conceptual framework to explicate the forms of contextualization learners may participate in when they engage in interdisciplinary data storytelling within PBL instruction. This framework adapts from Radinsky’s (2020) “modes of data narration,” Gebre & Polman’s (2020) conceptualization of “contextualization,” as well as specific data practices relevant to disciplines our initiative intersects with (i.e., ELA, social studies, and data science). The framework involves three main elements: connecting data to oneself, contextualizing with data representations, and grounding and situating data in texts, narratives, and inquiries. C onnecting data to oneself encompasses instances where learners “tell a story about oneself working with data” (Radinsky, 2020), connect data to self and familiar contexts, or “narrate oneself into a data-represented world” (Radinsky, 2020). Contextualizing with data representations —mirroring Radinsky (2020)’s “animating a data representation” — delineates processes of analyzing and interpreting data, making arguments, and integrating data into explanations. This form of contextualization can be leveraged to 1) depict change over time, 2) categorize demographic data, and 3) characterize similarity and differences among different locations, scale, and scope. Finally, grounding and situation data story in texts, narratives, and inquiries involves practices where learners link texts, “incorporate data into extant narrative,” (Radinsky, 2020) and structure questions to scaffold and/or further narratives. In the process of contextualizing data described above, we conjectured that learners would develop data agency and data identity (Tran et al., 2025), which encompasses interest, fluency, and relationships with data. We posit that learners manifest data agency when they carry out data practices to pursue goals, enact values, or act on their beliefs (Verhoeven et al., 2021). Our conceptualization of data identity builds on notions of STEM identity (e.g., Dou & Cian, 2022), specifically in how learners come to see themselves as “data people,” or people who find data as useful in their lives and have the capability to use it for valued purposes (Tran et al., 2025). Case studies Anita recalled an integrated process to learn about the mass incarceration of Japanese Americans  (Unit 2), including reading the memoir Farewell to Manzanar  (Wakatsuki Houston & Houston, 1973) in ELA class, as well as reviewing different documents, photos, and videos. Anita created a PowerPoint presentation to tell her “Manzanar Data Story.” Reading the Farewell to Manzanar memoir allowed Anita to surface texts, narratives, and inquiries that linked directly to the personal narration of Jeanne Wakatsuki who was herself forced to relocate 2806 to Manzanar at the age of seven. As she told the story of herself working with the data, Anita surfaced that “it was cool to see how the data connected to the book, like seeing Jeanne’s family in the data set was really interesting.” In her data analysis, Anita engaged in thinking with data representations, especially around impacts of location, scale, and scope. In particular, she  made a graph to show the Departure date and Destination State when the Wakatsuki family members left the camp (See Figure 1). In that graph, one person left late 1944 to Utah, five left during middle 1945 tonew Jersey, and ten did not leave until late 1945 to relocate to California. Anita interpreted these data points of time and locations, demonstrating how incarceration camps pulled families apart by separating them “geographically, physically, and emotionally.” Anita observed  that the different Destination states that Jeanne’s family members were relocated to after incarceration served as compelling evidence that backed her argument: “As you can see on the graph, the Wakatsuki family members all ended up departing Manzanar at different times, and most were destined to different states. Each individual dot on the graph is a Wakatsuki… The geographic changes at the end of incarceration camps for the captives were notable. This created real separation, physical separation.” In this instance, the student was able to draw evidence from the data representation –while using details and themes from the book to ground and enrich a claim that she had formulated.

Representation (Depict Change Over Time by Country of Origin) Alivia’s case is from  the last unit, which  required students to explore four different datasets covering modern immigration trends including current asylum and refugee seekers, historical census data, and ICE detention center data. Generally speaking, students expressed a sense of familiarity and resonance from discussing the data that shared some backgrounds with their own. Alivia also felt that sense of connection, “because I’m Dominican, I always like [to] talk about my country.” This familiar context and existing identification — a Dominican Americandaughter and granddaughter living in a vibrant Latin American immigrant community among peers who are mostly children of immigrants or immigrants themselves —lent her the confidence: “when we started the project, … it was already familiar to me. ” For her data story, Alivia made an infographic, weaving together data from the Transactional Records Access Clearinghouse, claims, and commentary about immigration patterns of ten countries and territories of her choice (see Figure 2). Interestingly, when brainstorming for her assignment, Alivia said that the word safety kept coming back to her. It was, she explained, a key reason many immigrants left their homes in the first place: “many immigrants thought … America would be a safe place for them and be where they can start their lives over.” Yet throughout the unit, she found herself contemplating whether all immigrants in fact experience safety when arriving in the U.S.  To foreground her key point, Alivia inserted a text at the top of her infographic: “In America, many people said that the United States is a welcoming place, but we have learned that this statement is not always applied to everyone equitably.” Such a composition reflected her effort to ground her data  story in a configuration of texts, narratives, and inquiries: Drawing from personal background knowledge and classroom readings about immigration laws and policy across U.S. history (texts), she confronted the hypocrisies and nuances in the national narrative that the U.S. is a welcoming place for all (narrative). This move also connected her data story to the unit’s overarching inquiry —“Who gets to be an American?” (inquiry) where students questioned who truly gets to feel safe, accepted, and protected in America. Alivia’s and Anita’s cases illustrate a nuanced and evolving relationship with data—one that reflects the intertwined development of data identity and data agency. Our analysis of the cases exemplifies how the two students utilized data in socially meaningful and personally relevant sensemaking, while adding to their repertoire of powerful skills and practices drawn from various disciplines (Tran et al., 2025). They not only drew on familiar connections but also took on the challenges to go beyond, as they evaluated and navigated the myriad contextual evidence available to them.
