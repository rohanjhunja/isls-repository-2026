# Do Award-Winning Serious Games Meet Benchmarks for Quality Learning Tools?

**Conference:** ISLS 2024

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Helping educators identify high-quality serious games without engaging in prolonged gameplay is an ongoing concern for game-based learning in formal education. We examined the websites of six award-winning video games to understand if, and to what extent, they described the five key benchmarks proposed by Dubé and colleagues (2020). Our analysis suggests that the evaluation model needs further refinement of definitions and clarifying contexts, such as formal settings, to be more useful.

## Introduction

Game-based learning (GBL) in formal education can foster critical thinking, problem-solving, and collaboration. However, incorporating games in the classroom has obstacles, including teachers’ inability to identify high-quality games in the curriculum (Foster & Shah, 2020). There is a lack of a unified framework for assessing games, which hinders teachers' adoption of GBL.

While Dubé  et al. (2020) have proposed five benchmarks to asse ss the quality of educational apps: curriculum, feedback, scaffolding, learning theory,  and the development team’s educational expertise, the initial use of their model provided only a binary yes/no re sponse, leaving ambiguity about the description qua lity. The goal of this study is to investigate the relationship between award-winning serious games created for educational purposes and Dubé et al.'s framework. The inquiry s eeks to determine how well these acclaimed serious games include detailed information about these criteria.

## Theoretical perspectives

Dubé et al. (2020), building on previous research (Vaala et al., 2015; Cayton-Hodges et al., 2015), proposed five key elements to identify high-quality educational apps: (1) curriculum-content taught schools; (2) feedback; (3) scaffolding; (4) learning theory, and (5) developme nt team-identifying expertise, including academic experts. They suggested adding these elements to descriptions for easier identification by teachers.

Using this framework, Dubé et al. (2020) studied 90 mathematics apps available on the Apple App Store. These apps evaluated only included an average of 1. 67 of the 5 educational benchmarks. They also used these benchmarks with simulated app descriptions to  evaluate whether educators valued these elements wh en considering an app and found they valued benchmarks over buzzwords (Montazami et al., 2022). These studies present a possible unifying approach for the initial evaluation of  apps broadly, including games. However, they used a binary yes/no approach. This r aised a question for us: To what extent do award-winning serious games for learning address Dubé et al’s (2020) five benchmarks of quality?

## Methodology

For this pilot, we evaluated six gold medal K-12 games from the 2023 Serious Play Conference (seriousplayconf.com): Pengeby (pengeby.dk/da), ANWB Streetwise (ranj.com/en/cases/streetwise-game-trafficsafety-anwb-ranj), Venture Valley (venturevalleygame.com), Head lines and High Water (fielddaylab.wisc.edu/play/headline), Mission US: N o Turning Back (mission-us.org/games/no-turning-back), and Math Ascension (math-ascension.com). They cover diverse topics such as financial literacy, entrepreneurship, road safety, US history, journalism, and mathematics.

The research team was made up of two researchers, a  lecturer, and a master’s student in the Learning Sciences and Technologies program of a university i n the northeastern US, both with K-12 teaching experience. We used a modified version of directed qualitative content analysis (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005), seeking n ot just the presence of Dubé et al.’s five criteria, but how clearly and adequately they were described. To follow Dubé et al.’s approach, we analyzed the g ame developers’ websites to locate information readily available instead of playing the games. Here, all six games included all five benchmarks in some capacity, highlighting the need for a more comprehensive tool  thandubé and colleague’s (2020). Thus, we restruc tured it to assess the quality of descriptions of the benchm arks, independently analyzed the content of the web site (including text, images, and document files), and ranked the inclusion of Dubé’s five elements using a Likert scale from 1 (not well described) to 5 (very well describ ed). We then met and discussed our results (see Tab le 1), resolving any discrepancies through further analysi s and discussion. Due to space, we only describe th e learning category in detail here, since it scored the lowest of the five elements.

## Discussion

While the benchmarks proposed by Dubé et al. could be useful, educators may gain a more thorough view of a game's value by analyzing the depth, accuracy, and relevance of these components’ descriptions. Additi onally, feedback and scaffolding are frequently implicit aspects of games and may not warrant as much focus. However, understanding the learning theories within games is  necessary to assess quality. Therefore, further cl arity of the description for learning theories is warranted. Dubé et al.’s 2019 review described learning theories used in GBL research studies. However, some, such as “construct ivist,” are broad and open to interpretation. More specific learning theory descriptions will make this criterion more useful. It is important to make educators aware of trustwor thy resources for identifying and evaluating high-quality games. Benchmarks like these could provide a common way to talk about these games. This could be useful for teacher professional development and for researchers. Future directions should include analyzing more games and investigating whether the gameplay reflects the benchmarks described on the game websites.
