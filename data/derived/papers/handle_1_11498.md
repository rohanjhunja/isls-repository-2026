# Toward Bridging the Gap: Undergraduate Perspectives on Implementing Self-Explanation as a Reading Strategy

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Research indicates that students underutilize self-explanation. Thematic analysis of survey data revealed that students value self-explanation for enhancing active reading, comprehension monitoring, and personal relevance. However, barriers such as time constraints, perceived effort, and fear of misapplication impede its use. Students assess the need for self-explanation based on their existing strategies and goals, underscoring the need for interventions that leverage effective application of self-explanation as a useful and meaningful learning strategy.

## Introduction

In our complex information-rich world, comprehension challenges limit one’s ability to t hink critically, solve problems, and to fully participate in society. Unfortunately, recent data shows that a growing  majority of adolescents and adults are underprepared for the literacy tasks they engage in at school and in their everyday life (e.g., NCES, 2024; OECD, 2024; Perin, 2020). Thus, there is a critical need to equip students with skills and strategies that can improve their comprehension.

Research in cognitive and educational psychology sh ows that self-explanation (SE), or explaining the text to one’s self during learning is an effective means of improving comprehension and learning across tasks and domains (e.g. Bisra et al., 2018). SE during reading not only encourages active processing but also invites learners to monitor their comprehension and to transform the  text content by putting it in their own words, mak ing inferences to bridge ideas in the text to one another, and/or drawing upon their own prior knowledge to “fill in the gaps” and elaborate on the text information (see McNamara, 20 04). SE is a one of several generative learning strategies that have, in recent decades, been champ ioned by educational researchers and learning scien tists (e.g., Dunlosky et al., 2013). Unfortunately, there is little evidence of these strategies being employed in classrooms or taken up in students’ study habits (e.g., Carpenter  et al., 2022). The current study is part of an eme rging effort to support students’ use of self-explanation in service of improving comprehension outcomes. This study addresses the following research question: What influences undergraduate students' adoption or  rejection of the self-explanation (SE) reading strategy?  By understanding what students find valuable about SE as well as their hesitations to adopt it, we candevelop interventions that address these perceptions and preferences.

## Method and results

129 undergraduates (Mage = 23.61, SD = 7.39; female = 89, male = 38, gender fluid = 1, n o response = 1) from a large urban university in the American southeast completed a survey with open-ended, multiple-choice, and Likert items about their reading and study habits. In the current study, we focus on their responses to items about reading habits and items specific to SE.

Most of the students (75%) indicated that they had heard of SE prior to starting the survey. Of these students, the majority of students indicate using t he strategy “sometimes” (n = 51) or “often” (n = 30). Openended responses about the utility of SE were  coded using thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006). The author read through all responses to produce an inductive list of themes. In a preliminary round of coding, t hemes were collapsed, and additional themes were generated. The themes were organized around reasons that students choose to use SE (Use) and reasons that they elect not to use the strategy (Rejection). The thematic analysis of the openended responses yielded nine themes (Table 1). They indicated that SE supported  active processing and that by having to talk aloud or talk through their thinking, SE “helps me stay focused”. In a similar vein, students used SE to engage in comprehension monitoring, often citing that SE helps them to “make sure the y understand”. Some students articulated the act of explaining as a sort of threshold check of overall understanding (e.g., “I can make sure I understood everything I just read”.) while others use SE as a tool for iden tifying specific areas of weakness (e.g., “Self-explaining helps me recognize areas where my understanding is unclear.”) Students recognized that academic texts are often d ense and complex, and they described how they use SE to help them to simplify the words and sentences. For example, one student wrote “writers use compl ex academic language [...] I talk myself through the reading as if I would have to verbally explain it to someone else because that keeps me from repeating complex wording, I don't really understand...”1 Many students cited that SE helped them to put the ideas of the text “in my own words”. In some cases, it was implied that using their own words was inten ded to simplify the content, but others revealed a different perceived benefit of SE: increasing personal relevance. This was the most prevalent reason that students cited for using SE (17%). Students noted: “I use it because p utting stuff into my own words can help me understa nd it more. Contextualization with my own experiences hel p me to truly remember the course material” and “I've learned I tend to resonate with new information bet ter when I connect it to my life”. This is consiste nt with the notion that SE encourages integration of information from prior knowledge; however, it is of note that the majority of these responses were specific to personal experi ences. Only two students explicitly mentioned that they could connect the information from the reading to their extant academic knowledge. Finally, many responses did not provide a specific aspect of SE that was useful, but rather that they had previously used it and it helped them to understand more deeply or to remember the information (e.g., “I think it is effective for me at times”; “I find it very effective and helps me retain the information”; “I like to use it because it makes understanding the info easier.”).

The first theme in why students reject the use of SE is that it is resource-intensive. This is consistent with the body of experimental work demonstrating that pr ompting students to SE greatly increases time on ta sk (e.g., Chi et al., 1994). Some students cited external time pressure as the constraint (“I seem not to find enough time in my schedule to fit extra techniques”), while others fe lt that amount of effort was too much (e.g., “It of ten gets exhausting”, “It is mentally draining”).

A second related theme was that students found SE unnecessary. For example, a student wrote “I find that it takes a lot more work and I can often understand the text well enough without having to do this.”, suggesting that students are evaluating the value of their tim e and effort relative to their comprehension goa ls (i.e., understanding “enough”). Indeed, others noted that they often find they do not need to SE but will do so when they encounter a particularly challenging passage. This suggests that these  students are engaging in comprehension monitoring and selectively employing SE as an external strategy. This is also consistent  with the themes related to comprehension monitoring and to SE’s utility in simplifying difficult texts described above. Other students stated that they do not use SE because they already use other strategies, such as rereading or explaining to others that better meet their needs. This was particularly true for students who were not previously aware of SE. One student wrote, “I'm not sure if I would try this strategy because it's not something that I'm familiar with. Before this survey, I would say that I'm a student that makes good grades so switching up my study strategies this far into college would not be a good idea in my opinion.” Finally, students expressed fear of incorrectly applying the SE strategy as a reason for not using it. Some students indicated that they did not know how to practically implement  the strategy. Others expressed fear over making incorrect connections, such as “Sometimes I find I may confuse myself if I relate it to something that it doesn’t really reflect.”; “Sometimes I am unsure of the material so I don't want to explain it to myself incorrectly and hang on to that answer”.

## Conclusions and future directions

While students recognize the potential benefits of self-explanation for supporting comprehension, barriers such as perceived effort, necessity, and anxiety about m isapplication influence their decision to reject th is strategy. This work suggests that activities and educational interventions that promote self-explanation could emphasize perceived strengths of SE, while mitigating concern s. One potentially fruitful opportunity might be to  consider these barriers in the context of Situated Expectancy-Value Theory (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020) to support students’ motivation to use more effortful, but more effective strategies. Future research will examine additional questions from the survey to understand other contextual fact ors. Future work could further explore how to integrate self-explanation into various learning environments to maximize its efficacy and support diverse learner needs.
