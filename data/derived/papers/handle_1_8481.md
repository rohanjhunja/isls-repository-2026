# Children’s Place-Based Gesture: An Understudied Resource in Socio-Ecological Sensemaking

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In the learning sciences, there has been a growing focus on the body and place, elevating the multi-modal and situated dimensions of learning. I bring attention to children’s place-based depictive gestures, revealing how such gestures are a generative but often overlooked resource in science sensemaking. Analysis focused on 11 and 12 year-old children’s gestures as they discussed what organisms needed to thrive in their schoolyard, within a late elementary school curriculum that centered on place-based approaches to teaching science and data science. Findings reveal children’s depictive gestures made visible their rich and varied knowledge of the schoolyard in collective discussions. Such gestures were also integral in children’s conjecturing, contesting and clarifying relationships within a socio-ecological system, layering together multiple ways of knowing. Implications for teaching and learning about socioecological systems are discussed.

## Introduction

Children have rich and varied knowledge about the s ocial, natural and built world around them, steeped  in the routines and rhythms of their daily lives (Nespor, 2010; Seyer-Ochi, 2006). Across childhood studies, geography, environmental education and science education field s, there has been detailed documentation of such understandings, enmeshed in smells, sights and soun ds of local places shaped by broader cultural, poli tical and historical forces (Lim & Barton, 2010; Mitchell & Elwood, 2012). Yet rarely do children have the opportunity to meaningfully interweave this multi-modal, multi-temporal knowledge into their disciplinary learning. Such place-based knowledge appears particularly gen erative for youths’ understandings of complex socio-ecological systems. Conceptualizing socio-ecological systems entails considering multiple interacting parts of a system, and their relationships with one another, across varying spatial and temporal scales (Pugh et al., 2019). This includes considering the abundance, scarcity and distribution of organisms and the relationships within and between these organisms and their environment (Korfiatis & Tunnicliffe, 2012) but also, how such syste ms are shaped by broader socio-political institutions and historical legacies (Davis & Schaeffer, 2019). Yet to date, there is limited research in this area because common app roaches to teaching about socio-ecological systems often removes learning from actual places that children know by focusing on far away, virtual, or simulated settings. In this study, I examine how children’s place-based depictive gestures emerged in discussion, supporting weaving together understandings of socio-ecological systems in relation with children’s knowledge of the built, natural and social worlds central to their daily li ves, particularly children’s memories and histories. Existing accounts of gesture in science education have point ed to the importance of this semiotic form (Crowder, 1996; Mathayas et al., 2019) but nascent research, especially in science education, learning sciences and environmental education research, has documented how place-based knowledge is depicted through gesture. In this study, I aim to understand how children’s place specific knowled ge emerges and becomes visible in science discussio ns through gesture, examining how such depictive gestu re supports children’s sensemaking about complex so cioecological systems as children interweave multiple ways of knowing (Warren et al, 2021). In this analysis, I ask, What place-specific gestures emerge in collective discussions? and How did this gestured local knowledge support sensemaking about complex socio-ecological systems? In the sections t hat follow, I describe the context of this study and me thods of analysis, followed by sharing key findings  and discussing implications for research and practice.

## Study Context

Data presented are drawn from a larger multi-year d esign-based research project that aimed to support children understanding socio-ecological systems through enga ging in science and data science practices, with a participatory GIS mapping technology, Local Ground (Van Wart et al., 2020) and local places central to the design (Lanouette, 2022; Lanouette, 2019). Key to this work was a commitment to expand what ways of knowing could be brought to bear as children learned about how socio-ecological systems functioned and thrived. The author and colleagues collaborated with a K-5 public elementary school in the Western United States, working closely with ICLS2022 Proceedings 1337 © ISLS two late elementary classes, the school’s science teacher, and the 4th and 5th grade homeroom teachers (Lanouette et al., 2016). In this analysis, the focus is on year 3 of the project, where I worked with one 5th grade science class with 27 students (Lanouette, 2022).

Across 9 weeks (18 class sessions, lasting 60-90 minutes each), I designed and taught the multi-week curriculum during the students’ science class time,  in collaboration with the science teacher. The cur riculum engaged students in two cycles of sampling, data vi sualization, and discussion. During these earlier l essons (Lessons 1-4), children conjectured potential important “parts” of the schoolyard soil ecology, with t he teacher posing the question “Who lives underfoot and what d o they need to thrive?” Children then visited diffe rent schoolyard locations of their choosing and selected an initial site for sampling to study in greater detail. At their sites, children collected a range of data about earthworms, other invertebrates, and plants’ root structures, as well as soil characteristics such as moisture, compaction, and composition. They also recorded notes about schoolyard activities that they thought might be important to understanding who might thrive and where, such foot  traffic, sound levels, and other children’s play activities. After several class sessions devoted to exploring relationships in the aggregated data using Local Ground participatory GIS maps and canonical data forms (L7-L12), students had the opportunity to select a second sampling sit e in Lesson 13 to further support studying relation ships emerging in the class data, as well as to explore puzzling patterns, outliers, or anomalies in the data. Data sources relevant to this analysis included fiv e class sessions where children discussed emergent relationships or patterns in their aggregated data (Lessons 11, 12, 16, 17, 18). These five lessons we re selected because they involved children discussing what orga nisms needed to thrive in their schoolyard, using t heir aggregated data to conjecture, contest or clarify their claims drawing on multiple sources of evidence. Analysis Drawing on multi-modal interaction analysis techniq ues (Stivers & Sidnell, 2005), a research assistant  and I analyzed video recordings of several whole class di scussions. We focused on children’s depictive gestures (Streeck, 2008) where the “hands depict by enacting their familiar, “real-world” capacities as users, transporters, experiencers, assemblers, molders, and shapers of t hings” (Streeck, 2008, p. 285). In the first phase of video analysis, we reviewed conversations in the five class sessions where children conjectured, contested, and clarified potential relationships or patterns in their data, related to who could thrive underfoot and where. Us ing video coding software (MAXQDA), we documented all instanc es of children’s gesture depicting place-based knowledge of the schoolyard. In the second phase of  analysis, we returned to these highlighted instanc es, examining how depictive gesture supported children’s collective sensemaking about relationships within the data and the schoolyard socio-ecological system. We also noted what place-based knowledge was being depicted (e.g., natural, built, social), what socio-ecological relationships were being discussed, and the work of depictive gesture in this collective sensemaking (e.g., elaborating o n data points, clarifying relationships among verte brates, invertebrates and the soil, showing transient eleme nts like sunlight and rainfall), to better understa nd the work these gestures were doing.

## Findings

In this section, I first provide a brief overview o f the place-based depictive gestures emergent in ch ildren’s sensemaking as they conjectured, contested and clar ified understandings of socio-ecological relationsh ips and processes. Next, I show one sequence of place-based  depictive gestures in more detail, briefly describ ing the context in which the gesture appears, what place-specific knowledge was elevated, and how the gesture supported children in refining understandings about how multiple parts of a local socio-ecological system come to function together and thrive.

## Overview: Children’s place-based depictive gestures

Children’s place-based gestures communicated varied knowledge about their schoolyard, evoking natural, built, material and social dimensions of the schoolyard oc curring across days, seasons and years. For example, such gestures included knowledge about environmental phenomena, such as the pathways of sunlight and shade throughout the day as well as seasonal patterns of rainfall and related puddle formation. Such knowled ge was often shared with sweeping hand gestures over the Local Ground map or creating imagined landscapes in front of themselves where varied environmental phenomena were overlaid. Children’s gestures also depicted knowledge of the social rhythms of the day, such as where people in the school community gather and activities they engage in. This included mimicking running bodies along a walkway with marching fingers or showing how 3rd graders dispersed during recess using undulating hands. Las tly, children’s place-based gesture included detail ed knowledge of built and material configurations  about their particular sampling sites or specific schoolyard locations. Gestures showed knowledge about the peri meter, composition and height of buildings, fences,  and ICLS2022 Proceedings 1338 © ISLS playground structures, depicted through fingers tracing building footprints in the air or bringing hands together to show angles and areas created by structures.

Example: Preeti and Isaac’s tucked away sampling site In this instance, Preeti and Isaac are contesting classmates’ earlier claims that earthworms need roots, shade and moist soil to thrive. Using their own experiences at their selected site and their site data, the pair argues that even though they had moist soil, shade and roots, they o nly found one earthworm at their hidden garden site. As the discussion ensues, a student asks about additional aspects of their garden sampling site to understand  their discrepant data and counter claims, asking “Do a lo t of people walk around there?” Isaac replies, “In our area? (pointing towards the map). Not at all! It actually is in a tight little corner.” As shown in Figure 1, Isaac then steps away from the interactive data map and orients his body towards his classmates on the rug below. Using gesture to provide an on-the ground perspective, Isaac says, “Because ours [our site] is like … See, the classroom is here, ac tually here (moving his hands together to classroom wall, see Figure 1a) and this wall is here and our site is fa::::r from where kids walk a lot (shifting hands to show his groups’ site to peers seated below and wiggling his right thumb to show walkers at a distance, see Figure 1b). Figure 1 (a, b): (a) Isaac’s initial gesture depicting their garden site in relation to the classroom buildings, facing towards himself, (b) Isaac at the end of a series o f gestures, depicting his actual site location in r elation to children’s daily foot traffic and the school building wall, from his classmates’ perspectives. Through this series of gestures, Isaac recreates his and Preeti’s tucked away garden site, depicting built structures (e.g. “the classroom is here”) and children’s movement through the space (e.g., “where kids walk a lot”, wiggling his thumb). In the process, he interweaves his firs t-person experiences collecting data with his daily understandings of being in that garden as he reason s about what earthworms need to thrive and consider ing the classes’ aggregated data. In doing so, multiple intersecting elements of the schoolyard ecosystem are considered and made visible to one another, interweaving the n atural, built and social layers of the schoolyard i nto the collective discussions about how socio-ecological s ystem function and thrive. Here, children making se nse of multi-variate relationships in their aggregated dat a (earthworm counts, soil moisture measurements) ar e interpreted in relation to more dynamic (e.g., shade, footsteps) and less dynamic (e.g., build structures) elements of the socio-ecological system across varying time spans.

## Discussion

Children’s descriptive gestures about the schoolyar d were shown to be a powerful resource in making vi sible emplaced knowledge in collective sensemaking about what organisms need to thrive. Additionally, childr en’s gestures made possible coordinating together multip le parts of complex socio-ecological systems, blend ing together natural, built and social dimensions of th e schoolyard across days, weeks and years to reason. Such coordination supported further considerations of un derlying mechanisms and explanations for relationsh ips emergent in the classes’ aggregated data. Together, this coupling of depictive gesture and place-based knowledge supported children bringing together expansive knowledge about a socio-ecological system as they conjectured, contested and clarified emergent relationships in their data and the system at large. Significance of this study are two-fold. First, analysis suggests the depth and breadth of children’s local knowledge as a generative resource in learning about complex socio-ecological systems. To date, existing research on learning and teaching about complex systems has tended to overlook children’s emplaced, historical and social ICLS2022 Proceedings 1339 © ISLS knowledge about place, particularly embodied forms. Second, this work contributes to existing research  related to gesture in science learning, elevating how depic tive gestures showing emplaced local knowledge can be a powerful resource in learning about complex socio-ecological systems. Combined, this analysis extends existing work on gesture in science education to become further situated within and about place while also making visible the ingenuity and expertise children bring to science sensemaking drawing on multi-modal resources like gesture. As the learning sciences and science education fiel ds further focus on place-based approaches to teach ing and learning, particularly approaches that elevate hete rogenous and expansive modalities for expression and sensemaking (Warren et al., 2021), young peoples’ p lace-based depictive gesture appears a generative resource warranting further study.
