# Cultivating Relational Trust in Community-Engaged Educational Research Partnerships for Environmental Justice

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Community-engaged educational research partnerships have the potential to catalyze new approaches to environmental justice teaching and learning by bringing together multiple actors with different lenses, positionalities, epistemologies, and expertise to unsettle normative approaches to educational practice. Such work, however, requires situating relational trust as central to partnership development. In this paper, we examine how relational trust was built across four different educational partnerships working toward environmental justice. We identify five themes that support developing and sustaining relational trust: (a) collectively working toward socio-ecological change, (b) naming and disrupting hierarchies, (c) purposeful processes, (d) centering relationality, and (e) redistributing resources. Theoretically, this paper advances our understanding of relational trust as central to partnership work; methodologically, it deepens our understanding of using partnership artifacts to understand partnership dynamics; and practically, it provides insights and tools for researchers who aspire to cultivate and sustain relational trust in partnership activities. Major issue(s) addressed In the face of ever-increasing environmental challenges, there is a growing need to innovate novel forms of teaching and learning that support youth and communities in understanding, interrogating, and taking action to address environmental injustice and to work toward socio-ecological well-being. Taking this as a point of departure, this paper examines community-engaged educational research partnerships as having the potential to catalyze novel frameworks, models, and approaches for environmental justice education. These types of partnerships involve collaborations that extend beyond researchers and school districts; they intentionally include researchers, youth, families, teachers, administrators, frontline communities, land workers, and other actors who are collectively working toward educational and environmental improvement. Such partnerships are uniquely positioned to support this work because they bring together people with different perspectives to jointly identify problems of practice, co-design potential solutions, and monitor their effectiveness across iterations (Bang et al., 2010; Coburn & Penuel, 2016; Ishimaru et al., 2016). Within these partnership processes, diverse sets of actors bring together different sets of expertise in order to envision, implement, and evaluate novel curricular, pedagogical, and material interventions in support of environmental justice education-from new infrastructures like green schoolyards and outdoor classrooms, tonew curriculum that engages environmental justice as an anchoring phenomenon of inquiry. In examining community-engaged educational research partnerships, we focus on the construct of relational trust. Relational trust is defined here as the willingness to participate in relationships that potentially involve risk, vulnerability, and tension on the basis of interactions premised on benevolence, competence, reliability, and respect (Kolleck, 2023). Specifically, we ask, how is relational trust built in community-engaged educational research partnerships working toward environmental justice? Theoretical and methodological approach(es) pursued Theoretically, we draw on literature on research-practice partnerships (Coburn & Penuel, 2016) and participatory design research (Bang & Vossoughi, 2016) to frame our study of relational trust. From these perspectives, we understand partnerships and design processes as spaces of learning in and of themselves, where researchers, teachers, and, in the present cases, additional community-based partners collaborate across boundaries tonegotiate 388 goals, engage in shared practices, and innovate pedagogical and curricular practices (Goldman et al., 2022; Morales-Doyle et al., 2025). Engaging in boundary crossing necessitates nurturing sustained relational trust. Cultivating relational trust happens over time and is a continuous process, entailing a deliberate, intentional focus on power and privilege given partners’ diverse histories, social positions, and range of prior experiences (Henrick et al., 2023; Vakil et al., 2016). When partners with diverse organizational and personal identities, structures, and routines come together, there is opportunity and risk, both of which may be amplified in work to address environmental justice given different histories of marginalization, contested meanings and values associated with the environment, and intersecting challenges that cut across political, cultural, and ecological lines (Morales-Doyle et al., 2025). This makes relational trust all the more important as an area of inquiry.

This makes relational trust all the more important as an area of inquiry. Methodologically, we take a multiple case study approach to examine the process of building relational trust in partnerships for environmental justice (Yin, 2018). We examine four cases of community-engaged educational research partnerships across California, where university-based researchers (all of whom are authors on this paper) are working collaboratively with classroom teachers, schools and school districts, community-based organizations, and/or young people to co-design new models of environmental justice education (see Table 1). These partnerships vary in their topical focus, conceptualization of environmental justice, organizational structure, maturity, and scale. Looking across multiple cases allows for theorizing how relational trust can be cultiv ated in community-engaged educational research partnerships, beyond describing the phenomena. In order to explore how relational trust emerges and is sustained, we developed and employed the methodological approach of artifact-driven interviews as part of a process of collective self-study (Douglas et al., 2015). This involved two components. First, researchers from each of the four focal partnerships-again, all of whom are authors on this paper-identified and annotated five to six partnership artifacts  from their respective projects that they felt illustrated how they were building relational trust with partners (see Table 1). Second, researchers from one partnership facilitated semi-structured interviews with researchers from another partnership, engaging in discussion to explore how the identified artifacts related to the formation of the partnership, equitable engagement across partners, and the kinds of routines, structures, and activities that supported the development of relational trust.  This cyclical process continued until all partnerships had interviewed and been interviewed, totaling four video-recorded interviews that each lasted between 60 and 90 minutes. This process of first identifying and then reflecting on partnership artifacts allowed researchers from each partnership to make their thinking about relational trust explicit.

We began our qualitative analyses of these data by machine transcribing the interviews and checking the transcripts for accuracy. Each team then developed an analytic memo for one of the four focal partnerships, synthesizing preliminary themes regarding how relational trust was being conceptualized and enacted by the team they interviewed; we did so to determine how each partnership was cultivating relational trust. Finally, we collectively looked across these memos to engage in a cross-comparative process of categorizing and re-categorizing preliminary themes using a fishbone diagram (Bryk et al., 2015) until we arrived at a final set of categories for building relational trust that we outline in the following section. ## Major findings

In examining how relational trust is built across community-engaged educational research partnerships, we identified five major themes that support its emergence: (a) collectively working toward socio-ecological change,

(b) naming and disrupting hierarchies, (c) purposeful processes, (d) centering relationality, and (e) redistributing

resources. We elaborate on each of these major themes below, offering examples from our data corpus (artifact descriptions and relevant excerpts from artifact-driven interviews) that illustrate them in the context of focal partnerships. Where possible and with appropriate permissions, we include photos of highlighted artifacts. Collectively working toward socio-ecological change Collectively working toward socio-ecological change was identified as an important dimension of relational trust as it is indicative of partners' efforts to work together toward real-world educational and environmental improvement. Both of these components  matter in building relational trust: the collectivity that teams develop when working toward shared purposes, as well as the orientation of goals toward making real-world socio-ecological change.

An example that illustrates the processes of building relational trust by collectively working toward socio-ecological change is the Commitment Tree from the SEEJuSt project (Artifact 2A, see Figure 1). During the artifact-driven interview, when discussing the Commitment Tree, the researcher explained the need to create a “shared mutual space” by having an “explicit conversation about what we are doing and why.” The Commitment Tree represents a tool to elicit the priorities of individuals in the group and work towards a shared vision. As a first step in creating the Commitment Tree, different stakeholders, including school district leaders, individual teachers, and researchers, discussed their personal commitments to the work. Then, project members organize d themselves into smaller co-design teams and generated visual representations that highlighted the principles they would like to draw upon to work towards their goals. As the researcher emphasized, in constructing these trees, small groups needed to decide what they would work towards “as a collective, not just you, but as a collective.” Artifact 2A, generated by three chemistry teachers, shows that these teachers collectively decided on committing to “empower civic-minded learners through science education that seeks justice, is equitable, and [is] centered on students,” guided by two principles: supporting sense-making and disrupting power hierarchies. Through these initial activities, the project team grew to know each other and began cultivating relational trust by feeling that they, as educators, were there to work together to better serve their students and their larger community. Another example comes from Artifact 4A, an article by the co-founders of Campus Canopy in a zine published by a statewide organization focused on climate education. The article served as an opportunity to communicate the work of the partnership to the broader public, describing Campus Canopy’s community-based approach to supporting habitat restoration projects in schoolyards, which began with building relationships with school communities. Collective action projects at two local schools are discussed: the restoration of a culvert at a high school campus, and the transformation of an asphalt playground into a nature playscape at an elementary school. These schools are in neighborhoods with less tree cover than nearby, wealthier neighborhoods-a marker of “t ree equity” (Myers et al., 2023). Engaging in the actual activity of transforming schoolyards served to strengthen the relational trust among participants; relational trust was built through the collective engagement of educational researchers, community partners, and schools in these action projects where material change was the guiding goal-not research for the sake of research. In an artifact-driven interview, one of the researchers shared how this was guided by “a community-based approach and really u nderstanding local communities. As we have gone through and worked on different projects, they all look different based on the needs of a very individual neighborhood and the landscape of that neighborhood.” A partnership-based approach toward school green ing that centers the needs of the individual community, rather than a one-size-fits-all approach to sustainable 390 schoolyards, supports a shared experience of learning through doing that situates socio-ecological change as community-grounded, supporting relational trust among schoolyard stakeholders. Figure 1 Artifact 2A. Commitment Tree Naming and disrupting hierarchies Building relational trust entails engaging with the hierarchies that are inherent to cross-sector partnerships. First and foremost, this entails identifying and naming power hierarchies that exist along the lines of roles (e.g., university researchers, school district administrators, small non-profit organization leaders), epistemologies (e.g., dominant science, community-based), and various identity markers (e.g., gender, race, language background). Then, it involves disrupting hierarchies to ensure that there are equitable opportunities for partners to contribute to collective work. This deliberate and intentional focus on power and privilege nurtures relational trust by supporting partnerships in valuing and honoring multiple perspectives (Henrick et al.,  2023). Artifact 3A is a co-developed set of environmental justice lenses used in the Precipitation Extremes Teacher Institute project. The artifact illustrates the importance of explicitly naming and identifying the powered dimensions of different knowledge syste ms as a central aspect of building relational trust among scientists, education researchers, and community-based environmental justice activists. When addressing climate and other environmental issues, Eurocentric science and related knowledge traditions are often privileged at the expense of recognizing the rich forms of knowledge that frontline communities hold. To disrupt these hierarchies, members of the interdisciplinary design team worked with a community-based environmental justice organization to develop a set of environmental justice lenses that broadened the knowledge systems that were elevated in their designs for teacher professional learning (see Reigh et al., 2025 for a full version of the lenses). In the artifact-driven interview, a researcher emphasized the importance of involving the community partner in the design phase, stating, “A subset of our design team was working with an educator from [a community-based organization]…getting her input on our design – all the way back from the justice lenses.”  The lenses explicitly centered embodied ways of knowing-including storytelling, emotion, and artistic expression-as inherent in the cultural wealth of BIPOC communities, to expand beyond Western hegemonic knowledge systems. The lenses also ex plicitly centered other academic knowledge traditions, including historical and political analysis, and uplifted the histories of resistance of BIPOC communities who are now on the frontlines of climate change. Enlisting the perspectives of frontline communities in designing professional development allowed for relational trust to emerge as different identities, positionalities, and knowledge bases served as a foundation for collective work.

As a second example, Artifact 4B from the Campus Canopy project illustrates challenges to building relational trust and the ineffectiveness of operating within traditional hierarchies (see Figure 2). The artifact is an aerial map of a tree planting project  that was rejected by the school district due to a lack of trust and a top down approach that did not include community voices. The initiative was led by an arborist, who approached the project as if they were the only expert in the room, neglecting to acknowledge the expertise of the community members on the planning committee, which included the co-founders of Campus Canopy. Through this process, Campus Canopy researchers learned about the importance of not only inviting voices from the community to be part of the conversation, but of actually centering community perspectives by listening to and incorporating community voices into shared decision making. As shared in an interview, one of the researchers stated, “You make sure if you’re throwing out an idea, you talk through why the idea wouldn’t work-not just plowing ahead with how you 391 wanted to do things in the beginning.” From this point on, Campus Canopy operated from the assumption that an inclusive process, with equal respect for all voices and active listening, supports relational trust and community engagement toward transformative outcomes.

Aerial Map Purposeful processes Purposeful processes are intentional, reflexive, and strategic systems for engaging in co-design that involve transparent communication, consensus-based decision making, and action in ways that are attuned to diverse partners’ needs, goals, and capacities. Through such processes, partners work to carefully attend to, incorporate, and build from the range of perspectives within a team, with a particular emphasis on privileging the perspectives of those most involved in the frontlines of environmental justice projects to ensure they are represented in design work. Simultaneously, researchers often take on the bulk of coordinating labor in partnership work as a way to respect the capacities of community partners and teachers. Purposeful processes help build relational trust as they allow for partnerships to endeavor toward shared goals in ways that are attentive to the diverse positions of team members.

One example from the From the Ground Up project is Artifact 1A, which is an agenda for a full-day of teacher professional learning that was jointly planned and facilitated by university researchers, teacher educators, and a community-based partner whose wo rk focused on urban agriculture (see Figure 3). Researchers audiorecorded the half-day planning meeting, with the specific goal of eliciting perspectives from the community-based partner about their vision for supporting teacher learning given their organization’s interest in  expanding their programming to work with K–12 teachers. Researchers then analyzed the audio recording of the planning meeting to propose a draft agenda. This agenda was shared back with the community-based partner for their input prior to the day of professional learning and was revised based on their feedback. This process speaks to the ways in which the co-design team made an effort to center the perspectives of community partners, ensure that different team members’ feedback was taken up in the resultant design, and allow for revision to account for diverse perspectives-part of the process of building relational trust.

Similarly, Artifact 2B from the SEEJuSt project illustrates efforts to engage in a purposeful process of leveraging assets of the partners. This artifact similarly shows the plan for the very first co-design meeting with participating teachers. During the session planning meeting, researchers and district teacher leaders made decisions about the goals, agenda, activities, and facilitation strategies. Facilitation was distributed among all the members to leverage the expertise of each individual. For example, one district teacher leader shared her experience of engaging in a ‘strawberry activity’ in a different professional development meeting that she attended. This activity guided the participants to trace the path of strawberries (i.e., where they came fro m and what went into the process) while taking one tiny bite each time. The team decided to integrate the strawberry activity into a model unit, inviting the district teacher leader to serve as a co-facilitator. The researchers continuously engaged in a purposeful and reflective process of working with broader partners while leveraging various assets of the district staff, teachers, students, and communities that they served. 392 Figure 3 Artifact 1A. Co-Design Session Agenda Centering relationality Another key way that relational trust emerged was through efforts to center relationality, operating from the premise that we must invest in our relationships with one another as full persons. Relationality is not just a relationality-our personal and professional relationships in ways attentive to dynamics of power, culture, and history-supports the development of relational trust because it allows for people to prioritize and account for partners’ full humanity, beyond any research-oriented goals. One example of centering relationality is Artifact 3B from a photovoice project related to the Precipitation Extremes Teacher Institute; this artifact represents the stance that working towards environmental justice requires seeing the ways that frontline communities experience climate and environmental impacts through their own eyes, rather than through the limiting frames that often organize science instruction (see Figure 4). The photovoice project was initiated by a community partner in the Precipitatio n Extremes Teacher Institute project to provide youth from their community whose homes were flooded during an extreme weather event an opportunity to share their experiences, fears, and future dreams related to the environment. The education researcher from this case supported the project by assisting youth in taking photographs and developing narratives to reflect their experiences. In their artifact interview, the education researcher emphasized, “That was how I built trust with these people was to just step into their world and do whatever it is that they needed me to do to advance their goals.” There was no explicit theoretically driven goal to the work; rather, the researcher’s intention was to better understand the perspectives of the youth and to support the work of the community partner. By investing in seeing the experiences of these youth and the community partners through their own eyes, with no explicit research agenda, the researcher worked to earn  relational trust that fostered future co-developed collaborations. Through this, the researcher deepened their respect for the community partner and their ways of working with youth toward climate action.

Another example is Artifact 1A from the From the Ground Up project (see again Figure 3 above). To develop the agenda, the co-design team held a half-day planning workshop at the urban farm where they would also host a session with K –12 teachers the following month. Being physically present on the farm reflects an important decision by researchers and teacher educators, some of whom had not been to the urban farm before. More immediately, visiting in person allowed the researchers and teacher educators to understand the affordances and constraints of the site in terms of possibilities for co-design activities. Perhaps more valuably, intentionally taking the time for in-person engagement fostered relationship-building with the community-based partner (as not all members of the team had met one another) and with the place itself. 393 Figure 4 Artifact 3B. Image from Photovoice Reception Redistributing resources The final aspect of the work of building relational trust is deliberately and equitably distributing resources. Resources in this sense include both material (e.g., time, funding) and social (e.g., recognition, status) assets. Our positioned to access funding and whose institutions were associated with a certain amount of prestige, can facilitate building relational trust with partners.

Artifact 1B, a conference proposal, exemplifies this process. During their artifact-driven interview, researchers from the From the Ground Up project shared a conference proposal jointly authored by researchers and the community-based partner organization. They used the artifact to discuss how such deep collaboration entails equitably “resourcing” the work to ensure all partners’ needs are met. They said, “If we’re working with their precious time, we need to be thinking about equitable compensation.” Funding for this project came from an internal grant opportunity at the researchers’ university. Researchers and the partner jointly identified an amount that would adequately support the partner organization’s expertise, time, use of space, etc. and built the rest of the budget around this, prioritizing resources for community partners. Relational trust, in this case, was something that the researchers worked to build from the process of grant writing-an early partnership activity-involving the equitable distribution of financial resources at a time when many community-based organizations face significant funding challenges and limited access to state and federal grants. This grant also paid for community partners’ conference registration. While Artifact 1B does not address this directly, the artifact-driven interview in which the researchers reflected on Artifact 1B opened up space for them to talk about equitable funding as contributing to relational trust.

Similarly, Artifact 2C illustrates the intentional work of redistributing resources, in particular, social recognition (see Figure 5). This artifact was the first page of a 2022 –2023 newsletter, which was created by one of the members of the SEEJuSt partne rship. At the top of the newsletter is an image of all participating teachers and researchers. The newsletter provided updates about the partnership and highlighted their work and impact. It was shared with the school and district leadership in order to re cognize the teachers who were involved in the project and their contributions to the work. In their interview, one  researcher explained, “One way in which we work toward building trust is honoring partners’ voices, expertise, and dedication in a way that matters for them. Sharing their good work and stories is so empowering for the educators, and they came to see that we do this work with and for them toward [the] public good, not just for us at the end of the day.” This artifact demonstrates researchers' role in building relational trust by honoring participants’ contributions in ways that are valuable for them; in this case, the researcher shared accomplishments with leaders  with decision-making power, including school and district administrators.

## Potential significance of the work

Theoretically, this paper aims to advance understandings of the concept of relational trust as central to the work of research-practice partnerships, with a particular focus on expanding teaching and learning to engage issues of environmental justice. Trus t is often taken for granted in partnership work-with scholars and practitioners nodding to its importance but not always elaborating on the conditions under which it emerges and the ways in which it develops in different contexts. Here, we empirically s tudy processes of partnering across multiple contexts to theorize the concept of relational trust (Henrick et al., 2023). By focusing our analytic lens on the dynamics of partnership work, we seek to make visible the ways in which  researchers, practitioners, and broader communities can engage in collaborative processes in endeavoring toward environmental justice. We identify several dimensions of relational trust that are interrelated yet distinct. For example, naming and disrupting hierarchies is something  that can be achieved through redistributing resources to honor different partners’ contributions and expertise, as well as  purposeful processes  that invite participants in a research-practice partnership to regularly identify, reflect on, monitor, and adjust partnership dynamics. We highlight this to note that relational trust is not developed in a vacuum or through one process, but, instead, involves engaging in multiple practices that work toward a common goal. Overall, this study has the potential to he lp advance an emergent body of research in the learning sciences focused on processes of partnering and the dynamics of partnership work toward social, environmental, and educational justice (Ahn et al., 2024; Farrell et al., 2022; Ishimaru et al., 2022; Penuel et al., 2020).

Methodologically, this paper advances an approach to studying and understanding educational partnerships. We identified and engaged with partnership artifacts to make sense of relational trust, understanding such artifacts as proxies for learning processes of the partners (Sandoval, 2014). We demonstrate how partnership artifacts can help make visible the extent to which project co-designers are engaged in the kinds of thinking, learning, and relational processes that undergird partnership work. Using deliberately selected partnership artifacts from multiple projects, we explored the unfolding of relational trust in various contexts using artifact-driven interviews with researchers as a way to make visible their thinking, as called for by Goldman et al. (2022). One limitation we note is that our methodological approach does not explicitly include community partners’ perspectives on relational trust. We see potential  in inviting community partners to identify and talk through partnership artifacts as a way to  further contextualize and deepen our theorizing of relational trust as part of building equitable research-practice partnerships in future work. Practically, this study provides important insights and tools for educators, researchers, and community members who aspire to cultivate, tend to, and sustain relational trust as they engage in meaningful partnership activities. Artifact-driven interviews w ith different team members in community-engaged educational research partnerships can help groups engage in reflexive practices whereby they co-construct understandings around dimensions of community-engaged educational research partnerships.
