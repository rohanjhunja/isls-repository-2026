# Exploring the Use of Generative AI to Identify ‘Coachable Moments’ in Classroom Discussions

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Our study explores the potential of generative AI (ChatGPT 4) to select segments from classroom text discussion to support a productive professional learning conversation. We compare the quality of segments chosen by GPT 4 to those chosen by an expert coach. Results show that nearly half (43%) of the GPT selected segments are of a similar quality to those chosen by the expert coach. An additional quarter are close to this level of quality.

## Introduction

In the present study, we examine the potential of generative AI (ChatGPT 4) to assist coaches to implement the Online Content-Focused Coaching (CFC)  program (Correnti et al., 2021). We focus specifically on the use of ChatGPT to select segments from teachers’ videoed classroom discussions—a labor intensive, complex task that sets the condition for a productive coach-teacher professional learning conversation.  Our questions are: 1) What is the potential of GPT-selected segments to support  a coach-teacher conversation that can advance  teachers’ knowledge and dialogic teaching skills? And 2) What are the patterns of similarities and differences between GPT 4 and an expert coach’s segment selections?

## Theoretical framework underlying segment selection in online CFC

Online Content-Focused Coaching (CFC), developed at University of Pittsburgh’s Learning Re search and Development Center, is comprised of a six-week workshop focused on  Questioning the Author (QtA) and Accountable Talk. The workshop is followed by individualized coaching to implement these models in classroom text discussions that increase students’ reading comprehension skill s. Each coaching cycle is co mprised of three phases: 1) a pre-conference where the teacher and coach choose pedagogical goals aligned with the instructional models; 2) asynchronous reflection around three video segments keyed to these goals; and 3) a post-conference where the coach guides teachers’ to connect specific classroom interactions to generalized categories of  practice (i.e., facilitation patterns), pedagogical goals and principles. Reflection that supports iterative movement through these levels of abstraction develops the integrated knowledge base that supports adaptive expertise and knowledge transfer (Bransford et al., 2005). Choosing the right lesson segment is critical because these segments provide the context and grist for teachers’ pedagogical reasoning. As a professional learning tool, segments therefore need to show rich evidence of student thinking linked to teacher talk moves and a generalized facilitation pattern relevant to pedagogical goals for dialogic text discussions.

Data sources Our data are drawn from the second year of a three-year study of Online CFC  in which a single expert coach worked with multiple cohorts of fourth and fifth grade teachers (N=8 teachers). Our final human-generated corpus was comprised of 21 lesson segments from 16 different classroom t ext discussions (reading lessons). We focus only on lessons where the teacher had chosen Accoun tability to Rigorous Thinking  (ART) as their pedagogical goal to facilitate a more precise comparison between the coach and ChatGPT selections. ChatGPT 4 was used to extract 21 segments to compare with the expert coach’s selections for t he same lessons (N=42 segments total). Because selecting ‘coachable moments’ from a transc ript requires ChatGPT to go beyond simple pattern recognition and simulate more advanced cognitive ab ilities of humans, our prompt has a high level of detail in the task specification and includes associated data, sub-tasks and justification-seeking (Karmaker & Feng, 2023).

## Analytic procedures and measures

Our analytic work began by noting the degree of overlap between GPT 4 and the expert coach’s segment selections for each case (complete, partial, or no overlap). T hen, we jointly analyzed and assigned a quality score for segments chosen by GPT  4 for each case (see rubric in Table  1). Disagreements were  discussed until  100% agreement was reached. Finally, we assembled matrices for each segment (human and machine extracted) using open and selective coding (Miles & Huberman, 1994) to identify key overall themes and issues related t o GPT 4’s selection process.

## Results

Our results suggest that GPT 4 can select high quality ‘coachable’ lesson segments. While we found no overlap in segments chosen by the expert coach and GPT, 43% percent of the GPT-selected segments were rated a 4, indicating that they evidenced a clear  link between teacher talk moves and student thinking  and a generalized facilitation pattern relevant to the goal of making classroom discussions more rigorous (ART)—fully meeting the quality level of the expert coach’s selections. Approximately 25% were rated a 3, indicating a clear facilitation pattern, but lack of suitability for studying ART. These segments usually drew from parts of lessons that were not squarely focused on active comprehension processes, e.g., where a teacher defined a vocabulary word or asked students to recall a previous discussion. The remaining GPT-selected segments were rated a 2, indicating  no potential for supporting a productive professional learning conversation. These segments were problematic due to facilitation patterns being embedded in overly long excerpts  and/or were missing a clear beginning or a critical part of a connected discussion. No segment was rated a 1.

## Scholarly and practical significance

Our study extends other research on AI-based assisted coaching  by exploring ChatGPT’s capability to extract high quality segments from whole transcripts in a comprehensive coaching program context (Wang & Demsky, 2023). Our emerging results suggest that ChatGPT has promise for perfor ming highly complex coaching tasks with fidelity. Future work is needed to improve the precision of the tool and study its implementation in schools.
