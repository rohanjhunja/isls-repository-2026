# Technocentrism in Visitors’ Views of Computing and Computing Education in Informal Spaces

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
In this paper, we investigate how technocentric discourse shapes interactions between visitors, facilitators, and a computational science exhibit in a Canadian science museum. Technocentrism can lead both educators and learners to centre the production of technical artifacts (e.g., code, simulations) or the hardware and software of a learning environment in ways that ignore the social, cultural, and political aspects of learning. Drawing on video-recorded interactions of visitors with a computational science exhibit, we highlight the emergence of technocentric frames of both code/coding and computing education, and how they may impact visitor engagement (or non-engagement) with the exhibit and reify hegemonic notions of computing. Rather than letting computational learning environments “speak for themselves,” explicit conversations about what computing is or should be are necessary to counteract technocentrism in computing education.

## Introduction and purpose

Technocentrism was defined by Papert (1987) as the fallacy of referring answers to all questions about technology to the technology itself, rather than the social, c ultural, and political supports that shape the use and meaningfulness of that technology. The pragmatic implications of technocentrism are of direct concern to us in the realm of computing education, given that technocentric discourses and pedagogies may hide the complexity of learning and teaching experiences that extend far beyond the computer (Sengupta et al., 2021) and m ay even further marginalize students who have been historic ally oppressed (Margolis et al., 2017; Vossoughi et  al., 2016).  Given this context, our work arises from the concern that the nature and role of technocentric discourse in computing education, especially in informal spaces,  remains under-investigated. In this article, we dr aw from empirical data collected at a computational science exhibit in a Canadian science museum to highlight the ways in which technocentrism can impact visitor percepti ons of computing education environments – even (or especially) those which purport to challenge technocentrism in their design.

## Background: Technocentrism and informal computing education

Our work arises from a concern that despite attention to designing learner-centered environments and pedagogies, technocentric discourses and practices reduce complex educational challenges to a matter “of buying into the right technology and creating structures that standardize and routinize [its] use” (Burch & Miglani, 2018; p. 610). We view technocentrism as an ideological position (Phi lip & Gupta, 2020) that also shapes epistemological, axiological, and methodological approaches in compu ting education research. Ideologically, technocentr ism positions computing education in a way that centers  the production of technical artifacts (e.g., compu ter code, models, simulations, etc.) over the complexity of human experiences (Sengupta et al., 2021). Technocentric views have also been shown to be instrumental in shaping post-secondary students’ perspectives of what should count as “real engineering” (Niles et al., 2020), potenti ally leading them to ignore the value of innovative  learning experiences. Methodologically, this restricts resea rch on the design of new technologies or environmen ts to questions of efficacy of design, rather than attempting to understand the complexity of the experiences of learning and teaching (Sengupta et al., 2021). Axiologically, technocentric approaches can lead us to position historically marginalized learners using deficit lenses (Vossoughi et al., 2016; Kayumova & Sengupta, 2022).  At th e same time, given the difficulty of talking about a specific form of technology without referring to the technology itself (Sengupta et al., 2021), as well as a vast body of research in computing education and popular media representations of coding that promote the “charism a” of the computer over social interactions essenti al for computing (Ames, 2019) we believe that technocentri c discourses may be shaping learning experiences of computing in both formal and informal spaces. This paper offers a preliminary exploration of this phen omenon in the context of informal computing education.

## Research design

Setting and goal ICLS2022 Proceedings 1017 © ISLS Hack the Flock, pictured in Figure 1, is a public computing environment (Sengupta & Shanahan, 2017) located in the Open Studio area of a science museum located in  a Western Canadian city. Public computing environments are “open-ended, public learning environments, in which visitors candirectly access, modify and create complex and authentic scientific work through interacting with open source computing platforms” (Sengupta & Shanahan, 2017, p. 1124). Our goal in this study is to explore technocentric discourses may be shaping visitors’ interactions with Hack the Flock, especially given that the desi gn of the exhibit explicitly seeks to decenter the technology (here understood as the programming language, the simulation code and the simulation display) in ways that may create tension with technocentric expectations (Sengupta et al., 2021) by supporting visitors through facilitation to center their own voices.

The simulation used in this exhibit is based on Reynolds’ (1987) flocking algorithm in which Boids (bird-droids) flock around the screen according to three parameters: alignment, cohesion, and separation. Visitors can use the computer terminal to directly change those parameters in the code as well as, for example, change thei r colour, shape, speed, and size, and even add images of bees and monkeys onto the screen. The computer code is written in P5, a professional text-based programming language which is based on Javascript and Processing. The exhibit is intended to include facilitation, either live (a  human facilitator at the exhibit) or in the form o f comments or instructions embedded in the code itself.

## Data collection and analysis

We video and audio recorded visitor interactions at  the exhibit, which captured discourse, physical mo vement, and changes made to the code. A total of 86 interactions were recorded, ranging in length from 50 seconds to 71 minutes (median length of 15:06). Each of these interactions included between 1-4 visitors of various ages (almost always including at least one child), one facilitator, and occasionally, a researcher (to answer questions as needed). We wish to be explicit that these videos of visitor/facilitator interactions were conducted in a naturalistic manner. Neither the facilitator nor the researcher explicitly asked the visitor(s) for their views on computing or the exhibit. However, during the interactions, some visitors made comments about computing education or the purpose of the exhibit, and a few specifically struck up conversations with the facilitator and/or the researcher on these topics. The first author watched all 86 interactions and flagged time periods in which visitors made comments about how the exhibit promoted, reified, or challenged their personal views of computing/computing education. This resulted in very few segments being flagged – 11 conversation segments across only 6 videos, possibly due to the young age of visitors (leading facilitators to focus on procedural aspects of working with the exhibit), and the fact that facilitators did not specifically ask visitors about their thoughts of computing/computing education. These segments were coded inductively using thematic analysis (Miles & Huberman, 1994) and constant comparison (Glaser, 1965). We compared the emergent codes against known perceptions and approaches in computing/computing education literature in terms of technocentrism (Papert, 1987; Sengupta et al., 2021). Our analysis focused on how visitors positioned code (i.e., assumptions about “real” code) coding (i.e., what coding in professional contexts looks like), and computing education (i.e., what computing education looks like).

## Findings

Technocentric frames of code and coding: “Real” code must be complex ICLS2022 Proceedings 1018 © ISLS The use of a text-based programming language at Hack the Flock was a deliberate choice to break down growing divides between professional computing (which typically uses text-based languages) and computing education for children in the form of block-based challenges. While our exhibit uses the original flocking code from Reynolds (1987), it is split into different files, one of which includes easy-to-change variables and functions as well as extended commenting that can act as instructions. This design choice resulted in two men questioning whether the code with which they were interacting was “actual code”: Visitor 1: What I'd really like to know is, [is] this the actual code behind something? Facilitator: Well [..] I mean, I guess this is the pretty version. Visitor 1:  - simplified. Anyways. This is the pretty version. Facilitator: A version with instructions.

Visitor 2: [sarcastically] What are you talking about, I am now officially a coder by coding this.

Facilitator: Yes. To be honest, I am not a coding expert by any means. I know the extent of how to do this… [the facilitator and men spend several minutes working at the exhibit] Facilitator: Thanks for experimenting. You learned how to code. [laughs] Visitor 2: [sarcastically] We're basically coders now.

The visitor’s sarcastic comment showed that since t his code had been reorganized (simplified) and incl uded instructions, it was not the same as real coding and therefore he implied that someone working with th is code would not be a “real” coder. Despite the fact that the code was originally authored by computational s cientists using a professionally used, text-based programming  language, even something as minor as the code’s organization and presentation diminished its authen tic value. This is a technocentric view that emphas izes the technical work of writing code, ignoring the expertise that comes with reading and commenting code as well. In particular, commenting code is a socially valued practice in the discipline of computing, and has been shown to be an effective pedagogical practice for teaching a nd learning coding (Vieira et al., 2019), so the fa ct that the inclusion of comments or “instructions” is seen to diminish the authenticity of the computing educatio n environment is in conflict with best practices from both computing education and the computing profession. Technocentric frames of computing education: Erasure of human-human interaction Western popular media has also promoted the idea of computing as an individual, anti-social endeavour (Lagesen, 2008). Along these lines, a woman remarked to the facilitator that while she thought the exhibit was a cool idea, there was no way that children under the age of 10 would spend more than three minutes at the exhibit (an assertion that our research has proved false). When the facilitator challenged that by pointing out that children spend more time when she is at the exhibit to help them understand it, the young woman argued: Young woman: But that's not the point of an exhibit right? Like, it's like human-exhibit interaction. I mean, human-human interaction is great. I honestly would've been like, “Oh cool,” tried to change something, done nothing, walked away. So I like, appreciated this. But at the same time, I think that if it was a bit more clear... While the visitor appreciated the help that she received from the facilitator and acknowledged that her interaction at the exhibit would have been much shorter without it, her words showcase a belief that human-exhibit interaction should be prioritized over human-human interaction at the exhibit. Her argument relies on a technocentric account of computing, in which the exhibit should “stand on its own” and the technology (the code, the hardware, etc.) is what determines whether or not a visitor can learn computing.  The young woman in this section is advo cating for a deliberate removal of “people” from the evaluation of an exhibit, that the need for a human facilitator is in fact a design flaw to be fixed. In fact, Hack the F lock is unable to “stand on its own” in the way tha t the young woman suggests. Even if visitors are simply able to sit down and start working at the exhibit, it is inherently tied to people through the values and visions of its des igners, the history and perceptions of computing that are held on individual and societal levels, and of course, in the actions and words of the facilitators who enliven it. Without facilitators to challenge exclusionary perceptions of computing (i.e., it is only for “tech-savvy” whi te men) by explicitly inviting all learners to engage with the exhibit (“try changing a number!”)  visitors who would otherwise have novel, playful, and exploratory experiences with computing may walk by the exhibit without even trying it

– perpetuating issues of equity in computing education.

## Discussion and Conclusion

While several facets of technocentrism are evident in the visitors’ interactions, perhaps most notable is the notion that facilitation is not the “point” of an exhibit,  as noted by several visitors in our study. This st ands in direct contrast with our observations as well as research on the role that facilitators play in promoting enjoyable learning experiences for visitors in science museums and oth er informal learning spaces (Dawson, 2019; Roque, 2 016; Tran, 2007). For Hack the Flock specifically, we believe that the human-human interaction of facilitation is a key factor deepening visitor engagement (median of over 15 minutes), evidenced by the fact that without facilitation, visitors typically spent less than a couple of minutes at the exhibit, or even walked by it altogether. Our wider concern is that technocentric notions of visitors may limit their participation in computational work. It is possible that visitor comments did not entirely stem from technocentric viewpoints and may have been influenced by other factors, such as uncertainty in the tone of a facilitator (in Finding 1), or their direct experiences working with particular segments of this exhibit’s code and not others. But from an ideological perspective (Philip & Gupta, 2020), their implicit assumptions of what “real coding” looks like were also clearly shaping their experiences, which in turn has implications for the  power relationships between visitors, facilitators  and computing. Future work should involve more in-depth  discussions and interviews with visitors around wh at computing has been, how it is currently viewed, and  what it could or should be. These conversations sh ould explicitly discuss design and pedagogical decisions  including choice of programming language, the role  of facilitators, and the expected types of interaction, in order to address hegemonic notions of computin g as antisocial, necessarily difficult, and only appropriate for privileged learners.
