# Building Student Epistemic Practices through Curriculum Design:  Investigating Learning Outcomes and Engagement

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
As scientific advancements grow more complex, the public increasingly struggles to critically evaluate scientific claims, partly due to limited understanding of how scientific knowledge is generated, validated, and applied. To address this challenge, science education has sought to embed epistemic practices (EP) into curricula, equipping students with the skills to make informed decisions on real-world scientific issues. However, while empirical studies have examined the impact of such curricula on students’ use of EP, few have utilized open-ended assessments within contextualized scenarios. To address this gap, we developed a contextualized scenario to assess the impact of a science curriculum on students’ epistemic practices. Pre-and post-implementation data from 75 high school students were analyzed to answer two questions: (1) Does the curriculum enhance students’ engagement in EP? and (2) Does it lead to measurable gains in EP skills? Findings provide empirical evidence that the curriculum significantly improved students’ epistemic practices.

## Introduction

As modern science continues to advance, the general  public  faces increasing difficulty in evaluating scientific claims (Herman et al., 2022; Osborne & P imentel, 2022, 2023). This challenge often stems from misunderstandings regarding how scientific kno wledge is generated, tested, and validated (Sinatra & Hofer, 2 021). Consequently, the scientific decision-making process can become contentious, with significant variations in opinions across social groups influenced by sociocultural membership, personal bias, and trust in science  (Herman et al., 2022). Given the growing complexity  of socioscientific issues and the inherent limitations of our knowledge and t ime, non-experts may either place their trust in unfounded claims or rely on information disseminated through social media. Both pathways carry the risk of accepting unfounded claims.

While the reasons behind the acceptance of flawed o r false information are multifaceted, Osborne and Pimentel (2022) argue that the educational system has not adequately equipped individuals to make informed decisions. They point out that a c ritical aspect of science education —understanding how the scientific community produces reliable know ledge—is often neglected in K-12 curricula. To address this gap, they advocate that students shoul d be equipped with the skills needed to engage with scientific discourse and make informed decisions in  their daily lives. A proposed strategy to combat misinformation and better prepare students for scie ntific discussions is to design curricula that imme rse them in authentic scientific practices. Recent research emphasizes that to equip high school students with these skills, science education should be restructu red to prioritize the epistemic practices central t o scientific inquiry (Chinn et al., 2023; Duschl, 2020; Yoon et al., 2023). These practices include evaluating the reliability of data, assessing the precision of methodologies, and comparing scientific findings across expert communities. Engaging students in authentic decision-making processes can strengthen their ability to critically evaluate scientific information and cultivate a more informed trust in science (Darner, 2019; Kienhues et al., 2020).

Although curricular designs to enhance student enga gement with scientific practices in K –12 education are increasingly available, assessments o f students ’ epistemic practices remain scarce, particularly those that use open-ended questions and written responses to elicit deep understanding (Lee et al., 2021). To address this gap, in the 2023-2024 school year, we developed a contextualized scenario to evaluate the impact of a science curriculum that we co-designed with high school science teachers over the past four years that aimed at improving epistemic practices among high school students (Chinn et al., 2023; Yoon et al., 2023). We collected data from 75 hig h school students both before and after implementing the curriculum  to assess its effectiveness in improving  students’ epistemic practices.  In this study, we aim to address two research question s: (1) Does the curriculum enhance students’ engagement in EP? (2) Does the curriculum lead to m easurable improvement in students’ epistemic practices? By addressing these questions, this stud y aims to examine the effectiveness of curriculum design in promoting high school students’ development in epistemic practices.

## Background

Embedding scientific practices in curriculum design The shift in science education towards emphasizing scientific practices has become an established standard in the field (NGSS, 2013). Advocates in the learning sciences have long argued for prioritizing the actual methods and reasoning strategies employed by scientists in educational settings. Duschl (2008) synthesized previous research, emphasizing that sci ence education should focus on the epistemic frameworks used in developing and evaluating scient ific knowledge. He advocated for designing learning environments that promote these epistemic practices. Central to these epistemic practices is the concept of epistemic cognition, which encompasses the beliefs and practices necessary for constructing, evaluating, and utilizing knowledge across various fields (Greene et al., 2016). Epistemic cognition pertains to an individual’s understanding of how kn owledge is generated (Chinn & Rinehart, 2016; Greene et al., 2016). This understanding is crucial  whenever individuals need to evaluate the credibil ity of sources, assess information, and make choices am ong various options when faced with complex problems.

To establish clear goals for implementing epistemic  practices, Barzilai and Chinn (2018) synthesized past research and proposed that the ove rarching aim of epistemic education is to enhance students’ epistemic performance, defined as achievi ng valuable epi stemic aims through competence. They introduced the apt-AIR framework, which outlines three essential component s of this educational approach. Epistemic Aims and Value  component encompasses  the objectives and significance that individuals attribute to their epistemic aims. Epistemic aims refer to specific goals established in various contexts, such as constructing robust scientific mo dels, cultivating accurate beliefs, avoiding false beliefs, comprehending theories, and formulating persuasive arguments. Individuals often assign varying degrees of value to different epistemic outcomes; for instance, one might prioritize understanding a cure for cancer over knowledge of typeface history due t o the former’s practical importance in achieving health-related objectives. Epistemic Ideals serve as the criteria used to evaluate the products of epistemic aims. These ideals serve as standards for assessing  various epistemic products, including explanations, knowledge claims, justifications, arguments, and evidence. For example, criteria for evaluating scientific models might include: (a) fitting all evidence to t he model, (b) providing explanations for observed phenomena, (c) clarity in presentation, (d) incorpo ration of underlying mechanisms, and (e) acknowledgment of inherent uncertainty or tentative ness. Similarly, assessing the strength of evidence might involve criteria such as: (a) adherence to co mmunity-accepted methodologies, (b) the ability to distinguish between competing theories or models, a nd (c) relevance to essential components o f the model rather than peripheral aspects. Reliable Epistemic Processes  are the procedures, strategies, and methods likely to achieve epistemic aims. These pro cesses include a variety of causal mechanisms employed in the creation and evaluation of epistemi c products. They are deemed reliable when they are more likely to yield  true beliefs or well-constructed models, rather than false beliefs or in adequate models. According to the apt-AIR model, a significant portion of epistemic cognition  involves understanding these causal processes that individuals use to generate epistemic products. These processes range widely, including conducting experiments, designing and implementing surveys for social science understanding, utilizing everyday observation techn iques for insights into one’s environment, and employing methodologies like meta-analysis for integrating evidence in informed decision-making. For example, in investigating a scien tific phenomenon, scientist s may consider and explore multiple perspectives or hypotheses in collaboration with other scientists, seek ample and systematic evidence in experimental design, and consistently evaluate and interpret evidence  in evidence-based reasoning. Ultimately, the goal of teaching epistemic practice s is to enhance students’ competence in scientific practices, enabling them to effectively engage with socioscientific issues and informed decision-making. Curricular design to promote epistemic practices Building on the understanding of fostering students ’ epistemic cognition and supported by a National Science Foundation (NSF) funded project, in the past four years, we developed five innovative curricula centered on agent-based modeling simulations for high school science. As an extension of the work using agent-based simulations, we constructed a unit and corres ponding model on a disease spread epid emic (Yoon et al., 2023). This Epidemic Model curriculum  that we report in this study  allows students to simulate and explore complex biological systems int eractively, providing a hands-on learning experience. The classroom materials are carefully designed to outline the scope and sequenc e of the lessons, ensuring a coherent and structured educati onal journey. By engaging with these simulations, students design experiments to test the effectivene ss of various mitigation factors, such as masking a nd vaccination, by collecting data on infection, recovery, and death rates within the simulated environment. They propose hypotheses, collect and analyze data, build and refine models, engage in collaborative discussions to resolve discrepancies, and ultimatel y draw evidence-based conclusions. These activities closely mirror the investigative processes utilized  by scientists, providing students with a realistic  and meaningful context to develop and exercise their epistemic practices. In Lesson 1 (Why Model), students collaborate on establishing criteria for c haracteristics or criteria for good scientific mode ls through hands-on activities and whole class evaluation and discussion. Core takeaway for students from Lesson 1 is that the model should be developed through systematic planning and investigation and should fit with all the evidence (or as much of the evidence as possible). In Lesson 2 (The Epidemic Model), the epidemic simulation is introduced, and students are primed to under stand that carrying out a scientific investigation involves iterative sensemaking. Durin g Lesson 3 (Reliable Strategies), students evaluate reliable epistemic practices relevant to the experi ments to be conducted in class. Three criteria or strategies are highlighted in Lesson 3: systematic planning and carrying out investigations, making su re the model fits the evidence, and control of variabl es. Specifically relevant to the six practices, dur ing Lesson 3,  students collectively develop criteria for good sci entific practices such as controlled comparisons, large samples, objective measures, pro per study duration, and multiple time measures. In Lesson 4 (Planning an Experiment), students formula te their research designs, making decisions about which mitigation factor to investigate and the epistemic practices they should follow when assessing how their chosen mitigation factor can contain the spre ad of disease. This includes determining the output metrics to compare their results (such as deceased, recovered, or infected rates) and setting up comparison groups to draw inferences about their chosen strate gies, such as comparing death rates associated with masking versus distancing. Students are given complete freedom in designing their experiments and are asked to justify their reasoning in worksheets. In Lesson 5 (Testing the Models), students are given opportunities to revise their methods based on peer  feedback and execute their revised experimental designs, collecting data using the simulation and deriving conclusions. They then compare their findings with those of groups examining similar mitigation f actors. Key outcomes of the lesson for students are seeking and using ample evidence and revising belie fs to fit evidence. During Lessons 6 (Productive Disagreement), students are introduced to resources  that facilitate constructive discussions about discrepancies in findings and ways to reconcile these differences without compromising the reliability of their claims. This discussion culminates in a vote to determ ine which study generates the most reliable claim. In Lesson 7 (Making Sense of Data), students  share the data publicly for peer review, compare i t with other groups of students, and critique scienti fic arguments based on criteria for good scientific practices. The unit concludes with Lesson 8 (Connec ting to the Public Sphere) in which students come to class consensus on which mitigation strategy rec ommendation is the best. They are encouraged to communicate their findings via a tweet, emphasizing the impor tance of maintaining transparency regarding the reliability of their claims.

The structure of the curricula, both in terms of content and classroom activities, was specifically crafted to promote six key epistemic practices of the apt-AIR framework: (1) developing arguments with evidence-based reasons, (2) considering and investigating mu ltiple perspectives or hypotheses, (3) seeking and using ample evidence, (4) seeking and u sing conclusive, systematic evidence, (5) consistently evaluating and interpreting evidence, and (6) aligning belie fs with evidence or modifying beliefs to fit discrepant evidence.

Assessment of epistemic practices Building on the discussion of embedding epistemic p ractices into curriculum design, it is equally important to examine the effectiveness of these curricula designs on students’ development of epistemic practices. Understanding how epistemic practices are measure d not only provides insight into the effectiveness of curriculum designs but also helps identify the specific ways in which students engage with, apply, and develop these critical skills. Below we review the literature of assessment of epistemic practices and introduce the design of our assessment scenario. Developing quantitative assessments for epistemic practices is challenging due to their complex nature. Several challenges complicate the developme nt of effective assessments. First, people’s epistemologies are often tacit (Chinn et al., 2011), meaning they may not be accustomed to explicitly articulating their epistemic understanding and proc esses. This tacit nature makes it difficult for participants to respond to or evaluate questions about their epistemic thinking. A further challenge arises from the metacognitive component of epistemic thinking (Barzilai & Zohar, 2016; Kuhn, 2001). People often find it difficult to report on their metacogn ition, and research shows that self-reports of metacognitive skills, whether prospective or retros pective, are often poorly correlated with actual, concurrent assessments of these skills (Veenman, 20 05). Therefore, abstract and ambiguous wording in questions, along with overly general items, can make it difficult for respondents to find a consistent point of reference. Self-report items often focus on formal epistemologies, and individuals may struggle to evaluate these items due to the disconnect between formal and practical epistemologies and reasoning processes (Sinatra & Chinn, 2012). Moreover, epistemic thinking can vary across domains and to pics. As a result, individuals may interpret and respond to assessment items differently depending on the specific domain or topic, leading to inconsistent responses based on their point of reference (Hofer, 2000; Lee & Tsai, 2012; Tsai, 2004). In terms of assessment format, open-ended questions, which prompt students to provide written responses, are particularly valuable for exploring complex ideas and gaining deeper insights into their understanding. Despite their effectiveness, these types of assessments are rarely utilized to evaluate high school students ’ epistemic practices. A review by Lee et al. (2021) found that among 40 studies, only one used open-ended questions to investigate epistemic practices.

To address these challenges, rather than merely sol iciting students’ views on the general or formal nature of knowledge, we developed an assessment that is designed to engage students in reasoning through a specific problem scenario. By prompting participants to evaluate the scenario with open-ended written responses, we aimed to uncover their implicit epistemic assu mptions and their ability in identifying and improv ing the epistemic strategies used in the s cenario. Additionally, by using this problem scenario, we were able to assess epistemic thinking at a topic-specific level, accounting for the varying influences of different topics and knowledge domains. In the assessment scenario we developed, two students (Antonio and Ben)  investigate the population dynamics of foxes, rabbits, and lettuce in an area using simulations. Initially, they form a hypothesis that a very high number of foxes will eliminate the rabbit population. They run the simulation for the first time and observe that the rabbit popu lation decreases initially but then increases over time, which contradicts their prediction.  Curious about the unexpected result, the students r un the simulation a second time. This time, they observe the predicted outcome: the rabbit population drops to zero, along with the fox population. To further test their hypothesis, they adjust the simulation to include both rabbits and lettuce and find that the result remains consis tent with their original prediction.  The students then discuss their findings with another group of two students (Carmen and Diana), who point out a “weird cycling” effect in the populations of rabbits and f oxes, where their numbers fluctuate up and down unpredictably. Reflecting on this, the original pai r concludes that the “weird cycling” might be due t o the other group using a different number of foxes i n their simulation, which could explain the variati on in results.  Based on their own simulation results, the two stud ents write up their conclusion: a high number of foxes in the area will eventually cause the rabbit population to die out. The simulation results are shown in tables and charts in the scenario. Three typical incomplete epistemic practices are embedded in the scenario: Lack of ample evidence in generali zing a conclusion, inconsistently evaluating and interpreting evidence (e.g., confirmation bias), and lack of considering multiple hypotheses through social negotiations. We asked students to write two  strategies that the two students used in the scena rio and three better strategies they could use. In the end, we asked students to evaluate the usefulness of the discussion that the two students had with the other group of two students. By prompting students to critique and suggest impro vements to the strategies used in the scenario, the assessment aims to elicit students’ u nderstanding of the six epistemic practices within a realistic, topic-specific context that emphasizes both critical  evaluation and constructive thinking. Specifically, this approach helps reveal whether students can recognize the limitations in the strategies used by the two students in the scenario, such as the lack of ample evidence, incon sistent interpretation of findings, and the absence of consideration for a lternative perspectives to resolve discrepancies. Additionally, it assesses whether students can prop ose improved strategies to add ress these epistemic flaws, demonstrating their grasp of the practices necessary for thorough and reliable scientific reasoning.

## Data collection

In the 2023-2024 school year, three teachers implemented the Epidemic Model curriculum. About a week before the implementations, we collected student pr e-implementation assessment data using the assessment scenario and student classroom experience survey data. The post-implementation assessment and classroom experience data were collected about 3 weeks after the implementation.

## Results

A repeated measures ANOVA was conducted to assess the effect of the Epidemic Model curriculum on students’ epistemic practices, as measured by pre-assessment and post-assessment scores. The analysis revealed a significant improvement in the total sco re from pre-assessment to post-assessment, F(1, 74) = 57.095, p = 0.000, indicating a statistically significant dif ference between the two time points. The effect size, as measured by partial eta squared (η²ₚ = 0.432), suggests a large effect, with 43.2% of the variance in the total assessment score attributable  to th e curriculum intervention. These findings demonstrate that the curriculum had a substantial impact on improving students’ epistemic practices over time.

In addition, a repeated  measures ANOVA was conducted to evaluate the effect  of the science curriculum on students’ classroom experiences on us ing epistemic practices across the five factors (se e Table 3). ## Discussion

The findings of this study contribute to the growin g body of literature emphasizing the importance of integrating epistemic practices into science educat ion. By engaging students in authentic scientific practices—such as proposing hypotheses, collecting and analyzing data, and reconciling differing viewpoints—this study demonstrates the curriculum’s effectiveness in enhancing students’ epistemic practices. The significant improvements in students’ epistemic practices, as shown by the pre-assessment and post-assessment scores, underscores the effectiveness of the curriculum designed around agent-based modeling simulations. This outcome aligns with prio r research supporting the integration of authentic scientific practices to foster students’ epistemic cognition and scientific practices (Chinn et al., 2 023; Duschl, 2020; Yoon et al., 2023). Specifically, the large effect size observed (partial eta squared = 0.432) suggests a substantial improvement in students’ und erstanding and application of core epistemic skills, highlighting the curriculum’s potential in actively  cultivating these prac tices. The significant improvements across all five factors further emphas ize the curriculum’s broad and nuanced impact on students’ classroom experiences with epistemic prac tices. The range of effect sizes across these facto rs highlights the potential for certain aspects of the  curriculum to differentially influence specific dimensions of students’ epistemic engagement. Speci fic factors, such as the critical evaluation of evidence (F2) and systematic testing (F1), showed c onsiderable gains, suggesting that targe ted curriculum components effectively reinforced these practices. This nuanced understanding highlights areas where specific curriculum strategies —such as group discussions for resolving discrepanci es or structured opportunities for experimental design—were particularly impactful. The study also addresses a critical gap in the literature on the assessment of epistemic practices, particularly in the context of high school science education. As discussed earlier, dev eloping quantitative assessments for epistemic practices is inherently challenging due to their tacit, metacognitive, and domain-specific nature (Barzilai & Zohar, 2016; Chinn et al., 2011). By employing an  assessment designed around a contextualized problem scenario with open-ended responses, we were able to engage students in a realistic scientific problem and articulate their epistemic reasoning in a way that is grounded in context, rather than abstract or general terms. As a result, the assessment allowed us to uncover students’ implicit epistemic assumptions—those that often remain unspoken—and evaluate their ability to critique strategies, propose solutions, and articulate the importance of systema tic inquiry depicted in the scenario.  This approach directly responds to the limitations highlighted in  prior research, where overly general self-report items or abstract measures often failed to capture the nu anced and dynamic nature of epistemic thinking (Lee et al., 2021; Sinatra & Chinn, 2012).

In conclusion, this study provides preliminary evidence that a well-designed curriculum to promote epistemic practices can significantly enhan ce students’ ability to critically evaluate scienti fic procedures. By embedding authentic scientific practices into classroom learning, educators can better prepare students to confront the complexities of modern science and contribute meaningfully to scientific discourse. As science education continues to evolve, the integration of epistemic practices will be crucial in cultivating a generation of informed and engaged  citizens.  While these results are promising, we acknowledge the limitations of this study. The sample size, while informative, may not fully capture the diverse student population in high school science c lassrooms. Expanding this research across varied educational c ontexts and demographic groups will be essential fo r generalizing the curriculum’s effectiveness. Additionally, while quantitative mea sures provided insight into the curriculum’s impact, integrating qualitative data, such as student refle ctions, could further enrich our understanding of h ow students perceive and engage with epistemic practices.
