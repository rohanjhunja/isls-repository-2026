# Learning as Participation at the Annual Meeting of the International Society of the Learning Sciences

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While researchers in other fields have begun to more regularly theorize the learning that occurs at academic conferences, the learning sciences have broadly ignored this topic. We use this paper to begin conceptualizing the kinds of learning that occur at the Annual Meeting of the International Society of the Learning Sciences (ISLS). Taking a theoretical approach, we map the learning landscape of ISLS by applying a participational lens. We propose valuable possibilities of learning that occur both during and beyond the sanctioned events of the conference. We then critically reorient this work towards missing forms of knowledge production as a pathway forward. This paper represents a starting point for a valuable conversation about the kinds of learning that should occur at ISLS in future iterations.

## Introduction

The Annual Meeting of the International Society of the Learning Sciences (ISLS) is at an important crossroads, with organizers  experimenting with conference structures (e.g., com munity day in Buffalo at ISLS 2024) and evolving some structures for hybrid participation in response to the need to include members who may not be able to participate in person  (e.g., a new hybrid symposium submission track). Further, the Society is only two conferences past the  three years of remote conferencing due to a confluence of (ongoing) pandemics. In this conceptual paper where we draw inspiration from the innovations of our colleagues  (e.g., Keifert, et al., 2023) and those in other professional conference spaces (e.g., the making and doing exhibition at 4S started in 2016; see Le Bo t & Noel,  2016). We adopt a theorization of learning as participation (Lave &  Wenger, 1992)  to conceptualize how learning happens at ISLS. We provide a pedagogical map of the conference to help guide and further motivate a growing conversation.

Beyond serving as opportunities to meet other professionals, “conferences function as embodied sites of knowledge production in their own right” where academic communities can circulate new ideas and build research identities (Henderson & Burford, 2020, p. 4). Learning within conferences happens across four different domains of knowledge: disciplinary knowledge, professional knowledge (knowledge about being an academic), sociocultural knowledge, and relational knowledge (building collegiate networks, comparing oneself to others) (Fakunle et al., 2019). Conference organizers mediate this learning through  the design of the event itself, with choices such as offering online attendance  providing opportunities to interact with all four domains  that do not exist within traditional face-to-face events  (Black et al., 2020). Beyond what individuals learn during the conference, the educative potential of these gatherings expands in multiple directions. Structures of conferences  often reify institutionalized forms of knowledge and knowledge production while silencing  racialized and gendered forms of scholarship from academics of non-dominant identities (Henderson & Burford, 2020). This occurs in part because most conferences rely on pedagogical approaches that mirror the banking approach to learning where an expert merely distributes knowledge to an audience (Belliappa, 2020). Taking this critique serious ly, organizers can challenge this model of learning by employing creative pedagogical approaches and embodying other theoriza tions of learning, such as implementing a n ethics of care (Burford et al., 2020; Woods & Elliott, 2023). Further analyses of conferences as sites of knowledge production can thus engage scholars in more equitable forms of learning within their designs. We use this paper to theorize the learning that happens at ISLS. We apply a participational lens (Lave & Wenger, 199 1; Lave, 1989)  by centering learning that occurs as individuals and groups construct knowledge, identities, and relationships through their active participation in social activity. We draw inspiration from Keifert et al. (2023), considering the temporal wings of the conference. We hope this work can engage the society in a conversation about what our annual conference is – as a series of learning spaces – and what it can be. The conference learning landscape from a participationist perspective In our conceptualization, the learning that happens in the conference fits into two broad categories: learning that happens during the conference as participants interact with each other  through sanctioned conference activities and learning that happens beyond the conference, both in terms of the organizational and the temporal bounds of the gathering. We position the conference as a conceptual example of communities of practice (Lave & Wenger,

1991) that continually define subsets of academic disciplines. In other words, the conferences represents a key

point in space and time where the ISLS community of  practice is collectively defined and where the community learns about itself. As McDonald and Star (2008) show,  communities of practice emerge from disciplines when and if they embody Wenger’s (1998) three foundation al components of these  social spaces: “a domain of knowledge that creates a common ground and sense of  common identity, a community of people who care about the domain and create the social fabric of learning, and a shared practice that the community develops to be effective in its domain” (p. 4). Conferences thus p rovide incredible opportunities to learn how to be members of a discipline via a specific academic community of practice because they provide access to all three of these pillars and opportunities to shape them as time goes on.  Conferences are the spaces where scholars – individually and collectively – can both help define their field(s) and make sense of their places within it. In other words, conferences are sites of identity work with professional, ontological, axiological, and sociopolitical implications. Here, we foreground the practices and disciplinary knowledge learned and indicate sociopolitical implications of the design choices. Figure 1 Diagram of Learning at ISLS Learning during the conference The sanctioned events at the conference are paper presentations, keynotes, poster sessions, plenaries/presidential sessions, workshops, and social events. ISLS has also been experimenting with different formats in the past years (e.g., the “community day” in 2024), although we do not theorize those here. We identify, broadly, three types of participation within these events  that collectively produce the learning praxis of the con ference: lecturing, dialogue, and creation. We take up these limiting categories for conceptual brevity and admit the need for further nuance in future work. This paper is a catalyst for an ongoing conversation rather than a completed analysis. P aper presentations (including long papers, short papers, and symposia), represent the dominant form of conference participation at ISLS. Keynote addresses and plenaries follow a similar format.  We combine these events in Figure 1. The paper session as a participation structure is shaped by the spatial design of the spaces they regularly occur in (lecture halls and classrooms with seating facin g forward) and the social norms present (e.g., participants do not interrupt presenters or talk du ring presentations and  are often implicitly discouraged from getting up and leaving during a presentation). The physical and social boundaries of this participation structure – while porous (we have all had that person next to u s who likes to talk about the paper presentation as  it’s happening, for instance) – delineate the participant as a passive listener rat her than an active member in the discussion of ideas, conceptions, and analyses through most of these sessions. The pedagogical model at the heart of paper sessions  mirrors an approach to teaching and learning that learning scientists have criticized for years. As Waks (2011) notes, Dewey criticized the reliance on straight-line communication (a speaker speaks, a listener listens) and advocated for transactional listening-in-conversation (a dialogue forms between teacher and learner). While some of the latter does exist within paper presentations (e.g., Q & A sessions), the time spent on delivering papers prioritizes straight-line communication and the structure of these dialogues limits their pedagogical potential (Wells & Arauz, 2006). This leads to an inability to engage what Matusov (2011) describes as an authorial form of teaching and learning, where the notion of authorship transitions to the learner/listener through pedagogical activity. Despite these critiques, lecturing as a pedagogy can engender learning if participants have previously had the opportunity to develop “well-differentiated knowledge about a domain” (Schwartz & Branford, 1998, p. 476) or they can talk about and act on what the lecturer discussed during or after the presentation (Crook, 2022). While the former cannot be assured, the opportunity to continue to discuss the presentations after the sessions end during the conference provides an opportunity for a ttendees to construct knowledge.

While authentically dialogic in design and practice, p oster sessions are  fundamentally the l east prestigious conference engagement both at ISLS and in many fiel ds. Such is the case, perhaps, because they are seen as spaces for emerging ideas, conceptualizations, and analyses. Further, as many disciplinary fields indicate explicitly, they are opportunities for emerging scholars and graduate students to begin their participation trajectory in the field (e.g., Watkins et al., 2006; Watkins, et al., 2014). As Watkins et al. (2006) point out, poster sessions are an effective way to logistically include more r esearch presentations. This is a n increase in opportunities to peripherally participate in conference communities of practice, rather than m oving the poster session towards being a participation structure aligned with  more full participation in the community.  From a participant perspective, poster sessions often shape  participation structures that are productive for learn ing about other scholars’ research. From a presenters’ perspective,  they also provide much more possibility for engage ment and feedback. Such an explicitly dialogic participation structure  is shaped by both the session layouts (physical and ideational) and the social norms present (e.g., participants can move around the room freely). The workshops at ISLS incorporate hands on approaches to learning alongside dialogic and lecture-based pedagogical praxes. The workshops embody what Papert  (1980) would famously describe as a constructionist approach to learning, one that positions “learning as building relationships between old and new knowl edge in interactions with others while creating artifacts of social relevance” (Kafai et al., 2009, p. 3). More thandeveloping new disciplinary knowledges, the incorporation of c onstructionist learning opportunities within the co nference creates opportunities for participants to develop a  deeper understanding of their identity as it relat es to the surrounding learning context (Holbert et al., 2020). Despite the potential of workshops as learning opportunities, these events also hold a fringe space at ISLS because they are scheduled during the “pre-conference” time. This once again contributes to the hierarchical nature of conference events. Although not explicitly constructed as pedagogical,  we also recognize the learning potential of the conference’s social events. In positioning the conference as a site for the construction of a community of practice, creating space to socialize with each other represe nts a crucial part of learning. This kind of social  practice was so important to organizers of ISLS conferences that went online (2020; 2021; 2022) that they set up gather.town as a digitial common space for attendees to have opportunities to socialize. These conversations, as opportunities to talk about the work of the learning sciences, ho ld the potential for participants to construct disciplinary and methodological knowledges, as informal conversations often do (Woods, 2023). Learning beyond the conference In conceptualizing the pedagogies of ISLS, we recognize that the learning associated with the conferenc e does not solely happen within the temporal or geographic  bounds of this gathering. The possibilities of learning start before the conference begins, continue after it has ended, and flourish beyond the scope of the in-person events. The speculative nature offers possibilities for developing more just forms of learning (Garcia & Mirra, 2024). Learning happens before the conference in the inter twined processes of imagining  and writing  new submissions of all kinds.  The call for papers  (CFP) produces what Woods (20 21) describes as an aesthetic pedagogy, one where a creative artifact (or, in this case, a CFP) acts as a pedagogical technology because engagement with that artifact creates opportunities to form new relationships to ideas, tools, and other participants. Beyond the process of imagining possible futures, l earning also occurs through the process of writing  (Carter, 2007; Litts, et al., 2022) one’s contribution, especially when feedback (such as submission review s) is provided. Therefore, a submission does not need to be accepted to act as a learning opportunity for potential participants. L earning beyond the boundaries of ISLS’s sanctioned activities occurs on social media  during the conference. Social media participation can contribute to shaping the communal identity and situated knowledge of the field. At its best, social media provides an opportunity to increase accessibility in a space often unwelcoming of certain identities and forms of knowledge, including knowledges which explicitly form from feminized and racialized bodies (Black et al., 2020). If the institutional nature of conferences reifies certain ways of being, then engaging in alternate forms of participation like social media can challenge those official ways of knowing. After ISLS comes to a close, the processes of learning continue on. And while a multitude of ripple effects beyond the scope of this paper surely exist, we wan t to recognize two in particular: the  influence of the proceedings and the  ongoing dialogue shared between participan ts. The proceedings provide an opportunity to both further define the domain of the learning sciences and to create more opportunities for legitimate peripheral participation as new, interested scholars engage at the edges of the field through interdisciplinary curiosity. Peripherality and marginality: The who and for whom of conferencing Undergirding our  conceptualization of the academic conference as a s ite of learning is an  important truth: academic conferences exist as extensions of sanctioned research apparatuses and higher education. As such, the violent financial and ontological roots of settler-colonial and anti-Black racism in western science and, in turn, the formation of universities (Paperson, 2017) encompasses our LS community.  We (the authors) have benefited from these unjust systems that often privilege our proximity to whiteness and our bodies as cis-gender men. In this way – from a perspective of peripherality as entry into the field of the learning sciences community of practice

– we find it important to ask: how are specific people, ideas, and perspectives reified as legitimate, which potential

entries into LS are disciplined out, and which make space at the margins (Black et al., 2020)? Within ou r conceptualization, the interlocking systems of oppression (Taylor, 2017) – including whiteness, racism, gender oppression, disability injustice, colonialization, anti-LGBTQ+ violence, and more – shape different scholars' orientation to the field and, in turn, the development and maintenance of participation structures theorized above. Further, within this hierarchical, powered, high stakes, social space for the field, marginality surfaces (e.g., Gholson & Martin, 2014). Drawing on Velamur (2024), we wonder how the officially sanctioned participation structures of ISLS also cr eate margins “as a place in flux” in the field of the learning sciences and who – and what ideas – become part of them  and shape them. Further, what does it look like to recognize the margins, their dynamic nature,  and with them make visible alternative pathways of legitimate peripheral participation into the communities of practice of the conference and society? A few recent  symposia at the annual conference present examples of such openings. The new hybrid symposium track, for example, opens the space to those who might not be able to physically attend the conference. This is particularly important to make pathways more accessible for disabled and immunocompromised scholars, as well as scholars worried about traveling amidst rising fascism and targeting of critical scholars. It also opens space for participants in the conference from other stakeholders like youth researchers or community researchers. Keifert et al., (2023)’s innovativ e approach to the symposium provided a distinct hybridity for opportunities to engage with ideas. Further, symposia exemplifying the power of storying and storywork (Mexi, et al., 2022) as a methodological practice and scholarship are great examples of openings for alternative knowledges and pathways to being and becoming a learning scientist. These are just a few of the openings we are sure are happening at our conference, creating new possibilities for different kinds of learning.

## Conclusion and (near) future research

We have undertaken this analysis as a way to begin t heorizing not only what currently happens at academic conferences like ISLS but to consider possible futures. As other scholars have shown, breaking the norms of academic organizing creates opportunities not only for new pedagogical formations but opportunities for equitable participation for all in the field (Belliappa, 2020; Henderson & Burford, 2020). Building on this assertion, we will challenge the sanctioned structure of the conference by enacting a series of “rogue poster sessions” in-between official conference sessions and in informal meetings spaces. By subverting the official program of the conference, we intend to create opportunities for new kinds of learning to emerge and contribute to the ongoing conversation about the future of ISLS, one that asks the followi ng questions: Who gets to learn here? What kinds of participations – and in turn ideas – get re-produced in this community of practice? Is learning  even the intended goal of the conferenc e at all? And, if so, how do we construct more (and more equi table) opportunities for that learning to occur?
