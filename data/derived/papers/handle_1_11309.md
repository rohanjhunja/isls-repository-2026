# The Role of Surprise in Teachers’ Construction of Teaching Successes and Failures

**Conference:** ISLS 2025

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Teacher surprise refers to classroom events or experiences that a teacher interprets as unexpected occurrences in their teaching practice. We investigate preservice teachers’ expression of, and narrative elaboration of surprising events as they publicly made sense of their field-teach successes and failures. Our findings suggest that there is a rich everyday language for capturing and describing classroom occurrences that somehow do not follow the expected or projected course of events—what we term the “language of surprise”—and its use by teachers and teacher educators can be a productive means for inquiring deeply into one’s classroom practice, its challenges and successes.

## Introduction

Engaging teachers in systematic reflection on their  classroom practice is a cornerstone of teacher education and professional development efforts (e.g., Gay & Kirkl and, 2003). Such activities can be structured in myriad ways (Horn & Campbell, 2015; Finkelstein et al., 2019) and a common approach consists in teachers considering their teaching successes and challenges (or failures) (e.g., Hawkman et al., 2022). Research in those contexts have shed light on teachers’ sensemaking practices and instructional decisions regarding judged-to-be noteworthy classroom events and have informed the design and refinement of teacher learning activities (e.g., Horn, 2010; Philip, 2011). We add to these efforts by investigating how teache r surprise plays out in teachers’ construction of accounts of success and failures in their teaching practice. As widely documented, teachers deploy and coordinate varied resources when making sense of their classroom experiences (Russ, Sherin, & Sherin, 2016), and we argue that expressions and elaborations of surprise serve  as one such resource. Based on sparse literature i n sociolinguistics (Heath et al., 2012) and  education (Hawkman et al., 2022; Rainio & Hofmann, 2021), teacher surprise refers to classroom events or experiences that a teacher interprets (on-the-fly or upon posterior reflection) as unexpected occurrences in their classroom practice. Our findings suggest that there is a rich everyday language for capturing and describing events that somehow do not follow the expected or projected course of events—what we call the “language of surprise” —and its use by teachers and teacher educators can b e a productive means for reasoning about classroom practice, its pitfalls and successes. Specifically, we ask: What is the role of surprise in teachers’ construction of teaching successes and fa ilures? And what are the implications for teacher e ducation? We pursue these questions in the context of preservice teachers  (PST) reflecting on their field-teach experience and sharing those reflections in class.

## Theoretical framework

We frame our inquiry from a social, cultural, historical, and political perspective and take it that meaning is jointly constructed in the ongoing interactions between par ticipants in a practice and is situated within broader historic events and struggles for power (Esmonde & Booker, 2 017; Lave & Wenger, 1991).  The meanings of teaching success, failure and surprise are therefore co-constructed in preservice teachers’ exchanges—before, during and after in-class presentations—even if initially voiced by a single person. Furthermore, we also take it that narrative constru ctions of teaching successes/failures, as well as a ny surprises arising in the process, draw from teachers’ past experiences and histories, including everyday notions of these terms and how they appear in different contexts of daily life, the rich discursive ways of expressing surprise (e.g., language, laughter, prosody, and facial expr essions; Heath et al., 2012), their careers as students, their ongoing participation in the teacher education program in which they were  enrolled, and many others. As previously mentioned, t hese function as resources (Russ et al., 2016) for making sense of classroom successes/failures and any associated surprises.

In addition, following our sociopolitical framing w e attend closely to how assumptions (Gupta et al.,

2022) may undergird teachers’ collective construction of successes/failures and surprises. Assumptions are the

tacit, taken-for-granted meanings “circulating” in any social exchange and they media te the stances that people take when narrating and/or making sense of these interactions. Assumptions, on their turn, may reflect or critique ideologies of power and social difference (Hall, 1996), shaping what a teacher notice s (Louie, 2018; Shah & Coles, 2020) in classroom interactions or what they choose to re flect upon, how they construct such narratives, and how they negotiate their arguments when sharing their work publicly.

## Empirical context and methods

The context of our research is a STEM teacher educa tion program housed at a large university in South Central United States. Preservice teachers enrolled in the program were undergraduates in STEM disciplines who concurrently pursued a teaching credential. We report on data co llected in the Spring ’22 semester instantiation of Project-Based Instruction (PBI), which was the capstone cou rse in the program’s curricular sequence. The course aimed to deepen PSTs’ understanding and prac tice of various genres of a mbitious disciplinary teaching, with a particular focus on the theory and practice of PBI (Petrosino et al., 2024). In line with that goal, a major component of the course required students to plan, design, and teach a 3-day PBI lesson at a local school, and they did so individually or in pairs. Upon returning from their field-teach practice, PSTs engaged in reflecting on those experiences and shared their thoughts publicly in class (again, individually or in pairs). The first and second authors shared teaching respon sibilities of the PBI course, with the first author serving as the instructor of record. Twelve PST students were enrolled in the class, and they represented a variety of disciplinary domains. In response  to students’ concerns voiced during the semester, w e (the teaching team) decided to center field-teach reflections on successes and failures, in the hopes that an extended discussion of the very nature of judgements of teaching success/failu re would lead to deepened considerations of systemic issues impinging on their ability to successfully implement ambitious instruction. Specifically, we posed: List 1 success and 1 failure in your field-teach and provide a vignette analysis illustrating each. In-class presentations (Figure 1a) lasted between 10 and 15 minutes, with occasional questions from the audience, and were followed by some 10 to 15 minutes of discussion and comments. In ethnographically-informed fashion (Marcus & Fischer, 1999), we collected about eight hours of video records of those presentations.

## Analysis and results

Due to various reasons, we could only secure study consent from seven preservice teachers in the class. Working on the reduced data corpus, we (the research team) proceeded to transcribe video  records, then individually watched and coded transcripts for all talk on succe sses and failures. On the first pass, attention was  trained on what PSTs outlined as teaching success/failure and how they explained their inferences; several rounds  of code refinement were carried out  subsequently. In that context, surprise emerged as prominent theme in PSTs’ narratives, presentation slides, and whole-class discussions, and proved to be important in their making sense of field-teach experiences.

We then collected all forms through which surprise was expressed, beyond the literal use of the word. By definition, references to unexpected occurrences were included, but also many other forms and variations, such as “unplanned for,” “unforeseen,” “incredible,” “shocking,” “I didn’t anticipate it,” “I couldn’t believe it,” “wow,” etc. In addition, as documented in sociolinguistics  studies (Heath et al., 2012), surprise is o ften manifested and communicated through the articulation of various such expressions, forms, and linguistic devices, such as prosody, facial expressions, laughter and others, and we attend to these various modalities to infer stances of surprise. Finally, with all instances of surprise mapped out, we honed into how expressions of surprise functioned in the construction of PSTs narratives of success and failure. Specifically, we first identified the object—i.e., the very cause(s)—of PSTs’ surprise, then scrutinized how it figured in their construction and elaboration of a fieldteach success/failure. Given space constraints, here we consider only one case of PSTs’ construction of success and one case of failure.

Angkor’s construction of “really high student engagement” as success Angkor was a computer science major and individuall y he designed and taught a lesson to an Intro to Computer Science class of mixed-grade high school students. Angkor’s PBI lesson mer ged coding and database queries to generate data analysis and visualization of what he  termed “ethics issues” (e.g., gender and racial di fferences in the job market). In doing so, he sought  to connect the lesson with students’ future profess ional concerns while also fostering their critical take on broader societal issues. When sharing his reflections with the class (4/18/2 022), Angkor stated that his field-teach success was the [~6:45] “… really high student engagement” that  his lesson generated. At that point, he had the fi rst slide (Figure 1b) in his presentation deck on the large s creen and it listed four factors that he judged cen tral to successful, “high student engagement: ” (1) the lesson topic connected to students’ future concerns with college (what he termed “engaging topic”); (2) designed inconsistencies in the dataset led to passionate discussion (“meaningfulness,” in Angkor’s language); (3) classroom discussions were built into the lesson (“presentation”); and (4) tasks were open-ended. As evidence of “high student engagement,” Angkor stated that students generated many solutions to the proposed tasks and raised [7:40] “a lot of natural ((student-initiated)) discussions” (as when he stated [6:48] “… it wasn’t just CS, you know”).

Tellingly, while his slide s did not contain any reference to surprise, Angkor’s  narrative began by invoking surprise to make sense of his field-teach success: 1 2 3 4 5 6 Angkor [6:43] Um… so one of my successes was that I had re ally high student engagement, which was really surprising for me, um:: especially compared to um::: CI ((a class in which he experienced his first field teach and which precedes PBI)), um… where I had extremely bad, um… student engagement… Um, I was also teaching physics  in CI…, which is not my area of expertise, so I'm sure I… I was confused. T he students were confused, they… it wasn't great. So yeah, I… I got to talk about what I knew here so it just worked out a lot better.

We interpret Angkor’s surprise to refer to  his own ability  to teach in ways that engage students. To explicate, according to Angkor’s account, in a prev ious field-teach (in the course Classroom Interactions, or CI, which immediately preceded PBI) he had failed to ob tain satisfactory student engagement. Having failed before, Angkor seemed to expect the same would happen in his PBI teach (lines 2-3) but, to his surprise, students showed high levels of participation, as illustrated in the  variety of student-generated solutions to proposed problems and “natural discussions” that students initiated.

We take it that Angkor’s on-the-fly use of the language of surprise to capture and explain his unexpected ability to engage students allowed him:

- To integrate experiences across field-teach practice: Surprise emerged as he compared-and-contrasted

his differential teaching performance across teaching contexts (from Classroom Interactions to PBI) and afforded him an opportunity to make sense of his past teaching challenges and how his improvement could be explained.

- Simultaneously, when reasoning about events in this  manner, Angkor went beyond technical

considerations about (PBI) lesson design and its ef fects on student participation (as inscribed in the analysis section of his slides) to include the centrality of teachers’ disciplinary knowledge and their ability to teach (“I got to talk about what I knew here so it just worked out a lot better,” line 6).

- To take on students’ perspective s: In the process, Angkor understood that his prior teaching (under)

performance (in CI) deeply impinged on students’ un derstanding (“ The students were confused,” line 5) and consequently their ability to participate in the le sson. By doing so, he moved to take students’ persp ectives on classroom activities (Jaber, 2021)—a key pedagogical practice. Jason and Jackie’s failure to prepare students for “the lab” Jackie and Jason were chemistry majors and together they designed and taught a 3-day lesson on the solubility of liquids to a combined class of 11th and 12th graders. Roughly, the lesson plan was to get students to explore some parameters affecting the solubility of liquids, then engage in formal calculations, and finish up by designing their own sodas at the lab. Their lesson failure came in the final day when students were to begin lab work. To prepare for that activity, the teaching team included a “pre-lab review” on the second day of the lesson (i.e., the day prior to the lab work), covering suc h issues as lab safety procedures, conceptual found ations (e.g., the meniscus line), and materials. As Jason remarked about that review’s outcome: [26:15] “But because, uh:: the students had… it seemed like they had a good grasp on the topics, we didn't have to review that much.”  He continued (4/11/22): 7 8 9 10 11 Jason [26:30] So when it came to like the day of the experiment, we found like… the students got all their materials and were in th eir groups and had their, uh: experiments all pre-planned out. But when we were like … okay, start um::: they didn’t know how to like… even turn on the hot plate… a lot of them… and we just assumed that they would know how to do that. While Jason and Jackie did not use the word ‘surprise,’ we argue that they were surprised by students’ inabilities—specifically, students’  purported lack of some basic knowledge of chemistry lab proced ures and concepts. To elaborate, having conducted a pre-lab review and assumed that 11 th and 12th grade students would know “the basics” of chemistry labs, the teaching t eam expected that students would be able to reproduce such knowledge on demand the next day at the lab (lines 7-8). To their surprise, however, students had difficulties with the task. Here, surprise was constructed by articul ating (1) assumptions (line 1 0) about what students should purportedly know at certain points in their careers  with (2) several expressions and discursive moves, including the repeated use of narratives such as “… even turn on the hot plate” (lines 9-10) and, later on, “don’t even know what the scale says…,” and “… like it’s pretty basic.” By repeatedly foregrounding the distance between expected and actual students’ abilities, and backed by laughter and smirks from classroom peers, Jason and Jackie seemed to make real their surprise with students’ inabilities. We argue that Jackie and Jason’s use of the language of surprise:

- Helped them to rationalize key aspects of their tea ching “failure.” While Jackie and Jason never

explicitly named their “failure,” they were clear a bout its effect —namely, students’ lack of progress in the all-important, final lab day. To explain that outcome, they mentioned two main factors: students’ lack of basic knowledge of chem labs and the teaching team’s own assumptions about what stud ents should know about that topic. Elaborating upon the surprising outcome thus  allowed them to justify their assumptions and to “ smooth away” some of their responsibility for the field-teach challenges they encountered.

- By doing so, Jason and Jackie also positioned their students as learners with deficits (Valencia, 1997).

Their arguments —unquestioned by class peers and instructional perso nnel—worked by naturalizing their assumptions about what students should know about a discipline and their ability to reproduce such knowledge on demand. Such assumptions are undergirded by ideo logies of social difference, and their taken-for-granted status functioned to reinscribe deficit views of students (Daniel et al., 2023) and to hide complexities of teaching.

## Discussion

The term surprise (and correlates) had no prior technical or practical use in the PBI course nor (to the best of our knowledge) in the teacher education program in whic h PSTs were enrolled; instead, it arose organically from PSTs’ attempt to make sense of, and to communicate certain aspects of their field-teach successes and failures. As there are no systematic studies of teacher surprise, we take our findings to be preliminary; still, some are robust and have substantive implications for teacher education:

a) There is a rich language for capturing and inter preting classroom phenomena that deviates from a

lesson’s expected or projected course of classroom events. Teacher educators may intentionally draw on  this language when guiding teachers’ reflections on clas sroom successes and challenges, and likely in other  contexts as well (e.g., Horn, 2010; Rainio & Hofmann, 2021).

b) At the core, surprises operate by way of comparisons across events, situations, or experiences (real or

expected/projected!). Because they involve contrasting two or more events, inquiring into surprising experiences or events afford teachers and teacher educators the  opportunity to deeply inquire into the nature of t hose events, to ponder the discontinuities between them, and thus to gain important insight into one’s teaching practices.

c) Specifically, attending to teachers’ use of the language of surprise in reflective practice can fac ilitate

PSTs to integrate experiences across teaching conte xts and thus develop more holistic, complex understandings of teaching practice. Crucially, it can support tea chers (preservice or otherwise) in unpacking common assumptions about learning and learners (Gupta et a l., 2022). We see this as key to guiding teachers in deconstructing implicit, naturalized notions about learners’ purported (in)abilities and how these sha pe their instructional decisions.

In closing, we hope to continue examining surprise in STEM teacher education, particularly as it may serve as a lever toward ideological change (Philip,  2011) in the classroom. In line with ISLS 25’s conference theme, in the long run this may inform teaching practices that can foster more sust ainable and just futures for all learners.
