# Quantum Computational Thinking: A Conceptual Exploration

**Conference:** ICLS 2026

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
Computational Thinking (CT) frameworks have expanded over the past two decades to address technological advances and sociotechnical concerns, yet they remain grounded in classical, deterministic computational logic. Quantum information science introduces fundamentally different principles for representing and processing information, requiring learners to engage with non-intuitive, probabilistic, and relational forms of reasoning that depart from classical computational models. Drawing on frameworks by Brennan and Resnick and Kafai et al., we explore what Quantum Computational Thinking (QCT) might entail and open a dialogue about how the learning sciences community might prepare learners for a quantum-mediated world, with attention to access, democratic participation, and the challenge s of developing new intuitions.

Definitions of  C omputational Thinking (CT) and its associated competencies have evolved in response to advances in computing and the growing sociotechnical impact of technology on daily life (Selby & Woollard, 2013). Early frameworks emphasized procedural thinking and programming (Papert, 1980/2020), while later frameworks expanded to center concepts, practices, and perspectives (e.g., Brennan & Resnick, 2012), and more recently broadened to include social, cultural, and social justice dimensions (Kafai et al., 2016; Kafai et al., 2019). As emergin g technologies continue to redefine computation, the rise of quantum information science (QIS) (Nielsen & Chuang, 2010) calls for a new kind of computational literacy that goes beyond binary, deterministic logic (Nita et al., 2023). QIS tech nologies, including computing, communication, and sensing, embody fundamentally different principles for representing, processing, and reasoning about information, challenging educators to articulate and cultivate Quantum Computational Thinking (QCT). Recent calls to broaden participation in QIS education recommend introducing it early in the K –12 learning trajectory (Franklin et al., 2020) so as to avoid concentrating participation among a small elite and thereby deepening existing inequities. Engaging students early demystifies quantum concepts and practices and  enables students to envision how quantum technologies might transform areas such as communication, security, or medicine.

This paper examines what QCT  might entail and why cultivating quantum literacy represents both an educational opportunity and a democratic necessity. We trace the evolution of CT frameworks, explore why quantum computing matters for learning and humandevelopment, e xamine core quantum concepts and their cognitive demands, and return to foundational questions that were debated in early CT discourse. Computational thinking Nearly two decades ago, Wing (2006) introduced Computational Thinking (CT) as “a universally applicable attitude and skill set everyone, not just computer scientists, would be eager to learn and use” (p. 33). Early work by Papert (1 980/2020) framed CT primarily in terms of procedural thinking and programming. Subsequent scholars broadened its scope: Grover and Pea (2013) identified transferable competencies such as abstraction, decomposition, and debugging; Brennan and Resnick (2012) reframed CT as the interplay of concepts, practices, and perspectives, shifting  attention toward epistemic and identity dimensions; and  Kafai (2016) proposed computational participation to foreground the social, cultural, and ethical aspects of computing. Yet across these perspectives, CT remains grounded in classical, deterministic, binary logic. When these frameworks were established, quantum technologies were largely theoretical (Nielsen & Chuang, 2010). Today, quantum information science (QIS) is rapidly transitioning from theory to application, promising transformative impacts. The question is no longer whether quantum technologies will redefine the computational landscape, but how soon and for whom. This shift calls for re-examining what it means to th ink computationally. Quantum Computational Thinking (QCT) does not replace classical CT, but extends it to address a distinct class of problems that quantum computation is uniquely suited to tackle. In the next section, we explore what QCT entails and how quantum concepts, practices, and perspectives candeepen and extend CT for the emerging quantum era. 1627 Why quantum computing matters Quantum computing exploits the laws of quantum mechanics rather than those of  classical physics, and is part of a broader family of emerging quantum technologies, including computers, sensors, communication systems, and cryptography (Nielsen & Chuang, 2010). The potential applications are vast. To name just a few, these technologies might accelerate material discovery for clean energy (Alexeev et al., 2024), optimize complex systems across industries (Sciorilli et al., 2025), and simulate molecular interactions to advance drug development (Santagati et al., 2024). Yet this same power poses serious risks, including the potential to break the predominant encryption methods used to secure internet communications (Mosca, 2018). Understanding quantum information science matters not only for physicists and computer scientists, but for anyone concerned with technology’s role in society. From a learning and humandevelopment perspective, quantum computing is a cultural tool that might  mediate human activity and reshape practices across domains (Vygotsky, 1978). As with writing, the printing press, the World Wide Web, and generative AI, successive innovations in information and communication technologies have continually reorganized cognitive work, redistributing intelligence across people, artifacts, and environments in ways that redefine which problems become thinkable (Pea, 1993). Quantum technologies promise a similarly transformative horizon. But how do quantum computers work, and what gives them their computational advantages? Quantum computing core concepts At their core, quantum computers can process information about all possible inputs simultaneously (Deutsch, 1985). When we read out an answer, however, the quantum computer outputs only one result, selected at random (Born, 1926; Deutsch, 1985). The key insight is that we can systematically reshape the probability distribution before observation by manipulating how these parallel computations interfere  with each other, causing incorrect answers to cancel out while the correct answer is amplified to emerge with high probability (Nielsen & Chuang, 2010). The central challenge lies in determining when such algorithmic approaches are possible and designing the procedures to implement them (Kerenidis & Cherrat, 2025). Three fundamental concepts underlie quantum computing and quantum information science more broadly: superposition, measurement, and entanglement. Superposition enables a qubit, the quantum version of a classical bit, to exist as a combination of 0 and 1, rather than being restricted to either. This does not mean the qubit is simultaneously 0 and 1 in the classical sense; rather, superposition represents a distinct quantum state that combines properties of both. With multiple qubits in superposition, a quantum computer simultaneously represents all possible combinations of 0s and 1s across all of those qubits, scaling exponentially: 100 qubits represent roughly 10 30 possible inputs at once. Measurement  forces a qubit from superposition into a definite classical state, 0 or 1, and this irreversibility creates a critical constrain for algorithm design: W e cannot observe qubits without destroying their superposition, and we cannot duplicate quantum states (the no-cloning theorem) (Scarani et al., 2005). Entanglement  correlates qubits such that the measurement outcome of one determines the outcome of another in ways with no classical explanation (Bell, 1964; Horodecki et al., 2009). Crucially, operations on one qubit alter the joint state shared between entangled qubits, enabling the manipulation of nonclassical correlations that underlies quantum speedups (Jozsa & Linden, 2003). These three concepts differ from classical, deterministic intuitions about information and computation. While classical computers can theoretically simulate quantum computers, doing so requires exponentially increasing resources as problems scale (Xu et al., 2023). The goal of quantum computing is therefore not to implement any algorithm on quantum hardware, but to identify algorithms that demonstrate quantum advantage

– that is, algorithms that outperform any known classical alternative (Preskill, 2018). Discovering such algorithms

is not merely a technical challenge; it demands entirely new computational intuitions with no classical equivalent (Aaronson, 2013), and requires recognizing which problems are well-suited to quantum approaches. QCT, we argue, is not simply a new technical skill set, but rather a fundamentally different approach to problem-solving. Exploring quantum computational thinking A useful starting point for investigating QCT is the computational thinking framework put forth by Brennan and Resnick (2012). Their framework operates at three levels (concepts, practices, and perspectives), capturing both cognitive and social dimensions of computation.  Concepts are the “[ideas] designers engage with as they program,” practices are what they “develop as they engage with the concepts,” and perspectives  are what the “designers form about the world around them and themselves.” While these components remain essential in quantum computing contexts, the  distinctive quantum phenomena of superposition, measurement, and entanglement introduce elements that extend beyond traditional CT frameworks. 1628 A s outlined above, at the conceptual level, quantum computing introduces randomness, interference, superposition, entanglement, and measurement. These concepts enable distinctive computational practices. One such practice is accepting probabilistic answers to gain efficiency: Rather than requiring algorithms that produce correct answers, we candesign algorithms that output correct answers with high probability. The Schwartz-Zippel algorithm for polynomial identity (zero) testing exemplifies this approach,  where if we evaluate the polynomial on enough inputs and they all evaluate to zero, then we can conclude with high likelihood that the polynomial is identically zero (Schwartz, 1980). As long as an algorithm is more likely to output the correct answer than not, multiple executions  yield correctness with confidence exceeding any desired threshold. This practice is not exclusively quantum, as it requires only randomness, which is widely believed (though not proven) to be simulatable on classical computers (I mpagliazzo & Widgerson, 2001). Quantum computing applies this practice through a distinctly quantum mechanism: amplitude amplification. Rather than solely relying on randomness, quantum algorithms leverage superposition and interference to systematically enhance the probabilities of correct outcomes. When qubits exist in superpositions of all possible states, carefully designed sequences  of operations shape interference patterns to amplify states that correspond to correct answers while suppressing those that correspond to incorrect ones. Grover’s search algorithm exemplifies this technique, achieving a quadratic speedup over classical search methods (Grover, 1996).

At the perspective level, quantum computing cultivates several orientations that transform how learners might view computing, knowledge, and participation. The first is understanding computing as a physical process

– that is, recognizing that computation operates through fundamental laws of the universe (Deutsch, 1985).

Deutsch’s (1985) insight that we can “program using physics” marks a conceptual shift that suggests computation is not purely abstract, but rather is materially embodied. Understanding this can help learners see computation as part of the natural world, connecting coding to broader scientific reasoning about systems and energy. A second perspective is recognizing that measurement affects systems. In classical computing, actions like checking a variable’s value or accessing files typically have a negligible impact on the system state. Quantum computing, on the other hand, demands deliberate attention to what we measure and when, as measurement alt ers the computational state. While the idea that observation influences outcomes is not unique to quantum systems  – people, for example, often behave differently when they know they are being observed (Mayo, 1933) – quantum computing renders this effect unavoidable, and theorizes it as governed by physical law rather than social dynamics. Another important perspective that quantum computing promotes is the ability to design under uncertainty. A quantum mindset encourages comfort with uncertainty and incomplete information. Learners learn to work productively with probability distributions, anticipate multiple potential outcomes, and design algorithms or arguments that perform well under indeterminacy. This contrasts with classical computational determinism and aligns with scientific reasoning under uncertainty. Extending Brennan and Resnick’s idea that perspectives involve seeing oneself in relation to others and to the world, quantum computational thinking adds an appreciation for epistemic humility  (Medina, 2013; Potter, 2022; Srivastava, 2025), which demands recognizing that uncertainty is intrinsic and is not a flaw in understanding. It also introduces a participatory perspective (Kafai & Proctor, 2022) that emphasizes seeing oneself as part of a sociotechnical system whose design and access are shaped by power and policy. Learners who grasp that access confers influence can better understand the ethical stakes of emerging quantum technologies. These perspectives offer value beyond quantum computing contexts, extending to broader domains of problem solving and scientific thinking. Their broader applicability suggests that quantum computing provides conceptual tools and practices that can mediate thinking and problem-solving in contexts well beyond quantum technologies themselves.

Kafai (2016) introduces the idea of computational participation, reframing computational thinking through a social lens that emphasizes the communal nature of sharing, modifying, and remixing code, rather than focusing exclusively on individual cognitive processes. Given that quantum technologies are currently large, expensive, and limited primarily to large corporations and research institutions, such participation remains heavily constrained. Most work on quantum computation is conducted not on physical devices but through simulation and theoretical analysis. Viewing quantum computational thinking through a social justice lens (Kafai et al., 2019), and considering what quantum technologies allow, it becomes clear that whoever controls access to these devices holds significant power. For example, if only a privileged few people get to leverage the perfect security of quantum communications while post-quantum encryption is not yet widely implemented, those individuals could potentially compromise others’ privacy by breaking conventional encryption with quantum computers. Similarly, if quantum machine learning algorithms prove effective for financial applications (e.g., stock exchange), those with access could exploit this advantage to accumulate wealth disproportionally. While these scenarios may or may not materialize, they warrant serious consideration in discussions of quantum technologies and their societal implications.

1629 We do not claim to have comprehensively discussed all relevant concepts, practices, and perspectives for quantum computational thinking  here. Identifying the full range of concepts, practices, and perspectives requires empirical investigation of how quantum information scientists actually think, work, and reason. We offer this framework as an invitation to pursue such research and to investigate how quantum technologies, as cultural tools that mediate human activity, might reshape learning, reasoning, and participation in an evolving technological landscape, while attending critically to questions of access, power, and justice as quantum technologies become integrated into society.

Open questions for the learning sciences community Quantum computational thinking raises many of the same questions that were once asked about classical computational thinking. During the 2010 Workshop on Computational Thinking, participants debated whether computational thinking should be taught to everyone, particularly in K-12 education (NRC, 2010). Some argued that not everyone would need computational thinking in their everyday lives, much like people do not need to know how their automatic transmissions work in their cars. Other workshop participants countered with rationales spanning multiple levels: CT enables personal empowerment and potentially liberating career pathways, enhances inquiry across disciplines, and is essential for succeeding in a technological society and maintaining economic competitiveness (NRC, 2010, p. 4).

Many of these same rationales apply to QCT, including its potential to support disciplinary inquiry, open new career pathways, and sustain competitiveness in emerging technologies. Yet the case for quantum literacy also rests on broader considerations that quantum computing shares with other consequential technologies, from writing to artificial intelligence. Crucially, when powerful computational capabilities become concentrated only in well-resourced institutions, broader literacy becomes essential to ens ure informed democratic participation in technology design and governance (Castells, 2010). Questions about research priorities, aims of regulation, security implications, and equity in access affect all members of society, yet risk being decided by only a  few technical elites without broader public understanding.

What distinguishes the quantum case, however, is the cognitive challenges it presents. Quantum computational thinking demands reasoning that fundamentally contradicts classical intuitions about information, causality, and computation. Unlike learning to us e AI systems, which build mostly on familiar computational concepts, quantum thinking requires developing entirely new intuitions. If these indeed represent genuinely novel modes of reasoning, as we have argued, there are profound implications for when and how QCT should be taught. Currently, most people first encounter quantum concepts in late undergraduate education, if at all (Nita et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2022). Yet developing genuine quantum intuition, rather than surface familiarity, may require extended engagement over time. This raises important questions about the curriculum learning activities needed: Should quantum concepts be introduced earlier? If so, how can they best be made developmentally appropriate? Our aim is not to provide definitive  answers, but to open a dialogue about how the learning sciences community might prepare learners for an increasingly quantum-mediated world.
