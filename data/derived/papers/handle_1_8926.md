# Young Children’s Use of Spatial Sensemaking Practices as Mediators of Spatial Skills During Informal STEM Programs

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
While considerable research has examined when young children develop spatial skills and how these predict their success in STEM engagement, this study adds to the limited research on the processes through which young children learn to reason spatially during everyday learning environments. Through investigations of science and engineering programs for 3–5-year-old children, we provide evidence for how these programs engage children in a diverse range of spatial sensemaking practices, such as gestures, sketching, and object manipulation, which in turn engage their use of multiple forms of spatial skills. Our findings suggest children used spatial sensemaking to solve engineering problems and make sense of science phenomena, setting the stage for future STEM learning.

## Introduction

Spatial thinking––the use of spatial concepts, spatial tools, and application of spatial reasoning–– is fundamental to everyday life as well as advancing access to sci ence, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STE M) pursuits (National Research Council, 2006). We apply the skills of spatial thinking when we use a map to navigate to a new location or use instructions to assemble furniture. Yet early differences in how children reason spatially have implications for their near-term and long-term  opportunities in pursuing STEM interests. Research demonstrates that preschool-age children’s spatial skills predict early success in mathematics (e.g., Gunderson et al., 2012) and has thus provides the link to later persistence in science (Wai et al., 2009). Spatial skills–application of cognitive processes to reason about spatial info rmation––can be improved through a wide-variety of interventions, even among very young children (Uttal et al., 2013; Yang et al., 2020). However, what is needed is more information on how children’s everyday learnin g contexts, such as museum settings and preschools,  can provide spatialized experiences for children that embed training for spatial thinking alongside play-based STEM engagement (Newcombe, 2013). This study considers h ow STEM programs in a science center provide an opportunity for a spatialized experience where children enact spatial practices in order to engage in spatial skills. Spatial Skills and Distributed Spatial Sensemaking Literature on how people engage in spatial thinking  often frames the process as a set of cognitive spa tial skills (e.g., Uttal et al., 2013). One benefit of categori zing spatial skills is the identification of correl ations between specific spatial skills and performance in STEM fie lds (Hegarty, 2014). Such findings suggest that sup porting flexible use of a broad range of spatial skills might benefit children in their early learning experiences. We drew on a taxonomy which classifies spatial skills along two orthogonal dimensions: intrinsic to extrinsic and static to dynamic (Uttal et al., 2013). For example, the spatial skill mental rotation (the ability to rotate 2D or 3D objects in one’s mind) is classified as intrinsic (within the object) and dynamic (active mental manipulation). We also use a distributed spatial sensemaking fram ework to better examine how the learning environment support children’s use of spatial skill s. Distributed spatial sensemaking frames children’ s use of spatial reasoning as practices situated in context and drawing on social and material resources (Ramey, Stevens, & Uttal, 2020). This framework helps us better understand children’s learning by situating spatial thinking in their everyday learning opportunities. Young children’s spatial thinking is malleable (e.g., Yang et al., 20 20) so it is important for research to identify the range of conditions that improve children’s spatial thinking. The distributed spatial sensemaking framework was used to identify how and when the children used spatial sensemaking practices – defined as “strategies for making sense of spatial information that relied on communication with other individuals (learners or instructors) or interactio ns with external objects and representations” (Rame y & Uttal, 2017, p. 12). While this framework has been used to  uncover the ways middle school students use spatia l sensemaking (e.g., Ramey & Uttal, 2017), our research demonstrates the utility of this framework to understand the processes through which spatial thinking is lea rned, enacted, and leads to constructing explanatio ns and solving problems in everyday learning contexts for preschool-age children. Drawing on these frameworks, we conducted a qualitative study of three preschool STEM programs, guided by the following research question: In what ways do spatial sensemaking practices engage children’s use of spatial skills during STEM programs? ## Methodology

Our methodological approach drew on interaction analysis because it is grounded in a belief that “knowledge and action are fundamentally social in origin, organization, and use, and are situated in particular social and material ecologies” (Jordan & Henderson, 1995, p. 41). Gathering video-data is well-suited to interaction analysis because it allowed us to generate patterns from multiple sets of observations. Further, video analysis allowed us to consider the embodied nature of children’s meaning-making with their hands, bodies, and material manipulations. To investigate children’s spatial sensemaking, we c hose three preschool STEM programs at a science center located in a town in the North Eastern United States. The programs selected for their potential for prompting children’s spatial thinking: a 5-day engineering ca mp, a 30-min program about lunar craters, and a 30-min program about shadows. Participants included the preschool-age (age 3-5 years) children attending each program: n=8, n=10, and n=6, respectively, and the early chi ldhood educators who led each program. Each program  was video recorded using multiple camera angles. We sel ected only the video sections from the engineering camp showing children working towards solving each day’s  main engineering challenge as our pre-coding sugge sted these would provide a rich source of data on young children’s spatial thinking (~1.5 hrs / day) for an alysis. We analyzed the full data sets from the other two programs (~30 min each).

Using Vosiac for video coding, we first broadly coded to identify events in which children and educators engaged in spatial sensemaking practices. Within these events, we applied three coding schemes. (1) We  began with coding children’s spatial sensemaking practices (SSPs) (Ramey & Uttal, 2017): spatial talk, gestures, object manipulation, body movement, and sketching. (2) We coded clusters of SSPs for spatial skills using Utt al et al. (2013)’s typology, with sub-codes defined in Ramey et al. (2020). The four spatial skills are: intrinsic-static: “Perceiving objects, paths or spatial configurations amid distracting background information,” intrinsic-dynamic: “Piecing together objects into more complex configu rations, visualizing and mentally transforming obje cts,” extrinsic-static: “Understanding abstract spatial principles, such as horizontal invariance or verticality,” and extrinsic-dynamic “Visualizing an environment in its entirety from a different position” (Uttal et al., 2013, p. 4).

(3) We coded each event for social and material resources mediating their production: educators, other children,

physical materials, sketches, and tools (Ramey et al., 2020). (4) We coded for preschool-age children’s evidencebased explanations (e.g., Plummer & Ricketts, 2018) and engineering problem-solving (e.g., NRC, 2013). To improve the validity of our coding, we used existing frameworks from the literature (e.g., Ramey & Uttal, 2017; Uttal et al., 2013; NRC, 2013). The fi rst author coded the video data and then reviewed a ll coding with the second author to improve reliability. All codes were agreed upon after discussion. Working ac ross our coded videos, we defined themes from patterns observed across the code groups (Braun & Clarke, 2006).

## Findings

Our analyses resulted in three major themes. First, we demonstrated how the preschool-age children engaged in a range of spatial skills, during science and enginee ring activities, using multiple spatial sensemaking  practices. Second, we identified the important role played by educators and the material resources provided to the children in how they engaged with spatial thinking. Third, the children used spatial sensemaking practices as an essential component of constructing evidence-based explanations in science and solving problems in engineering. Below, we provide excerpts from the programs illustrating these themes. Each science program and each day of the engineering camp engaged children in a few spatial sensemaking practices, which in turn supported one or two spatial skills. However, across all three programs, we found children engaged in a broad range of prac tices and three of the four spatial skill quadrants. And, individual spatial sensemaking practices (e.g., sketching, gesturing) engaged children in diverse spatial skills, depending on the program. ## Discussion

Our analysis demonstrates the diverse set of practi ces preschool-age children used to apply spatial sk ills while engaging in solving problems and investigating science phenomena. Across a selection of STEM programs,  we identified children’s use of spatial skills within three quadrants of Uttal et al. (2013)’s taxonomy. Further, our analysis reveals that young children draw on both personal resources (spatial sensemaking practices) and external resources (educators and the materials provided in the workshop) to facilitate their application of th ose spatial skills to solve spatial problems. Thus, we make the following contributions to the literature on young children’s spatial thinking. First, this study address gaps in  the literature about how STEM education may engage  young children’s early spatial skills through participation in distributed spatial sensemaking, though more research on the efficacy of such programs is still needed. Second, our study provides insight into methods that may improve young children’s spatial skills using alternatives to traditional models of spatial training or decont extualized instruction (e.g., Yang et al., 2020). While spatia lized instruction is largely absent from formal ear ly education curricula (Pritulsky et al., 2020), our findings su ggest that play-based, STEM activities may engage c hildren in distributed spatial sensemaking providing opportunities to develop spatial skills.
