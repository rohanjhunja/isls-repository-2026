# Game-Based Punctuation Training to Promote Clause Analysis: A Proposal

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
High-school-level standardized exams devote 10-15% of its content to grammar and punctuation skills, yet the rules of and best practices for training punctuation are often elusive. We describe an ongoing pilot study where students preparing for the ACT/SAT participate in game-based training sessions for advanced English punctuation. Game levels require active organization of sentences, and students are supported through (1) text from a virtual agent, (2) hints, and (3) a visual cognitive organizer.

## Introduction

In this proposed study, we explore the impact of a browser-based ACT/SAT-level grammar training game on students’ grammar performance, simultaneously comparing two implementations of a learning support tool. First, for the quantitative component, we use pre-/posttest scores and in-game performance data to assess whether mandatory or optional learning support is more effective and determine what factors contribute most to improving learning and engagement. Second, for the qualitative component, we use gameplay observations and learner interviews to identify patterns in learner confidence and motivation during gameplay, as well as tutors’ preferences and questions surrounding the use of games in teaching punctuation strategies to their students. We supplement our investigation with interviews with online tutors working 1-on-1 with the same population of students to better understand tutors’ perspectives on student needs and obstacles facing educators concerning the use of such online tools in remote learning settings.

## Background

In this study, we investigate the potential of a web browser-based game for training grammar skills at the college entrance exam level (e.g., ACT/SAT). Students often struggle remembering and applying punctuation rules, particularly in complex, multi-part sentences. Practicing using correct punctuation in a computer game with access to sentence organization tools may help students (1) identify patterns in how sentence parts are joined, and (2) accurately complete punctuation-related problems in standardized testing situations. The general question of providing assessments and learning support in game-based learning has been the subject of reviews concerning best design practices (Ke, F., & Shute, V., 2015). Second, the practical importance of punctuation skills for students preparing for the ACT/SAT is evident in that one fourth of each exam is devoted to English grammar and editing strategies. Game-based learning can reduce anxiety for students (Yang et al., 2018). Wouters et al. (2015) also note that instruction can be helpful in game-based learning, especially when it involves identifying relevant new information for learners. This provides motivation for the qualitative component of this study, wherein we investigate the role instructors (in this case, tutors) can play in game-based learning for their students—if overt instruction by a human is helpful for students during gameplay, then it is important to better understand the perspectives and attitudes of tutors toward engaging with their students in digital game-based learning. Because the ACT and SAT exams are specifically for high school students preparing for collegiate study in the US, this criterion helps limit the subject pool to high school students for whom advanced English punctuation skills are relevant. Recruitment focuses on students either preparing for the ACT/SAT or independently studying English as a second language because these are the two groups for whom the intervention and research questions are most pertinent.

## Methods

The learning intervention is a browser-based punctuation training game, wherein subjects create a virtual character and help “decode encrypted messages” by assigning correct punctuation to multi-clause sentences. Each level comprises a single sentence divided into four parts (Figure 1), in-game support such as pop-up text hints (Figure 2), and an organizer tool displayed as a screen overlay where subjects can label sentences and see common punctuation patterns as a reference while solving each level (Figure 3). As a web application, students can access this through most browsers on most computers, tablets, and smartphones, but subjects are recommended to use computers or Chromebooks or tablets while completing the 21 game levels for this study, to ensure consistent performance by the software. Learners are individuals preparing for the ACT or SAT exam, ICLS2022 Proceedings 2184 © ISLS or are studying English as a 2nd language. Learners are excluded if they cannot complete the intervention either in a session with their tutor or independently outside of tutoring sessions (they must not complete only half of the intervention outside of session, for example). Learners are also excluded if they are below grade 9. There are two types of data collection. First, learners take a pre-test (10 questions) in ACT/SAT English format, and after learners complete 21 game levels, they are given a post-test. Pre-test and post-test scores are saved automatically, as well as game performance data for each round they complete, including how much time they spend on certain game-tasks, how many times they answered correctly or incorrectly, and how long they viewed feedback text or the organizer tool. The second type of data collection is an open-ended survey for learners and informal interviews with professional online tutors who have a chance to use the intervention with their student(s). These are brief interviews with open-ended questions where tutors can share their general thoughts and experience with the intervention and related learning games. Pre-/post-test data and in-game performance data are all collected automatically through the RPG Maker MV game engine. Recruitment of learners began in July, 2021 and co ntinues on while learners participate in the intervention. This depends on learner availability. Learner surveys will be administered after learners finish the main portion of gameplay (21 levels + post-test). Informal interviews with tutors will take place sometime convenient for them after their students have completed the intervention (1-3 weeks after). Analysis Quantitative data is coded anonymously, downloaded, and analyzed using IBM’s SPSS software. Survey and interview data is coded using a qualitative framework. For the qualitative component of this mixed methods study, main themes that arise through analysis of interviews will be summarized and presented to subjects who participated in the interviews by email within 1-6 months of the completion of research procedures. Subjects will have the chance to voice agreement or disagreement (text responses by email) with how their stories will be represented and the researchers will update qualitative summaries accordingly. Like all other data in the study, this will be stored using the coded name of the participant and under the same password protection. Finally, we will continue to explore results to identify game performance and affective patterns as students engage with the game. By comparing data from the two groups, we address whether the delivery of learning support made an impact on how students engage with the game. We will also address to what extent student interaction with in-game support features interacted with learning outcomes or affective states during gameplay, such as engagement and confidence level.
