# Students' Ideas About Evidentiary Fit and Its Role in Modeling Practice

**Conference:** ISLS 2022

## Abstract & Introduction

### Abstract
A core aspect of scientific inquiry is developing and revising models. This practice is supported by shared epistemic criteria to evaluate the “goodness” of models (and other epistemic products). A prominent criterion used by scientists is evidential fit. The use of epistemic criteria is gaining traction in science education; however, there is a gap in the research about the extent to which students view evidential fit as a central epistemic criterion of scientific models and what sense they make of the role of evidence in modeling. Using the Grasp of Evidence framework, we examined how students reasoned about evidential fit and its relationship to model goodness. We found that students understood that evidential fit confers accuracy and considered nuances of evidential reasoning, such as relevance and sufficiency. We discuss educational implications of these findings for advancing students’ evidential reasoning with scientific models.

## Introduction

Recent reforms in science education in the US have emphasized engaging students in knowledge productio n practices (NGSS, 2013; NRC, 2012). Knowledge produc tion practices, such as modeling, have therefore increasingly been a focal point in science curricul a. Giere (2004) defined models as, “…idealizations that scientists use to represent aspects of the world for specified purposes” (p. 742). Examples of models include the Copernican and Ptolemaic models of the solar system (Frigg et al., 2020) and Darwin’s model of natural selection (Passmore & Stewart, 2002). The practice of modelin g involves creating, evaluating, and refining model s to explain and predict phenomena (Ke & Schwarz, 2020; Kenyan et al., 2008; Schwarz et al., 2009). Scientific models are created and evaluated based on shared epistemic criteria for what counts as good models in science (Kuhn, 1977; Longino, 2002). Examples of epistemic criteria are fit with empirical evidence, explanatory power, fit with other established theor ies, conceptual consistency and coherence, and pars imony (Kuhn, 1977; Longino, 2002). Fit with evidence is considered as a central, perhaps the most important, criterion (Longino, 1996). Scientists create, evaluate and revise models in light of new evidence and even discard them if the model fails to fit a growing body of evidence. Fit with evidence is important because models with good evidence fit are more likely to resemble the world in desired respects (Bonjour, 2009; Strevens, 2020). In this study we used the term accuracy to mean how closely the model resembles reality in the desired respects. Although evidential fit is a central criterion for  making and evaluating scientific models, scientists often need to make tradeoffs among criteria (Kuhn, 1977). For example, a complex model that fits the evidence might not be parsimonious. A simpler model might offer greater explanatory power but not fit the available evidence as well as a more complex model (Longino, 1996). Scientists consider the tradeoffs between evidential fit and other criteria such as explanatory power, complexity, and  conceptual coherence in pursuit of an aim. For exa mple, models that will be shared for use by the public mi ght need to be parsimonious; however, models that w ill be shared with scientists who have expert domain knowledge can be complex. Therefore, a complex model that fits all available evidence might be altered to fit the most relevant evidence and still be understandable. Although scientists grasp the connection between ev idential fit and accuracy, there has been little research on how science students understand the rel ationship between these ideas. Prior research has n ot yet investigated how students view the connection betwe en evidential fit and accuracy; whether students se e evidential fit, and accuracy as connected, and further, whether students see evidential fit as conferring accuracy. We also do not know how students prioritize criteria in instances when criteria need to be weighed against each other; for example, whether models should fit the e vidence and be complex or whether they should fit l ess available evidence but be simple and easy to understand. Prior research has shown that fifth-grade students were able to develop normative criteria for scientific models as a class community (Murphy et al., 2021). These included the criteria that models should be ICLS2022 Proceedings 519 © ISLS understandable and that they should be supported by relevant evidence. Students are also able to prioritize criteria and provide justifications for why criteria are imp ortant including meta epistemic justifications abou t criteria importance in relation to each other (Murphy et al., 2021). Av-Shalom et al., (2018) found that all cl ass criteria lists that were developed during the intervention i ncluded some form of fit with evidence, which shows  that students are aware of the importance of the criteri on fit with evidence (Av-Shalom et al., 2018). Furt her, AvShalom et al., (2018) found that students are able to reason that scientific models should fit most of the evidence and it is preferable that there be no countereviden ce. Ryu and Sandoval (2012) found that upper elemen tary students were able to construct and evaluate argume nts according to evidential criteria; however, Sand oval and Millwood (2005) found that although students stated  that fit with evidence was important, 73% of stude nts did not provide sufficient data to support a claim and failed to explain how data supported claims. Although prior research has shown that students prioritize evidential criteria, less is known about what fit with evidence means to students, how fit with evidence relates to other criteria, and how students would reason about tradeoffs between different criteria (in inst ances when criteria compete). To address these issu es, we conducted an interview study with fifth graders following a five-week inquiry project in which students frequently used and discussed criteria for good models. Our specific research questions were as follows:

1. How do students conceptualize the relationship between evidence fit and accuracy?

2. How do students navigate the tension between potent ially competing criteria? (e.g., when more

understandable models are simpler but don’t fit the evidence)?

## Theoretical framework: Grasp of evidence

To guide our thinking and analysis, we used the Grasp of Evidence framework (Duncan et al., 2018). To grasp evidence means to understand how experts construct,  evaluate, and use evidence and how lay people can adequately use evidence themselves. Part of being able to engage with evidence involves meta-awareness of how evidence is constructed and how to adequately critique evidence which can be guided by ideals (epistemic criteria). Therefore, understanding how epistemic criteria gui de evidence construction and critique can help to d evelop sophisticated evidentiary reasoning. The Grasp of E vidence framework unpacks five dimensions of expert  and lay evidentiary reasoning: evidence analysis, evidence evaluation, evidence interpretation, evidence integration, and laypeople’s use of evidence. The first four dimensions capture experts’ evidentiary practices, which are the processes that scientists engage in when using evidence to further scientific knowledge. The fifth dimension, lay peoples’ evidentiary practices, captures the ways that laypeople can reason about evidence themselves. Although experts and lay peoples’ evidentiary practices differ, it is helpful for laypeople to understand scientists’ evidentiary practices so they can understand how scientific knowledge is produced. Gaining exposure to experts’ evidentiary reasoning skills can enable laypeople to better assess the science they encounter in their everyday lives by giving them insight into appropriate methods and validity of conclusions. Further, laypeople candevelop an understanding of the cognitive division of labor as they see that scientific knowledge is produced by many highly specialized credible experts which might engender trust in science. We were interested in how students conceptualize evidence in relation to scientific models therefore the dimension of the GoE framework that helped to guide analysis was dimension four, evidence interpretation which looks at how evidence gains significance in relatio n to a claim, argument, or theory. Before we explai n this dimension more fully, it is useful to consider how we define evidence. We lean on Walton et al’s., (20 08) definition: data becomes evidence when it is used in support of a claim. Looking at evidence in relation to models considers how evidence is explained by a model and how well the evidence supports the model. The aim o f evidence interpretation is seeking strong evidence to determine model validity.  Ideals (criteria) guide attainment of the aim; in this case, seeking strong evidence t o determine model validity. Laypeople can assess wh ether evidence is strong by considering the following ideals: relevance: how relevant the evidence is to the model; core support or refutation: whether the evidence supports core (versus peripheral); parts of the model; diagnosticity: precise fit with the model; comprehensiveness: the extent to which the evidence addresses multiple components of the model; and directness: how direct is the chain of reasoning. Engaging in  an assessment of the aims and ideals when interpreting evidence means that studen ts can understand how scientists interpret evidence  which should help guide them to interpret evidence in relation to models and decide between competing models. Therefore, in this study we considered how students interpreted evidence in relation to scientific models by analyzing how students saw evidence as supportin g models in terms of conferring accuracy. We were a lso curious how students would reason about the interplay between evidential fit for scientific models in terms of how much evidence is deemed sufficient to support a mod el and how contradictory evidence would impact mode l accuracy. We also wondered whether students saw evi dential fit as the eminent criteria or, like scient ists, they would make tradeoffs between other criteria depending on the aim. We will explore these questions in the results. ICLS2022 Proceedings 520 © ISLS

## Context and participants

Data from this study came from an implementation of  a five-week model-based inquiry unit in a public c harter school in the Northeast of the United States. Two fifth-grade classes (termed 5A and 5B) participated, with a total of 50 students. The demographics of the school were 60% White, 22% Asian, 9% African American, 7% Latinx, and 2% other; 5% of students were eligible for free  and reduced lunch. The school performs above avera ge on standardized state tests on Mathematics, Language Arts and Science. In the unit, students developed models to explain the phenomenon of eutrophication, which is the process by which an excess of nutrients causes an increase in plant growth which depletes the oxygen supply and causes fish to die by suffocation. Students built an understanding of eutrophication through analyzing reports of empirical studies and interacting with a simulation. Based on these two types of evidence, students created models in a new modeling software called [MEME] (Danish et al., 201 9) to explain why fish were dying in a local pond. The models consisted of components (parts) that were linked by mechanisms (processes) to create a causal account of why the fish were dying. The evidence was stored in [redacted], and students could link the evidence to the parts of the model to support the components and mechanisms.

The evidence consisted of reports of empirical rese arch and NetLogo simulations. Students sought to account for both types of evidence as they developed their models of eutrophication. Examples of reports given to students were an autopsy report explaining why t he fish died, graphs showing the amount of sunlight  and nutrients in the pond, and a fertilizer label to sh ow the chemical ingredients accompanied with report s of how fertilizer increased plant growth in a pond ecosystem. The agent based NetLogo simulations (Tisue & Wilensky,

2004) showed how the variables sunlight, fertilizer, and oxygen impacted fish death on a micro and a macro scale.

Model creation and evaluation were guided by a lis t of epistemic criteria that was developed by each class. The class criteria list was made by analyzing pairs of models about scientific processes, such as fossilization and life cycle of the frog, and coming up with a list of agreed and shared upon criteria. Half-way through the unit the students revised their class criteria lists bas ed on observations and critiques to their models du ring a gallery walk. The final class criteria list featured normative criteria such as models should make sense; each component (part) and mechanism (process) should have a clear explanation supported by reasons and evidence; be organized, clear, and explain what is happening; and models should include relevant evidence (Murphy et al, 2021).

## Data collection and analysis

At the end of the unit, seventeen students were int erviewed in dyads. The data for this study was take n from interviews with all nine dyads from class 5A. The data from this study specifically focused on a task that assessed students’ conceptions of model evidence fit. In the  task, students were asked a series of questions th at probed students’ conceptions about the relation between evidence and models. In the first part of the interview task students were asked the following questions to probe the relationship between evidential fit and accuracy: 1) What does it mean for a model to be accurate? 2) What is more important, accuracy or fitting the evidence?3) Can a model be accurate but not fit the evidence 4) Can a model fi t the evidence but not be accurate? 5) Does a model need to fit all the evidence? 6) Can a model that does n ot fit all the evidence be right? 7) What if there is evidence  that contradicts the model? The results from these questions will be explored in the results section under research question one. In the second part of the interview task, students were asked questions to probe their understanding o f the tension between scientific models that fit the evidence and are complex versus scientific models t hat are understandable but do not fit the evidence: 1) Which is more important for a good model, that it is understandable or that it fits the evidence? 2) Suppose scientists  wanted to build a model to explain why mosquito bi tes itch. Suppose that one scientist comes up with a clear, e asy to understand model, but it doesn’t fit the evi dence and another scientist comes up with a model that is com plex and harder to understand, but it fits all the evidence. Which model would be better? The results will be explored in the results section under research question two. The interviews were videotaped, and audio recorded,  and later transcribed. The transcripts were then reviewed in two rounds of analysis. First, the transcript of each dyad was analyzed to build an understanding of individual students’ concepts relating to model evidence fit across the interview task. Second, the transcripts were analyzed by comparing the responses of the dyads in  a cross-comparison method (Glaser, 1965) to build an understanding of students’ evidentiary concepts acr oss students. The rounds of analysis helped us see patterns within students, such as whether students saw evidential fit as conferring accuracy, and across students, such as why students thought that models did (or did not) n eed to fit all available evidence and how students saw contradictory evidence as impacting models (Duncan et al., 2018). Any issues that arose were resolved by presenting the data to the larger research team, reviewing the data, and discussing interpretations until we reached ICLS2022 Proceedings 521 © ISLS consensus. The data is presented in terms of numbers of students who gave responses (instead of number of dyads) because every student did not respond to every ques tion (sometimes one student in the dyad spoke but n ot the other) and sometimes students in a dyad disagreed. Therefore, the totals do not always add up to eighteen students for every question. The findings from the data analysis are presented in the next section.

## Research question 1: How do students conceptualize the relationship between

evidence fit and accuracy?

These analyses examined students’ responses to the seven questions in the first part of the interview (see above). The responses to the question, “What does it mean for a model to be accurate?” provide us with insights regarding what students meant by “accuracy.” Seven students responded that a model is accurate if it has “true information,” “true evidence,” or “matches real life.” Students d id not explain what they meant by true information and true evidence, but we assumed they are different ways of saying the same thing: a model is accurate if the evidence or information that informs the model is also accurate. We think this because, throughout the unit, teach ers and students often referred to evidence as “true inform ation”. These seven students therefore held a defin ition of accuracy that approximates scientists’ definition of accuracy. The following excerpt from Rochelle and Iona show their reasoning that models are accurate if they match real life: Rochelle: What it means to be accurate is like if say it’s a simulation or something like we did in class… if we do like the exact same things that we did in the simulations in real life it will turn out the same and that’s like accurate.

Iona: And then the simulation would be accurate because it’s similar to real life. Eight students said that a model is accurate if it has true information or true evidence, but they also added in at least one other criteria: makes sense, on topic, has all the criteria (on the class criteria list), is in order, neat, and complete. These students therefore saw a wider range of criteria as conferring evidence. Three stu dents explained that a model is accurate if it is on topic, makes sense, and is relevant, without any reference to evidence or true information.

We also found that students were able to reason abo ut how evidential fit impacts model accuracy. In response to the question, “ What is more important, accuracy or fitting the evi dence?” Thirteen students stated that accuracy was more important because if the evidence is wrong then the model will be wrong. The following quote from Maggie illustrates this understanding: Maggie: I think that it’s more important to be accurate because the evidence could be not true so it (the model) will fit it (the evidence) and still be not true. Two students responded that fit with evidence was b etter because it means the model will be accurate; further, if the evidence is true, then the model will provide a better understanding. Therefore, all fifteen students that prioritized either accuracy or evidential fit did so based on the same understanding: fit with evidence confers accuracy. Finally, two students explicitly stated t hat both accuracy and fit with evidence was needed because accurate evidence makes the model accurate and inco rrect evidence makes the model incorrect, showing t hat fifteen students understood that incorrect evidence would impact model accuracy. When asked “Can a model be accurate but not fit the evidence?” nine students responded that a model cannot be accurate without fitting the evidence because the evidence must be accurate for the model to be accurate; again, showing that students see incorrect evidence  impacting model accuracy. However, another common response (from nine students) was that a model can be accurate without fitting the evidence if the evidence is off topic, therefore showing that they have considered how irrelevant evidence (an aspect of evidence interpretation of GoE) could impact model accuracy (Duncan et al., 2018). We saw the same kind of evidentiary reasoni ng to the question “Can a model fit the evidence but not be accurate?” Twelve students responded that a model could fit the evidence but not be accurate because the ev idence is inaccurate. Three students asserted that it is not possible for a model to fit the evidence but not be accurate because if the model was accurate then it will fit the evidence, therefore showing understanding that evid ence fit confers accuracy. Two students responded t hat a model could fit the evidence but not be accurate because the evidence is irrelevant to the model. Thus, ten students used the idea of evidence-model relevance to account for instances when models can fit the evidence and not be accurate, as well as instances in which models can be accurate without fitting the evidence (Duncan et al., 2018). ICLS2022 Proceedings 522 © ISLS When asked “Does a model need to fit all the evidence?” students considered how much evidence was sufficient for a good model-evidence fit. Six stude nts said that a model should fit most of the eviden ce, five students said that a model should fit some but not all the evidence, and one student said a model should fit all the evidence. The justifications students provided for why models do not need to support all evidence show ed that they thought about how much evidence was sufficient  for scientific models in terms of relevance and comprehensiveness, which are ideals in the GoE framework (Duncan et al., 2018). Two students explained that a model does not need to fit all the evidence but tha t including the most relevant evidence was better. Another student claimed that it was sufficient to explain p art of the model, therefore going against the ideal  of comprehensiveness in the GoE framework (Duncan et al., 2018). In response to whether models needed to fit all the  evidence, two students (from the same dyad) questioned whether fit with all evidence was even attainable. First, the dyad thought about the evidence they had encountered in the unit. A few studies showed the chemical makeup of fertilizer and how fertilizer increased plant growth in ecosystems. The dyad reflected on this study and stated that the scientist might not know enough about the exact chemicals in the fertilizer that caused e utrophication, therefore the scientist might not ha ve enough domain knowledge to know if the evidence adequately  fits. Second, the dyad also reflected on when they  built their own model in the unit and how they used the m ost relevant evidence to support the model. Some st udies were ruled out as the unit progressed, so students did not link every piece of evidence to their final model. Due to these reasons, the dyad wondered whether fit with all evidence was even attainable. Students were able to reason about whether all the evidence needs to be right that supports a model. In response to the question, “Can a model that does not fit all the evidence be right?” Nine students responded that it was possible for a model to be right and not fit all the evidence. Eight of these students responded by thinking about either the evidence or the model in parts. Fo ur students responded that a model that does not fi t all the evidence can be right because part of the model wil l not be supported but the other parts will be supp orted, therefore going against the comprehensiveness ideal in the GoE framework (Duncan et al., 2018); wherea s four students responded that a model can still be right because part of the evidence does not fit, either b ecause it is irrelevant (one student), inaccurate (one student), or does not make sense (two students). Two students reasoned about evidence relevance by considering how a model can fit only the relevant evidence and still be right; a model does not need to fit irrelevant evidence. Three stu dents responded that the model would not be right b ecause it would not be accurate. Thus, students’ responses showed that they specified conditions under which models might be accurate even though they did not fit all the evidence (cf. Duncan et al., 2018). Research question 2: How do students navigate the tension between potentially competing criteria? (e.g., when more understandable models are simpler but don’t fit the evidence)?

In this section we will explore students’ concepts about the relationship between models that fit the evidence but are complex and hard to understand, and models that do not fit the evidence but are simple and underst andable. We chose this contrast because in class discussions, some students expressed a preference for simpler models that were easier to understand. But which model should b e preferred when simpler and easier-to-understand models do not account for the evidence? How do students think about the trade-offs between these criteria? In this section of the interview, we found that stu dents’ responses shifted from the first to the seco nd question. The questions were: 1) Which is more important for a good model, that it is understandable or fits the evidence? 2) Suppose scientists wanted to build a m odel to explain why mosquito bites itch. Suppose th at one scientist comes up with a clear, easy to understand  model, but it doesn’t fit the evidence and another  scientist comes up with a model that is complex and harder to understand, but it fits all the evidence. Which model would be better? In response to the first, general question about wh ich was better-understandability or evidential fit, more than half of students (ten out of eighteen) pr ioritized understandability because then they know they have the right evidence. These ten students therefore co nflated understandability with evidence fit. The fo llowing transcript excerpt shows how Iris conflated understandability and evidence fit: Iris: Ummm, understandable. Because if you understand (the model) most likely it will have the right evidence.

Int: Okay Iris: Because you can't understand a model if the evidence is not correct… but if the model's accurate then the evidence will make sense.

In response to the second question, nine students t hought that it was important for models to be both understandable and fit the evidence. This time, stu dents did not conflate understandability with evide ntial fit. Instead, students saw the criteria as separate and viewed them both as important. Four students explicitly stated that the more complex model was better because it meant the model was accurate; however, they emphasized that a model should both fit the evidence and be underst andable. All nine students explained how a complex model could be understandable: the scientist can explain the model to the audience, the audience can look up the words in the dictionary, the scientist can show where the  model starts, the scientist can make an easy one t hat is understandable, and complex models are also underst andable. The following excerpt from Axel highlights  that both understandability and complexity were needed because complex models can still be understandable: Axel: Well, I pick the one that you can understand, but it's hard (to choose) because if it’s just simple, easy, and it doesn't fit the evidence (the simple model) then you don't actually figure out the answer... And even if it's hard (the complex model) and not easy to understand, you can still understand some of it and figure out how they… and make an easy one, and one that has good, that has good evidence. You can combine the two. A smaller number of students, five, responded that the complex model that fit the evidence was more important than the model that was simple and easy to understand. These students prioritized the complex, hard to understand model over the simple, easy to understand model, because it fit the evidence and would be accurate.

## Discussion

Overall, the findings showed that most fifth-grade students in the intervention understood evidence as conferring accuracy and that incorrect evidence would make the  model incorrect. Students’ understanding of fit wi th evidence therefore somewhat parallels scientists’ understanding of the importance of the criterion evi dential fit for scientific models. Students also showed nuance in their evidentiary reasoning by thinking about how irrelevant ICLS2022 Proceedings 524 © ISLS evidence would not impact model accuracy and how mu ch evidence was sufficient by thinking about whethe r evidence could fit parts of the model or whether part of the evidence was enough. Students reasoned about the interplay of competing criteria by considering whether a more complex model that fit the evidence but was not understanda ble was preferable to a simple and clear model that  was understandable but did not fit the evidence. Initially, more than half of students conflated understandability and fit with evidence (they stated that understandable models will fit the evidence and vice versa); howev er, in response to a second, more concrete scenario, stude nts shifted their explanation to view understandabi lity and evidence fit as separate and important criteria for scientific models. The way students were able to reason about competing criteria of scientific models again mirrors the tradeoffs scientists make between criteria i n pursuit of an aim. Scientists hold empirical consistency as a key criterion of scientific models (Longino, 1996);  however, they recognize that this criterion cannot always be stringently applied (Hogan and Maglienti, 2001). The findings contribute to research by showing how students reasoned about evidence in relation to scientific models, that students understood evidenc e as conferring accuracy, and that students can pri oritize seemingly competing criteria. The results support p revious research that showed students understand th e importance of fit with evidence as a criterion (Av-Shalom et al., 2018; Murphy et al., 2021); however, they also extend prior research by showing that students in this study did not think models need to fit all available evidence and by reasoning about how counter evidence can mak e a model stronger (not weaker) by providing a counterclaim against which the scientist can strengthen the model. The findings also extend previous research by Ryu and Sandoval (2010) by showing that students have the capabilities to understand that evidential criteria for scientific products, such as models, are important for conferring accuracy, and the research by Sandov al and Millwood (2005) by determining not only that eviden tial fit is important but why, and how much evidenc e is sufficient to support models. Overall, students showed more nuanced and complex reasoning about evidential fit as a criterion for scientific models than previously shown by exhibiting reasoning at the metacognitive level about standards for model accuracy and what they deem to be sufficient standards of evidence for model accuracy.

## Implications

The results of the intervention show that engaging students in collective negotiations about how evidence supports a model, adapting a model to evidence, and discussing criteria utility and value, supports students’ metaepistemic scientific reasoning about the nuances in the relat ion between evidential fit and scientific models. T he findings therefore point to suggestions for science education. Students can be engaged in fruitful discussions, specifically about how relevant, and irrelevant evidence impacts model accuracy, how much evidence is sufficient for a model to be accurate, and how contradictory evidence impa cts model accuracy. It will be helpful for students  to understand which criteria confer accuracy (evidence, true information, reasons etc.) and which criteri a are important but do not impact accuracy, such as understandability. Students can also be engaged in discussions that weigh criteria against each other in pursuit of an ai m. For example, whether models should fit all available evidence and thus be complex and harder to understand or whether models should be simpler and easier to unde rstand but fit less evidence. When students are dis cussing tradeoffs, teachers can point out that they are tal king about the relevance of two or more criteria on  their class criteria lists and this is what scientists do, thus developing students lay understanding of experts’ reasoning about scientific models. The recommendations will assist students in developing a grasp of evidence (Duncan et al.,

2018) and a grasp of practice (Ford, 2008) as stude nts deepen both scientific practice and metacogniti ve

understanding of the criterion evidential fit in relation to models.
